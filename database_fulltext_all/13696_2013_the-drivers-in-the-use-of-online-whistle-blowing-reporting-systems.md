Ab stract: Online whistle-blowing reporting systems (WBRS) have become increasingly prevalent channels for reporting organizational failures. The Sarbanes–Oxley Act and similar international laws now require firms to establish whistle-blowing (WB) procedures and WBRSs, increasing the importance of WB research and applications. Although the literature has addressed conventional WB behavior, it has not explained or measured the use of WBRSs in online contexts that could significantly alter elements of anonymity, trust, and risk for those using such reporting tools. This study proposes the WBRS model (WBRS-M). Using actual working professionals in an online experiment of hypothetical scenarios, we empirically tested the WBRS-M for reporting computer abuse and find that anonymity, trust, and risk are highly salient in the WBRS context. Our findings suggest that we have an improved WB model with increased explanatory power. Organizations can make WB less of a professional taboo by enhancing WBRS users’ perceptions of trust and anonymity. We also demonstrate that anonymity means more than the mere lack of identification, which is not as important in this context as other elements of anonymity.

Key word s and p hrases: anonymity, computer abuse, IT artifacts, organizational failure, organizational governance, risk, trust, whistle-blowing, whistle-blowing reporting systems.

Org anizational ethical f ailures [6, 15, 86], including organizational fraud, abuse, misconduct, deviance, and other unethical behavior, are a global problem that have received increasing attention in research and practice. An organizational failure is “a single major incident, or cumulative series of incidents, resulting from the action (or inaction) of organizational agents that threatens the legitimacy of the organization and has the potential to harm the well-being of one or more of the organization’s stakeholders” [15, p. 128].

An effective means of uncovering and resolving organizational failures is whistleblowing (WB), “the disclosure by organization members . . . of illegal, immoral, or illegitimate practices under the control of their employers, to persons or organizations that may be able to effect action” [68, p. 680]. Research in the management and accounting fields has identified the importance of WB and its impact on organizational governance and financial returns (e.g., [6, 18, 76, 90]). WB has even been applied to report bad news in problematic software projects (e.g., [31, 32, 33, 34, 77, 95, 96]) and to report sexual harassment [42, 72, 86].

Recently, WB and information technology (IT) artifacts have collided because of social and legal developments. Legislation such as the Sarbanes–Oxley Act, the Federal Civil False Claims Act in the United States, and similar legislation in other countries have required publicly traded companies to establish channels through which whistle-blowers can anonymously report failures [6].<sup>1</sup> Thus, many organizations have opted to expand organizational governance to protect whistle-blowers and thus provide additional assurance for their stakeholders and society [18, 22]. Because of this trend, the use of online whistle-blowing reporting systems (WBRS)<sup>2</sup> has become an increasingly important method for internal control and reception of WB reports [19]. Recent events and downturns in various markets have further raised societal concerns about organizational failures, which have further increased the demand for WBRSs and WB protections. WBRSs allow users to submit WB reports anonymously via the Internet. WBRSs are different from traditional means of WB—such as notifying one’s superior or ombudsman; or using telephone hotlines, post office boxes, and other internal methods—because WBRSs require interaction with a computer system, which complicates the concept of WB because users must be able to understand the system to use it successfully.

Although WB research has been applied effectively in many contexts, it has not been developed or extended with IT in mind as the key mechanism. We propose that WB processes can be enhanced through carefully designed WBRSs because of the unique properties of trust, risk, and anonymity that are offered by WBRSs. By using WBRSs, additional IT artifact issues related to trust, risk, and anonymity offer several compelling research opportunities that should be considered [11, 15, 34]. In addition, our findings point to a variety of ways that practitioners can encourage the use of WBRSs by enhancing users’ perceptions of trust and anonymity in the WBRS, which should decrease expectations of potential retaliation against the whistle-blower from the organization. Importantly, we demonstrate that the conceptualization of anonymity is more expansive than the mere lack of identification, which is not as important in this context.

Given these opportunities, we propose a new WB model that measures anonymity, trust, and risk uniquely in the context of WBRS use. We name our model the “WBRS model,” or WBRS-M for short, and proceed as follows. First, we review the relevant WB literature and propose the WBRS-M. Next, we explain our methodology for testing the WBRS-M using an experiment with full-time professionals who reacted to hypothetical vignettes that described the use of a WBRS to report different kinds of computer abuse. We then discuss the results of our data analysis, followed by the contributions made by these results. Finally, we discuss the implications of the contributions of the research and practice, the limitations of the study, and future research opportunities.

## Background on Whistle-Blowing

## Scope of the WBRS Model

Sub stantial WB research has b een p ub lished ; thus, first it is crucial to explain the scope of the extant literature that is the foundation of our WBRS-M. The specific focus of the WBRS-M is to examine what best predicts the decision process that forms the intentions of whistle-blowers by using a WBRS—mostly in terms of risk, trust, and confidence in their anonymity when using the system. The WBRS-M does not consider other situational predictors or demographic variables, such as personality type or type of wrongdoing. In addition, the WBRS-M does not consider WB-related processes that are not directly involved in the WB decision, such as retaliation against the whistleblower, whistle-blower effectiveness, and the final organizational outcomes of WB. Such a holistic view of every contextual and process aspects of WB was proposed in a process model by Miceli and Near [62]. For theoretical concision and maximum contribution, we focus on a causal subset of their process model (Stage 2) that focuses only on the decision process preceding WB.

## Core Predictors of Intentions to Whistle-Blow

Researchers have explored what individual, situational, and organizational factors encourage WB. The WBRS-M adopts the assumption from much of the WB research that an individual does not choose to whistle-blow based on traditional cost-benefit calculus, as is commonly seen in other risky decisions that have potentially high benefits (e.g., [16, 45, 65, 71]). The key difference is that there are few, if any, personal benefits to WB. In fact, the risk of verbal abuse, intimidation, and job loss are high for whistle-blowers in large organizations, despite the various regulations that are supposed to protect them.

The WBRS-M builds on the underlying process that is undertaken by a whistleblower prior to act [64, 67, 69], as has been further supported in recent IT‑ related WB models [76, 77]. This process is based on the theoretical phenomenon of the “bystander effect” [40]—in which people “stand on the sidelines” and do not report inappropriate behavior, even though they know the behavior is wrong. In applying this theory to WB within the organization, four steps have been identified that must be followed for WB to occur. We build specifically on the parsimonious model proposed by Miceli and Near [62, 64] that later became the “prosocial organization behavior (POB) model” [64]. We adopt the underlying paths of this models because it provides the most accepted causal paths in WB to date in the literature.

As in the POB model [76, 77], the core propositions of the ethical decision to blow the whistle are as follows: (1) a problem must be perceived to be major before an individual will consider reporting it to an authority who can rectify it or prevent it from recurring [62, 67]; (2) the individual must have a sense of personal responsibility, or the obligation to report it [62, 67, 69] grows with the perception of the size of that problem; and (3) the stronger the individual’s sense of personal responsibility is, the stronger his or her motivation will be to report it [62, 67, 102], and the greater the likelihood will be that he or she will actually follow through and report the problem. One of the key foundations of the POB model is expectancy theory, which is leveraged to further explain motivations to whistle-blow. Before WB becomes relevant in an organizational context, at least one stakeholder who is involved in or witnesses an organizational failure must determine if it should be reported to an authority that can rectify the problem or prevent it from recurring [62, 67]. We term this initial perception as the failure ought to be reported [62, 69]. Next, a person’s assessment that he or she has a personal responsibility to report the failure is defined as the obligation that the individual should be the one to notify someone who can correct the given organizational failure [62, 67, 69]. If the individual perceives that he or she should report the organizational failure, he or she becomes more motivated because of the increased perception of responsibility [62, 67, 102].

Finally, an individual is motivated to whistle-blow when he or she believes that some positive change will occur in the organization as a result [67, 68, 69]. This causal link is based on expectancy theory [105], which is further extended by the reinforcement notions from cognitive learning theory [94]. Expectancy theory posits that individuals engage in behaviors when they perceive that it is possible to achieve desired outcomes [105]. Hence, the expectation that a desired action will occur is dependent on an individual’s ability to perform the action and the desirability of performing that action.

Cognitive learning theory [94] asserts that individuals learn about the effectiveness of WB through their experiences of the reinforcement that organizations provided to previous WB attempts. In a WB context, the desirability of the expected action is assumed to be positive; otherwise, the whistle-blower would be unmotivated to whistle-blow. If a potential whistle-blower sees that frequent organizational failures have occurred without resolution, or that previous whistle-blowers have been retaliated against, such negative reinforcement diminishes the desirability of WB. This reduced desirability then diminishes the motivation to act. Conversely, if organizational failures are infrequent or previous whistle-blowers have been rewarded, the desirability of a WB report would be more positive, thereby increasing the individual’s motivation to whistle-blow.

We found only two studies that validate the entire WB causal chain in one model (e.g., [76, 77]). Instead, the research has examined only portions of the WB chain or primarily on identifying the factors that may encourage or discourage WB [6, 18, 25, 59, 60, 61, 62, 64, 68, 69, 72, 90]. Recent research [76, 77] has extended this same model to examine the willingness of an IT manager to report bad news regarding a software project to upper management [67].

## The Whistle-Blowing Reporting System Model

In p rop osing our WBRS-M, we replicate the assumptions and basic causal chain originally proposed by Miceli and Near [62, 67] and validated by Park et al. [76, 77]. WBRS-M builds on this causal chain with extensions from Smith and Keil [95] and Street [100] that explicitly posit the importance of the risks of WB, the trustworthiness of the entity that receives the WB report, and the level of anonymity afforded by particular reporting channels. We explain these additions by first modeling the risk to the organization and to the self. We then address two forms of trust: trust in the report-receiving authority and trust in the WBRS. Finally, we explore the confidence of the user in the anonymity of the WBRS. We explain the motivation for these additions and the subsequent hypotheses next.

## Adding Risk to Organization Versus Risk to Self

Although the risk of reporting is endemic to WB because of the threat of retaliation<sup>3</sup> [34, 57, 86], the risk of reporting through WBRSs has not been modeled or measured explicitly in the research. Rather, the seriousness of the wrongdoing has been examined; however, the focus has remained primarily on the type of wrongdoing, such as fraud, sexual harassment, theft, waste, mismanagement, safety problems, and illegal discrimination [4, 63, 72]. Although the type of wrongdoing might signal risk to some degree, it is not an established, validated risk measure. Modeling and measuring risk itself is a more direct approach. Furthermore, the few studies that have included the seriousness of the type of wrongdoing have discussed the risk of retaliation, but have not measured it using an empirical model [44, 57]. The omission of the measurement of both the seriousness of the incident and general risk to the whistle-blower in extant models is a surprising oversight that, if addressed, could add greatly to the explanatory power of WB models. Building on the work of Lewis [44] and Smith and Keil [95], we propose that both of these types of risks (i.e., to self and to organization) are important considerations for the whistle-blower and should be accounted for explicitly in empirical models. In other words, we add the potential risk to the organization from the organizational failure itself [72, 95] and the risk to the potential whistle-blower of reporting the failure [86, 95].

## Perceived Risk to the Organization Encourages Whistle-Blowing

Some degree of risk of harm to the organization must be perceived in the organiza tional failure by the potential whistle-blower. For a potential whistle-blower to judge that a failure ought to be reported, he or she should perceive a nonroutine problem that potentially puts the organization, its stakeholders, or the general public at risk of harm [67, 72]. Such failures need to be serious and thus potentially detrimental to the organization beyond the routine problems that normally arise in the course of business [15]. Even if the organizational failure is highly problematic, if no one perceives it as such—whether because of a lack of ethics, lack of training, or general lack of understanding—it falls outside the scope of the WBRS-M. This occurs because of the lack of appreciation of organizational risk and lack of recognition that the failure ought to be reported. Sexual harassment is a good example of why this assumption is essential to the WBRS-M; the reality is that not every professional evaluates various forms of sexual harassment as serious, and even worse, some people are still prone to not taking it seriously at all [72, 86].

In the WBRS-M, the more serious the potential organizational failure, the more likely it is that management will emphasize to employees the importance of prevention [60, 68, 72, 86]. Very risky behaviors that can lead to significant failures are more likely to be widely known, documented, and discussed within an organization because of their potential impact. Such failures thus put an offender in jeopardy of suffering adverse consequences if caught [86]. Such high-risk scenarios include overt sexual harassment, fraud, catastrophic project mismanagement, and organizational deviance.

Conversely, when an organizational failure is perceived as less risky and damaging to an organization, the less likely it is that a potential whistle-blower will decide that it must be reported [67, 72].<sup>4</sup>

Recent research has shown that the type of wrongdoing that occurs in an organization differentially affects the willingness to whistle-blow [72, 86], although a set of direct measures of the seriousness of each type of risk is lacking. Nevertheless, the literature has shown that unethical behavior that has a greater likelihood of causing significant damage is more likely to be reported than less damaging behavior [57, 69, 72, 102]. Based on this background, and in the unique context of WBRSs, we predict the following:

Hypothesis 1a: The greater the perceived risk of the failure to the organization, the more the whistle-blower will believe that the failure ought to be reported.

Likewise, if the underlying assumptions of WB hold, the more serious the organizational failure is perceived by a potential whistle-blower, the more likely he or she will feel a personal responsibility to report it. Building on the expectancy theory [105], an increased level of risk to an organization increases the likelihood of negative outcomes for the organization. Consequently, it is more likely that upper-level management heeds the whistle-blower’s attempt to resolve the identified situation, resulting in a greater expectancy that the WB will yield positive outcomes. The increase in positive expectations will result in greater motivation and a sense of responsibility to whistleblow [66]. Similar arguments were made by Park and Keil [76], Park et al. [77], and Smith and Keil [95]. We thus replicate and test this core WB process in the context of WBRSs, proposing the following:

Hypothesis 1b: The greater the perceived risk of the failure to the organization, the more likely the whistle-blower feels a personal responsibility to report the failure.

## Perceived Personal Risk to the Whistle-Blower Discourages Whistle-Blowing

In contrast to the perceived risk of the failure to the organization, we posit that increased personal risks associated with WB discourage WB. We embrace the definition of risk beliefs as “the trustor’s belief about [the] likelihoods of gains or losses outside of considerations that involve the relationship with the particular trustee” [52, p. 726]. Notably, risk beliefs are contextual factors that are independent of the characteristics of the trustee [72]. If the risk beliefs are high, the trusting intentions required for WB increase. Research shows that the decision maker’s perception of risk is composed of the probability of negative outcomes and the magnitude of loss likely to be caused by those negative outcomes, which are defined as the “expected value of the loss” [95, p. 75]. We thus define the personal risk of reporting failure as a whistle-blower’s assessment of the likelihood and magnitude of the potential personal losses that might result from reporting organizational failures [17, 34].

A core WB assumption that the WBRS-M builds on is that potential whistle-blowers need to judge that because an organizational failure that they have witnessed is nonroutine, they do not have the ability or authority to directly resolve the failure and thus recognize the need for nonroutine third-party authority intervention to resolve the failure [17, 67, 68, 72]. This need for outside intervention partially increases personal risk because of a lack of control and uncertainty about the outcome. Furthermore, depending on the scope of the problem, WB can put the potential whistle-blower at risk of retaliation from the organization or its members because of the disclosure of privileged information that has been entrusted to the whistle-blower, who has now violated that trust [11, 67]. Even if the organization supports the WB act, the perpetrator(s) of the organizational failure could also seek a form of retaliation. Conversely, if an individual recognizes a routine business problem and feels comfortable reporting the problem through his or her normal chain of command, then it likely falls outside of WB. Examples include reporting likely missed sales forecasts, potential cost budget overruns, or issues with a subordinate who is performing poorly.

Important to the WBRS-M is that a whistle-blower’s sense of personal risk acts as a counterargument to the perceived risk to the organization. In other words, an individual is caught in the conundrum of whether to protect him- or herself or the organization. Self-protection discourages the reporting of failures, whereas protecting the organization encourages reporting. Another striking trade-off, as observed by Metzger [58] is the negative correlation between trust and risk, in which consumers do a cost-benefit analysis. In this study, consumers compared risk, trust, and benefits before they decided to disclose information, supporting the notion that trust is an antidote to risk. Other studies have empirically supported the notion of balancing trust against risk in making disclosures and other related trusting-intentions decisions (e.g., [8, 16, 51, 52, 55, 78]).

These concepts, findings, and explanations naturally extend to the WB context, because a whistle-blower needs to weigh risk and trust in a given scenario before disclosing potentially damaging information [72]. Again, a key caveat is that an individual’s WB calculus is more likely to be focused on risk and trust—not benefits— because explicit benefits are hard to find in WB [11, 67, 86]. To illustrate, in a difficult economic climate, a whistle-blower’s dependence on his or her job, both economically and emotionally, will provide the additional perceived risk of WB [59, p. 690]. First, the individual must assess the likelihood of succeeding in this task. Second, the magnitude of loss should be determined for such a failure, which can be everything from shame and embarrassment to job loss, lawsuits, or even jail [86]. By combining these two assessments, a whistle-blower can calculate the risk involved in reporting an incident to some other party [95]. In summary, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 2a: The higher the personal risk of reporting the organizational failure to a report-receiving authority, the less the whistle-blower will believe that the failure ought to be reported.

Building on the same background as the one used to establish Hypothesis 2a, we now consider how personal risk may affect one’s personal sense of responsibility to report an organizational failure. To explain this link, we return to the bystander effect, which helps explain WB [40], that is, people observe inappropriate behavior and do not report it, even though they know the behavior is wrong. Originally, this theory focused on violent criminal acts, but it has been extended to pertain to virtually any situation in which someone does not voice concerns about problems, including failures in organizational settings [1, 17, 23]. But the bystander effect is only relevant in a context in which the personal risk of reporting is invoked; otherwise, people report problems more willingly [1, 17].

A key contribution of the bystander effect is an explanation of the internal rationalization as to why a witness convinces him- or herself to shed personal responsibility to report a failure. Several studies have suggested four reasons why witnesses often rationalize away their inherent personal responsibility to speak up [1, 17, 23, 57, 97]. First, there is a perceived diffusion of responsibility, in which witnesses feel a diminished sense of responsibility when in a large group of people that allows them to avoid personal risk. A corollary of this expectation is the increased likelihood that large organizations tend to manifest this effect more than small organizations. Second, there is a desire to behave in socially acceptable ways, and thus to not want to take the personal risk to defy social norms. Namely, when witnesses see that others are not speaking up, they quickly evaluate that taking personal responsibility and speaking up, ostensibly against the silent group, will be viewed as socially unacceptable. Third, ambiguous situations increase the risk by promising highly uncertain outcomes—such as those involving legal complexity—which likely require higher levels of authority and expertise than those possessed by the witness to resolve. Fourth are all of the foreseen personal risks involved in speaking up, including retaliation, embarrassment, being viewed as a troublemaker, and straining important relationships. Hence, personal risk is a core trigger and explanation as to why people rationalize away personal responsibility under such circumstances. Accordingly, we predict the following:

Hypothesis 2b: The higher the personal risk of reporting the organizational failure to a report-receiving authority, the less the whistle-blower will feel the responsibility to report the failure.

## Adding Two Forms of Trust to the Model

As indicated above, risk and trust are often considered together, because it is hard to think of one without the other [52]. Although employees are believed to be more likely to whistle-blow if they trust the authority to which they report [34, 44, 95], trust in the receiving authority or in the WBRS artifact itself has not been directly measured in the extant WB research; it has only been theorized or implied [44, 95]. Thus, for theoretical completeness, we add two forms of trust to the WBRS-M: trust in the report-receiving authority and trust in the WBRS information quality.

The research has defined trust as the willingness to take a risk, and the level of trust can be an indication of the amount of risk one is willing to take [91]. Likewise, trust is often conceptualized as a means of coping with perceived risks [21]; thus, the greater the risk perceived in a given situation, the greater the amount of trust required to cope with the risk [91, 107]. By WB, an employer manifests trust in the report-receiving authority and is willing to accept the potentially negative outcomes resulting from his or her accusation. Because previous IT research has found that trust in the tool is separable from the person or organization behind the tool [56, 104], the WBRS-M considers these to be two important salient sources of trust.

## Trust in the Whistle-Blowing Reporting System’s Information Quality

Research has shown that people form trusting beliefs not only in other people but also in inanimate objects, such as technology [56, 104, 106] and information [35]. Although the concept of trust involves important differences depending on the target of the trust, there are several similarities. First, trust requires the trustor to safely rely or depend on the trustee [53]. When it comes to trust in people, it is manifested when the trustor allows him- or herself to become vulnerable to the trustee. With respect to trust in technology, it is manifested when people rely on the IT artifact to accomplish a task [53, 106]. Similarly, in regard to trust in information, trust is exhibited when people rely on information to make decisions and whether or not to accept it as factually correct [35, 50].

Second, trust is based on the perceived characteristics of the target. When the target is a person, trust is generally based on the extent to which the trustee exhibits the characteristics of benevolence, integrity, and competence [52]. When the target of trust is technology, the relevant characteristics include functionality, reliability, and helpfulness [56]. Finally, trust in information is dependent on the individual’s perceptions of the quality of the information, which includes, among other things, accuracy, representativeness, currency, coverage, and believability [24, 35].

Information quality influences people’s trust in information [50] and trust in information sources, such as community-driven knowledge sites [36] and health intermediaries [98, 108]. As information quality increases, trust increases, and intentions to use the information sources increase. These findings are highly relevant to the WB context because a WBRS is a key information source for making decisions about purported organizational failures [19].

In the context of this study, we are interested in the quality of the output of information from the WBRS. Our trust focus is thus labeled trust in WBRS information quality. Trust in WBRS information quality means that the whistle-blower believes that any information reported anonymously will be maintained in an accurate and representative form and not corrupted via processing by the system [37, 73]. Because the WBRS users do not have a practical way to verify the data reported through the system, using the WBRS first requires trust in the integrity of the information. For instance, the accuracy and quality of the information will be preserved, that erroneous and misrepresentative data will not be added, that confidential data will not be revealed, and so on [73]. Based on DeLone and McLean [13], we further assert that increased trust in the information quality of the WBRS enables the whistle-blower to expect more positive reporting outcomes when he or she intends to whistle-blow.

Because the core WB research has posited that individuals form their intentions based on the expectation that the WB will produce positive outcomes, trust enables the whistle-blower to discount the perceived potential for negative outcomes, as stated in Gefen et al. [21] and McKnight et al. [54]. Thus, the higher the WBRS user’s trust in the information quality, the more likely the user will be to use the system to report confidential information to others. We thus hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 3: A potential whistle-blower’s increased trust in the WBRS information quality increases his or her willingness to report the failure.

## Trust in the Report-Receiving Authority

In WB, the most salient target of trust outside the WBRS, is the organizational authority that receives the WB report [15, 95]. People are more likely to whistle-blow if they feel they can trust the authority to whom they are reporting, which is counterbalanced by how risky and costly they perceive an incident to be [15, 34, 95]. Trust is an area in which cognitive learning theory [94] shows promise in predicting WB behavior. Previous positive and negative experiences with organizational leaders—whether or not they are directly related to WB—positively and negatively reinforce trust in those leaders.

The potential for trust to override the fear of retaliation is driven by a broad knowledge of the importance of trust and risk in online settings. A strong predictive connection exists between trusting beliefs and trusting intentions [54]. Based on the trusting beliefs literature, specifically on Internet vendor trust [48, 54], we thus define trust in the report-receiving authority as the potential whistle-blower’s belief that the reportreceiving authority will act with benevolence, integrity, and competence [52] toward the whistle-blower.<sup>5</sup> Hence, if the authority is perceived as trustworthy—whether or not the authority provides any assurance of protection—a potential whistle-blower is much more likely to feel safe and be willing to whistle-blow. Similarly, individuals are more likely to trust organizations after major failures occur if the individuals trust the higher-level managers or other individuals within the governance structure [15]. In summary, we predict the following:

Hypothesis 4: A potential whistle-blower’s increased trust in the report-receiving authority increases his or her willingness to report the failure.

## Adding Confidence in Whistle-Blowing Reporting System Anonymity

Although the WB literature points to anonymity as a highly promising factor that should affect a whistle-blower’s calculation of risk and trust in WB regarding a particular incident [34, 59, 67, 68, 100], the research does not directly measure anonymity as provided through an IT artifact. This gap is notable because anonymity is often assumed to be a critical part of the requirement in WBRS to encourage reporting [19].

Instead, the WB literature that refers to anonymity focuses on the more limited idea of the inability to identify the individual [28, 34, 44, 59, 67, 68, 72], which is distinct from the more expansive conceptualization of anonymity afforded by WBRSs [79]. The related research on reporting poor project performance has indicated that the anonymity of the reporting tool increases the motivation to report bad news to upperlevel management [34].

Anonymity researchers have also shown that in a social context, there is a lot more to anonymity than a simple lack of identification (e.g., [79]). Our conceptualization of anonymity is based on the conceptualization by Pinsonneault and Heppel [79], who arguably provided the most robust conceptualization and measurement of anonymity using a system in a rich social context. We thus define confidence in WBRS anonymity as the degree to which an individual feels free from social evaluation and from retaliation threats from the organization or its members for reporting computer abuse in the organization’s WBRS. Notably, “anonymity can only significantly affect disinhibition, and other behaviors in general, when social evaluation is an important source of inhibition” [79, p. 97]. This conceptualization is particularly salient in the WB context because of the potential for social impact and retaliation. Meanwhile, the mere lack of identification through an online tool has been shown to produce only marginal anonymity, because individuals are able to construe and infer an identity through other contextual information [84, 85]. Therefore, anonymity is recognized as pivotal to whistle-blowers’ willingness to divulge information [85].

Pinsonneault and Heppel [79] conceptualized anonymity into five formative factors that we adopt in our study: lack of identification, diffused responsibility, lack of proximity, lack of knowledge of others, and confidence in the system to function. Lack of identification has already been addressed to some extent in the literature, but the other four constructs are new to the WB literature (for full definitions, see Table 1).

These five factors of WBRS anonymity are theorized to be effective because they promote disinhibition, which occurs when one feels free to perform public behaviors. Disinhibition is predicted by the degree to which one experiences private selfawareness, which is defined as an individual’s focus on his or her internal aspects [79]. These internal aspects may include perceptions, thoughts, feelings, standards, or values. If individuals feel low levels of private self-awareness, they are likely to experience deindividuation, the transformation of a group of individuals into a united entity that seems to respond as one collective mind. When individuals are deindividuated, they become immersed in the group and are less able to regulate their own behaviors based on their own internal processes, standards, and values, and instead rely on decisions of the group. Group member behaviors are thus regulated based on group, rather than individual, internal norms and standards.

Given this background, there are several mechanisms and consequences of WBRS anonymity that merit emphasis for our research model: the less people believe their comments can be identified, the more they feel a lack of direct responsibility for the consequences of their comments [14, 92], and thus the freer they feel to provide disinhibited comments. Moreover, the farther away people are from other socially relevant people [87], the less personally relevant identifying information others will know about them [83]. By implementing an online WBRS, the socially construed context and cues that would be readily available to individuals within an organization would be further stripped away, due to the lack of such a context afforded in an online anonymizing system [27]. Similarly, the more confident people feel that a system will not reveal their identity, the more they will experience disinhibition through decreased public self-awareness [41, 83] and that disinhibition will cause them to experience decreased perceptions of the risk of reporting organizational failures in terms of social risks and retaliation.

As individuals perceive less public self-awareness, they foresee fewer potential negative outcomes of WB, resulting in less overall perceived risk in WB because the risk of potential retaliation diminishes [79, 83]. As the level of perceived risk decreases for the whistle-blower, the potential for trust increases, as fewer negative outcomes are perceived, and the whistle-blower is more likely to consider potential positive outcomes, resulting in trust, as stated by McKnight et al. [54, 55]. Trust is inhibited when the whistle-blower experiences higher levels of risk, which would necessitate a greater level of trust to overcome the perceived risks. Lowered perceptions of risk enable reliance on the entire WBRS, which consists of both the tool used to report and the authority that receives the report, because the likelihood of retaliation and other negative outcomes have been minimized. By minimizing the potential for negative outcomes, the lower perception of risk of WB enables the whistle-blower to trust in both the WBRS and in the report-receiving authority. A related aspect of anonymity and trust in online environments on which research is shedding light is trust in the IT artifact used to share sensitive information and how the beliefs regarding anonymity relate to this kind of trust (e.g., [103]). Trust in the IT artifact means that “one securely depends or relies on the technology instead of trying to control the technology” [53, p. 329].

Trust in an IT artifact increases when a user believes the system “to have the functionality or functional capability to do some task the trustor wants done,” and consistently and reliably performs “what it is designed to do without frequent ‘crashing,’ delays, or unexpected results” [53, p. 330]. In WB, anonymity is crucial for the WBRS because anonymity is pivotal to the whistle-blower’s willingness to divulge information [85]. Given the high risks associated with identification, including job loss, ostracism, and threats, users must substantially trust or feel secure about depending on the WBRS before they use the system.

We hypothesize that users’ confidence in WBRS anonymity will increase their trust in the output (i.e., information) of the WBRS. As per Kim et al. [37] and Nicolaou and McKnight [73], information quality is key to perception of the WBRS as effective. Thus, the perception that the WB process is anonymous will necessarily include the belief that the information reported through the WBRS is trustworthy, that is, accurate, representative, and free from information captured behind the scenes that the whistle-blower did not intend to report [24, 53, 77]. ## Methodology

We tested our p rop osed mod el using an online experiment grounded in the highly salient context of computer abuse.<sup>6</sup> We chose computer abuse because it is a current organizational problem that is pervasive and widely known and understood [10, 81]. Conveniently, this form of organizational failure represents a spectrum of mild to severe risk exposure for an organization and allows us to test a more natural range of risks.

To test WB in a computer abuse context, we used a hypothetical scenario method to provide the treatments of our online experiment. Several studies involving questions about unethical, antisocial, deviant, and criminal behaviors have determined the appropriateness of this method for researching content such as ours [75, 80]. This method is also used frequently in management studies of computer-related abuse and ethics issues [10, 51, 93]. A thorough review in Siponen and Vance [93] established several strengths and advantages of the scenario method for tracking disclosures or actual behaviors, including greater accuracy and validity.

A key methodological advantage of the scenario method for our study is its ability to measure intention indirectly. Such measurement is important in the context of ethical behavior, in which the participants are prone to respond in socially desirable ways or to conceal their true tendencies [101]. The scenarios describe the behavior of another person in hypothetical terms, thus making the reporting less intimidating or risky [26].

## Scenario Design

To increase the generalizability of our research, the participants responded to randomized, hypothetical scenarios concerning the disclosure of computer abuse through a WBRS in a professional setting. Our vignettes were created to randomly manipulate six independent variables with two conditions each, to represent the range of risk and various circumstances under which the reporting occurred. Our software truly randomized every manipulation to create 1 of 64 potential vignettes for each participant.<sup>7</sup> Table 1 describes the operationalization of each manipulation. (The full text of the scenarios is available upon request of the authors.) Two careful rounds of scenario and pilot testing were conducted to ensure that these manipulations worked properly.<sup>8</sup>

## Participants

Our study used a carefully selected, paid panel of 202 online participants, who were full-time professionals. To ensure anonymity and increase generalizability, we hired a professional market research and survey firm to recruit the participants. The firm randomly targeted qualified participants from its list of over 2 million panelists and only provided us with complete, attentive responses.<sup>9</sup> We received the human subjects approval for the studies, provided full disclosure, and guaranteed anonymity. The participants’ average age was 41.3 years (SD [standard deviation] = 13.4), and average work experience was 20.0 years (SD = 13.5); 48 percent were male and 52 percent were female. In terms of educational attainment, 14.4 percent had completed only high school, 37.8 percent had completed some college, 37.3 percent possessed an undergraduate degree, 9.5 percent a master’s degree, and 1.0 percent a doctoral degree.

Online panels are used frequently in behavioral research (e.g., [2, 3]). Collecting data through market research firms offers several advantages for gaining high-quality data from working professionals [3]. In our study, we never had access to the participants names, and that level of anonymity is particularly useful because it engenders honest self-report responses to questions regarding sensitive topics, such as internal computer abuse in the workplace [3]. Moreover, Internet panels allow researchers examining topics of a sensitive nature to receive responses that are less inhibited by social desirability effects because anonymity is ensured [3]. Moreover, data collected over the Internet via a panel of respondents are more reflective of the broader population than data collected in more restricted settings [2, 5].

## Measures

The Appendix details our measures and their sources in the literature. We first gathered background information on the respondents’ disposition to trust, gender, age, education, and work experience. After the participants received and processed their randomized scenarios, they filled out the postexperiment measures. These included the risk beliefs concerning the scenario and risk beliefs concerning reporting the incident, the five anonymity subconstructs, belief that the failure ought to be reported, perceived responsibility to report, willingness to report, trusting beliefs, and trust in the reporting tool.

## Discussion

Our results sup ort the WBRS-M. First, we replicated the core paths from the literature that failure ought to be reported significantly predicts responsibility to report failure, which subsequently predicts willingness to report failure. Second, we found that the perceived risk of the failure to the organization (i.e., computer abuse) increased both the belief that the failure ought to be reported (H1a) and that the whistle-blower has an increased responsibility to report the failure (H1b). Third, we found that the personal risk of reporting the failure was a significant negative predictor of the whistleblower’s belief that the failure ought to be reported (H2a) and of responsibility to report the failure (H2b).

Fourth, the two forms of trust also proved to be important predictors of WB. We found that trust in WBRS information quality increased the whistle-blower’s willingness to report the failure (H3). Trust in report-receiving authority increased the willingness to report the failure (H4).

Fifth, confidence in WBRS anonymity was a key construct that directly influenced the perceived risk and trust that influenced WB. Confidence in WBRS anonymity significantly decreased the personal risk of reporting the failure (H5). Confidence in WBRS anonymity significantly increased trust in report-receiving authority (H6a) and trust in the WBRS information quality (H6b). Finally, both women and those with more education had a lower willingness to report the failure than men or those with less education.

## Contributions to Research and Theory

The three key contributions of our model are (1) the relationship between trust and risk in WB, (2) our multidimensional conceptualization of confidence in WBRS anonymity, and (3) the high variance explained in predicting WB intentions.

## The Relationship Between Trust and Risk in Whistle-Blowing

The WB literature has generally assumed the presence of risk with WB because of the high likelihood of retaliation; however, it has only empirically validated this relationship in the context of reporting bad news for software development projects [34, 95]. However, this type of organizational failure (e.g., computer abuse) affects the whistle-blower’s perception that the failure ought to be reported. By including both the perceived risk of the failure to the organization and the personal risk of reporting the failure, we add the key considerations of WB that are important in organizations and better explain how risk might work in WB.

We found that both forms of risk affected the degree to which participants thought that the failure ought to be reported. By separating risk into two components, we were able to identify the differential effects of risk to the individual and risk to the individual’s organization and found that perceived risk to the individual’s organization was the more important factor. Notably, risk to the organization encouraged WB, whereas WB was discouraged by risk to self.

Although trust has been discussed in the WB literature, this is the first study to model and empirically measure any form of trust in a WB model. We conceptualized trust as two distinct constructs to be manipulated: trust in the report-receiving authority and trust in the WBRS information quality. (We also used disposition to trust as a covariate that turned out to be nonsignificant.) Our results show that not only are these two forms of trust theoretically and empirically distinct, they each contribute significantly to willingness to report the failure. Thus, both forms of trust meaningfully extend the WB model in explaining users’ willingness to report, beyond their responsibility to report failure.

Based on an extensive literature review of risk and trust, we model risk first and trust second. Our research indicates that some risk must predate trust, because trust is built on the existence of risk [52, 55].<sup>10</sup> Reporting an organizational failure is an act that opens the potential whistle-blower up to future retaliation. Thus, the whistle-blower must have some trust in both the process and the report-receiving entity to subvert the identified risks.

Our results indicate that the two risks (to self and organization) are important predictors of the first portion of the WBRS-M: that the failure ought to be reported and the individual’s responsibility is to report riskier types of failures only. Both forms of trust are also important for the last portion of the model related to the individual’s willingness to report failure. Thus, the presence of trust on the part of the whistleblower directly affects his or her willingness to whistle-blow, whereas risk produces only a small impact by means of the core WB process.

To further establish that we correctly modeled risk and trust by placing them in different portions of the model, we performed a post hoc analysis to examine if they were all modeled as equal, simultaneous predictors of the willingness to whistle-blow. R<sup>2</sup> only increased from 0.727 to 0.728; adding the two forms of risk added no meaningful further explanation of the willingness to report failure. Likewise, neither form of risk had a significant influence on WB intentions; however, both forms of trust exerted a significant influence. These results validate that the risk constructs should indeed appear earlier as part of the decision.

## Multidimensional Conceptualization of Anonymity

The addition of the IT artifact-based anonymity construct (i.e., confidence in WBRS anonymity) was also a key contribution to research. First, anonymity is a WB factor that is widely acknowledged as important in the WB literature, but is rarely measured directly. The few times that it has been measured, only the identification component of anonymity was considered. Thus, our test of the WBRS-M constitutes the first time that an extensive conceptualization of anonymity has been modeled and tested in the WB literature. Our conceptualization of anonymity is particularly useful in our context, because WB decisions require substantial trust in and vulnerability (assuming risk) to another party. Therefore, a major contribution of this study is that it theorizes and provides empirical support for the influence of WBRS anonymity on trust in this context.

Given that confidence in WBRS anonymity was a significant predictor of the two forms of trust in the model, we explore how its various dimensions may alter the personal risk of reporting failure and the two forms of trust. This post hoc analysis models the subdimensions of anonymity onto each of these three constructs separately and explores whether they influence the given construct significantly and in which direction (see Figure 3).

The results of this post hoc analysis reveal several interesting relationships that should be pursued in future research. Notably, lack of identification was not a significant predictor of any of the intermediate constructs; rather, the strongest factor was confidence that the WBRS will function, followed by a lack of knowledge of others. A likely explanation is that a lack of identification alone does not provide true anonymity, especially in WB cases, where an individual’s identity can often be guessed based on contextual information (i.e., knowledge of others). These results point to why this richer conceptualization of anonymity is highly salient to WB.

## Contributions to Practice

This study has several important findings that are relevant for practice. First, extending the reporting of computer abuse in an organization is a natural and highly relevant extension of WB research because this domain encompasses the three salient elements of WB outlined in Near and Miceli [69]: (1) a wrongdoer commits an act believed to be improper, (2) a whistle-blower observes the wrongdoing and reports it to an authority, and (3) an authority receives the report of wrongdoing. We show that WB and WBRS can be applied successfully within the context of computer abuse. Because billions of dollars are lost to computer abuse, it is important to identify socio-technical methods for preventing, deterring, or discovering when organizational failures occur that go beyond the use of the controls and information security policies [10, 81, 99]. Managers could thus adapt the WBRS mechanisms to include this threat and thereby be better informed of its pervasiveness within the organization. Ostensibly, if the WBRS-M works well in explaining and predicting ways to increase WB when computer abuse occurs, it should likely work with other forms of organizational failures.

Second, our study highlights the importance of the use of a WBRS to encourage organizational members to whistle-blow. Important to practice, trust is most likely to be engendered through the effective design of a usable and accurate WBRS [38]. The impact of the individual’s trust in the IT artifact is particularly intriguing, given that the artifact is under the full control of the designers and can perhaps be most easily improved. To increase trust in the IT artifact (i.e., WBRS), the reliability, dependability, and quality of the user interface are pivotal: “the entire system infrastructure should demonstrate quality, for deficient software at one level may hurt perceptions at several levels” [53, p. 330].

We suggest that WBRS developers focus on design considerations that can positively influence the five subdimensions: confidence in WBRS anonymity: lack of identification, diffused responsibility, proximity, knowledge of others, and confidence in the system to function (perhaps with the strongest emphasis on system functionality and knowledge of others, because these were the strongest influencers in our study). To illustrate, there needs to be an improvement in the whistle-blower’s perceived anonymity in the WBRS beyond the simple system deidentification of the individual. These systems should also emphasize, through cues, prompts, and in its display, the importance of the whistle-blower reporting the organizational failure. Positive feedback could be used to increase the perceived responsibility of the whistle-blower toward WB. For example, the subcomponent “diffused responsibility” results suggest that all organization members should have equal responsibility for reporting failures—not just internal audit and management.

The results also indicate that all contextual information should be stripped from WBRS reports and the WBRS data collection process. If a whistle-blower can be identified by a small group of individuals who are very familiar with each other, the perceived anonymity of the whistle-blower may be reduced sharply because these individuals would be able to infer the individual’s identity from a few cues [79, 84] (i.e., knowledge of others).

The results further indicate the importance of the user’s confidence in the system to function concerning confidence in WBRS anonymity. This component suggests that practitioners should work to train WBRS users by providing workshops and tutorials on how to submit or complete reports. Moreover, it would be beneficial to use a proven, third-party vendor to provide a tool that is certified to never capture userrelated information (including name, IP [Internet Protocol] address, work location), and to explicitly communicate this to end users to increase the assurance of anonymity provided by the WBRS.

Moreover, we find that the WBRS should not be located in public or common areas, because this increases the risk of WB through the proximity construct. Instead, WBRSs should be available online at all hours and through various platforms and locations, so that the whistle-blower can choose the best and therefore least risky circumstance. By reducing the perception that others can “eavesdrop” and observe the actual usage of the system, the whistle-blower will face fewer risks in reporting an organizational failure. Again, this is where the role of third parties providing WBRSs proves immensely important.

Furthermore, trust in the WBRS can be built through consistent and reliable availability, so that potential whistle-blowers have increased confidence that the system will function. If employees are unable to access or effectively use the tool, it is unlikely that they will trust such a tool to report incidents. Thus, all aspects of the WBRS, including the user interface, should demonstrate high system quality to the user because this will increase trust in the IT artifact [53]. Again, this points to the use of a certified third party specializing in hosting, securing, and delivering such tools, rather than building such a tool in-house. Third-party certification processes similar to TRU STe or Verisign could also be efficient [49].

Third, we show the importance of trust in the report-receiving authority in fostering the willingness to report failure. Trust is built slowly over time [43] and damaged trust from organizational-level failures is particularly difficult to repair [15, 22]. Hence, it is crucial for managers (and organizations in general) to develop reputations for being trustworthy and for dealing with WB cases professionally, consistently, fairly, and anonymously. Successive positive interactions between managers and potential whistle-blowers can build such trustworthiness. A key predictor of WB from cognitive learning theory explains that individuals learn about the effectiveness of WB through their experience with the negative or positive reinforcement that organizations provided in previous WB attempts [94]. WB reports should thus be handled in a predictable, reliable, fair, and consistent manner by the report-receiving authority. Arguably, nothing can undermine trust in the report-receiving authority more than cases of inequality, a lack of thorough investigations, and inconsistency, as explained by the organization justice literature [12, 30, 74].

## Limitations and Future Research

The generalizability of our study is limited in several ways, thus providing opportunities for future research. First, we did not examine actual computer abuse incidents and WB with an actual WBRS. Hence, the next natural step in future research would be to provide various WBRS prototypes that manipulate anonymity, along with scenarios. Data on actual WB, such as other unethical behaviors, is difficult to collect using traditional techniques [101], but would be immensely valuable to this line of research. Another approach would be to ask respondents to disclose their actual experiences. The most effective approach for gathering WB data has not been conclusively determined in the literature. Second, we collected data from only U.S. respondents. Culture has been shown to be a highly salient predictor of trust and IT artifact use [45, 47, 82, 109]. Thus, cross-cultural considerations of WB and WBRS need to be theorized and studied to test and extend the WBRS-M [9, 46].

Second, an individual’s perceptions of risk might actually play a bigger role in our model under the circumstances of a real-life WB scenario as opposed to a hypothetical scenario where, in reality, there is less at stake. Although we demonstrated that personal risk is an important part of our model, it was likely downplayed empirically and would be stronger in real-life situations.

Third, future research could explore how the design of the WBRS and the process of using a third party could further improve the perception of isolation and thereby reduce the risk of reporting. WB via one’s own computer could be perceived as particularly risky, regardless of technical assurances. Thus, the willingness to report may be improved if it occurs at a third-party location. Likewise, it would be useful to assess the impact of browsing anonymizer software on personal risk. Along these lines, WBRS researchers could examine the use of linguistics natural-language processing systems to rewrite reports to decrease the likelihood that a person’s writing style will be recognizable.

Finally, future research could also explore whether additional forms of trust should be considered in the context of WB. This is an opportunity to go beyond trust involving the IT artifact and trust-receiving authority to consider institution-based trust, a global trust construct related to trust in society’s institutions (e.g., in terms of their structural and legal assurances). Given the legal implications of many WB cases, institutionbased trust and legal knowledge could play important roles in WB. Moreover, the constructs of distrust should be considered because emerging literature is revealing that trust and distrust are related and often important to consider together, although they are separate. As separate constructs, their predictors and effects on WB could be highly nuanced. Future research should thus endeavor to address the degree to which distrust is an important consideration in WB.

## Conclusion

WB has long b een an imp ortant org anizational p henomenon that potentially places the organization and the whistle-blower at great risk. WB has gained increased prominence in practice and in the literature as widespread organizational failures have led to additional laws and WBRS to facilitate WB. Of particular importance is the Sarbanes–Oxley Act and similar international laws that require firms to establish WB procedures and WBRSs. In this context, we proposed a new model, the WBRS-M, to study the willingness to whistle-blow via a WBRS, with the addition of trust, risk, and the multidimensional construct of anonymity. We found that these nuanced conceptualizations and measures contribute important knowledge on WB that should enhance both research and practice in this area.
