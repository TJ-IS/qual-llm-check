# Making e-Government systems workable: Exploring the evolution of frames

Bijan Azad <sup>a,\*</sup>, Samer Faraj <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Business Information & Decision Systems, Olayan School of Business, American University of Beirut, Bliss Street, 11-0236, Beirut, Lebanon <sup>b</sup> Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, Que., Canada H3A 1G5

Available online 30 January 2008

## Abstract

As with other IT projects, implementing workable e-Government systems requires bringing together diferent stakeholders and bridging their perspectives during implementation. These perspectives often take the form of competing technology frames consisting of diferentiated beliefs, interests, technology evaluation routines and artifact characteristics that need to be recognized and reconciled in order to make e-Government systems workable. Prior research, while recognizing the importance of frame evolution in making systems workable, has rarely described the underlying processes. This paper builds on a 10-year e-Government implementation to describe and analyze the processes of frame evolution—namely, frame diferentiation, frame adaptation, and frame stabilization—that can help translate and reconcile competing frames into a truce frame. The examined properties of these specific processes are described and their dynamics are analyzed, thereby extending the e-Government implementation literature through a deeper understanding of frame evolution processes.

## 1. Introduction

As with other IT projects, the implementation of e-Government<sup>2</sup> (e-Gov) needs to recognize the role of different stakeholders and integrate their perspectives to make the target systems workable (Fountain, 2001; Danziger, 2004; Norris and Moon, 2005; Andersen and Henriksen, 2006). These diferent perspectives take the form of competing technology frames consisting of diferent beliefs, interests, technology evaluation routines and artifact characteristics (Garud and Rappa, 1994; Faraj et al., 2004; Luna-Reyes et al., 2005; Chou et al., in press). Researchers have elucidated how, from a technology frames standpoint, diverse stakeholder interpretive schemes, perceptions, and reframing are essential to understanding system development and implementation (Orlikowski and Gash, 1994; Pinch and Bijker, 1987). Indeed, agreement on system design and function is usually considered an indication that stakeholders’ technology frame is stabilized around a dominant frame (Bijker, 1993; Orlikowski, 1992b).

The technology frame lens has been fruitfully applied in a variety of settings and has enhanced our understanding of the interaction between the interpretive schemes of stakeholders and IT-driven organizational changes (Davidson, 2002; Barrett, 1999). However, technology frame research has been criticized for overemphasizing frame diferences while underplaying the changing aspects of frames, i.e., the course of frame evolution and its processes (Davidson, 2006). Furthermore, the prevailing approach to frames has primarily been socio-cognitive, so relatively little attention has been paid to the important and complementary role of social and political interests (Lin and Silva, 2005). Therefore, there is a need to study how technology frames evolve during complex IT implementations and how frames evolve and stabilize around e-Gov systems with workable arrangements (Orlikowski and Yates, 2006; Wagner and Newell, 2006; Danziger, 2004).

In this paper we analyze certain technology frame evolution processes which were examined during the implementation of an e-Gov project. e-Gov implementation ofers a rich context to explore frame evolution processes. This context, which is rare in prior research, is strongest in the public sector. This is because, the greater diversity of stakeholders’ technology frames in the public sector is a fact of life, and socio-political interests play a simultaneous yet distinct role in shaping the frames (Andersen and Henriksen, 2006). The focus of this research is on describing and analyzing the frame translation processes. In other words, the focus is primarily on understanding the ‘‘why” and the ‘‘how” of frame evolution, which creates a path toward relative frame stability in the context of the project under analysis. We find that three potentially generic frame translation processes (frame diferentiation, frame adaptation and frame stabilization) can lead to a deeper understanding of the underlying frame evolution dynamics during an e-Gov project implementation.

In this paper, these frame evolution processes are examined and illustrated through a case study of the implementation of a 10-year, \$25 million e-Gov World Bank project in a challenging institutional environment—post-civil war reconstruction of a Mediterranean country. The project is considered a rare success according to external oversight bodies, since it produced results despite the challenging institutional environment within which it was implemented (UNDP, 2005a,b; World Bank, 2001, 2003, 2005). The contribution of this paper is twofold: First, it points out the utility of viewing the implementation of an e-Gov change efort through the ‘‘technology frame” lens, highlighting the underlying frame evolution processes. Second, it heeds the call in the technology frame literature (Davidson, 2006) to focus on frame changes and develop a better understanding of the underlying processes.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Stable technology frames and making systems workable

Research emphasizing the contextual aspects of e-Government adoption has long emphasized the importance of taking into account the diverse perspectives of key stakeholders on system design and function (Fountain, 2001; Danziger, 2004; Heintze and Bretschneider, 2000; Moon and Norris, 2005; Andersen and Henriksen, 2006). Indeed, diferences in stakes, resistance and conflict often take the form of struggles around technology frames. Not surprisingly, the technologies that are widely accepted and used are those that achieve stabilization of their technology frame within and among the diverse stakeholder groups (Bijker, 1993; Orlikowski and Gash, 1994). One stream of research (e.g., Davidson, 2002) emphasizes the cognitive aspects of technology frames among members of the relevant social group, which is consistent with the work of Orlikowski and Gash (1994). Another stream of research emphasizes the stabilization aspects (e.g., Lin and Silva, 2005) of the technology frames, which is consistent with Bijker’s (1993) formulation.

Both research streams have produced a deeper understanding of frame congruence and a better understanding of its impact on implementation outcomes. For example, researchers have highlighted frame incongruence as the cause of project failure in such diverse settings as groupware (Orlikowski and Gash, 1994), ERP implementation (Wagner and Newell, 2004), the introduction of IS in a telecommunication setting (McGovern and Hicks, 2004) and the IT requirements definition in a health care insurance organization (Davidson, 2002). Major diferences in technology frames among stakeholders and a failure to converge around a common frame can play a major role in stalemating an IT project or may even cause it to fail outright (Barrett, 1999). Recent research has recognized the importance of frame stability for successful implementation, or frame instability, in the case of failure (Cadili and Whitley, 2005; Wilson and Howcroft, 2005).

How frames evolve toward relative stability remains an open research question. Indeed, Davidson (2006) has identified a dearth of technology frame research from a process perspective—i.e., there is little information available about the framing/reframing process and its underlying dynamics. Thus, obtaining a deeper understanding of how technology frames are situated and ultimately transformed is important for improving the efectiveness of IT and e-Gov implementation. Some IT and e-Gov researchers have started to acknowledge that as frame stability evolves, it often entails a delicate balance between multiple interests, which is achieved through active translation of stakeholders’ frames (Lin and Silva, 2005; Chou et al., in press). The process of making IT and e-Gov systems ‘‘workable” (Orlikowski and Yates, 2006; Wagner and Newell, 2006; Luna-Reyes et al., 2005) can be seen as largely a matter of evolving a stable frame. This evolution can be a contingent and contested process involving repeated interactions wherein stakeholder groups advance competing technology frames that reflect their unique beliefs, interests, technology evaluation routines and artifact characteristics.

## 2.2. Frame evolution through frame translation

As can be seen from the details of the case presented in this paper, workable implementation of IT requires a highly contextualized process (Hackney et al., 2006) involving deployment and significant functionality trade-ofs. This is needed in order to enact an emergent system artifact (Desouza et al., 2007) that can take account of specific transition and developing country environments (Chau et al., 2002). Achieving a workable system may not require a priori consensus or full-scale agreement, as is argued in much of the IT implementation literature. Instead, an operational solution can reflect a fragile equilibrium among competing frames, rather than acceptance of a dominant frame. Thus, we suggest that the process of making a system workable is contingent on shaping and melding competing frames into a somewhat stable arrangement. Feldman and Pentland (2003) present an illuminating description of stabilized routines as localized cessation of ‘‘procedural warfare” among organizational groups. Along the same lines, we suggest that a truce frame represents a similar armistice regarding the features and changes brought about through IT and e-Gov implementation.

Frame evolution entails a complex and emergent process of translation of competing frames, which in turn requires intensive negotiations (Callon, 1986). Translation refers to the process by which core actors enroll others in support of a central vision, shared interest or joint actors’ association (Latour, 1987). Thus, discovery of such processes is important in furthering technology frame research. The focus on the translation of frames entails an explicit recognition of the fact that in such organizational settings, implementation is the process of making systems workable, a process that is situated, contested and temporally unfolding. It should be noted that ‘‘by translation we understand all the negotiations, intrigues, calculations, acts of persuasion” which lead to a relatively stable configuration of technology (Callon and Latour, 1981, p. 279).

Furthermore, negotiations, in tandem with ‘‘calculative and persuasive acts,” can be expected to figure prominently in the realization of translation in practice. These calculative and persuasive acts are goal-directed IT implementation activities that are imbued with agency (Strauss, 1979; Emirbayer and Mische, 1998; Boudreau and Robey, 2005). Some IT and e-Gov implementation researchers have emphasized the importance of conflict and negotiation in creating a path towards the mutual conditioning of systems and organizations (Ovaska et al., 2005; Wagner and Newell, 2006; Luna-Reyes et al., 2005; Chou et al., in press). Although these researchers have established the centrality of negotiations for evolving a stable frame, the underlying process and context of such negotiations remain unexplored. Our focal research interest is in analyzing the latter negotiation process and context. This is expected to deepen our understanding of how the translation of frames unfolds towards a situated truce frame.

## 2.3. The process of frame translation: an analytical framework

We suggest that e-Gov frame evolution, as a set of situated practices (Lynch, 2001; Schatzki, 2001; Orlikowski, 2002), involves three interrelated translation processes: frame diferentiation, frame adaptation and frame stabilization. In our research, we try to unpack these frame translation processes to provide a deeper understanding of the underlying dynamics of frame evolution during the e-Gov implementation.

First, frame diferentiation refers to the process through which multiple perspectives come into interaction with each other and then solidify into opposing frames, which often lead to a blockage and sometimes to an ultimate stalemate. This process highlights the fact that multiple perspectives are a given in any public organizational setting where e-Gov implementation is attempted. However, the diferentiation of these perspectives in the form of opposing frames is a product of the situated and unfolding process of a new technology design that interacts in practice with the organizational context. In particular, the artifactual characteristics of the system design, vis-a\`-vis the existing work practices, may not be fully appreciated by the social actors until interaction has actually taken place. Subsequently, when they come into contact with the system via this interaction, either directly, through a pilot implementation, or indirectly, through social communication channels, then the conflicting aspects of these artifacts are inscribed into diferentiated frames. The result can either be a stalemate or a blockage that prevents any further progress in the e-Gov project.

Second, frame adaptation refers to the process by which stakeholder groups start from opposing frames that are based on their group beliefs, interests and evaluation routines, but are able to modify their frames, evolving toward a truce frame. This is the process whereby ‘‘the diferent sorts of displacements and transformation [through] negotiations, and adjustments” gives rise to a truce frame (Callon, 1986, p. 224). On the one hand, a coalition of users who started out tightly ‘‘wedded” to their existing work practices and administrative procedures come to accept that these are not ‘‘untouchable”. On the other hand, the group of actors who inscribed their reform-based goals into the system design drop their demand that these be implemented without changes. In other words, assuming that there is pressure or desire for ‘‘getting things done” (Strauss, 1979) or making systems workable (Wagner and Newell, 2006; Orlikowski and Yates, 2006), these stakeholder groups are likely to negotiate changes to the system design configuration and existing work arrangements. The dynamic is a move from contestation and stalemate toward the spirit of ‘‘getting things done” based on negotiations the outcomes and processes of which are expected to be influenced by the following factors: the balance of power between the two groups; each side’s stake in the negotiations; whether or not either group believes it has an option not to negotiate; key contextual factor(s); and important trigger events.

Third, frame stabilization refers to the process through which the truce frame evolves towards relative stability in an emergent fashion. During this process, although highly diferentiated frames have been left behind, the struggles between the designers and users continue, albeit in milder form, as tactical games. During this stabilization process each side tries to further inscribe ‘‘minor” changes into the truce frame to make it more consistent with their initial frames. One sub-process at this stage entails the calculative and persuasive component of translation and, as per our definition above, involves a goal-directed and agency-driven activity (Emirbayer and Mische, 1998; Boudreau and Robey, 2005). This includes attempts by the designers to convince users to adopt and use the system, as well as mirror-image attempts by users to bypass or circumvent system design features that they consider disadvantageous. The goal of this frame stabilization sub-process is to create an ‘‘obligatory passage point”, i.e., an inevitable implementation path or technology features that key stakeholders must accept to achieve their goals or perform their tasks (Latour, 1988, p. 44).

Another sub-process entails a discernible and growing perception that the system, or at least a significant bundle of technology functionality, has reached a ‘‘taken-for-granted” status and thus can no longer be openly opposed as before. This frame emerges when the system acquires ‘‘black-box” status where previously separate parts are rendered invisible and taken-for-granted, so that they are no longer subject to questioning (Latour, 1987, p. 116). In this context, a specific e-Gov system configuration gradually achieves black-box status when the underlying system is no longer actively contested by opposing stakeholder groups, which have come to regard its constituent elements as a single invisible entity.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Case background

The case study revolves around a project to computerize land records, called the Cadastre Operations Modernization and Automation Project (hereinafter referred to as the Project), which took place under the auspices of the General Directorate of Land Registration and Survey agency (hereinafter referred to as the Land Registry) in a Mediterranean country. This \$25 million project was initiated in 1994 by a loan agreement between the World Bank and the national government. Following a debilitating civil strife, the government’s administrative apparatus was in need of radical transformation, as many of the country’s land records were in paper format and were in poor shape. The project protocol specified three goals: IT-based improvements in service delivery to citizens and businesses; reform of administrative organization and internal operations; and building the technology capacity of the agency (World Bank, 1994).

According to World Bank estimates, the volume of real estate transactions in the country was between \$4 billion and \$6 billion per annum (World Bank, 1994). The fees paid to the government were between \$110 million and \$140 million per annum, which constituted close to 10% of the government’s budget, making them an essential source of revenue. However, the World Bank had also determined that citizens and businesses incurred additional costs for real estate transactions that were equal to the formal government fees. According to the World Bank’s estimates, half of these ‘‘informal costs” were due to bribes and the other half were due to ‘‘burden of transaction costs,” including delays, productivity losses, lost revenues, etc. Therefore, both the national government and the World Bank regarded this sector as a priority for reform to reduce corruption, raise operational eficiency and enhance revenues. Thus, over and above the stated goal of modernizing and digitizing land records, the project implicitly required the use of IT to support greater administrative and workflow transparency. This, it was hoped, would help eliminate some of the estimated \$50 million burden that corruption imposed on citizens.

The planning and implementation of the project spanned more than 11 years, from October 1994 to December 2005. According to a recent independent evaluation by the World Bank, ‘‘Overall work on this component has progressed well and has obtained excellent results.” (World Bank, 2003, p. 4). However, at several junctures during implementation, the continuation and completion of this project was uncertain. There were several instances when the project was stalled due to disagreements over its scope and goals. Two key stakeholder groups, the Project Implementation Unit (hereinafter referred to as the Project Unit) and employees and middle managers<sup>3</sup> of the registries within the Land Registry, were the primary antagonists. The other actors included foreign aid donors (meaning, loosely, a group of organizations that usually provides international funding in the form of grants and loans for reform projects including e-Gov), advisors, and the contractors who developed the system.

## 3.2. Research method

Given our interest in contemporary events, interpretations and up-close examination of the frame evolution processes that were at work on the road toward frame stability, a qualitative case study method was considered a suitable choice (Yin, 2003; Lee, 1989; Benbasat et al., 1987). The case study was conducted by the two authors, who formed an insider–outsider research team as recommended by Eisenhardt (1989). This approach has been used with success in IS research (Pare´, 2004). The ‘‘insider” researcher had joined the Project in August 1995, and served until July 1998 as its overall architect. Subsequently, from June 1998 through Feb ruary 2004, he served as director of the Project. Because of his direct involvement in the events being analyzed, this researcher was uniquely sensitive to technology frame diferences and the process by which they evolved toward relative stability. The ‘‘outside” researcher was involved in all aspects of the data collection and research from September 2005 to September 2006.

The primary sources of information for the case study were stakeholder interviews and project-related documents (memos, reports and meeting minutes). The interviews were conducted from December 2005 until May 2006 and during February–March 2007. Sixteen interviewees sat for a total of 29 interviews, with an average session length of 90 min. The interview data were used to uncover the existence of a relatively stable truce frame, the diferent frames that had existed initially and the frame translation processes that appeared to have taken place. In parallel with this, project reports, memos and meeting minutes were used to corroborate the diferences between the initial frames and the characteristics of the frame translation processes. The list of interviewees and the specific documents consulted are summarized in Appendices A and B.

This type of case study research is subject to criticism due to potential researcher familiarity bias. However, a series of safeguards were developed to mitigate this. First, the insider–outsider team system can help reduce familiarity bias, since the outsider researcher can serve in a ‘‘devil’s advocate” role (Eisenhardt, 1989; Montealegre and Keil, 2000). Second, to reduce retrospective bias the outsider researcher was the lead participant in the majority of the interviews. Third, as a control, a detailed description of frames and proposed frame evolution processes was prepared and shown to two key former project stakeholders (with difering initial frames), and their comments were incorporated, which also helped reduce researcher bias.

## 3.3. Data collection and data analysis protocols

In our research we have taken a process-based approach to the study of the frame evolution processes. Our process-based approach is consistent with the work of Robey and Newman (1996) and Van De Ven (1992), but is more closely patterned on Pettigrew (1997) and Pettigrew et al. (2001). We utilize Pettigrew’s (1997) working definition of a process as ‘‘a sequence of individual and collective events, actions, and activities unfolding over time and in context” (p. 338). Practically speaking, the word ‘‘how” may capture the spirit of this definition. However, we also attempt to describe, analyze, and explain the what and why of certain sequences of actions in the frame evolution process by individual actors and groups. In addition, our approach attempts to incorporate the following elements (Strauss and Corbin, 1998, pp. 164–169): (a) situatedness—i.e., studying action processes within their context; (b) a temporal view, that is, studying events as processes of actions and interactions that unfold over time; (c) linking process events with outcomes; and (d) admittedly, we are able to ofer necessary but not suficient explanations of the outcomes.

Initially, the researchers were concerned with discovering the existence of a ‘‘dominant frame” and how it evolved into a ‘‘final” frame. To ascertain the existence of a relatively stable frame, the stakeholders were asked to express their general perceptions regarding the system that was implemented and in use. Depending on the responses, the authors probed further by asking follow-up questions (such as ‘‘why” or ‘‘what exactly”) to elicit more detailed responses—to get at the actors’ beliefs, interests, evaluation routines and artifact characteristics. Subsequently, the actors were asked whether their perceptions had remained the same as they were initially, how and why they had changed if they had (or why not, if they had not changed), what exactly these perceptions were, etc.

Our initial analysis during data collection indicated that there was indeed relative frame stability, but it was fairly fragile. We discovered that a bundling tactic was employed by the Project Unit, which played a carrotand-stick game. That is, the prized functionalities were bundled with non-prized functionalities. There were many painstaking negotiations over key functionalities of the system, in the light of the planned changes to existing work practices. These adjustments and the highly contextualized negotiation factors that contributed to them, however, were only cursorily touched upon in our initial focus on dominant frames. Subsequently, we shifted our focus toward the adjustments to the system, the work practices and especially the negotiations that contributed to them. All of these factors were incorporated into our analysis. A more complete and coherent picture of the frame evolution process emerged out of this reworking of the case data, a picture that we consider to be organic and holistic in its treatment of the frame translation process.<sup>4</sup> Notably, some of these negotiation categories fit loosely within the view of ‘‘negotiation as a social process” (Kramer and Messick, 1995) for ‘‘getting things done” (Strauss, 1979), although this was not what we had originally expected.

Our research approach is based on an iterative process of analysis, which starts out with a tentative framework that is primarily shaped and modified by the facts of the case (see Strauss and Corbin, 1994; Locke, 1996). The final framework was richer and more comprehensive than the one we started with, especially regarding the importance of the social process of negotiation in the overall frame evolution and translation dynamics. Thus, in contrast to pure grounded theory approaches, we let prior theory and professional experiences guide our data analysis, in accordance with Boudreau and Robey’s (2005) recommendation.

## 4. Findings

We present our findings in five sections. First, the case details are used to illustrate the emergence of differences in technology frames among stakeholder groups, which were so significant at first that they almost stalemated the project. We refer to this as frame diferentiation. Second, we ofer explanations of the major adaptations and adjustments to the initial frames that moved the project forward toward a truce frame. We refer to this as frame adaptation. Third, we analyze the events of the case and show that the negotiation process and contextual factors played key roles in the enactment of this truce frame, serving as another component of the frame adaptation process. Fourth, we trace some of tactics employed by the Project Unit and the registrars to inscribe specific functionalities into the evolving system, thereby creating obligatory points of passage. Finally, we show that the e-Gov system has achieved a level of relative frame stability due to its blackbox behavior.

## 4.1. Emergence of diferentiated frames: system design vs. current work practices

The focus of our analytical findings is on a 22-month implementation window. During this period, two major competing technology frames emerged and became associated with the key stakeholder groups. One frame was associated with the grouping that consisted of the Project Unit and donors. The other frame was associated with middle managers and most registry employees, along with certain allies such as an influential advisor to the Land Registry. Sometimes the Registry System development contractor was involved with the latter group. The two groups exhibited competing frames, several aspects of which were completely opposed to each other. For example, a former high-level advisor with close links to the Land Registry pointed to the administrative burden imposed by the system as a core reason for frame diferences:

Look, although the system is now in use, it did not start like this . . . there was no incentive for them [employees] to comply with extensive data coding and all the extra computer tasks which the system was bringing about. The employees just wanted to do their work. They cannot be bothered with all this coding and computer software, and so on . . .

The Project Unit, on the other hand, regarded certain elements of the project, such as the database codes for converting paper title registers, as a foundation for the standardization of information. As the former National Project Coordinator said:

Data coding is essential for making sure that all registries enter the same kind of information . . . when people come and ask for their title abstract the content should not be dependent on what the employee at the time decided to write or type . . . A two-bedroom apartment is recorded the same way in all 9 registries . . . Down the line we need this kind of information for statistical analysis and fee-setting policies . . .

Since they had previously enjoyed a high degree of autonomy, the employees tended to view negatively those features of the system that they perceived as making centralized control of the registration process possible. By contrast, the national coordinator’s positive perspective was in line with the e-Gov project’s reform and eficiency goals. This viewpoint includes the beliefs, interests, evaluation routines, and artifact character istics that characterize the Project Unit technology frame. Although the Project Unit and employees/middle managers initially exhibited competing frames, later on the frames converged. ## 4.2.1. Initial system design vs. work practices

Since the process of coding transactions manually had led to large variations, the system’s original data model incorporated over 800 transaction classification codes in order to reflect existing standard operating procedures. Another important design feature was that almost all transactions incorporated automatic time-expiry control based on business rules encoded within the software. These were extracted from the regulations that mandated specific validity periods for active or open transactions. For example, a property transfer transaction involving the sale of an inherited property was supposed to be advertised in the newspaper if there were any absentee owners. Once a specified amount of time had passed without the absentee owners appearing, the transaction could proceed by default. In addition, the design initially favored by the Project Unit incorporated automatic fee calculation. Each transaction required the calculation of a number of fees, some fixed and some based on property value, as per oficial regulations (which were not necessarily the same thing as actual practice). Automating the fee calculation was meant to cut down on favoritism and corruption as well as to streamline the reporting of fees to the treasury. In the past there had been numerous cases of discrepancies due to rounding errors or unaccounted for fees. These features, along with some minor addi tional innovations, were inscribed in the Registry System design to address longstanding public concerns about ineficiency and corruption.

Another source of contention soon emerged: how historical and current information was to be coded into title records. From the Project Unit’s perspective, the coding scheme that was first proposed was unwieldy (it contained some 800 codes), but necessary to put an end to recording practices that varied widely across sites and individual employees. This variability had been tolerated historically, but it resulted in an uneven quality of information recording and content. Another reason for the coding scheme was to make the resulting data compatible with the Registry System’s reporting functions. Moreover, the coding scheme was considered an important part of the government’s revenue control and reporting functions, which were dependent on its implementation.

On the other side, the employees and middle management were adamant that the system’s design was a nuisance, and they would be better of with ‘‘a word processing system, PCs and a few printers,” as one Registry Area Oficer put it. This opposition was fairly dormant after the design and physical development of the system was complete and the pilot system and converted digital data had been delivered. It was not until the user acceptance testing period that opposition to the system and especially the coding of data began to solidify. At first blush, the appearance of opposition at this point was puzzling, since representatives from 5 of the 9 regional registries had been involved in the development of the coding scheme and had reviewed the definition of the system requirements. It is fair to say that the majority of registry employees and managers did not take seriously the prospect that the Registry System would actually materialize until onsite testing began. A Registry Area Oficer made the following comment:

I was frankly surprised that they managed to actually develop and test a system because we had 3 or 4 contractors come and go over the years and they had shown system prototypes to the higher-ups, but it was clear none of those met the requirements of the Lebanese registry law . . . People in our country really attach a lot of traditional value to land . . . and computers do not mix well with this culturally important factor . . .

A similar attitude was widespread among the lower echelons of the Registry. The thinking was that most national governments had big ideas about computerization, most of which ‘‘die of natural causes,” as another middle manager at the registry put it. This perception is further reflected in the views of some of the managers. For example, the former registrar of the capital city reported:

During the pilot the recorders really disliked this coding thing, and the registry area oficers were up in arms that fees were being assigned by the system, not by them . . . A lot of complaints about the fact that they are neither typists nor junior employees. . . Also some people thought that rounding being done by the machine shortchanges the citizen [laughter] . . . I had been an area oficer myself and I know why that is something that is dear to them . . . So the Project Unit ofered some of the area oficers who were older computer assistants to help them with the typing . . . Some took it, others declined.

Some recorders and registry area oficers had sent memoranda in which they formally asked that the new registry system be restricted to typing in and storing title register content. (These title registers would akin to word processing files.) The result would have been that abstracts could have been produced using printers instead of being hand copied. The Project Unit attributed these managers’ opposition to the fact that most employees were benefiting from the old ad hoc approach by which information was recorded in paper registers. Under the old regime, enforcement of standard controls on their work and policing of corrupt behavior was almost impossible.

On the whole, the manifest skepticism and disapproval was indicative of the opposition of the employees/ middle management. The late crystallization of this opposition, which came out in the open only after the pilot program and user acceptance testing were done, is a testament to the fact that in an environment such as the Land Registry, there is immense cynicism about any reforms, especially if they are initiated by the national government with the help of international donors. However, the advent of the pilot system forced employees and middle managers to interact with it both materially and symbolically. This led to an interactional discovery by the rank and file, who found that a lot of the features of the system were designed for control and standardization purposes—a fact that they might have understood only in the abstract until then. Thus, they interpreted the system’s material functionality as a serious attempt to limit the autonomy and latitude that they had previously enjoyed in interpreting the regulations at their discretion.

## 4.2.2. Reworked system design and work practices

In the end, the Registry and the Project Unit agreed to a ‘‘reworked” version of the computerized registry system, with adjustments to some key aspects of the initial design. The process lasted approximately 4 months, and its eventual success was due in no small measure to pressure from outside the agency (which we discuss in the next section). The revision incorporated adjustments in the coding (cutting the 800 categories down to 150), relaxation of the rules on automatic expiration and an optional override of the automatic fee calculation. In return, each of the main occupational groups in the organization, i.e., Registrars (top managers), Registry Area Oficers (middle managers), and Recorders (employees) agreed to use the system to perform registration application tasks. They also agreed to use computerized work procedures, which ensured that the system could not be bypassed (see below). These adjustments were painstakingly hammered out by a small joint working group consisting of representatives of the Project Unit, the Registry, the contractor and the data entry subcontractor. This group met over several months and worked on specific requests for adaptations and adjustments.

In the end, about a dozen major adjustments to the system were adopted. Some of these may have been major concessions in the eyes of some of the Project Unit members, but the national coordinator summed up the situation accurately:

Look, from my perspective we are moving from the ‘‘slide rule” era to the ‘‘computer era,” the 21st century. We are skipping the ‘‘calculator” stage . . . We should keep our eyes on the prize, which is that we want the organization to start using computerized procedures and learn to use them, and over time they will realize that they cannot do without them. Then you can start introducing enhanced controls and greater standardization . . . although some of the reductions in the scope of the system’s functionality were significant, I think it is better to have an automated process with some parts subject to employee discretion than a broken manual process that citizens are unhappy about and that perpetuates the culture of ineficiency.

A registrar ofered the following opinion on his acquiescence to the adjusted registry practices:

Frankly, I do not think we can really avoid moving to computers for too long. I mean, neighboring countries like Jordan and Turkey are already heavy users in their government administration, and it really looks bad for us if people ask and we say we are still using paper and ink, which dates back to the French Mandate period . . . also I think that the changes to the system are really important . . . [although] we do not want to replace the professional judgment of a lawyer as a registration judge with a computer to decide whether a specific transaction has expired or not . . .

From the perspective of the Project Unit, the adaptations and adjustments amounted in the aggregate to a compromise on its original position of on the system’s design. At the same time, the Registry organization as a whole was still not convinced of the system’s necessity, and despite a high level of usage there continued to be grumbling and complaints. Nevertheless, the revamped procedures for registration of real estate transactions were adopted by all registries, and computerization became mandatory. ## 4.3. Frame adaptations: the context of negotiations

As mentioned above, the Registry System implementation was completed in 22 months. This period started with the pilot project and ended with a gradual district-by-district implementation of the system in the capital city’s registry (which had the largest number of transactions and generated the most revenue for the government). This version of the system reflected several changes in the design configuration to make it ‘‘workable.” A former head of the registry ofice characterized this situation as follows:

If there is a will, there is a way . . . Our people [employees] are very set in their ways and also benefit from it privately . . . Here comes a computer and says you need to type in data codes for everything . . . but in the end, both sides compromised . . . Going to 150 codes from 800 codes made it impossible for the employees to say no to the system in front of the Minister . . . It is embarrassing in a large meeting in front of the ‘‘boss” to be seen as totally uncompromising when the other side has compromised in a major way.

This manager’s point is consistent with the view that negotiation is a way of ‘‘‘getting things accomplished’ when parties need to deal with each other to get those things done” (Strauss, 1979, p. 2). Driven by a desire to proceed with the e-Gov project, and with the help of a give-and-take process, some changes in the Registry System were negotiated and an artifact evolved that was operationally acceptable to both sides. In other words, although the two stakeholder groups had begun with severe diferences in their technology frames, in practice, a truce frame had evolved. This truce frame emerged during negotiations and was in turn afected by the following contextual influences—namely, the balance of power, the stakes in the negotiation, whether either side ever had an option not to negotiate, key contextual factor(s) and the triggering event(s) (Strauss, 1979, p. 234). ## 4.3.1. Balance of power among stakeholder groups

The Project Unit was entrusted with the implementation authority for the Registry System, and according to the loan agreement documents it was also given the same line authority as a cabinet-level agency. However, in practice, it lacked practical authority to enforce decisions regarding the use of the Registry System. This organizational configuration was based on a mutual agreement with the national government recognizing the limited technical capacity of the Land Registry. Another dificulty that contributed to this decision was the general notion that this agency would face significant challenges in reforming itself due to vested interests. This was compounded by the fact that there was cynicism in the rank and file regarding the longevity of any project, let alone an automation project since ‘‘ministers came and went but the bureaucracy was a permanent fixture” (according to a middle manager).

Until the completion of the pilot project and user acceptance testing, this cynical perception was the prevailing wisdom. During the 22-month period within the project implementation process, whose milestones are highlighted in detail in Appendix C, the Minister in charge put a committee of the Land Registry and his own advisors in charge of overseeing project progress. This was in response to the delays in the project that had resulted from the disagreements between the Project Unit and the contractor over the technical scope of the project, and between the Project Unit and the Land Registry over system design features. However, the donors, namely the World Bank and UNDP, were quite vocal in communicating their concern over the delays and any attempt to significantly cut the scope of the project scope or alter its design in any way that would impact on the development goals of the e-Gov project.

The balance of power at this stage was such that the Land Registry employees and middle managers saw the Project Unit as not in a position to bargain efectively. The appointment of the oversight committee was regarded by some as an efort to ‘‘stall the project” (according to a former Land Registry manager who served on the oversight committee). This balance of power, however, significantly shifted after the election of the new government and the return of the former minister who had inaugurated the loan agreement and started the project in the first place. After the acceptance testing, the opposition to the System became very visible, and included written letters following the former minister’s return to ofice. A former Land Registry manager’s explanation for this behavior is consistent with this view of the balance of power:

Since the end of pilot and user acceptance testing coincided with Minister [Y] coming back to power, I think our employees realized that we cannot avoid the system . . . I heard some talk before that the system was being tested, etc., but the vocal opposition right after his return tells me that everybody kind of woke up to the fact that this system is coming and they cannot avoid it . . .

In other words, the balance of power between the Project Unit and the Land Registry became more equal after the return of the former minister. It is probably safe to say that this was a major contributing factor to the start of adjustment negotiations and the development of a workable system.

## 4.3.2. Stakes in negotiations vs. options not to negotiate

The Land Registry employees/middle managers became aware of their stake in modifying the system design. This helped convince them to engage in negotiations. The Land Registry actors realized that unless the pilot system features were adjusted soon, the (to be implemented) system would seriously curtail their autonomy and the amount of latitude they had in interpreting the regulations and using their ‘‘signature” power for personal purposes. This most likely played a key role in bringing the employees/middle managers to the negotiation table.

The stakes for the Project Unit were diferent. The professional reputation of the whole unit and its key senior members could be marred by an incomplete project. Things would be even worse if it were cancelled. So the unit’s initial insistence that the full scope of the system be left untouched, and that it incorporate the reform-based design, gave way to a pragmatic approach of implementing the system with some adjustments to the certain critical features. We have already described the national project coordinator’s views on this shift to a modus operandi of ‘‘getting things done.”

It is fair to say that neither side was left with any real options other than negotiation. From the standpoint of the Land Registry, an implemented system with no adjustments was much worse than extracting some concessions regarding the ‘‘undesirable” design features. For the Project Unit, any further delay could result in the project being classified as unsatisfactory by the World Bank and other donors. This would lead to a whole series of remedial steps which could be highly damaging to the professional reputation of the team, and might also have resulted in a temporary freezing of funds for other projects, a highly undesirable outcome that was to be avoided at all costs, according to the national project coordinator.

## 4.3.3. Key contextual factors and triggering event

Two contextual factors were crucial in moving the participants to engage in negotiations for ‘‘getting things done” and evolving the truce frame. The first factor was the return of the former government to ofice, including the return of the former minister who had inaugurated the project in the first place. The second factor was the World Bank’s ‘‘Country Portfolio Performance Review,” which indicated that unless the ‘‘lead” projects (one of which was the e-Gov project) ‘‘got moving” the country risked funding cuts or cancellation of its whole portfolio, consisting of 11 projects worth more than \$500 million. Both of these events played a crucial role in bringing the two groups of actors to the negotiating table. The return of the former minister was a clear sign of things to come, from the Registry’s point of view. The World Bank’s policy statement was a dire warning that progressing projects (albeit with compromises) are better than cancelled ones. In addition, there was a clear risk of a cancellation ripple efect that could have afected other projects that were important to the government.

A key triggering event was a meeting held by the new minister after his return to ofice, in which he reaf firmed his commitment to the project but also mentioned that he wanted ‘‘a project that works for everybody, not a perfect project.” This was a ministry-wide meeting, during which the minister publicly stated that the reform projects that he had started had to be completed. He specifically singled out the computerization of all ofices and emphasized ‘‘the land registry moving into the 21st century based on the fruits of the e-Gov project.” He also said he wanted ‘‘working systems and not perfect ones, though I am not willing to compromise on some of the key reform ideas that are in the computerization projects.” This meeting, we surmise, may have been a watershed event. The evidence is that shortly afterwards, the Land Registry, the Project Unit, the contractor and the subcontractor put in place a working group that negotiated changes to the system design and accompanying work practices in painstaking detail. ## 4.4. Frame stabilization via obligatory points of passage

Here we provide a glimpse of the goal-directed and agency-driven (Emirbayer and Mische, 1998) aspects of stabilization tactics employed by the actors. Each actor tried to position bundles of features or certain inscriptions as obligatory passage points. Three instances of this behavior are depicted below: signature inscription, functionality bundling, and ‘‘signed print-out”.

## 4.4.1. Signature inscription

From the registrars’ perspective, one of the main dangers of the computerization of land records was a potential loss in their power. By law, the validity and legality of any given land record and the afidavits related to it must be vouched for by regional registrars, who afix their signatures to all records. Their signatures are legally binding, and the registrars are responsible for the accuracy of all written information. The arrival of computerized records introduced changes to these responsibilities and work practices. In efect, if valid land records are stored in digital form, the need to validate each transaction and afidavit is reduced. In a properly designed and managed information system, the control function is centralized and requires only the initial validation. Land records would thus no longer open to easy change and require less involvement by the regional registrar.

The registrars quickly understood the implications of such a change and rapidly challenged the assumptions underlying the system’s design. They turned to existing law to show that only registrars have signatory power, and that they are the only ones legally entitled to vouch for the validity of land records. They complained to the minister and sympathetic members of parliament about what they saw as an unjustified abrogation of their prerogatives and legal responsibilities. The Project Unit had to change the computerized work process and reintroduce the registrar into the process of record validation and afidavit generation, via electronic signatures. On the one hand, this amounted to a step back in terms of automation and system functionality. On the other hand, political necessity made this step necessary, as the registrars formed a key group whose opposition could have derailed the project. Thus, the registrars made themselves an obligatory passage point via the new electronic signature feature of the system.

## 4.4.2. Functionality bundling and passing through ‘‘Undesirable Functions”

The perceived utility of the system among the employees and managers was not uniform, even a couple of years after its implementation. Some appeared to use the system only grudgingly, some were indiferent and others regarded the system very positively. However, it was interesting to note that all those interviewed indicated that system use was high. This apparent contradiction may be explained in part by two separate dynamics. First, most of the ‘‘undesirable” system functionalities were designed to reduce or eliminate potentially lucrative employee work practices. Second, employees’ perceptions might have been due to a pattern of system usage that was based on a compulsory path ‘‘connecting” specific points of passage that had to be navigated in order to obtain a highly prized functionality. This highly prized feature was the digital title abstract. As the national Project coordinator said,

We knew early on that the recorders really thought digital title abstracts would be very useful and they would use the system to do just that . . . but after the diferences on several functionalities surfaced we kind of made it like an Easter egg hunt. . .but an egg hunt where you must use a map you are given with the only path to the egg location marked on it . . . In the Registry System, the egg is the digital abstract and the path involves using other features, like coded data . . .

The computerized extraction and printing of information in the form of title abstracts replaced the previous efortful and time-consuming practice of manual copying (which entailed the need for the recorders to physically carry bulky and heavy newspaper-sized bound folios to their desks). One recorder said:

Look, as you see we can now enter a parcel number and then click the print button on the screen and we get the afidavit [title abstract] . . . This used to take a lot of time. Sometimes most of my days used to be tied up with producing maybe ten of these a day, and [that was] only a couple of years ago. . . It has made a part of my job easier.

Others tended to share this view, albeit with diferent nuances:

There are some problems with the printer, especially jams and running out of ink sometimes, but afidavits [title abstracts] are fast by the system . . . But I have explained to the IT people and the registrar and assistant registrar that having the daily work order number assigned by the system is cumbersome and inflexible . . . Sometimes the users do not pay their fees and these transactions just stay in the system. That is not our fault, but we can get blamed for them.

Both of these comments by recorders confirm that some functionalities were perceived less positively, or were less desired, e.g., the coding of transaction types using pull-down menus, and enforced transparent routing of transactions between employees. The Project Unit appeared to have understood this. As a result, it packaged and marketed the registry system functions as an inseparable whole, where, for example, coded data and automated transactions (such as the automatic transfer of registration applications and code look-ups between employees) were required to capture data and enter them into the registry system so that the title abstract could be printed from them. The advisor to the Land Registry described his view of this packaging as follows:

I think it was really clever to repeatedly mention and demonstrate simply to everybody that data has to get into the system before it can be gotten out . . . Otherwise, how can you get a title abstract . . . not from nothing . . . [and] . . . the people [employees] gradually accepted that there is no other way of getting at title registry abstracts!

## 4.4.3. Signed print-out form

The adjustments to the work procedures of the registry were apparently cosmetic and simple, but in reality they had been carefully developed to ensure that the computerized system could not be easily bypassed. For example, a procedure was negotiated whereby at the beginning of the transaction cycle the registry area oficer who receives all applications generates a computer-printed slip. In addition, at the end of the cycle, the recorder generates another computer-printed slip indicating that the transaction has been committed to the database as the ‘‘transaction of record.” Both of these slips are signed by the registrar to signify that the transaction is considered administratively complete. This paper slip mechanism was instituted as a part of the overall adjustments to system design that were negotiated during the registry implementation in the capital. The Project Unit’s reasoning was that unless there was a mechanism to ensure compliance with system use, the users could easily revert first to partial use of the system and then to total non-use. Therefore, with the cooperation of the registrars, an agreement was worked out whereby the registrar will not accept any transaction for final approval in the file, unless it contains these two slips. On the one hand, as a system designer remarked, ‘‘it is ironic to enforce computer system usage through a paper-based approach.” On the other hand, this shows the extent of the adjustments to the system and the kind of improvisations that the Project Unit and the Land Registry worked out to ensure that the system would be used. This agreement and its implementation were unequivocally dependent on the cooperation of the registrar. Thus, turning the use of the system into an obligatory passage point for transactions served the interests of both the registrars (who gained better control over the work process) and the Project Unit (which got the compulsory system usage it desired).

## 4.5. Frame stabilization via black-boxing

Here we aim to find out whether the Registry System has achieved a degree of relative frame stability by asking whether the key stakeholders’ perceptions correspond to the information infrastructure characteristics scheme proposed by Star and Ruhleder (1996). At least four of the attributes in Star and Ruhleder’s scheme— embeddedness, visibility upon breakdown, acceptance/learning as a part of community of practice, and acceptance by membership—may be inferred from the opinions of several Land Registry recorders. For example, one recorder pointed out:

I am using the system and I think it is useful . . . especially on the days when the power or network go down . . . but I learned from recorders in other districts who were already using it . . . how we have to enter information from menus.

One implication of this statement is that the system is used and has become embedded within daily routines. However, in order to get to that point, this recorder relied on other recorders to learn how to use the system and for reassurance that it was ‘‘OK to use the system.” This was a self-reinforcing process with positive feedback: as more recorders use the system, it gains more legitimacy and its usage expands even more.

Another characteristic of Star and Ruhleder’s framework is that it is ‘‘built on an installed base.” In other words, the new system should provide a balance between new features and prior work practices. To achieve this, it must be retrofitted within an existing information environment. That is necessary for the system to be considered black-boxed. A corollary of this was observed by a registrar:

I originally thought that we should just type all the new title register entries but leave the old ones intact. You know we have these title registers and they go back to 1926. . . Now when I heard the project wanted to do data-entry on all these, I thought OK, but then they started talking about coding, and I was hesitant about some data entry clerk coding a 1940s title register . . . but the scanned copy was made available and we were told that the error was going to be less with less typing and just coding . . . and at the end the codes were three-quarters of their initial size.

The inclusion of historic records in the system is a measure of the extent of frame stability and black-boxing. Digitizing archival records all the way back to 1926 is a strong signal that the system is achieving the characteristics of true information infrastructure. Any land registry information system has direct legal implications beyond its administrative utility, and must maintain both backward and forward compatibility within the land records environment as a link in the chain of evidence. The fact that the previously contested coding scheme appeared to satisfy both the need to digitally convert the historical data and also provided a useful way to enter new land transactions appeared to be a major part of the reason why it was possible to create a relatively stable truce frame around the Registry System.

In general, the informants’ comments supported the view that an operational Registry System, as a negotiated set of adjustments to the original design, appeared to function increasingly like an information infrastructure along the lines sketched by Star and Ruhleder. Comments by the majority of the informants corresponded with at least one and sometimes up to three or four of the characteristics listed by Star and Ruhleder. Only one of their proposed attributes, embodiment of standards, did not appear among the comments made by the informants. Overall, based on these informants’ observations we may surmise that the standing of the Registry System among the stakeholder group of employees and middle managers was slowly approaching a black-box status. This black-box behavior is taken here to indicate that a black-boxing process was at work, leading to the relative stability of the negotiated truce frame.

## 5. Discussion

Using a detailed case study of an e-Gov project, we have provided a rich description of how two key stakeholder groups started out with diferent technology frames, struggled to impose and defend their perspectives, and finally ended up with a frame that reflected some commonalities in their beliefs, interests, technology evaluation routines and artifact characteristics. Through a detailed analysis of the way the process unfolded, we have found evidence of certain frame evolution processes at work—frame diferentiation, frame adaptation and frame stabilization. These, we would argue, aid in the translation of one frame into another.

Our findings in the context of the e-Gov case presented here extend and enrich previous technology frame research in a number of ways. First, the organizational environment was extremely challenging—the civil administration of a government plagued with vested interests and corrupt practices (World Bank, 1994, 2005). For example, the need for the Project Unit to negotiate and change its frame had much to do with the institutional setting. A second aspect of this study is its reliance on an insider–outsider research team. Researchers with insuficient engagement with the site are often unable to comprehend the richness of experiences, dilemmas, struggles and complex interactions that characterize unfolding practices (see Bourdieu, 1990; Lynch, 2001). Much richness was gained from using a combination of research perspectives, as the (former) insider’s deep knowledge of events and situated activity was matched with the outsider’s focus on under standing practice and technology-in-use. During the interviews, which were led by the outsider researcher, the ability of the former insider to remind interviewees of past events helped generate a richer set of materials than would have been possible if either of the two authors had done the interviews alone.

Our paper subscribes to a process perspective on technology frames that is in harmony with Davidson’s (2006, p. 30) call to move ‘‘from frames to framing.” Much technology frame research to date has focused on the identification of frame diferences and understanding how they afect IT implementation problems (e.g., Orlikowski and Gash, 1994; Bijker, 1993). But in most IT projects that entail social and organizational change, ‘‘people’s technological frames often need to be changed to accommodate a new technology” as Orlikowski writes (1992a), p. 367). This frame change ‘‘imperative” is very important, but it has received less attention than it deserves. Indeed, Davidson’s (2006) call for greater attention to framing/reframing is predicated on the importance of focusing on dynamic aspects of frames. Our analysis contributes by explaining how frames can and do change through a situated and complex process of evolution. The Land Registry employees and managers started by ofering an oppositional technology frame to that championed by the Project Unit, which almost caused the abandonment of the e-Gov project. Their views on data coding, automatic fee calculation, and other functionalities reflected their group’s beliefs, interests, evaluation routines and artifact characteristics. However, as the implementation devolved into a process of dialogic interactions, negotiation of meanings and specifications, and political intervention, a negotiated truce frame was arrived at.

The term truce frame is important because it signifies that while consensus has not been achieved, conflict has ceased. In other words, there was general willingness to move ahead with the Registry System implementation. Our results build on the notion of ‘‘routine as truce” (Nelson and Winter, 1982), whereby ‘‘organizational participants may agree to disagree and move on in order to get the work done” (Feldman and Pentland, 2003, p. 98). The findings specifically extend this notion and demonstrate that such a truce is based on framing and thus has distinct interpretive characteristics—it is a social construction that is established in practice. Thus, our findings demonstrate that frame evolution through frame translation is a negotiated process. We have also shown that frame stability is highly tentative, reflecting the social shaping of an organizational truce to merely proceed with the project.

Our findings extend beyond frame recognition, confirming the importance of studying unfolding processes and active negotiations to address competing interests. Prior frame research has focused more on the so-called frame content and less on frame structure (Davidson, 2006). By frame structure we mean those properties of framing that remain similar across contexts. The elements of framing/reframing, particularly the specific processes of frame translation on the path to frame evolution, can be key aspects of frame structure. In other words, assuming that a degree of relative frame incongruence is fairly commonplace, especially in challenging environments, a key research question concerns the processes by which frame translation unfolds in practice. In a manner consistent with Pettigrew et al.’s (2001) process-based theorizing, our findings demonstrate the existence and workings of these generic frame translation processes, i.e., frame diferentiation, frame adaptation and frame stabilization. Thus, our findings have extended the research by pointing to the existence of these three framing properties.

Prior IT and e-Gov research on frames has firmly established the importance of negotiations in achieving a stable frame (Wagner and Newell, 2006; Andersen and Henriksen, 2006). The literature has focused on the need for and presence of negotiations. Our findings extend this research by highlighting the specific contexts in which these frame negotiations are unfolding as situated practices during the frame adaptation process. Specifically, we have used the constructs from the negotiations as a social process of ‘‘getting things accomplished” (Strauss, 1979, p. 2). This has helped us sketch out how the specific adaptations to the system design, such as coding scheme reductions, were negotiated and inscribed, subject to the specific contextual factors of the negotiations. These factors afecting the negotiations include the balance of power between stakeholder groups, the stakes, the presence or absence of options not to negotiate, key contextual factors and trigger events. We also extend the literature on frame translation as situated practices by establishing that some of the contextual factors that afected the negotiations were themselves changing during both the implementation and the negotiations, which renders the whole process of frame adaptation a ‘‘practical accomplish ment” (Lynch, 2001).

IT and e-Gov researchers have recently focused on the situated practices of designers’ and users’ agency during the implementation process. For example, Boudreau and Robey (2005) and Luna-Reyes et al.

(2005), have illustrated the significance of user opposition as a form of agency leading to emergent outcomes within IT and e-Gov implementations. Our findings on frame stabilization via obligatory points of passage help unpack how and by what means such an agency (e.g., opposition on the part of registry employees or system designers’ inflexibility) can be subjected to cooptation and influence. Specifically, the packaging and marketing of the Registry System as an inseparable whole containing bundles of both desired and ‘‘undesirable” functionalities, and the institution of the ‘‘signed print-out form,” indicate that user opposition as a form of agency is partially negotiable (or its mirror image, system designer inflexibility vis-a\`-vis the registrar’s ‘‘sig nature inscription”).

Our analysis of frame stabilization via black-boxing as a translation process focused on the fact that in order for frames to be stabilized, the artifact had to become relatively invisible and unquestioned. In other words, to the extent that the perceived characteristics of the system are similar to the black-box properties of an information infrastructure, i.e., it tends to ‘‘fade into the woodwork” (Bowker and Star, 1999, p. 34), it can be said to have achieved a tentative black-box status. In spite of the initial contestation around data coding, most employees have been co-opted to use the system. Alternatively, it could be argued that the system that was actually implemented was co-opted to conform to existing work practices. A possible conclusion in challenging institutional settings could be that black-boxing status is really less about imposing technology frames inscribed in computer-based work practices than it is about adaptation through negotiation as a social process.

Our findings on the truce frame have potentially important implications for e-Gov implementation practice. Traditionally, the practical approaches to implementation emphasize getting the e-Gov requirements right upfront as much as possible to minimize the negative impact of the design-implementation gap (e.g., Heeks, 2002). Our findings, on the other hand, point to the emergent nature of some key resultant system function alities, via the truce frame. In particular, it is clear that these truce functionalities came about not so much because of the ‘‘right” requirements, but because of an active process of negotiation between the e-Gov designers and users—based on a willingness to move on with the routine work of the organization. Thus, the findings demonstrate that, first, most e-Gov functionalities that alter processes of work inevitably touch upon sensitive matters of occupational roles and accumulated job skills of users. Second, in order to bring about a workable system, the importance of right requirements should not overshadow the pragmatic goal of ending up with an implemented e-Gov system. More specifically, to arrive at such a workable solution, it is important to prioritize system functionalities, and be prepared for negotiating some away, in order to keep others. This is probably as important, if not more so, as getting the requirements right in the first place.

This study has limitations that must be recognized and addressed in future work. Our primary goal was to understand in detail the frame evolution pathway and the associated unfolding of frame translation processes. But first of all, the insights we have gained here are from a single case in a challenging environment. Thus, our findings may be limited, as some of the observed characteristics may be specific to developing and transition economies. Additional research is needed to corroborate the extent to which these specific processes are applicable in other IT and e-Gov implementation contexts. Second, it would be useful to know to what extent the negotiation factors that led to the enactment of frame adaptations and adjustments are applicable in other contexts. Third, the theoretical insights on the frame evolution process are based on our findings regarding the emergence of a collective approach of ‘‘getting things done” through negotiations among stakeholder groups. Further research is needed to provide a better understanding of contextual dynamics wherein this collective approach may not emerge, as well as the associated processes surrounding its absence.

## 6. Conclusions

This paper contributes to the theoretical understanding of making systems workable by focusing on the technology frame evolution processes as a common aspect of e-Gov implementation in public sector organizations. The case we chose allowed us to use the situated practices as a basis for theoretical understanding of the processes of frame translation. This in turn enabled us to theorize about the related processes that underpin the negotiated approach to the enactment of adaptations and adjustments to system design and work practices. The enactment of frame adjustments is predicated on specific negotiation processes and their contexts. Our findings suggest that unpacking frame translation processes and their social context is helpful in developing a more in-depth theoretical understanding of framing/reframing. Furthermore, we believe that our focus on negotiations within the frame translation process helps open up a fruitful area of investigation that may lead to a richer understanding of negotiation as a social process, which can aid in improving e-Gov implementation practices.
