## A B S T R A C T

We explored how IT disclosure affects information quality in the market and identified the factors that influence managers’ choices in IT disclosure quality. Adopting a comprehensive view of IT investments disclosure mechanisms, we found that IT disclosure quality was positively associated with information quality. Specifically, our results showed that an increase in quantitative information in IT disclosure could improve the information environment in the market, thereby reducing the perception of information risk among market participants. We also found that managers’ disclosures were less quantitative when the IT investment was more asset-specific in relation to higher agency costs.

## 1. Introduction

Unlike discretionary expenditures such as R&D and advertising, firms are not required to disclose in their financial statements the total amount of their IT investments. However, over the last three decades, IT has emerged as a critical component in sustaining and transforming business processes. Spending on IP equipment and software, including computers, peripheral equipment, and communications technology, was \$562.9 billion in 2008, the largest category of corporate fixed investment [16]. Despite the significance of IT-related activities and expenses, few studies have investigated the effect of manager IT disclosure behavior on a firm’s information environment in the market. By measuring the stock market’s response, prior research showed that events such as IT investment disclosure can be beneficial to both investors and firms as it can reduce the uncertainty of firm performance and potentially increase value in the market [9,12,18]. Mauldin and Richtermeyer [15] documented managers’ IT disclosure behavior, showing that firms often disclose diverse information about ERP software in their annual report and, furthermore, that the choice to disclose is significantly associated with capital market transactions, firm performance, firm size, and industry.

To better understand the incentive for managers to disclose IT investment, we investigated two related research questions. What type of IT-related information do managers choose to disclose (e.g., quantitative or qualitative)? How do these IT-disclosure choices improve information quality in the market (measured as public information precision).

To investigate this question, we carefully reviewed IT announcements dated 1985–1999 and constructed IT disclosure quality measures that gauged the degree of quantitative or qualitative information provided in these investment disclosures. Specifically, quantitative IT investment disclosures included monetary or numeric values to indicate how firms had invested in IT expenditures, whereas qualitative IT disclosures included descriptive information that most firms used in evaluating the impact of their IT investments.

The second question we address was: what is the impact of factors that expose firms to the risk of undervaluation and thus undermine the credibility of IT disclosure quality? Because prior research suggested that the IT disclosure itself played a critical role in valuation, we further investigated those factors that influence a manager’s choice of IT disclosure quality: strategic IT role, IT asset specificity, and growth opportunity.

## 2. Background and hypotheses

Public reporting should provide users with forward-looking information, including the management’s plan while discussing risks, and uncertainties. Although some authoritative guidelines exist, such as SFAS No. 86 (Financial Accounting Standards Board, Statement of Financial Accounting Standards No. 86), SOP 98-1 (Accounting for the Costs of Computer Software Developed or Obtained for Internal Use), and EITF 97-13 (Accounting for Costs Incurred in Connection with a Consulting Contract or an Internal Project That Combines Business Process Reengineering and Information Technology Transformation), they are neither explicit nor comprehensive in terms of IT expenditures. For example, while computer hardware costs are typically capitalized and depreciated over a defined time period, system conversion or training related costs are expensed immediately. However, internally incurred development costs of computer software are not expensed until the technological feasibility of the product is established, at which point the firm can capitalize and subsequently amortize additional development costs. The FASB defines technological feasibility as completion of a detailed program design or completion of a working model.

Therefore, because current regulatory standards are unclear and incomplete regarding disclosure of IT investments, significant variations in practice are possible. This may lead some firms to decide that it is in their own best interests to disclose less information – a situation less than optimal for investors.

The economic benefits of voluntary disclosure have been well documented. Prior literature has often noted that a manager who seeks to maximize firm value and is in possession of private information will have an incentive to disclose it if it conveys good news to investors. Earnings forecasts signal changes to the market value of the firm and drive the investor’s perception of the managers’ ability to anticipate future changes in the firm’s economic environment. In sum, voluntary disclosure improves the firm’s value by reducing information asymmetry between managers and investors and generally by lowering the cost of capital. Estimation of the economic value of IT-related activities is somewhat subjective (e.g., whether to use annualized or cumulative expenses, the scope or duration of activities considered, etc.). Firm managers should carefully consider which facts to release and how they will be interpreted by the market. For example, prior work showed that capital markets tend to react positively and immediately to the disclosure of IT investments; thus such disclosures eventually improve perceived firm value. Managers can choose the type and amount of information they disclose; hence, managers can use public disclosure strategically for their company.

However, the effect of disclosure cannot always be guaranteed. Because managers have self-serving incentives, investors react only when the disclosure is perceived as credible. Thus managers should increase the credibility of disclosures by providing more specific and verifiable statements [13]. In the context of IT investments, credibility is even more important because of their risky and complex nature. Therefore, we argue that investors will regard the disclosure as credible, and the perceived economic benefits will be strengthened, when the disclosure provides specific information on the firm’s plans, costs and benefits.

## 2.1. IT disclosure quality

Our study focused on the quality of public releases made voluntarily by firms about their IT-related activities (IT disclosure quality); these include information on IT spending, IT types in different stages, or IT management. They are either quantitative (e.g., a 5% increase in sales, or a reduction of inventory holding costs by 13%) or qualitative (e.g., improving decision-making by having more information available). Therefore to measure disclosure quality on IT investments, we first had to create indices for quantitative and qualitative disclosure quality.

The first index, quantitative information (see Appendix A), was intended to describe how the firm had invested IT expenditures using monetary or numeric values. We used a coding instrument that was based on IT payoff by Kudyba and Dewan [14] involving three subcategories: (1) IT spending, such as the direct effect of previous, current, or future IT spending, changes in IT spending, and comparisons with competitors’ spending; (2) IT productivity, including actual IT development achievements (e.g., output, efficiency, decreased machine time, etc.) or human resource-level (sales per employee, etc.); and (3) IT returns, such as growth, profitability (e.g., ROA, ROS, ROE, and market share), etc.

The second index, qualitative information (see Appendix B), was for descriptive information that most firms use in evaluating the impact of IT investments. Assessment instruments for such information were based on feedback from interviews with two experienced practitioners to produce a set of six categories for assessing IT disclosure quality. Initially, we reviewed all IT investment announcements and documented the IT aspects disclosed in them. Next, we identified the six most common aspects of IT that were contained in our sample. At the suggestion of a referee of this paper, we partly used longitudinal data, but, as it was not our main focus, we did not analyse any dynamic effects. To verify whether the qualitative aspects of IT disclosure quality represented current practice, one of the co-authors discussed this question with two CIOs from Research In Motion (RIM) and updated the categories per their suggestions. All changes were related to refinements of the qualitative categories. In general, the CIOs agreed with the items on our list. Essentially, the criteria used for qualitative information revolved around: (1) Status: IT project status (e.g., proposed time for IT launch, estimated time to completion, or progress notice); (2) IT acquisition: (e.g., through purchase, adoption, or development); (3) OppRisks: (potential opportunities or risks from technological, strategic, and organizational aspects); (4) Market (market or environmental factors); (5) Competition: (at the industry level); and (6) Contract: (potential achievements from the legal relationships with customers or suppliers).

Previous studies have listed the types of IT information that can increase firm value. We specifically focused on quantitative information because the disclosure of monetary or numeric information improves the credibility of IT information in the market. In contrast, our qualitative measure were based on previous studies in which investment professionals had used surveys and on interviews with knowledgeable practitioners to determine the important types of qualitative information affecting the firm’s valuation.

## 2.2. Information quality in the market

Prior research defined information quality as public information precision. When firms announce news in the market, both public and private information precisions are needed. Obviously, private information precision is idiosyncratic and dependent on the individual making the announcement. An increase in information precision reduces market uncertainty, however, the effect on information asymmetry of public vs. private information precision leads to different levels of information risk. An increase in public information precision lessens information asymmetry through a reduction in the market’s private information needs. In contrast, an increase in private information precision raises information asymmetry. Thus, an increase in public information precision can improve firm value in the market by moderating information risk, while an increase in private information precision cannot be clearly determined.

In our study, the public information precision around IT investments disclosure was employed as a proxy for information quality in the market. Prior research has argued that as information quality increases, managers have an incentive to disclose more, because investors regard such disclosures as being more informative and thus subsequently have a more positive reaction to the disclosure [11]. Thus, we expected that managers would be more likely to disclose IT-related activities when the firm’s public information precision was higher. Thus:

H1. Firms’ IT disclosure quality is positively associated with the level of public information precision in the market.

## 2.3. Strategic IT role

Firms signal their value by investing in new technology. Thus, IT investment value can be considered to be a strategic IT role. Dehning et al. identified three such roles

\- automation (e.g., replacing human labor by automated processes);

\- information (e.g., providing information about business activities to senior management and employees throughout the firm); and

\- transformation (e.g., redefining business and industry processes and relationships).

Based on these, we re-classified IT investments disclosure into two strategic role categories: risk-averse, where firms use IT to automate human labor and invest in IT in order to improve the efficiency of existing business processes, and risk-taking, by redefining, innovating, and enhancing their overall enterprisewide risk to further business management beyond simple automation using IT initiatives. Although such innovations often prove unsuccessful, these investments produce a high-risk, high ROI scenario. According to Dehning et al. firms with transformational IT investments introduce radical business processes that select value chain participants and create new market structures as a way of better positioning themselves within the industry.

We therefore expected that transformational IT investment disclosures would lead to greater benefits by providing favourable information of the firms’ prospects and managers’ ability to enhance long-term outcome and enterprise-wide risk management. Thus, managers are more likely to disclose this type of IT investment, because the IT would be more value-relevant.

H2. Firms making transformational IT investments are more likely to make a higher quality disclosure of their IT investments than firms making automational and informational IT investments.

## 2.4. IT asset specificity

A resource is asset-specific when it cannot be readily redeployed by other firms. Asset specificity has been acknowledged as a critical dimension of IT, given most applications are proprietary in nature and developed to satisfy the firm’s specific needs. Thus IT functions are becoming increasingly standardized or specific. In addition, market transactions involving highly specialized assets create relational dependency among partners, with high transaction costs

The risk of highly asset-specific IT, can be substantial. Firms that invest highly in such systems are likely to generate agency problems that could lead to cost overruns, lengthy implementation times, and project failure. In addition, an IT-induced agent risk arises when highly asset-specific IT resources have received attention in practice, reducing firm value in the market. Therefore, such risk factors could seriously discourage managers from disclosing an IT investment that is highly asset-specific.

H3. Firms making high asset-specific IT investments are less likely to make a higher quality disclosure on their IT investments than firms making low asset-specific IT investments.

## 2.5. Growth opportunities

When the firm is viewed as having low growth potential, management has an incentive to disclose all news that may indicate improved growth. Prior studies documented that IT investment was positively associated with a firm’s growth potential in the market [6,10]. To improve firm value, managers are likely to disclose IT investment when the firm’s growth potential has been perceived as being poor.

Bushman and Smith [5] suggested that an expanded disclosure contributed directly to a firm’s economic performance by reducing agency conflicts (e.g., by ensuring efficient management of assets in place and encouraging better project selection). Such reduction in agency conflicts positively affects perceived growth potential in the market. The disclosure of IT investment then can mitigate information asymmetry between managers and investors, and might reduce the possibility of agency conflict by providing more detailed information on the firm’s investment strategy. Thus, management in firms having lower perceived growth prospects will be more motivated to disclose favourable information.

H4. Firms with low growth prospects in the market are more likely to make a higher quality disclosure on IT investments than firms with high growth prospects.

## 3. Research and methodology

## 3.1. Sample

We collected data from three different sources: publications by Im et al. and Chatterjee et al. and a search of Lexis-Nexis. To be consistent with the study by, Oh et al. our sample covered the years from 1985 to 1999. There are two advantages from using these years: the year 2000, included work in fixing the Y2K problem and dealing with the dot-com bubble. Also in this period there was an increase in IT investments due to ERP adoptions, RFID applications, etc., with a focus on pre-2000 data, our study may be perceived as being out of step with current practice, but our measures were developed by incorporating current practitioners’ recommendations and thus they should be comparable with current practice.

We validated our samples by employing the same approach as that used by Oh et al. We started with the set from prior publications of 352 IT announcements, from which we deleted 68 because we could not find enough data in the original announcement (the content of the articles were sparse, or the announcements conflicted with other material, such as M&A, dividend, or earnings news). Then another 91 announcements were deleted because of a missing value in Compustat or the duplication of the events between the two publications. After deleting another 44 announcements with a missing value in IBES, 149 announcements remained from other peoples’ publications. We then made our own search of daily publications, such as business newspapers, national newspapers, regional newspapers, PR Newswire, and Business Wire in Lexis-Nexus from January 1, 1998 to December 31, 1999. Due to our consistent definition of IT, only early (1998–1999) IT investments disclosures were considered by using keywords such as computer, IT, system, hardware, software, client/server, DSS, EDI, ERP, Internet, etc. along with action verbs, such as announce, contract, develop, implement, launch, purchase, etc.

One of the authors examined the content of these disclosures. After excluding announcements that involved other major events such as earnings, dividends, mergers, and acquisitions, 157 were identified. To enforce reliability, 29 were removed because of missing values in I/B/E/S variables. Only announcements with more than three analysts following were kept, leaving 128 announcements to add to our study. The final sample was therefore of 277 (149 from prior studies and 128 from our own search).

## 3.2. Measurement of IT investments disclosure quality

Our quantitative IT investments disclosure score was based on the literature that had examined IT investment and firm performance as a measure of disclosure. The quantitative IT disclosure score (QTindex) consisted of monetary or numeric values representing the benefits and costs.

Our qualitative IT investments disclosure score was based on interviews with practitioners and a review of all announcements in our samples. The qualitative IT investments disclosure score (QLindex) included descriptive information of most firms; this is useful in assessing the impact of IT investments, such as their potential opportunities or risks from activities, acquisitions (e.g., through purchase or merger), market competition, or contractual relationships.

Using the instruments shown in Appendices A and B, we assigned a disclosure score to each firm in the sample for each of the two types of disclosure. Specifically, we allocated one point for specific or numeric values (quantitative disclosure index score) and one point for each of the six descriptive pieces of information (qualitative disclosure index score). To ensure that the index captured the level of disclosure rather than the frequency of disclosure, we only coded the firm as disclosing an item once, even when it was discussed several times. We added the scores for each firm and use a log transformation of the sum plus one to arrive at our measure of IT disclosure quality. To verify the IT investments disclosure score, all announcements were independently coded by one of the authors and two graduate students. The percentage of agreement and inter-rater reliability among these three independent coders was above 97% for the quantitative disclosure index score and 89% for the qualitative one. Following the coding process, the observed disagreements were reconciled through discussions between the three coders.

## 3.3. Measurement of independent variables

Our primary objective was to prove our hypothesis that IT investments disclosure was influenced by information quality, strategic IT role, IT asset specificity, and growth opportunities.

## 3.3.1. Information quality

Our measure of information quality was based on the BKLS approach [7], which includes several conceptual measures to use when examining the stock market’s information environment. Based on the assumption that analysts’ forecasts are unbiased, BKLS measures how analysts’ public and private precision can be estimated. The empirical validity of the BKLS [3] model assumes that the analysts’ earnings forecasts are unbiased and that individual earnings forecast errors solely reflect the properties that information analysts want to convey; obviously, violation of these assumptions can weaken the empirical validity, though the BKLS model has been successfully used in prior studies [4].

In our study, we focused only on the public information precision as a proxy for information quality in the market; apparently public information precision is positively related to information quality in the market, while an increase in private information precision can actually reduce its quality, and firm value in the market is higher when the firm’s public information precision is higher.

Thus, we ran the following prediction model using analysts forecast error and dispersion as proxies of the information environment, and we defined public information precision in the market as:

$$
\mathrm{PUB} = \frac {\mathrm{SE} - (D / N)}{\left[ (\mathrm{SE} - (D / N) + D) \right] ^ {2}}\tag{1}
$$

where:

$$
D = \frac {1}{(N - 1)} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \operatorname{Var} (\mu_ {i} - \mu) \text {   and   } \mathrm{SE} = E [ (y - \mu) ^ {2} ]
$$

E is an expected value; N is a number of analysts following the firm; y is earnings that analysts forecast; $\mu _ { i }$ is an analyst i’s earnings forecast; m is a mean value of the earnings forecasts.

In the BKLS model, the precision of public information is constructed from observable properties of analysts’ forecasts, such as the forecast dispersion (D) (normalized by the absolute value of the actual EPS), the squared error in the mean forecast (SE) (normalized by the absolute value of the actual EPS), and the number of forecasts (N). The expected forecast dispersion (D) was the variance of analysts’ earnings forecasts issued during the year in which IT investment was announced, divided by the absolute value of the actual EPS. The squared error in the mean forecast (SE) was estimated using the difference between actual earnings and mean forecast for the period, also divided by the absolute value of actual EPS. Public information precision (PUB) was estimated using SE and D in the equations, and it was winsorized at the 5th percentile.

## 3.3.2. Strategic IT role

Following the recommendation of Dehning et al., we defined the variable strategic IT role (STRAIT) to be equal to one if firms engaged in transformative IT strategic investments and zero for automational or informational IT strategic investments. Our interrater reliability for STRAIT was 0.9, with all differences reconciled between the coders.

## 3.3.3. IT asset specificity

We defined the variable IT asset specificity (ASSETSPEC) to be equal to one if the IT investments disclosure focused on high asset specificity; otherwise, as in prior studies, we assumed it to be zero. When the IT investments disclosure focused on ASSETSPEC, a firm required specialized equipment, operating procedures, or systems tailored for use in a single organization, physical- or humanspecific systems, and long-term co-development between the user and the supplier. Our inter-rater reliability for ASSETSPEC was 0.84, with all differences reconciled between the coders.

## 3.3.4. Growth opportunities

Our growth measure was the market-to-book ratio (MB), as used in prior research. MB was estimated by the market capitalization at the end of the quarter immediately prior to the disclosure divided by total shareholders’ equity at the end of the quarter prior to the disclosure. In our third hypothesis, we expected that firms with lower growth prospects in the market were more likely to disclose their IT investment. Therefore, we expected a negative coefficient estimate of MB.

## 3.4. Control variables

Our five control variables were: firm size (SIZE) (prior research supported a positive association between managers’ disclosure choice and firm size [1], the market beta (BETA) (shown previously to be associated with the firms’ disclosure policy), the number of analysts following a firm (ACOV), the current earnings surprise (ESUR) (to control for earnings uncertainty), the high-tech industry membership (HTEC) [2] argued that high-tech firms are more (or less) likely to disclose IT in response to the informational demand in the market due to higher proprietary costs or uncertainty), industry fixed effects based on two-digit SIC codes, and time variation through fixed year effects.

## 4.1. Sensitivity analyses

We examined the effect of IT investments disclosure quality when information is disclosed by a client rather than a vendor. As each IT investment is made, clients or vendors may decide but is not required to disclose this fact to interested stakeholders thus, the disclosure quality may be affected by who discloses the information. In untabulated tests, we examine whether our results were sensitive to who controls the disclosure by including an indicator of 1 when the investment was announced by a client. Our results were qualitatively similar and our inferences did not depend on the disclosure agent.

Voluntary disclosure is a substitute or complementary action for mandatory disclosure. Managers may disclose more when they feel that their earnings are not large enough to show the firm’s real value. Alternatively, managers whose earnings quality is high may still disclose more because they feel that investors value it. We therefore performed a sensitivity test by including the earnings quality measure (accruals quality) as an additional explanatory variable; we estimated this using McNichols’ [17] modified approach of Dechow and Dichev’s [8] model. The sample size consequently decreased to 189 because of missing variables required to make the estimate the accrual quality. Our results (untabulated) for this sample are slightly stronger and our inferences did not change, providing some assurance that our findings were unlikely to be driven by omitting corporate characteristics for our sample firms.

## 5. Discussion

We examined IT-related voluntary disclosures for 277 firms and found significant diversity in the firms' informational environment. Although IT-related information was potentially costly to disclose, our data showed that firms did indeed disclose both quantitative and qualitative information about their IT activities. However, with voluntary disclosure of IT-related actives, a wider disclosure would assist shareholders and investors in understanding the overall payoff of IT in the firm. In addition, apparently managers are more likely to disclose quantitative information related to IT when the information quality in the market is higher.

Approximately 70% of the firms we surveyed qualitatively disclose market and environmental conditions, potential achievements from the contractual relationships with another parties and potential opportunities or risks. Management teams may also want to show that they are undertaking value added IT applications to become more productive, innovative, competitive and thus more profitable. Highly asset-specific IT is not well explained by a quantitative perspective, and transformative IT is much better explained by it. However, this may not be perceived by the market as credible as quantitative disclosure. In addition, firms with low growth prospects are more likely to make higher quality disclosures on IT investments than firms with high growth prospects, consistent with the suggestion of Bushman and Smith that these firms disclose more quantitative information, and thus managers believe that quantitative IT disclosure can reduce agency conflicts by reducing information asymmetry between managers and investors.

Our findings have important implications for IS practitioners. Even though IT investments disclosure is not mandated, SEC regulatory standards on MD&A may require firm disclosure about ERP in their annual filings. Such disclosure is consistent with the Jenkin’s report recommendation that opportunities be disclosed and may also provide justification for material capital expenditures disclosed in MD&A. Disclosures help investors understand the key IT activities that create short- or long-term shareholder value. Thus, this information may help stockholders by reducing information search costs and allowing them to improve their prospects.

Given the current lack of guidelines for IT disclosure, our study provides some insight for practitioners and policymakers by suggesting how they can determine disclosure quality and maximize its economic benefits. We therefore suggest that more guidance from policymakers is needed to improve disclosure practices for IT-related disclosures.

There were several limitations to our analyses. First, the fundamental assumption was that IT disclosure was driven by factors that affected information risk perceived by stockholders in stock markets; managers may have other incentives to disclose; e.g., when a manager intends to sell the business in the near future, he or she may make a disclosure to attract potential buyers. By eliminating such disclosures, we have avoided compounding effect, thus our selection of proxy variables based on information costs and benefits may also be subject to criticism. Second, subjectivity was involved in the identification and characterization of the contents of IT investments disclosures due to a general vagueness in the announcements. Third, factors such as IT intensity, the current state of IT, and several new areas of IT investments could play a critical role in determining the costs or benefits of IT investments disclosure.

Researchers and managers can gain deeper insights into why and how the capital market responds to disclosures of IT-related activities. Investigating voluntary disclosure behavior is important, since the protection and increase of firm value is a key indicator of the effectiveness of strategically oriented accounting and ITrelated initiatives. Disclosure of IT investments provides valuerelevant, forward-looking information to the capital market by improving information quality. Voluntary IT disclosures decrease information asymmetry aid in assessing the amount, timing, and uncertainty of future cash flows by including information about costs and benefits.
