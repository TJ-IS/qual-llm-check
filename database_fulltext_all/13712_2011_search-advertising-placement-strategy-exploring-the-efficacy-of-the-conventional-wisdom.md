## A B S T R A C T

Search advertising is one of the most important forms of electronic commerce. While click-through rates are considered a key measure of search advertising effectiveness by search providers, brand attention can also be a valuable objective. Our paper reports on an experiment that investigated how search advertisement placement affected search users’ brand recall and recognition. The results showed that semantically associated search ads generated significantly higher levels of brand attention than contextually associated ones. Significant interaction effects were found among search ad position keyword association, and search result quality. Implications for decision makers are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Search engines play a major role in many companies’ Internet marketing strategies [7,16]. The popularity of search engines as a gateway for accessing Internet resources has given rise to an important form of electronic commerce: search advertising. Search advertising allows advertisers to target their ads with search keywords, and display them alongside search results. A report by PricewaterhouseCoopers stated that search advertising revenue in the US reached \$5.1 billion in the first half of 2009 and represented 47% of all Internet advertising revenues, while banner ads generated \$2.4 billion in the same period. Search advertising revenue growth may be partially attributable to the revolutionary auction-based pay-for-click advertising model. Despite its importance, search advertising has not received as much attention in research as other forms of advertising [5].

Search advertising differs from banner advertising in two critical aspects. First, most search ads are text-based, consisting of a title, a description, and a link to a landing page. Advertisers have little latitude in controlling design elements such as color, image, and animation commonly used in banner advertising to attract users’ attention, or make their ads stand out. Second, unlike banner ads that may be displayed on any part of a web page, search ads are displayed together with competing advertisements on either the top or right-hand side of the search results. Although major search engines such as Google, Yahoo!, and Microsoft use different algorithms for ad placement, the search ad’s position is determined by the keyword’s popularity and how much an advertiser is willing to pay when the ad is clicked. Given these constraints, advertisers face two crucial decisions in planning a search advertising campaign:

(1) with what keywords should the search ad be associated, and (2) how much should we bid for these keywords.

The first decision determines the search result pages on which the ad will be presented, while the second determines the page location at which the ad will appear.

As a service to their subscribers, search-advertising programs offer advice on where and how search ads should be placed. For instance, Google recommends that one should only bid on keywords that are ‘‘relevant’’ to the products or services to be advertised. It provides a keyword selection tool which generates ‘‘relevant’’ keywords based on a search term. It also suggests that top-positioned ads will produce better results than side-positioned ads by charging higher prices for top-positioned ads. Since Google and other search engines have access to proprietary information about how users interact with and respond to search ads, few have questioned the validity and applicability of this ‘‘conventional wisdom’’.

Given the pay-for-click model employed by search advertising programs, it is reasonable to assume that their advice is geared toward increasing the click-through rate, which is defined as the number of clicks divided by the total number of times an ad is displayed. While click-through is an important measure of advertising effectiveness, brand awareness and attitude toward the ad are also valuable measures [13]. Consequently, an important research consideration is whether advertisers should pursue marketing campaign objectives other than those driven by the desire to maximize click-throughs.

One may argue that a click-through maximizing strategy should also achieve brand awareness objectives, because attention to a search ad is a prerequisite to click-through; but it seems plausible that search users may notice an ad and form positive impressions about the brand without clicking. With a pay-for-click model, companies may be able to maximize the value of their search advertising budgets if they are able to generate brand awareness using a minimum number of click-throughs. Furthermore, companies can avoid bidding-wars by exploring alternative ad placement strategies.

Our research therefore focused on the impact of a search ad on brand attention, which was defined as a search user’s recall and recognition of a brand displayed in a search ad. Specifically, we examined how the placement of a search ad could affect brand recall and recognition by a search-user through the theoretical lens of the limited capacity model of attention and the theories of search behavior. We investigated three variables related to search ad recall and recognition: ad positioning, search ad–keyword association, and search result quality. Here we report on the results of an experiment conducted to assess the effects of these variables on search users’ recall and recognition of search ads.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Online advertising effectiveness

Building on advertising research, scholars have endeavored to understand the differences between online and traditional advertising media. An issue that has received considerable attention is how to measure online advertising effectiveness. Since interactivity is a key characteristic of the online medium, it has been argued that the web should be viewed primarily as a direct marketing tool. A banner ad on a website, for example, is similar to a coupon in a newspaper: its effectiveness is often measured by its redemption rate. Click-through, as the online analog to coupon redemption, has been regarded as the most important measure of online advertisement effectiveness. This view is supported by the practice of companies paying for advertisement clicks. In a study of online advertising practices, the majority of the media directors reported using only transaction-based measures such as click-through rates, registrations, and purchases to evaluate online advertising effectiveness [15].

Studies have investigated the effects of various banner formats and presentations on click-through. Chandon et al. [2] found that large-sized, animated, and targeted banners generated higher click-through rates. Lohtia et al. [10] investigated the impact of interactivity, color, and animation and found that the effects of content and design elements of banner advertisements were different for B2B and B2C web sites. Color was also thought to make an email message more emotionally evocative and thus increase the chance of an advertisement being clicked. From the information overload perspective, Wu and Yuan [19] studied how different types of display color and highlighting configurations could affect information search performance.

Other researchers, however, have suggested that online advertising can also impact brand awareness, attitude, and purchase intentions even in the absence of click-through. Yoo [20] argued that exposure to web advertising can lead to both directed and non-directed brand attention. While directed brand attention can lead to the conscious processing of an advertisement and possibly result in a click-through, non-directed brand attention can invoke subconscious processing which may affect implicit memory and attitudes toward the brand. Burns and Lutz [1] compared six different online ad formats and concluded that attitude toward online advertising format was directly related to attitude toward the advertisement. Yoon and Lee [21] compared the effects of clicked and non-clicked banner ads on brand awareness, brand attitudes, and implicit memory, finding that exposure effects produced by non-clicked banner ads were as strong as those produced by clicked banner ads. Therefore, by focusing on click-through rates alone, advertisers are probably undervaluing the effects of online advertising, limiting the applications of online advertising.

Although researchers have recognized the need to address nontransactional measures of online advertising effectiveness, most existing studies have focused on banner ads. There is a gap in our understanding of how search ads affect non-transactional measures of online effectiveness, such as brand attention. For our study, we defined brand attention as the ability of search users to recall and recognize advertised brands from search ads. Brand attention has been widely used in the traditional as well as online advertising literature as a measure of advertising effectiveness.

## 2.2. Limited capacity model of attention

The limited capacity model of attention, developed from cognitive psychology, is a general model for investigating how people process television and other types of mediated messages; in it there are two fundamental assumptions:

1. Information processing is one of the most frequent and important tasks people perform; it consists of three subprocesses: encoding (where people perceive stimuli and develop a mental representation of the information), storage (which connects the new information representation to that stored in memory), and retrieval (which manages and activates stored information representations in response to needs of the other two sub-processes).

2. People have limited ability for processing information [9] as there is a limited set of mental or cognitive resources available at any given time.

Building on these assumptions, the model suggests that cognitive resources are allocated to competing information processing tasks in the sub-processes through controlled (intentional) and automatic (unintentional) mechanisms. The controlled mechanism involves people consciously allocating cognitive resources to specific tasks and is employed when people have clear goals. For example, when a TV quiz show viewer tries to come up with the answer before it is revealed, he or she consciously concentrates cognitive resources to retrieving relevant information, On the other hand. the automatic mechanism assigns cognitive resources unconsciously in response to some stimulus; for instance, the TV viewer may suddenly notice a moving car in the background.

Stimuli that may invoke the automatic mechanism are believed to fall into two categories: those activated by information or messages that are relevant to the goals and needs of the individual or those activated by information or messaging that is unusual or unexpected.

Both the controlled and automatic mechanisms can simultaneously and independently allocate cognitive resources to different information processing tasks. However, tasks supported by the controlled mechanism are the primary tasks, receiving a higher priority in resource allocation than secondary tasks (supported by the automatic mechanism). The amount of cognitive resources allocated to the primary tasks depends on task intensity. When primary tasks require greater attention, more cognitive resources will be assigned to them and thus fewer resources will be available for secondary tasks.

Although the limited capacity model was originally used to investigate television messages, it is general and not limited to any specific media or types of message and content. Lee and Faber, for example, applied the model to examining the effects of embedded advertisements in video games on brand memory. However, no studies appear to have applied the limited capacity model to the search advertising context.

## 2.3. Theories of search behavior

Internet search has emerged as an important research topic in e-commerce and a number of theoretical frameworks are suggested based on human information retrieval, cognitive psychology, and consumer information search in order to model user search behavior [12,18]. The searches can be classified as either general or specific; in a general search, users explore the web by performing nondirected search on topics in which they have intrinsic interest and long-term involvement. However, a specific search involves a goal-oriented, directed search on a topic of current interest. Internet users are more likely to utilize a search engine when they are engaged in a specific information search.

Goal-oriented search may be characterized as a series of interactive episodes in a problem-solving process [17]. From that perspective, the user’s search behavior is not a single event; it involves various search strategies, stages, and goal states. When using a specific search engine, it can be viewed as a highly involved process of interactions between a user and the search engine. The process generally begins with a user needing information and deciding to use a search engine. Next, the user must determine which keyword will be most likely to generate relevant information. After querying the search engine, users evaluate the returned search results and determine whether they contain satisfactory information. If the search engine fails to provide relevant results, the user may choose a different keyword and repeat the search until the information needs are met. Results may also prompt the user to change search strategies or even modify the search goals between search episodes.

There are a number of factors affecting how quickly a search user can locate relevant information. Different structural and technical properties, such as engine size and depth, produce different results and performance for the same phrase. Users’ web experience and domain knowledge may also affect the search performance. Holscher and Strube [6] compared web search behavior of experienced and novice users and found that novices were more likely to use a search engine. Additionally, they had to modify or submit new queries more often due to insufficient specificity in their queries. Finally, different search styles and strategies can also affect search outcomes: by examining the logs of multiple search engines, it has been found that about half of the users examined only one or two pages of search results and only about half of the users modified the initial query in their search session.

## 3. Hypothesis development

Search engine users tend to engage in a specific information search with the clear goal of finding relevant information on a specific topic from the results generated by the engine. Therefore, during a search episode, the controlling mechanism allocates substantial cognitive resources to the primary task of evaluating search results. Any other processing tasks will occur through the automatic mechanism.

Users may pay attention to a search ad in a search episode because either it is evaluated as a part of the search results or the search ad invokes the automatic mechanism and is allocated secondary cognitive resources. Thus a search ad may be noticed between episodes because users explore information beyond the results and contemplate query modification.

Advertisers are limited in their ability to generate brand attention due to the advertising standards (e.g., limited color, animation, and other design elements) imposed by search providers. Consequently, the crucial decisions that advertisers are able to make to affect brand attention are the selection of the keywords with which their ads are associated and their placement on the result pages. Another relevant factor that should be considered is the effect of search engine result quality on their selection of keywords and also the placement of their ads on search results pages. Despite the fact that the advertisers have no real influence over search quality results, they should have a functional understanding of the possible effect of search quality.

## 3.1. Search ad positioning

Advertisers have some control over the search ad’s display position (top or side) on the result page. By outbidding competitors on a keyword, companies can ensure that their search ads will be displayed on the top of a page above organic search results. Alternatively, lower bids will lead to positions on the side of the page. Since Google and other search engines charge a premium for top-positioned ads, it is reasonable to assume that search engine providers have determined that search ads displayed at the top of a page enjoy greater click-through rates.

Prior research examined the placement of advertisements in movies and computer games and found that prominently placed ads in movies and games generate better brand attention than those placed in a peripheral area. Similarly, in performing Internet search tasks, users tend to focus their attention on the search results and evaluate them by looking first at the top of the page. A top-positioned search ad is displayed immediately above the search results, often with similar formatting and a slightly different background color. Consequently, a top-positioned search ad has a potential advantage in being evaluated as a part of a user’s search results. When users do not distinguish the search ad from the search results, there is a greater likelihood that the search ad will be cognitively processed as part of the primary task. However, when the search ad is displayed on the side of a page, it is less likely to be seen as a part of the primary task, thereby requiring additional cognitive capacity to process. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H1. A search ad displayed on top of the search results will generate greater brand attention than one displayed on the side of the search results.

## 3.2. Search ad–keyword association

Traditional media advertising research has shown that the context in which an ad appears can have a significant effect on how it is processed and received. When information in the ad is irrelevant to, or incongruent with, the medium, consumers seem to have difficulty in resolving the disparate information and are less likely to process it effectively, resulting in a less favorable attitude toward the ad. In contrast, highly relevant and congruent information can be easily integrated into the consumers’ mental models and thus be fully processed, producing positive attitudes toward the ad.

Several empirical studies have provided support for these propositions in an online environment. Rodgers [14] examined the effects of Internet sponsorship linkages on sponsor recall, attitude toward the sponsor, and intention to purchase; she found that relevant Internet sponsors were regarded more positively on all three dependent variables than irrelevant sponsors. Apparently, banner ad relevance affects the customer attitude to website content and its interaction with website reputation and product involvement on advertising effectiveness: i.e., advertisement relevance has significant impact on ad effectiveness for highinvolvement products.

In Internet search, advertisement relevance can be characterized by the association, or relationship, between the search ad and the search keyword. While advertisers are free to choose any set of keywords, it is unlikely that they would choose keywords at random (or select completely unrelated keywords). In our study, we focused on two specific types of search ad–keyword associations: semantic and contextual [3]. A semantic association refers to a search ad–keyword relationship where the keyword has synonymous or similar meanings to the product being promoted in the search ad. For example, ‘‘camera’’ is a keyword that is synonymous with Canon Corporation’s camera products. Lexical collocations, i.e., multi-word expressions, such as ‘‘digital camera’’ or ‘‘excellent camera’’, can also be seen as semantically associated with a Canon search ad for its camera products. A contextual association refers to a relationship where the search ad and keyword share a common context. For instance, a travel agency may decide to use the search keyword ‘‘camera’’ in the belief that someone who has bought a new camera may be planning a vacation. A semantically associated search ad may contain words and terms similar to the search results and seem to search users as information relevant to their goals. Therefore, the semantically associated search ad is more likely to activate an automatic mechanism of cognitive processing than would a contextually associated search ad. Thus,

H2. A semantically associated search ad will generate greater brand attention than will a contextually associated search ad.

The effects of search ad–keyword association on brand attention can be moderated by the search ad position. Users are likely to treat a top-positioned search ad as part of the search results, especially for semantically associated search ads, which may share common words and terms with the search results and thus fit the users’ mental model of what they expect from a keyword search; for contextually associated search ads displayed on top, users may sense the difference between the ad and the search results and decide to skip the search ads.

When displayed on the side, however, contextually associated search ads may activate the automatic mechanism for secondary cognitive processing because they are unusual or unexpected. Incongruent information in a contextually associated ad may provide a pronounced contrast, drawing more attention to them, and thus inducing greater brand attention [4]. Moore et al. [11] examined the effects of banner advertiser-web site context congruity on user-attention and attitude toward the advertisement. Their results showed that incongruity had a more positive effect on brand attention; i.e., its recall and recognition, while congruity had a more favorable effect on attitudes toward the advertisement. Therefore, the advantage that semantically associated search ads enjoy over contextually associated ones may not be as pronounced as any on the side because both types of search ads rely on secondary cognitive capacity for processing.

H3. There will be a position - association interaction with brand attention such that semantically associated search ads will generate even greater brand attention than contextually associated search ads when placed in the top position than in the side.

## 3.3. Search result quality

Search result quality is the extent to which the results match the user’s information needs in a search episode. Thus search result quality is not synonymous with the quality of the search engine. It is jointly determined by the search user and the search engine. For a given keyword, a search engine may fail to generate accurate and concise results due to: shortcomings in the search engine’s algorithms, multiple interpretations of the keywords, or lack of information about those keywords used [8]. On the other hand, users may choose incorrect or overly general keywords for the search. For example, someone may be interested in ‘‘laptop computers’’ but search with the keyword ‘‘computer’’. No matter how good the search engine is the user is likely to be disappointed by the search results. Studies have shown that misspecification of keywords is a key reason for low efficacy in online searches.

Search result quality can affect the likelihood that the ads displayed by the search engine will be noticed. When the quality of search results is high, a user is more likely to focus attention on the information presented in the results, thus, consuming most of the available cognitive capacity with the primary task and leaving less capacity for secondary processing. High quality search results may also reduce the amount of time a user consumes in the completion of the search; the user may follow a link to the information and not return to the result pages. On the other hand, if the search result quality is low and the links provide less relevant information, users are more likely to return to the result pages for more information or to consider using an alternate keyword in another search. Consequently, in the case of poorer search quality results, relatively less of the user’s available cognitive capacity will be consumed by the primary task of processing the information in the search results, leaving more cognitive capacity for secondary processing. Thus,

H4. Search ads will generate greater brand attention when displayed on low quality search result pages than on high quality search result pages.

Furthermore, the effects of search ad position and search ad– keyword association on brand attention may interact with search result quality. Specifically, on high quality result pages, users are presented with a list of relevant links and their attention will naturally focus on these results. Since a top-positioned, semantic search ad shares similar characteristics to the other results, it may be evaluated and processed just like any other. A top-positioned, contextual search ad will stand out in high quality result pages as an advertisement to be skipped. After evaluating several relevant result links from the top, however, users may broaden the scope of their attention and be willing to look at other topics. Therefore, to the extent that a side-positioned search ad is noticed on high quality result pages, it is possibly due to its difference from the rest of the results on the page.

H5. When the search result quality is high, a semantically associated ad will generate greater brand attention than a contextually associated ad in the top position, but a contextually associated ad will generate greater brand attention than a semantically associated ad in the side position.

When the search result quality is low, the user must try to find any relevant information on the result pages before electing to perform another search. When the search result quality is low with few of the links providing relevant information, users are more likely to explore other information on the page, including search ads. Also, instead of assessing the links sequentially, the user may begin to survey the entire page for anything that appears to be relevant. So, in the absence of highly relevant search results the user can allocate a greater proportion of his or her cognitive capacity to secondary tasks, and be more likely to consider search ads (automatic processing). Simultaneously, the user will allocate primary cognitive processing capacity to the task of identifying anything relevant to the keyword used in the search. Consequently, semantically related search ads will tend to be noticed more, regardless of their location, compared to contextually related ads when search result quality is low. Therefore,

H6. When the search result quality is low, a semantically associated search ad will generate greater brand attention than a contextually associated search ad in both top and side positions.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Experimental design and procedure

To test our hypotheses, we designed an experiment that manipulated two levels of three variables: search ad position, search ad–keyword association, and search result quality, resulting in a 2 (top position vs. side position) - 2 (semantic vs. contextual) - 2 (high vs. low quality) factor design. A website was constructed to mimic a search engine following the Google layout to increase external validity. On the first page, a search bar and a search button were presented. For each keyword used in the experiment, ten result pages with navigational links between the pages were developed based on real Google search results. These thus contained real contents and hyperlinks that would have been presented if the user had entered the keyword into the Google search engine. An advertisement area was created either on the top or the right-hand side of each result page (see Appendix). The website was hosted on a university server and could be accessed via the Internet with proper user authentication.

Two hundred and fifty undergraduate and MBA students from two US Mid-Atlantic universities participated in the experiment; they were given extra credit as an incentive to participate. The experiment was conducted during normal class sessions. At the start, participants were told that they would be evaluating a prototype of a new search engine. We further told them that the extra credit they would receive was based solely upon their participation, not on the opinions they expressed. Participants were then given the URL and a password to access one of the eight randomly assigned experimental conditions and given corresponding web pages by the web server.

Participants were first presented with a set of questions to determine their familiarity with conducting an Internet search and their knowledge of digital cameras. Next, participants were presented with the following scenario.

They were considering purchasing a new digital camera and had decided to search the web for information to support their decision.

They were asked to perform a search using the ‘‘new search engine’’ with the keyword ‘‘digital camera’’. After exploring the search results, participants were asked to complete an online questionnaire that assessed brand attention and perceived search engine quality, as well as collecting user demographic information. Perceived search engine quality and the demographic information were collected as control variables. Measures were taken to ensure that participants could not consult the result pages while answering the questions. The entire experiment session took about 25–30 min to complete.

## 4.2. Variable manipulation

## 4.2.1. Ad position

Modeled after Google’s search site, a search ad consisted of a title, a brief description, and a hyperlink to a landing page. For the top-positioned ad, the ad was displayed immediately above the search results and consisted of two lines with the title on one line and the hyperlink and description on the other. The advertisement area had a light yellow color as background and the term ‘‘Sponsored Link’’ on the top right. Participants who were presented with the side-positioned ad saw the ad on the righthand side of the page. The ad consisted of four lines: one title line, two description lines, and one hyperlink line. The background color for these search ads was white, as was the rest of the page. The term ‘‘Sponsored Link’’ was placed above the search ad. For both top- and side-positioned ads, there was a search ad displayed on the search result pages.

## 4.2.2. Search ad–keyword association

Search ads were developed for two fictitious companies; the first was an online retailer called digipix.com that sold digital cameras and the second was a travel agency named TrueVacation.com. Two versions of the same ad were created for each company: one for the top position and the other for the side position. Home pages for each company were created as the landing page when participants clicked on the link in the search ad. Participants assigned to the semantic association condition were exposed to the digipix.com ad but participants assigned to the contextual association condition were given the TrueVaction.com ad.

## 4.2.3. Search result quality

To manipulate the quality of search results, two sets of search results for the keyword ‘‘digital camera’’ were developed. For the high quality results condition, we utilized the search results returned when using the keyword search term ‘‘digital camera’’ for the Google website and adapted them as the result pages for our ‘‘new search engine’’ used in the experiment. Since Google is currently the most popular search engine and dominates the search market, it is reasonable to believe that these results include the best available information from the Internet and are of high quality. For the low quality search results condition, we substituted the keyword search term ‘‘digital’’ in another Google search (instead of ‘‘digital camera’’) and used the results to develop the pages for the low quality condition. Although there was some digital camera information, the search results for ‘‘digital’’ were much broader and included information such as digital history, digital TV, digital library, and so on. The search result quality manipulation was validated by the perceived search result quality.

## 4.2.4. Dependent variables

In our study, brand attention was defined as the participants ability to recall and recognize the search ad displayed with the search results. To assess recall, participants were asked to write down the name of the advertiser seen on the search result pages. The recall score was coded as 1 if participants correctly named the advertiser, or 0 if they failed to recall the name of the advertiser. The next question, on a separate web page, asked participants to choose the advertiser among a list of six company names. The recognition score was also coded as: 1 if participants chose the correct company, or 0 if their choice was incorrect. Brand attention was measured as the sum of the recall and recognition scores.

## 5. Results

Before testing the stated hypotheses, we performed analyses to ensure that the randomly assigned participants in the eight experiment conditions had similar familiarity with search engines and comparable involvement and familiarity with digital cameras. The results showed that none of the factors was significantly different across experiment conditions, so they were not considered as covariates in our analyses.

To test Hypotheses 1–4, we conducted a three-factor (search ad–keyword association, ad position, search quality) univariate ANOVA with the brand attention (recall and recognition) measure as the dependent variable. The results of this analysis are shown in Table 1. We hypothesized that a search ad placed on the top of the search result page would generate more attention than that of one placed on the side of the page. Although the attention measure was greater for the top-positioned ad (M = 0.56) than the sidepositioned ad $\left( M = 0 . 4 6 \right)$ , the difference in attention between the two conditions was not significant, F (1, 242) = 1.29, p = 0.26. Hence, Hypothesis 1 was not supported.

As predicted in Hypothesis 2, the ANOVA results found a significant main effect of search ad–keyword association on attention, $F \left( 1 , 2 4 2 \right) = 2 2 . 6 5 , p < 0 . 0 1$ . That is, participants who were shown a search ad that was semantically associated with the keyword (M = 0.71) were able to recall and recognize the search ad more often than those shown a contextually associated search ad (M = 0.30). The main effect of search ad–keyword association on attention was qualified by its significant interaction with the ad position, (F $( 1 , 2 4 2 ) = 4 . 4 4 , p = 0 . 0 4 )$ , as shown in Fig. 1. The results suggested that the difference in attention between a semantic search ad versus a contextual search ad was significantly greater when placed on the top of a page rather than on the side, and a contextual search ad placed on the side received more attention than when it was placed on top, supporting Hypothesis 3.

We proposed in Hypothesis 4 that a search ad would generate greater brand attention when displayed on low quality result pages than when it was placed on high quality result pages. The results, however, did not reveal a significant search quality main effect, F (1, 242) = 0.01, p = 0.93. The means of attention score for high quality (M = 0.51) and for low quality (M = 0.51) conditions did not differ. Therefore, there was no support for Hypothesis 4.

The effect of search quality on attention was found in the significant three-way (search ad–keyword association - ad position - search quality) interaction, $F \ ( 1 , \ 2 4 2 ) = 6 . 2 6 , \ p = 0 . 0 1$ . To further explore the interaction effects of search quality, we divided the sample data into two sets based on search quality and performed a two-factor (search ad–keyword association and ad position) univariate ANOVA on each data set. The results of this analysis are also shown in Table 1.

In addition, the main effect of search ad position was not significant in either search quality condition. ## 6. Discussion

Our study demonstrated that semantically associated search ads received significantly more attention than contextually associated search ads. The theories of search behavior suggested that search users are more goal-oriented than casual web browsers and typically have a predefined, conscious mental model for satisfying their needs when interacting with a search engine. This goal orientation leads search users to focus only on information fitting their mental model. Therefore, to the extent that a search ad could blend in with the search results and be a part of the primary task, it is more likely to be noticed and evaluated. A semantically associated search ad, containing similar words to the search keyword, has an advantage over a contextually associated search ad in blending in with the search results.

The results of our study showed that a top-positioned search ad did not generate more attention than a side-positioned search ad. According to conventional wisdom, the top position is the prime real estate in search marketing. Since companies often have to pay a substantial premium in order to obtain the top position, our results raise the question whether companies should pay a premium and if there is a need to be the highest bidder for all keywords. At least, companies should be cautious about pursuing the top ad position as a uniform bidding strategy.

The significant interaction effect between ad position and ad– keyword association indicated that the difference in brand attention between semantically and contextually associated search ads was greater at the top position than at the side. In other words, the top position offered even more significant advantage for semantically associated search ads. Due to their similarity to search results, semantically associated search ads placed at the top position are more likely to be evaluated as a part of search results. At the side position, however, both semantically and contextually associated search ads might require secondary attention resources for processing.

Our study suggested that search result quality was not a significant factor in affecting whether search users paid more or less attention to search ads. This may be because it is extremely easy for search users to perform repeated searches. When search users realize their search results do not contain the desired information they can quickly conduct another query. However, search result quality was found to moderate the interactions between ad position and ad–keyword association.

Our study makes a number of contributions. First, we applied the limited capacity model of attention to explain the interactions between search users and search ads and found strong support for many of the propositions derived from the theoretical model. Our study demonstrated that the limited capacity model of attention is a valuable theoretical lens for investigating the effects of search advertisement. Second, we showed that search ads could make impressions on, and garner brand attention from, users even without being clicked. The implication is that, when examining the effects of search ads, we should not only measure click-through rates but also evaluate their impact on brand attention. Third, we examined how the effects of search ads on brand attention varied, based on their position.

Advertisers need to take into account the interactions among keyword selection, search ad position, and search result quality when formulating their search ad bidding strategies. While it may be easier to follow a simple strategy of focusing on semantically related keywords at the top position, there are a limited number of semantically related keywords suitable for a company’s brands or products and companies may find themselves in direct competition with major rivals in a bidding war for the same keywords. Companies can better use their advertising budgets in employing creativity and imagination to identify relevant contextually related keywords with less competition so that their search ads are displayed more often with the same budget.

Although advertisers have no direct control over search result quality, more specific and precise keywords are likely to produce better quality results than keywords that are general in nature. Consequently, advertisers may develop placement strategies based on keyword specificity. By taking advantage of the pay-for-click business model, advertisers can also explore ways to maximize brand attention but minimize clicks so that search ads can be freely displayed. As more advertisers move to use search ads to gain brand attention, search providers may consider adjusting the payfor-click model.

There are a number of limitations of our study. In online advertising research, it is common that experiments with fictitious websites are often used to evaluate the effects of online ads as they give researchers complete control over experimental conditions. With a custom built ‘‘search engine’’, however, one concern is that there are only a few major search engines on the market and subjects could easily detect their differences in functionality and design. To address this issue, we mimicked the look and feel of Google when constructing the experimental website. Also, we told subjects that it was an experimental search engine and requested they use it in the same way as they would use a regular search engine. Another concern is the use of graduate and undergraduate students as experimental subjects. Although students are often employed in electronic commerce research, because they are available and yet thought to be representative of Internet users, a cross-sectional sample of more general Internet users would have provided more robust and generalizable results.

As an initial study, we focused on the effects of search ad placement on brand attention. While important, brand attention is not the only outcome that is of interest to companies. Other dependent measures such as attitude toward the brand, attitude toward the search ad, and click-through rates are also relevant to understanding search ad effectiveness and placement decisions.

## 7. Conclusion

Evidence points to search advertising as one of the most important modalities of e-commerce. As such, companies require a clear strategy for placing search ads in order to achieve their campaign objectives. Companies are increasingly recognizing the role of non-transactional performance metrics, such as brand attention, in assessing the performance of their online advertising efforts. Recognizing the differences in information processing between search users and web browsers, we applied the limitedcapacity model of attention to explore the ad placement conditions under which brand names shown in search ads would be noticed and recalled by search users. Results provided useful insights into the interactions between search users’ information processing and their attention to search ads placed in different positions and contexts.
