# Turning Liabilities of Global Operations into Assets: IT-enabled Social Integration Capacity and Exploratory Innovation

Abstract. Although the ability to produce exploratory innovations is important for firm performance, firms face difficulties in producing exploratory innovations because knowledge is often distributed across cultures and geographies. In this study, we examine whether information technology (IT) helps firms to overcome the liabilities of global operations particularly when it comes to creation of exploratory innovations. We argue that in formation technologies that promote social integration facilitate more novel knowledge recombinations that help to overcome the difficulties arising from global operations. We draw on the knowledge-based view of the firm and hypothesize that IT-enabled social integration capacity influences exploratory innovation by enabling firms to leverage global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion. Our empirical analyses using archival panel data from 120 public U.S. manufacturing firms from 2003 to 2014 support these hypotheses. A key implication of our results is that IT helps firms to achieve greater exploratory innovation by turning the potential liabilities of cultural diversity and geographical dispersion associated with global operations into assets.

## 1. Introduction

Exploratory innovations based on creating new knowledge or using novel recombinations of knowledge elements are important for a firm’s overall performance (Phelps 2010). Such innovations involve challenging existing approaches through search, discovery, experimentation, and risk taking (Katila and Ahuja 2002). Arguably, it should be easy for global firms to produce exploratory innovations because of the advantages associated with global operations, such as global cultural differences and global geographical dispersion that expand the diversity of a firm’s knowledge bases and enhance the novelty of knowledge available for exploratory innovation (Reus and Lamont 2009, Venaik et al. 2005). However, globalization is a double-edged sword for firms pursuing exploratory innovation because of the challenges in integrating knowledge that is distributed worldwide. In other words, globalization can be a liability because global cultural differences and geographical dispersion may hinder a firm’s ability to recognize, assimilate and develop new knowledge (Verbeke and Asmussen 2016, Hong and Pavlou 2017). For instance, despite its global presence and its large culturally diverse workforce, Nokia Corporation struggled to develop new products, and some argued that Nokia needed to broaden its global view to cope with rising competition in the smartphone market (Johnson 2011).

One way to overcome the challenges in managing knowledge in global firms is to promote social interactions and social connectedness to facilitate the development of social capital. For example, social connectedness boosts informal knowledge exchange and access to knowledge sources, helping individuals to combine and develop new knowledge leading to exploratory innovation (Jansen et al. 2006, Joshi et al. 2010). Often, firms use information technologies (IT) to solve problems of physical and temporal separation because IT can mimic face-to-face interactions and communications to build relationships and trust (Ettlie and Pavlou 2006, Pavlou and Dimoka 2006, Ou et al. 2014). Because IT fosters communication, social interactions, and collaboration (Susarla et al. 2012), IT can play a salient role in driving exploratory innovation in culturally diverse and geographically dispersed firms. In particular, enterprise social IT systems develop social capital by promoting social interactions and connectedness, suggesting that these systems help to socially integrate a firm (Joshi et al. 2010, Gomez´ et al. 2017). Firms such as Wells Fargo, NorthWestern Mutual, and General Motors use enterprise social systems to improve their innovation capabilities (Standing and Kiniti 2011, Ravichandran et al. 2017). Other firms recognize the value of enterprise social systems in promoting innovation and leveraging geographical dispersion and cultural differences. For instance, the storage vendor Seagate Technology, a global company, is a strong advocate of enterprise social systems, especially wikis. Seagate set up a wiki (IT@Seagate), which the IT department uses to generate ideas. The Chief Information Officer (CIO) of Seagate credits the wiki’s informality with fostering interactions and transparency across the culturally diverse and globally dispersed firm. The wiki expanded beyond the IT department so that other Seagate executives use it to interact with their staff, and the wiki became a key informationsharing and collaboration platform for the generation and cross-pollination of ideas (O’Keefe and Chen 2011).

In this study, we examine how IT-enabled social integration capacity (IT-SIC) influences firm exploratory innovation in the presence of global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion.<sup>1</sup> We pose the following research questions: (1) How do IT-SIC and global cultural diversity jointly influence firm exploratory innovation? (2) How do IT-SIC and global geographical dispersion jointly influence firm exploratory innovation? We draw on the knowledgebased view of the firm and propose that IT-SIC has positive joint effects with global cultural diversity and with global geographical dispersion on exploratory innovation. We build on the premise that a firm’s global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion could have both positive and negative implications for knowledge creation, which, in turn, is associated with exploratory innovation.<sup>2</sup> Our principal thesis is that the combinations of IT-SIC with global cultural diversity and IT-SIC with global geographical dispersion positively influence exploratory innovation. We test our hypotheses using archival panel data of 120 U.S. manufacturing firms in the years 2003–2014. Our results suggest that IT-SIC helps firms that are culturally diverse and geographically dispersed globally to achieve exploratory innovation. The study contributes to IT innovation research and practice by showing how IT-SIC shapes exploratory innovation jointly with global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion. Our findings are important because they bridge two streams of work that have separately focused on how IT relates to innovation (e.g., Pavlou and El Sawy 2006, Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018) and to global operations (e.g., Ravichandran et al. 2009, Mithas et al. 2017) but have rarely focused on how IT and salient aspects of global operations interact to influence a critical facet of innovation.<sup>3</sup> A key implication of our results is that IT helps firms to achieve greater exploratory innovation by turning the potential liabilities of cultural diversity and geographical dispersion associated with global operations into assets. In other words, firms must synchronize their IT and globalization strategies to become more innovative and successful.

## 2. Theory and Hypotheses 2.1. IT and Exploratory Innovation

Although the role of IT in innovation has received significant research attention (Ravichandran et al. 2017, Saldanha et al. 2017), few studies focus on how IT enables specific and key facets of innovation, such as exploratory innovation. Past research suggests that exploratory innovations are outcomes of a firm searching for external knowledge in the form of new opportunities and experimenting with new knowledge elements for broadening its existing innovation domain into new and distant domains (Benner and Tushman 2002). Exploration refers to “things captured by terms such as search, variation, risk taking, experimentation, play, flexibility, discovery, innovation” (March 1991, p. 71). Exploratory innovations shift a firm away from its current knowledge base and lead it toward discovery and experimentation that is inherent with the search for novelty, with firms venturing into new areas and substantially deviating from prior innovation trajectories (Benner and Tushman 2002).

We argue that certain IT-enabled capabilities increase exploratory innovation by enabling search and discovery because IT enhances the firm’s ability to explore new knowledge. We focus on IT-SIC because IT-SIC systems help develop social capital, facilitate social interactions, and enhance social connectedness— factors associated with exploratory innovation (Tsai and Ghoshal 1998, Subramaniam and Youndt 2005, Jansen et al. 2006, Phelps 2010). We define IT-enabled social integration capacity (IT-SIC) as a firm’s use of enterprise social systems (social networks, wikis, blogs, microblogs, virtual worlds, groupware, really simple syndication (RSS) feeds, mashups, folksonomies, internal crowdsourcing) that enable “the development of social capital through direct human interactions and discourse” (Joshi et al. 2010, p. 475; Kane et al. 2014).

Table 1 shows three distinguishing features of IT-SIC that facilitate knowledge creation and sharing. First, IT-SIC systems are conversational (Wagner 2004) and offer flexibility in categorizing or structuring content because unstructured content is transformed by users into knowledge (McAfee 2006). IT-SIC systems help users to interact in novel ways that were once difficult in online or off-line settings (Kane et al. 2014). Their conversational nature encourages online social interactions and encourages the formation of informal communities or connections that develop social capital. Social interactions are channels of knowledge flows (Tsai and Ghoshal 1998). For instance, blogs, wikis, and social networks stimulate informal interactions and collaborations that create new knowledge by identifying patterns among user contributions (Wagner 2004, Huang et al. 2018). The conversational nature of IT-SIC systems helps employees to get feedback or transform the knowledge of others into their own knowledge and encapsulate it in the firm (Neeley and Leonardi 2018). The unstructured data in IT-SIC is transformed or refined by users collectively, facilitating new knowledge development. For example, tagging in blogs or mashups helps to categorize information, thus helping to develop new knowledge.

Second, unlike other technologies, the greater content visibility, network effects, threaded nature of discussions, and content persistence in IT-SIC systems enable recursive and adaptive communication and knowledge building (Argyris and Ransbotham 2016, Neeley and Leonardi 2018). Greater content visibility facilitates information accumulation from multiple knowledge sources because of more social interactions, thereby expanding the scope of knowledge and creating an open environment (Leonardi 2014). For example, content visibility in blogs and wikis promotes participatory content aggregation. IT-SIC systems are subject to network effects as more people engage in authoring, linking, or tagging content (McAfee 2006), thus aiding knowledge exchange and knowledge creation (Tsai and Ghoshal 1998). Thus, IT-SIC’s greater content visibility and network effects boost knowledge sharing, knowledge combination, and knowledge shaping via collaboration (Majchrzak et al. 2013a).

Third, IT-SIC systems enable the creation of digital profiles that facilitate access to weak ties via the transparency of user network connections (Kane et al. 2014). Digital profiles contain users’ identity and history of public communications in the IT-SIC system and provide features to view or add content to other users profiles. A user can observe the interactions and learn about knowledge shared between others even if the user has no direct connection to them. Users who find knowledge shared by others to whom they are not directly connected can tap this knowledge by establishing direct connections via IT-SIC systems. Thus, weak ties in IT-SIC systems bring different circles of knowledge together and help users form new bonds between existing circles. Weak ties encourage knowledge sharing, thereby boosting the potential for knowledge generation and recombination. Thus, digital profiles help locate employee knowledge expertise (Jarrahi and Sawyer 2013). The ability to find, connect, and share knowledge with subject matter experts gen erates ideas by combining skills. For example, microblogs, mashups, and wikis help knowledge workers use keyword searches to identify or find digital profiles of other employees with relevant skills and knowledge

Although the features of IT-SIC suggest a positive effect on knowledge exchange, the literature argues for mixed effects of IT-SIC (see Table A3 in the online appendix). On the positive side, IT-SIC systems foster knowledge sharing (Neeley and Leonardi 2018), enhance employee engagement and creativity (Koch et al. 2012), provide access to multiple information sources, and help locate expertise (Gray et al. 2011). On the negative side, IT-SIC systems may cause IT-culture conflict (Koch et al. 2013), productivity loss, or self-preservation behavior (Majchrzak et al. 2013b, Leonardi 2014) IT-SIC systems may also distract or cause hesitation to share knowledge (Argyris and Ransbotham 2016).

## 2.2. IT and Global Operations

IT facilitates global operations by boosting foreign responsiveness (Mithas et al. 2017), facilitating communication across geographical boundaries (Andersen and Foss 2005), supporting the coordination of international activities (Boudreau et al. 1998), facilitating the leveraging of assets across borders (Chari et al. 2007), and supporting the management of cultural differences (Shachaf 2008) and local demands (Kettinger et al. 2010). Global operations often require staff to be temporally or geographically distant, and IT combats such challenges by fostering communication across locations. The international business literature has highlighted salient aspects of global operations, such as institutional differences (Powell and Rhee 2016), tax regime differences (Zhou 2014), political differences (Salomon and Wu 2012), cultural differences (Flores and Aguilera 2007), and geographical distances (Flores and Aguilera 2007)

Our study focuses on cultural diversity and geographical dispersion because our focal IT (i.e., IT-SIC systems) is a set of social technologies that people from different cultures use to connect across distances and to exchange knowledge. We study the joint effect of IT-SIC with global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion on exploratory innovation. These two salient features are frictions associated with global operations, which can impede knowledge flows for exploratory innovation. First, global cultural diversity reflects the cultural differences between countries where the firm operates on the dimensions of power distance, individualism, uncertainty avoidance, and masculinity (Shin et al. 2017, Beugelsdijk et al. 2018). Greater differences in these values between countries imply higher global cultural diversity (Kogut and Singh 1988). Global cultural diversity can hinder collaboration because barriers to communication and cooperation arise from a lack of understanding of different cultures (Beugelsdijk et al. 2018). Second, global geographical dispersion reflects the physical remoteness between countries where the firm operates (Eden and Miller 2004). Geographical dispersion increases the difficulties and search cost to locate knowledge (Lahiri 2010).

## 2.3. Knowledge-Based View, IT-SIC, and Exploratory Innovation

We use the knowledge-based view as a theoretical underpinning for our study because exploratory innovation involves the discovery of novel knowledge el ements, their recombination and synthesis (Yayavaram and Ahuja 2008). The knowledge-based view posits that (1) knowledge is a key resource for value creation; (2) knowledge creation involves the development of novel knowledge elements and recombinations via organizational learning; (3) at a microfoundationa level, individuals are the locus of knowledge, and an organization serves as a medium for individuals to combine, share, and aggregate knowledge; and (4) an effective exchange of knowledge elements is associated with learning and problem solving that deliver value in forms such as innovations (Yayavaram and Ahuja 2008). Knowledge creation and recombination are closely linked to organizational learning because individuals in the organization learn based on existing knowledge, and individuals use this learning to process new knowledge and recombine it.

IT-SIC offers improvements in generating, capturing, and sharing knowledge and finding and tapping knowledge sources (McAfee 2009) because of the following features of IT-SIC: (1) conversational and unstructured user-generated information; (2) greater content visibility and network effects; and (3) digital profiles, weak ties, and transparency of user network connections (see Table 1). IT-SIC assists exploratory innovation because of the knowledge implications stemming from its key features (see column (3) of Table 1). First, IT-SIC helps users harness unstructured information, learn and assimilate it into knowledge, and apply it to commercial ends in the spirit of exploratory innovations in new areas (McAfee 2006, Joshi et al. 2010). As unstructured information is collectively learned, transformed, and iteratively refined by users, the collective wisdom of crowds reveals new patterns of knowledge, thereby boosting opportunities for exploratory innovation. Second, the content visibility and network effects of IT-SIC enhance social interactions among a large number of knowledge employees, facilitate learning, knowledge exchange, experimentation with new ideas, and development of novel knowledge recombinations and reconfigurations in new areas (Argyris and Ransbotham 2016). Third, IT-SIC fills knowledge gaps by helping employees to access expert knowledge and indirect ties, which can generate knowledge in new areas.

2.3.1. Effect of IT-SIC and Global Cultural Diversity on Exploratory Innovation. We argue that IT-SIC positively moderates the effect of global cultural diversity on exploratory innovation for three reasons (see column (4) of Table 1). First, global cultural diversity gives firms the potential to learn across cultures with unique competences and perspectives rooted in distinct national cultures (Morosini et al. 1998). The elevated combination potential in culturally diverse firms means that employees can learn from diverse practices (Reus and Lamont 2009). However, the enhanced combination potential does not, by itself, translate into synergistic benefit (Larsson and Finkelstein 1999) because “mere existence of diversity does not enhance learning. It only creates the potential for learning. To exploit this potential, the organization must create mechanisms and systems for such learning to take place” (Ghoshal 1987, p. 432). We suggest that IT-SIC is one mechanism that exploits the elevated learning potential because of its network effects that attract a variety of participants (Wattal et al. 2010). The enhanced learning opportunity afforded by IT-SIC’s network effects is amplified when culturally diverse employees exchange knowledge on IT-SIC platforms. IT-SIC’s content visibility encourages employee participation, which, in culturally diverse firms, leads to exploratory innovation because of the higher combination potential and wide span of ideas and perspectives. For example, the visibility of communications on blogs or social networks helps employees across cultures to contrib ute unique perspectives and ideas in new areas. With higher combination potential to be realized, the diversity of viewpoints in culturally diverse firms combined with IT-SIC enables exploratory innovations. For instance, Nokia used internal wikis, blogs, and crowdsourcing systems to manage global initiatives and imbibe local interests and cultures in new product development.

Second, learning based on presence in various global cultures is constrained by hindrances to information sharing because of social barriers that make communication across cultures difficult. Diverse cultures may create conflict, frictions, and communication breakdowns between employees, thus hindering information flow and impeding employee cooperation (Gomez-Mejia and Palich 1997). This hinders knowledge sharing and synergies, making it difficult to exploit expertise and hurting the firm’s exploratory innovations (Hambrick et al. 1996). Because of the conversational and open nature of interactions that create shared frames of reference, communications via IT-SIC among culturally diverse employees can mitigate frictions and misunderstandings (Joshi et al. 2010). The organizational learning literature suggests that these shared frames reconcile and integrate individual understandings (Leudar 1991). Thus, IT-SIC facilitates exploratory innovation by identifying different viewpoints and practices in the presence of global cultural diversity by providing abundant and dissimilar knowledge elements and by organizing unstructured information via users’ collective and iterative refinement. The combination of global cultural diversity and IT-SIC eventually helps develop novel and refined patterns and schemas that are different from the firm’s prior schemas, directing the firm toward exploratory innovation. For instance, tagging in wikis and microblogs facilitates the structuring of content that may uncover novel knowledge (McAfee 2009, Wattal et al. 2010).

Third, when employees use IT-SIC to build digital profiles on social networks, interaction opportunities may arise from informal connections and weak ties (Zhang et al. 2018). Informal networks, weak ties, and social capital enabled by IT-SIC are catalysts of organizational learning and knowledge transfer, particularly when one’s network spans communities with diverse knowledge bases. When a diverse knowledge base embedded in a culturally diverse firm is combined with the greater enablement of weak ties and transparency of user network connections via IT-SIC, the combination leads to greater generation of exploratory innovations. In line with the preceding arguments, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1. IT-enabled social integration capacity positively moderates the effect of global cultural diversity on firm exploratory innovation.

2.3.2. Effect of IT-SIC and Global Geographical Dispersion on Exploratory Innovation. We argue that IT-SIC positively moderates the effect of global geographical dispersion on exploratory innovation for three reasons (see column (5) of Table 1). First, access to weak ties is crucial for exploratory innovation. Weak ties are those ties that are formed with distant employees or acquaintances who are unlikely to exchange information directly. In geographically dispersed firms, it is difficult for employees to access expertise beyond national boundaries. Individuals without access to weak ties may be deprived of access to knowledge, including knowledge created or sourced externally by geographically distant subsidiaries in other nations (Venaik et al. 2005). Social profiles and network effects of IT-SIC are useful in leveraging weak ties by facilitating open access to expert knowledge (McAfee 2006). Thus, IT-SIC has a greater impact on exploratory innovation in firms geographically dispersed across countries because of the higher number of weak ties found in such firms. For example, Yammer helped engineers of a U.S. subsidiary of GlobalMoves (a pseudonym) to share knowledge with employees in foreign locations with whom they had little prior exposure (Neeley and Leonardi 2018).

Second, firms in distant countries face challenges because of time differences. Because IT-SIC facilitates both synchronous and asynchronous information exchange with greater visibility of content (McAfee 2009), IT-SIC helps maintain continuity in the knowledgegeneration processes despite time differences. For example, by using blogs, employees in China can build upon blog entries made by employees in the United States despite the different working hours in these countries. IT-SIC bridges cross-national geographical distance by making knowledge and expertise accessible to firm units across the world based on virtual proximity. Knowledge is sticky by nature and distributed in clusters (Tallman and Phene 2007). A combination of global geographical dispersion and IT-SIC leverages knowledge elements gathered from geographically dispersed countries because IT-SIC makes such knowledge “unstick” from its geographical cluster. Thus, IT-SIC’s network effects enable knowledge to spill over and flow “intentionally and unintentionally” across national boundaries (Tallman and Phene 2007, p. 253).

Third, national borders impede exploratory innovation because of the difficulty in building informal linkages (Morescalchi et al. 2015). Informal linkages are effective for knowledge transfer and creation across subsidiaries in different nations because such linkages increase awareness of the existence and location of various types of knowledge (Lahiri 2010). IT-SIC’s conversational nature facilitates social interactions and informal linkages, thus aiding knowledge sharing among employees in distant countries. Thus, IT-SIC bridges cross-national geographical gaps to a large extent, that is, IT-SIC enables the recombination of diverse knowledge elements in firms that are geographically dispersed across nations, thereby generating exploratory innovations. IT-SIC facilitates the sharing and organization of unstructured content across countries by iterative refinement. Firms with IT-SIC and global geographical dispersion are better prepared to assimilate and integrate ideas from unstructured sources in distant countries. They can recombine and reconfigure such content into knowledge elements to search for opportunities and develop innovations outside of existing domains. Hence, we posit the following:

Hypothesis 2. IT-enabled social integration capacity positively moderates the effect of global geographical dispersion on firm exploratory innovation.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Data

We collected data from multiple secondary sources. First, we obtained IT-SIC data from news announcements of firms in the United States over the 2003–2014 period. The collection of secondary data in the form of news announcement counts of firms’ initiatives is a well-established approach used to assess IT capabilities. Popular computer journals such as Computerworld, eWeek, and InformationWeek, have been used as data sources in many studies for more than 20 years because they cover 80%–90% of news about corporate IT spending and IT practices in the United States (Bharadwaj 2000). Based on this prior research, we used the Factiva database, which includes news from more than 30,000 sources and main computer journals, such as Computerworld, Networkworld, eWeek, eWeek security watch, Infoworld, and InformationWeek. These journals are comprehensive sources that consistently report IT spending and implementations. Thus, our data sources are comprehensive and broad, covering major news events about firms’ IT use.<sup>4</sup>

Second, we combined several patent data sets to compute exploratory innovation measures. We first collected firm-level patent data from two patent data sets developed by Hall et al. (2001) and Kogan et al. (2017). These data sets contain information about all U.S. patents granted by the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (Chen et al. 2016). We extended the patent data using the Google patent database. These databases contain the application date, grant date, and identifiers of patent assignee firms. Third, we used a proprietary database that collates data on firms’ subsidiaries and their locations from exhibit 21 of firms’ SEC 10-K reports. Exhibit 21 of form 10-K provides a detailed list of locations of a firm’s significant subsidiaries globally, and such data have been used in studies involving locations of subsidiaries (e.g., Mithas et al. 2017).

Fourth, we use CEPII’s GeoDist database (Mayer and Zignago 2011) to obtain data on the geographical coordinates of the capital cities of countries and to compute geographical distances. GeoDist has been widely used in prior research (e.g., Mayer and Zignago 2005, Hong and Pavlou 2017). We used data on subsidiaries and from GeoDist to compute global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion. Prior studies have also used subsidiaries to compute global cultural diversity (Gomez-Mejia and Palich 1997) and global geographical dispersion (Ravichandran et al. 2009) of firms. Finally, we collected data on control variables from Compustat. After merging the data sets, our sample contains approximately 650 firm-year observations from 120 manufacturing firms publicly traded in the United States for the time period 2003–2014. Because our dependent variable is based on patents, firms in manufacturing industries are more relevant for our study because service firms do not have a common patenting practice (Joshi et al. 2010).

## 3.2. Variables

3.2.1. IT-SIC. IT-SIC measures the use of IT applications that facilitate online collaboration, knowledge exchange, and cultivation of social interactions. IT-SIC applications include wikis, blogs, microblogs, enterprise social networking sites, virtual worlds, groupware, folksonomies, mashups, RSS feeds, and internal crowdsourcing because these systems are recognized as critical for social integration capacity (Joshi et al. 2010, Kaplan and Haenlein 2010, Kane et al. 2014). Our approach of measuring IT-SIC by IT applications that map to this capacity follows prior studies that use secondary data on IT applications to assess IT-enabled firm capabilities (Chi et al. 2010, Joshi et al. 2010). Following prior research, we measure IT-SIC by a cumulative count of enterprise social system implementations as indicated in the firms news announcements each year (Chi et al. 2010, Joshi et al. 2010). Online Appendix B provides a detailed road map and coding scheme that we followed for identifying a firm’s IT-SIC.

3.2.2. Exploratory Innovation. Following past studies, we use patent information to construct an exploratory innovation ratio that captures the intensity of a firm’s innovation in new areas and the extent of deviation from its existing innovation trajectories (Benner and Tushman 2002, Gao et al. 2018). Patent-based measures are widely used for innovation because they are externally validated via the patent examination pro cess. We define a firm’s existing patent pool in year t as the combination of its portfolio of patents applied for before year t and the backward citations made by these patents. A patent is categorized as exploratory if 80% or more of its backward citations are based on new knowledge outside of a firm’s existing patent pool (i.e., not citing the firm’s existing patents or backward citations made by those patents). We compute firm exploratory innovation (explore) in year t as the number of exploratory patents applied for in year t (and eventually granted) divided by the total number of patents applied for in year t (and eventually granted). A higher ratio indicates that more of the firm’s patents are dedicated to exploratory innovation that broadens its innovation domain (Benner and Tushman 2002). The use of patent application date instead of issue date is a common practice in the literature because it is the earliest point at which we can identify firm innovation, closely represents the time of knowledge creation when patentable work was actually completed, and avoids methodological issues from arbitrary lags between patent filing and patent issue (Sampson 2007).

For robustness, we use alternate measures of exploratory innovation. First, we use a 60% threshold and a 100% threshold to categorize patents as exploratory, similar to prior research (Benner and Tushman 2002). Second, we use a measure of exploratory innovation that represents the scope of search for new knowledge, measured as the proportion of previously unused citations (new citations) in a firm’s total number of citations in the focal year (Phelps 2010).

3.2.3. Global Cultural Diversity. This captures the extent to which the firm is spread across culturally different countries around the globe. The subsidiaries of firms “reflect the values, norms, and locally accepted practices of the societies in which they operate” (Rosenzweig and Singh 1991, p. 345). Following prior research (Gomez-Mejia et al. 2010), we use data on the subsidiaries of firms to measure global cultural diversity. We first computed the cultural distance for each subsidiary pair by using Kogut and Singh’s (1988) approach of averaging the deviations in Hofstede’s cultural dimension scores of the two countries where each subsidiary in the pair is located after adjusting for differences in variation of each dimension. This measure of cultural distance is commonly used in international business and management literatures (Hewett et al. 2003, Campbell et al. 2012) and is based on deviation of the two countries along Hofstede cultura dimensions (i.e., power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance; Hofstede et al. 2010). Global cultural diversity of the firm i in the focal year t is computed as a composite index by averaging the cultural distances for all the possible pairs of subsidiaries of the firm (Gomez-Mejia and Palich 1997). Thus,

$$
\text { Global   Cultural   Diversity } _ {i t} = \frac {1}{N _ {i t}} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {N _ {i t}} \left\{\frac {1}{4} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {4} \frac {\left(D _ {m j} - D _ {m k}\right) ^ {2}}{V _ {m}} \right\},
$$

where $D _ { m j }$ is the score on Hofstede’s cultural dimension m for the country where subsidiary j is located, $D _ { m k }$ is the score on cultural dimension m for the country where subsidiary k is located, $V _ { m }$ is the variance of score on cultural dimension $m ,$ and $N _ { i t }$ is the number of all the possible pairs of subsidiaries of firm i in year t.

3.2.4. Global Geographical Dispersion. This captures the extent to which the firm is dispersed across countries. First, for each possible pair of subsidiaries of the firm, we compute the great-circle distance between the capital cities of the countries in the pair. The greatcircle distance between the capital cities of countries is a widely used measure of geographical distance (e.g., Slangen and Beugelsdijk 2010). The geographical coordinates of the capital cities of countries are obtained from the CEPII GeoDist database (Mayer and Zignago 2011). Next, global geographical dispersion of the firm i in the focal year t is calculated by averaging the great-circle distances (between the country capital cities) for all the possible pairs of subsidiaries of the firm (Gomez-Mejia and Palich 1997). We use logged values to reduce skewness. Thus,

$$
\text { Global   Geographical   Dispersion } _ {i t} = \log \left(\frac {1}{N _ {i t}} \sum^ {N _ {i t}} G _ {j k}\right),
$$

where $G _ { j k }$ is the great-circle distance between the capital cities of the countries (in kilometers) in which subsidiary j and subsidiary k of the firm are located, and $N _ { i t }$ is the number of all the possible pairs of subsidiaries of firm i in year t.

3.2.5. Control Variables. We control for firm size because it may impact the pursuit of exploratory innovation (Alexiev et al. 2010). We control for patent stock because a larger stock of citable patents may lower exploratory innovation (Phelps 2010). R&D intensity controls for innovation investment (Rojas et al. 2018). Current ratio and prior profitability account for slack resources, and technological diversity controls for diversity that may affect exploration (Phelps 2010). OtherIT controls for other IT applications that may impact exploration (Xue et al. 2012). Age controls for the lower tendency of older firms to explore new domains (Sørensen and Stuart 2000). Industry control variables account for factors that are similar for firms in the same industry. Industry concentration controls for competitive pressure that may call for exploration, and industry dynamism controls for turbulence that can hinder exploration (Lavie et al. 2010). Sector and year dummies control for industry idiosyncrasies and time effects, such as macroeconomic variations.

## 3.3. Estimation Approach and Econometric Considerations

Consistent with prior research, we lag the independent and control variables by one year from the dependent variable, thus incorporating a time lag from the inputs to the output and reducing concerns of reverse causality and simultaneity (Saldanha et al. 2017). We estimate the following regression model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & E x p l o r e _ {i t + 1} \\ & \quad = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} I T - S I C _ {i t} \\ & \quad \quad + \beta_ {2} G l o b a l C u l t u r a l D i v e r s i t y _ {i t} \\ & \quad \quad + \beta_ {3} G l o b a l G e o g r a p h i c a l D i s p e r s i o n _ {i t} \\ & \quad \quad + \beta_ {4} I T - S I C _ {i t} \times G l o b a l C u l t u r a l D i v e r s i t y _ {i t} \\ & \quad \quad + \beta_ {5} I T - S I C _ {i t} \times G l o b a l G e o g r a p h i c a l D i s p e r s i o n _ {i t} \\ & \quad \quad + \beta_ {c} C o n t r o l s _ {i t} + \mu_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t + 1}, \end{array}
$$

where the βs are the coefficients, $\mu$ are the firm fixed effects, and ε is the error term.

We first estimate models using fixed-effects estimators (with robust standard errors) to account for unobserved firm heterogeneity. Fixed-effects models control for factors that are relatively invariant within firms and over time $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , firm traditions). Such heterogeneity includes factors that may influence IT-SIC and exploratory innovation, such as the motivations to invest in IT-SIC and in exploratory innovation. Models such as fixed-effects or random-effects have been used in prior studies on exploratory innovation (Phelps 2010). Fixed-effects models are conservative because they are estimated using variation in the variables over time within each firm and not using variation across firms.

To assess whether autocorrelation is an issue, we perform robustness tests by imposing an autoregressive AR1 structure in the error terms to account for autocorrelation. We estimate models using a first-order autoregressive fixed-effects estimator. We check for autocorrelation using the Baltagi–Wu locally best invariant test, which is a modified Durbin–Watson test for serial autocorrelation in unequally spaced unbalanced panel data (Baltagi and Wu 1999). We also use Wooldridge’s (2002) test for serial correlation in panel data models. Based on idiosyncratic errors of a linear panel data model, this is a Wald test of the null hypothesis of no autocorrelation. These tests did not detect any evidence of autocorrelation.

3.3.1. Accounting for Endogeneity. It is important to account for three considerations related to endogeneity. First, exploratory innovation may be sticky, that is, a firm’s exploratory innovation in year t may be a significant predictor of its exploratory innovation in year t + 1, implying a dynamic effect. Second, the relationships may be confounded by unobserved (by researchers) firm characteristics. For instance, firms with entrepreneurial managers may be more inclined to invest in IT-SIC (or expand a firm’s cultural diversity or geographical dispersion) and may also be more likely to pursue exploratory innovation. Third, there may be reverse causality because firms pursuing exploratory innovation may be more likely to invest in IT-SIC. Thus, IT-SIC, global cultural diversity, and global geographical dispersion may be endogenous. To account for such endogeneity considerations, we use the Arellano–Bond dynamic panel model, which is a generalized method of moments (GMM) estimator (Arellano and Bond 1991). This estimator constructs instruments from moment conditions, utilizing the panel’s time dimension. The estimator forms moment conditions using lagged levels of the dependent variable and endogenous variables with the first differences of the disturbances (Arellano and Bond 1991). The Arellano–Bond estimator is widely used in prior studies in information systems and management to address endogeneity (e.g., Tambe and Hitt 2013). Prior research recognizes the dynamic panel model as an effective way to address endogeneity involving the influence of firm variables on firm performance because the model addresses problems of dynamic endogeneity, simultaneity, and unobserved heterogeneity (e.g., Wintoki et al. 2012).

The Arellano–Bond model accounts for these considerations and suits our context for at least three reasons. First, it includes the lagged dependent variable as a regressor, thus “adding dynamics to the model” (Greene 2003, p. 307). Thus, the model accounts for the possibility that current realizations of the dependent variable may be influenced by its past value. Second, because the model uses first differences, time-invariant heterogeneity across firms is not an alternative explanation for our findings (Arellano and Bond 1991). Third, because the model allows for endogenous (i.e., not strictly exogenous) regressors, it accounts for “feedback effects,” that is, current values of the regressors may be influenced by prior shocks to the dependent variable (Arellano and Bond 1991) For example, a shock in exploratory innovation in one year may spur changes in IT-SIC or geographical dispersion the next year.

We use the one-step version of the Arellano–Bond estimator because the two-step version may have downward-biased standard errors, which may inflate significances (Bond 2002). We use robust standard errors to allow for heteroscedasticity. We repeated the analyses using two-step estimators and Windmeijer (2005) corrected cluster-robust standard errors, which gave qualitatively similar results. Arellano and Bond (1991) show that lagged values of the dependent variable are valid instruments for the first differenced dependent variable, and lagged values of the endogenous variables can serve as their own instruments. We treat our focal variables (IT-SIC, global geographical dispersion, and global cultural diversity) and other key firm control variables (e.g., R&D intensity) as endogenous, that is, not strictly exogenous. As in prior studies (Pfaffermayr 1999), we treat the industry control variables as exogenous because they are largely beyond a firm’s control and are unlikely to be correlated with the error terms. Because the firms in our sample are fairly established, shocks in exploratory innovation are unlikely to influence firm age in future periods. Thus, we treat firm age as exogenous, similar to prior research (Saldanha et al. 2013). We perform Sargan’s J-tests (Sargan 1958) of instrument validity that examine the moment conditions used in the estimations. The null is not rejected in all models, indicating that the instruments are appropriate (Sargan 1958, Arellano and Bond 1991). For robustness, we repeated the analyses by treating all firm variables as endogenous and found similar results.

We treat the endogenous variables as predetermined, that is, they may be influenced by the error terms from prior periods. This is realistic in the context of exploratory innovation. For example, it is realistic to assume that the errors (i.e., shocks in exploratory innovation) may influence future R&D investments rather than contemporaneous R&D investments. Thus, for predetermined variables, the first lag and higher lags are valid instruments (Arellano and Bond 1991). The first lag is a valid instrument because it is correlated with the errors dated t <sup>−</sup> 2 or earlier. Treating endogenous variables as predetermined (correlated with past disturbances) is similar to past studies (e.g., Kitchens et al. 2018). For robustness, we allow for the endogenous variables to be correlated with contemporaneous and past values of the errors, and we estimated models using second and higher lags as instruments. The results are substantively unchanged.<sup>5</sup>

We also note that, because our key focus is on the interaction effect of IT-SIC and global cultural diversity and on the interaction effect of IT-SIC with global geographical dispersion, endogeneity is less of a concern. This is because, as noted by prior research (Tambe et al. 2012, Ravichandran et al. 2017), interaction effect estimates are less subject to bias than main effect estimates because biases that affect the interaction term must be present only at the confluence of all factors in the term and not when the factors are present individually. In our study, there is unlikely to be a factor that is present at the confluence of IT-SIC and global cultural diversity (or at the confluence of IT-SIC and global geographical dispersion) but not when IT-SIC, global cultural diversity, or global geographical dispersion are individually present. For example, unobservable managerial acumen may explain why firms invest more in IT-SIC and are more adept at generating exploratory innovation. However, it is unlikely that unobservable managerial acumen matters only when both IT-SIC and global cultural diversity are high but does not matter when IT-SIC is high and global cultural diversity is low. As Tambe et al. (2012, p. 851) note, “that would require a much more unusual sort of unobservable factor,” which increased exploratory innovation “only when the other inputs were present as a group, but not individually.”

A key consideration for the GMM estimator is the possibility for a large number of instruments to inflate the p-values (Roodman 2009b). To assess the sensitivity of our results to this bias, we use multiple specifications by limiting the number of lagged instruments. Although there is no formal guideline on the number of instruments in prior literature, prior research proposes that a rule of thumb is to restrict the instruments to be fewer than the number of units (Roodman 2009a, b). Our results are retained and are not sensitive to the limits on the number of instruments. A crucial assumption of the Arellano–Bond dynamic panel estimator is the lack of autocorrelation in the error terms. By construction, the residuals of the differenced equation exhibit serial correlation but should not exhibit secondorder autocorrelation if the assumption of serial independence in the original errors is true (Arellano and Bond 1991). If the residuals of the differenced equation are autocorrelated beyond the first order, the lagged values of the dependent variable are not valid instruments for the lagged difference of the dependent variable. In our models, Arellano and Bond (1991) tests do not reject the null of no second-order autocorrelation in the first-differenced errors, thus rendering confidence in the validity of the results (Arellano and Bond 1991). Although these tests suggest that autocorrelation is not a problem, we also estimated dynamic panel models allowing for AR1 autocorrelation in the original residuals. As Baum (2006) suggests, if we assume that the residuals follow an AR1 process, then lags of three or higher are valid instruments for the differenced equation. The results are unchanged, further suggesting that autocorrelation is not a concern.

The first-difference transformation in the dynamic panel estimator magnifies the gaps in the data because one period of missing data are replaced with two missing differences. For robustness, we estimated models using forward orthogonal deviations (Arellano and Bover 1995, Roodman 2009a). Forward orthogonal deviation transforms each observation by subtracting the average of future observations, which is defined regardless of gaps for all but the last observation in each panel. The results using the forward orthogonal deviation transformation are qualitatively unchanged. As another robustness test, we use the System GMM estimator (Arellano and Bover 1995, Blundell and Bond 1998), which uses additiona moment conditions in which the lagged differences of the dependent variable are orthogonal to the levels of disturbances. Again, the results are substantively retained. The qualitatively unchanged results in all our sensitivity analyses suggest that the results are robust to variations in instrument sets and lags used.

## 4. Results

Table 4 shows the fixed-effects results. Columns (1) and (2) show the results using the 80% threshold. Column (1) shows that the main effect of IT-SIC is nonsignificant (p > 0.10), and global cultural diversity $( \beta = - 0 . 4 3 1 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and global geographical dispersion (β = <sup>−</sup>0.609, p < 0.01) have negative and significant coefficients as expected. The coefficient of IT-SIC × global cultural diversity is positive and significant (column (2), β = 0.241, p < 0.01). Hence, Hypothesis 1 is supported. The coefficient of IT-SIC × global geographical dispersion is positive and significant (column (2), $\beta = 0 . 1 7 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Hence, Hypothesis 2 is supported. The F-tests of interactions are significant, suggesting that the interactions are not jointly zero. The coeffficients of the control variables are broadly in expected directions. For example, firms with higher prior profitability, lower patent stock, higher competition, and lower age have higher exploratory innovation.

We conduct several robustness tests and sensitivity analyses. First, we estimate models using a 60% threshold (columns (3) and (4) in Table 4) and a 100% threshold (columns (5) and (6) in Table 4) to define exploratory innovation. The results are similar to those using the 80% threshold. Second, we estimate models that account for autocorrelated errors. The results are qualitatively unchanged (see Table A5 in the online appendix). We report the Baltagi and Wu (1999) locally best invariant (LBI) statistic, which is the equivalent of the Durbin–Watson statistic for serial correlation in unbalanced panel data. An LBI value far below two suggests serial correlation. In all our models, the Baltagi–Wu LBI values are close to two. These results suggest that autocorrelation is not an issue for our estimations. Third, a potential concern is sample bias given that we have an unbalanced panel and we use fixed-effects models. To address this concern, we use Wooldridge’s (2002) method, which suggests that sample bias is not problematic if the selection is uncorrelated with the idiosyncratic error.<sup>6</sup> We estimated fixed-effects models by adding a selection indicator with a one-period lag. This variable models the presence of firms by indicating which years are missing for each firm. The indicator is statistically nonsignificant, and the results remain qualitatively unchanged, implying no sample imbalance bias (Wooldridge 2002).

Fourth, we use dynamic panel models to account for endogeneity. Table 5 shows these estimates. Using an 80% threshold, the coefficients of IT-SIC × global cultural diversity (column (2), $\beta = 0 . 2 8 2 , \ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and IT-SIC × global geographical dispersion (column (2), $\beta = 0 . 1 7 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ are positive and significant, supporting Hypotheses 1 and 2, respectively. The results using the 60% and 100% thresholds (columns (3)–(6) in Table 5) are qualitatively unchanged. Fifth, results using the forward orthogonal deviation transformation are substantively unchanged (see Table A6 in the online appendix). We also got similar results using (1) dynamic panel models accounting for autocorrelated errors and (2) system GMM models. Sixth, using search scope as a measure of exploratory innovation (Katila and Ahuja 2002), results are retained (see Table A7 in the online appendix).

Seventh, because explore is a ratio, we repeated our analysis using the log-odds transformation, ln (explore/ 1 <sup>−</sup> explore) (Greene 2003, Phelps 2010).<sup>7</sup> This gave substantively unchanged results. Similar to prior research (Phelps 2010, Mithas et al. 2013), for ease of interpretation, we report results using the untransformed dependent variable. Eighth, we ran a principal component analysis, which verified that systems that comprise the IT-SIC load positively and significantly on their first principal component. We repeated our analyses using the first principal component to measure IT-SIC and found unchanged results. Ninth, using a two-year (instead of a one-year) lag between the independent and dependent variables gave similar results. Tenth, because variance inflation factors are below two (well below suggested limits), multicollinearity is not a problem (Greene 2003). Although common method bias is not a concern because all the variables are obtained from different sources, Harman one-factor tests suggest no bias. Eleventh, we find no significant joint influence of IT-SIC with global cultural diversity or global geographical dispersion on exploitative innovation. This resonates with our argument that global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion provide diverse and new knowledge which, when combined with IT-SIC, help the firm to go beyond current domains, thus spurring exploratory innovation, not exploitative innovation. Finally, following prior research (Atasoy et al. 2016), we exploit the time dimension of the data and run tests similar to the Granger causality test to address concerns of reverse causality. We regress IT-SIC on the lagged values of explore, global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion, controlling for other variables. The coefficients of explore, global cultural diversity, and global geographical dispersion are statistically nonsignificant, suggesting that past values of these variables do not predict current IT-SIC use. We also regressed global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion separately on lagged values of explore and IT-SIC, controlling for other variables. The coefficients of explore and IT-SIC are statistically nonsignificant, suggesting that past values of these variables do not predict current global cultural diversity or global geographical dispersion. These robustness tests further mitigate concerns of reverse causality.

However, at high IT-SIC global cultural diversity positively influences explore. At high IT-SIC, a firm with low global cultural diversity has an explore value of 0.38, and a firm with high globa cultural diversity has an explore value of 0.55. This dif ference of 0.17 in explore is substantive given that the mean value of explore in our sample is 0.42. At high IT-SIC, a firm with low global geographical dispersion has an explore value of 0.40, and a firm with high global geographical dispersion has an explore value of 0.54. The difference of 0.14 is substantive given that the mean value of explore is 0.42. Thus, the graphs suggest that the joint effects are significant in magnitude.

A firm that is less dispersed may be characterized by a similarity of ideas that is not conducive to leveraging IT-SIC for exploratory innovation. This finding resonates with prior studies that find IT may not impact firm outcomes by itself, but IT impacts firm outcomes jointly with contextual complements or conditions (e.g., Ray et al. 2005, Saldanha et al. 2013).

To deepen our understanding, we conducted informal discussions with senior managers of four global firms who were familiar with the IT-SIC systems in their firms.<sup>8</sup> Taken together, our findings and discussions with the senior managers suggest that IT-SIC enhances exploratory innovation in firms that are culturally diverse and geographically dispersed across countries.

## 5.2. Research Contributions and Implications

This study makes several research contributions. First, we contribute to IT innovation literature by showing how IT enhances firm exploratory innovation, extending prior work on IT-enabled exploratory innovation (e.g., Xue et al. 2012) by articulating the conditions under which specific IT capabilities influence exploratory innovation. This adds to prior findings that show that firms with higher IT intensity achieve greater innovation (e.g., Ravichandran et al. 2017) and responds to calls to study “how IT facilitates information and knowledge flows to improve real-time coordination and overcome geographical and economic barriers as firms globalize their operations” (Mithas et al. 2017, p. 440). Specifically, our study highlights the implications of IT-SIC for firm exploratory innovation in the context of global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion. Our findings suggest that dispersed knowledge sources of global firms demand mechanisms such as IT-SIC to create new knowledge.

Second, this work contributes to the knowledgebased view by providing novel insights on IT-SIC’s role in overcoming the challenges to knowledge creation, sharing, and recombination that stem from geographical distance and cultural differences. This contribution suggests that managers should consider the geographical and cultural distribution of their firm when selecting IT systems to exchange knowledge for exploratory innovation. Our study reveals the value of adopting a digital and knowledge-based perspective to understand the joint effects of IT and organizational contexts on exploratory innovation. Specifically, IT-SIC enables the discovery of novel knowledge elements, their recombination and synthesis to develop exploratory innovation via conversations and interactions, content visibility, transparency, and network ties across culturally diverse and geographically dispersed firms.

Finally, we extend the literature on the salience of cultural or geographical differences across countries in the context of global operations or international work relationships (Hong and Pavlou 2017, Mithas et al. 2017, Huang et al. 2018). For instance, Hong and Pavlou (2017) suggest that time zone and culture differences impede communication. We contribute by showing that IT-SIC increases exploratory innovation jointly with global cultural diversity and with geographical dispersion. Our findings suggest that, in culturally diverse firms, IT-SIC fosters social interactions and helps overcome collaboration barriers to leverage knowledge diversity, thereby driving exploratory innovation. IT-SIC helps firms and their employees to integrate perspectives, share diverse knowledge, and mitigate frictions resulting from global cultural diversity. IT-SIC combines with global geographical dispersion to enhance exploratory innovation by increasing access to weak ties and unstructured content, overcoming time differences, and building informal linkages across geographically dispersed firms. Our findings suggest that IT-SIC alone is not enough for exploratory innovation but that firms benefit when IT-SIC is leveraged in unison with global cultural diversity and with global geographical dispersion. In other words, although IT alone may not be enough to impact exploratory innovation, the combination of IT with organizational elements in configurations impact exploratory innovation.

## 5.3. Managerial and Policy Implications

Our study provides important implications for managing exploratory innovation in a global context. Our results suggest that challenges managers face from the global geographical spread of their firms can be addressed by leveraging IT-SIC to enable weak ties, overcome time differences barriers, and develop informal linkages. Our findings suggest that managers should view IT-SIC as a lever to overcome the challenges from global cultural diversity and geographical dispersion to achieve exploratory innovation. Instead of viewing global operations and IT in isolation, managers must consider configurations of IT resources and aspects of global operations that enhance exploratory innovation (Park and Mithas 2020). In other words, they must synchronize their globalization and IT strategies to become more innovative and successful (Srivastava et al. 2013).

This study has policy implications. One key issue is that governments and firms need to ensure privacy protection for employees while using IT-SIC systems. Some of the issues to be addressed include identity theft, data-handling transparency, and consent for data storage. Sharing knowledge, via IT-SIC, across national boundaries for innovation amplifies the need for governments and firms to pay heed to these issues because of the differences in intellectual property regimes across firms’ global operations. IT-SIC systems can motivate deeper discussions of labor policy because of a rising trend of cyberbullying and e-harassment in the workplace. Such negative experiences or apprehensions can harm the social interactions and open knowledge exchange that IT-SIC facilitates with potential negative implication for exploratory innovation. Hence, governments and firms need to institute policies to combat such risks in the context of IT-SIC.

## 5.4. Limitations, Future Research, and Concluding Remarks

We acknowledge the study’s limitations. First, our sample of firms listed in the United States limits generalizability. Future work can test our model in other countries. Second, although our patent-based measure of exploratory innovation follows past research and facilitates our longitudinal setting, future work can use other approaches, such as surveys. Third, although our measures of global cultural diversity and global geographical dispersion account for firm density in terms of the number of subsidiaries in each country, future research can build on our work and adapt or weight these measures to reflect the distribution of employees in each subsidiary. The lack of publicly available data on annual employee distribution at the subsidiary level precludes such a robustness check at this time.

Future research can study the implications of IT-SIC for the strategies regarding technological architectures to support innovation, for example, how firms can integrate IT-SIC with emerging technologies, such as blockchain, artificial intelligence, internet of things, and analytics to enhance innovation. It would also be useful to study how IT-SIC used in immersive environments (e.g., virtual reality) can overcome the challenges posed by global operations. Future studies can also examine how firms should address governance issues to achieve innovation using methods such as fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis, which model conjunctural, asymmetric, and equifinal causality in configurations (e.g., Leonhardt et al. 2018, Park and Mithas 2020).

To conclude, based on insights from two research streams (IT and innovation and IT and global operations), we proposed and tested a research model for IT-SIC’s role in exploratory innovation. This studyadvances the literature by taking into account the global context in which firms operate and how IT-SIC helps firms leverage this context to bolster exploratory innovation. More generally, our theorization and findings pave avenues for managers and scholars as they think about the innovation implications of IT in the face of continued global expansion of firms.
