## Abstract

We investigate information systems (IS) projects as a liminal space ‘betwixt and between’ the status quo and the new environment, using a case study of the implementation of an enterprise system (ES). This liminal space provides a stabilizing platform whereupon the project team can develop new and potentially transformative IS. However, after a project team has completed its initial IS design for roll-out, this liminal space must be bridged to incorporate process-generated learning and new systems back into the organizational working environment. We demonstrate how this bridging involves negotiations that attempt to reconcile divergent perspectives by adopting a conciliatory or peacemaking attitude. As such, our analysis focuses on the IS project as a multi-phased process that includes the creation of a liminal space for the project team during development and on the negotiations that ensure the ES becomes a working IS in the post-implementation environment.

## Introduction

n this paper, we consider organizational and technical challenges of large-scale information systems (IS) projects by focusing on the enterprise systems (ES) project team tasked with its implementation as a contemporary example of IS projects. The IS project team is typically composed of both IS personnel and representatives from the departments where the system will be employed. The IS project provides a learning experience for the IS team, which needs to creatively explore how the potentially powerful ES package can be exploited to transform organizational processes (Newell et al., 2004). Simultaneously, the IS project team must ensure that end-users also accept the organizational changes that the project team is endorsing and learn to use the IS (Ahn and Skudlark, 1997).

The need for a mutual connectivity between the learning within the IS project team and the learning across the organization can be challenging because, to varying degrees, IS projects operate independently from the rest of the organization. Indeed, it is this relative autonomy, and the degree of decision discretion this confers on IS project teams, that promotes more creative and innovative solution development since it allows teams to respond to external demands, unconstrained by the normal hierarchical and functional boundaries (Gann and Salter, 2000). At the same time, however, Scarbrough et al. (2004) demonstrated how this space between the IS project and the wider organization can have a detrimental impact on knowledge transfer from the project to end-users, describing this as a learning boundary.

We use the concept of the liminal process as a lens for examining how and why large, organization-wide IS projects (with ES projects being a contemporary example) often encounter difficulties in creating technical and organizational solutions that meet expectations when the change solution is finally rolled-out. Liminality describes the transition experiences involved in the process of separating from one (the old) state of being and incorporating into another (the new). This concept is particularly useful because it specifically deals with transformatory learning processes during transition periods and boundaries (i.e., learning boundaries). Thus, while these two issues – of using IS projects to stimulate transformations and of the problems associated with incorporating such transformations into ongoing organizations – have been discussed in the literature, there are few theoretical lenses that conceptually bind and explain both process elements together.

The liminal experience, occurring during the transition, when one is between the old and the new, provides opportunity for reflection and experimentation free from the constraints of the established or new norms but also creates a barrier that complicates incorporation. In this paper, we conceive the IS project as a liminal space between the old (pre-ES) and new (rolled-out ES) situation. The liminality lens, thus, reveals the space that provides freedom for a project team to be creative (Howard-Grenville et al., 2011) and how the same space results in a learning boundary (Scarbrough et al., 2004) that may negatively impact ES package adoption. Resistance is then mobilized by endusers to the extent they do not experience the liminal process, and they do not understand the rationale behind the promoted ‘solution’ – particularly when the ‘solution’ creates more rather than less work in the immediate term. This boundary, and resulting tension between the IS project team and users not involved in the team, highlights how knowledge generation and knowledge transfer are inexorably intertwined, with knowledge transfer not occurring independently of, nor in sequence to, knowledge generation especially in the context of disruptive kinds of change projects (Newell et al., 2004). Furthermore, we explore how the dialectical tension resulting from the clash between those who have and have not been ‘converted’ (i.e., between those involved in and not involved in the liminal project space) can be resolved in the post-implementation period. Our research questions are therefore:

RQ1: How does the liminal space created by setting up an independent project affect the ability of the project team to develop a transformatory IT-led change agenda?

RQ2: How does the liminal space, which creates a learning boundary between those within and outside the project team, create resistance at roll-out and how can this be resolved?

## Advancing liminality in ES

‘Liminal’ is derived from the Latin word ‘limen’, which means threshold. The term was used by van Gennup (1909) when he was writing about rites of passage. He identified three phases as a person moves from one social status to another – separation, transition and incorporation. It is during the transition phase that people experience the liminal condition; after they have been separated from their previous way of life but before they enter a new social category and new way of life.

The transition from childhood to adulthood is marked in Western society by a number of different rites of passage, for example being able to vote, being able to buy an alcoholic drink, moving away from home to go to college. Thus, the transition occurs gradually through a series of passage points so that the separation and incorporation does not translate to a single, clear-cut separation (from the old social status), through a transition phase and finally to an exit (into the new social status). However, the child-toadult transition is often more distinct. For example, the Maasai people in Kenya send adolescent boys away from the village to live together for 8–12 years and when the ‘boys’ return to the village, they return as the next generation of ‘adult’ male warriors.

The liminal condition varies in intensity in the examples offered. While in Western society there are a series of cyclical separations, transitions and incorporation phases so that individuals gradually shift to adulthood, for the Maasai, the transitory state is a significant period of time, during which there is a clear separation, and the return to the village is a single passage point back to incorporation. The latter, more isolating transitory state provides more opportunity for radical change – the boy that left the village is very different to the man who returns – but can also make reentry difficult, precisely because the degree of change experienced by the boy/man thus isolated while village life itself may have changed very little. In this paper, we explore this form of relatively extreme liminality because it roughly approximates the IS project team experience in the case study.

During the transitory or liminal phase, those involved are ‘betwixt and between the positions assigned and arrayed by law, custom, convention and ceremonial’ (Turner, 1977: 95), living ‘at the limits of existing structures’ (Tempest and Starkey, 2004: 507). The transition phase is thus liminal in the sense that it is a period of ambiguity, when the person is in a social and cultural limbo. This limbo state has both negative and positive connotations: people in a liminal phase are temporarily undefined, beyond the normative social structure. For example, in the United States during this liminal phase a ‘young adult’ is one who does not yet have the full rights of an adult and is also no longer a child with limited responsibilities. This weakens them, since they have no rights over others. But it also liberates them from structural obligations’ (Turner, 1982: 27). In this sense, liminality can be both painful and enjoyable at the same time (Rottenburg, 2000), freeing the person from institutional constraints but at the same time marginalizing them.

The term liminal has been used to describe the condition of temporary employees in flexible organizations (Garsten, 1999); to discuss the consulting experience (Czarniawska and Mazza, 2003); and to consider the impact on individual and organizational learning (Tempest and Starkey, 2004). In this paper, we use the term to describe and analyze the experience and impact on individuals assigned to work on an IS project since, as we will show, many of the conditions of liminality apply to such project teams. Moreover, we will show how some of the ambiguous and paradoxical aspects of liminality that have been identified in the literature help us examine and explain not only these project members’ experiences but also the dynamics and outcomes of the projects. This paper thus builds on existing work in the IS field that has considered the change process that constitutes IS projects, recognizing how this is best theorized as iterations of social and technical change and order over time (Cherns, 1976). What has not been considered previously, however, is the opportunity to conceptualize the project as a unique phase – not one to be transitioned out of as quickly as possible – that influences the appropriateness and inventiveness of newly created systems. Nor has project isolation been explored relative to impacts on the future – and necessary – acceptance of the IS. Because the liminal lens provides a clear line of sight into both of these issues, we believe it can enhance our understanding, particularly as it impacts successful implementation of the new IS. We next consider some of the characteristics of liminal space and liminal personae that we can apply to IS project work.

One characteristic of a liminal phase is that it is temporary, hence Garsten’s (1999) use of the concept to explore the experience of temporary workers. Organizations use temporary employees precisely because this allows them to be more flexible – adding and reducing headcount as and when needed – and in doing this creating ‘liminal subjects in flexible organizations’ (Garsten, 1999). Project teams are similar in that they also give organizations additional flexibility – to develop some innovative process while businessas-usual continues in the rest of the organization.

A second characteristic is that it is ambiguous, since people in liminal space ‘slip through the network of classifications that normally locate states and positions in cultural space’ (Garsten, 1999: 606), offering both risks and opportunities simultaneously and for both the individuals and the organizations involved (Tempest and Starkey, 2004). For Turner (1982), this ambiguity provides those in a liminal phase with the opportunity to upset normative orders and transcend institutional boundaries, a seedbed of cultural and social creativity. The ambiguity exists because activities are performed ‘backstage’ – separated from mainstream organizational activities. This enables project team members to think, act, and envision arrangements unconstrained by existing work practices.

This is related to the third characteristic – freedom. Those in the liminal space are freed from structural and institutional constraints and obligations – ‘whereas structure entails systems of classifications, models of thinking about culture and nature and ordering life, institutionalization and norm governing, the liminal phase involves a challenge of structure and its attributes’ (Garsten, 1999: 605). This interstructural aspect of liminality provides two types of freedom: freedom from institutional obligations (technical and bureaucratic) prescribed by the organization and freedom to enter new symbolic worlds and to transcend social structural limitations and to play (Turner, 1982). Likewise, Hagen et al. (2003) discuss how the liminal space provided by education can provide the opportunity to critically examine dominant orthodoxies and be creative.

A final characteristic of a liminal phase is that it engenders communitas – a strong sense of community with those sharing the experience, even while it creates a divide between those ‘on the outside’ (Czarniawska and Mazza, 2003). Communitas is important because, as Wagner (1998) argues, knowledge generation rests on sense-making, and sense-making occurs within the context of communities of practice. The paradox is that while there may be a strong community among those sharing the liminal space, the corresponding disconnection from those within the existing ongoing organizational structure threatens the organization’s social capital (Tempest and Starkey, 2004). More importantly, the knowledge generation and sense-making that has occurred among those in the IS project team is not extended to the rest of the organization who will therefore remain rooted in the old practices. This sets the scene for active resistance to new IS-supported arrangements (Pickering, 1995) at roll-out.

Yet, in the context of an integrated IS solution like an ES, its functionality can only be exploited if, upon deployment, all end-users actually use the ES, inputting data to match how it has been configured. If data are not input correctly (e.g., using fields in ways not intended) or if individuals continue to use non-ES solutions (e.g., using a self-created MS Excel spreadsheet) then the ES will not provide the overview of operations across departments nor will people in one department be able to seamlessly use the ES to support their own processes. For these reasons, incorporation can be an especially crucial process in the context of integrated IS projects where other organizational change projects (such as a culture change project) may be less catastrophically implicated by only partial incorporation in different departments.

This discussion of liminality, as it may apply to an IS project team, thus recognizes its paradoxical nature, the positive connotations – play and transcendence of existing institutional structures that provides the potential opportunity for those involved to think creatively and come up with transformational organizational arrangements – but also the negative connotations of marginality and exclusion among those not involved. Moreover, the move from the transition phase to incorporation is crucial. It is likely that where the transitory liminal phase is extreme (i.e., there is a prolonged period of separation), there is more opportunity for a radical or transformational solution to be generated by the IS project team because their thinking can be less constrained by current practices. This may set the stage for a more difficult incorporation because it creates the potential for a dialectical tension at roll-out. How this tension is handled will then be a crucial issue for the survival of the project team’s promoted solution, at least in organizational contexts where those resisting have power to make themselves heard.

## Methodology

To advance the conceptualization of liminality, we draw on data from an in-depth qualitative case study of a ‘big-bang’ ES implementation at a prestigious university we call ‘Ivy’. The first author was employed as a staff accountant and was member of the ES project’s General Accounting team with the case organization from 1992 to 1996. Upon termination from her position she received permission to research the project. The field study was conducted between June 1999 and August 2000, with follow-up interviews in 2002 and 2005. In total, the first author conducted 129 narrative interviews with 56 unique actors over several different phases of the project (see Table 1). While the majority of data are a decade old, the first author’s knowledge of both the data and the case organization prompted her to revisit the data to advance the conceptual frame of liminality, which appears to have explanatory relevance for understanding both how the project team was able to develop a transformational ES and why they experienced difficulties during roll-out. Her intimate familiarity with the data and longstanding connection to Ivy provides the ratioanale for re-analyzing the data to investigate the utility and strength of the liminal lens for understanding the ES project.

This study is informed by an interpretive epistemology (Walsham, 1993; Klein and Myers, 1999); that is, we sought out multiple interpretations of events from those experiencing the project (actors). As such, our case is designed to seek ‘validityynot [from] the representativeness of the case in a statistical sense or a single narrative, but on the plausibility and cogency of the logical reasoning used in describing the results and in drawing conclusions from them’ (Walsham, 1993: 15). Key participants were interviewed about their project experiences, observations were conducted, and a systematic review of official project documentation was undertaken in each case. The emergent narratives, based on the 129 narrative interview transcripts related to the ES project and subsequent installation and use, characterize the actors’ experiences and provide a map of relationships between actors and issues.

The interviews adhered to the narrative interviewing convention, which provides a time frame to help structure the interview (e.g., ‘Tell me what has happened since we last met.’) and then encourages uninterrupted storytelling related to issues of central importance for the interviewee at the time (Bauer, 1996). All interviews were tape-recorded and transcribed. Our interpretive approach seeks to present a narrative that illustrates not only significant events but also how events inform the iterations of IS-led change over time. When a reference was made to a group, or an agency was attributed to nonhumans such as the ES, the field researcher arranged to interview a delegate and review technological documentation when it was available.

Indeed, during change processes, individuals and groups are forming interpretive procedures that help them evaluate and judge the increasingly uncertain and complex organization. While we have come to expect stories of conflict and dissent during IS-enabled change, our data show that reparation of discord is equally present at later stages of an IS project.

When analyzing the data, we found that the content of the narrative interviews tended to cluster around important issues of negotiation at the time of the interview. These individual narratives have woven within them the connections and politics that highlight the basis for coordination and controversy (Bruner, 1990). We analyzed the connections between narratives in order to highlight the multiple perspectives that existed within the University at a given moment in time and how those perspectives influenced the trajectory of the project. These narratives were organized into visual networks in order to illuminate the interconnectivity involved in negotiating through IS-enabled change. This subsequently led us to emphasize both the importance of liminal space – the transition phase of the liminal process and the subsequent incorporation – experienced by the IS project team and their efforts to respectfully address dissent in the post-implementation environment.

## Case description and analysis

The case description and analysis is divided into two sections. In the first, we consider the liminal experience that was created during the transitory phase when the IS project was created and undertook the ES development work. In the second, we explore what happened when the IS project team presented their developed solution to the rest of the organization during the incorporation phase.

Creating a transformational ES during the transitional phase ‘Ivy’ University is one of the eight Ivy League institutions located in the Northeast region of the United States grouped together for their academic excellence and selective private education. The governance structure of Ivy is relatively decentralized with administration taking place both in the disparate academic and support departments and then consolidated and reconciled centrally in order to report financial status and meet regulatory concerns. In the late 1990s, Ivy experienced an increasingly complicated regulatory environment as an institution with a multi-billion dollar endowment and the recipient of sponsored research funds and governmental grants making the fiduciary responsibilities significant. In this climate, Ivy embarked on an IS project in order to replace all administrative systems and create an integrated operating platform for the 21st century. The enterprise-computing model was expected to increase fiduciary control and decrease audit risk while decentralizing more administrative responsibilities to academic and support departments. Ivy contracted with a software vendor (‘Vision’) to develop two new modules and then implement an enterprise solution for its higher education context. In the summer of 2000, after a year-long delay, the entire ES suite was implemented across Ivy and, at that time, the academic departments refused to work with the financial management module and the IS project team had to develop customized software to accommodate their demands.

Given the congruity between the liminal process – the process of change that includes separation, transition and incorporation – and the formation of an IS project team to implement a new ES, it will be useful to consider the characteristics of liminality (Table 3). These characteristics act as mechanisms for creating an in-between space within which project work exists and assist the movement of the Ivy project team into the transitional stage where the liminal condition is experienced. In the discussion section we then consider the final phase of incorporation to understand how attention to the concept of liminality can lead to positive and negative implications for individuals, groups and organizations.

Ivy sought to modernize its administrative infrastructure for the 21st century and decided to do this by separating the initiative from the daily operations by creating a project with two Vision developers and a full-time core of Ivy employees. While the Vision developers were allocated to multiple clients and rarely on-site the first year, the core Ivy team formed what they called the ‘nucleus’, an indispensable small group whose departmental jobs were back-filled for the initiative’s duration. Supporting the project with resources enabled the work to get done without taxing University daily operations. The people selected for the project initiative were, thus, completely separated from their previous job responsibilities and moved to a separated project space. The following quote from a project manager demonstrates:

One of the things that we have that is very goodyis a very good project infrastructure. So it probably cost [Ivy] a whole lot, a bunch more than they would have liked to have it cost. Although everybody is packed in like sardinesyin terms of having a workspace, having the kind of equipment you need to do the work – printers, copiers, faxes, conference rooms, wipe boards – all those kinds of things that you need, the project was really very well-organized. – It was never impossible to get what you needed. It helps us make really good progress managing this new [ES] creature that we’re implementing. (Summer 1999)

Similar to the Maasai tribal custom, Ivy employees were sent away from the main University campus to work together with other select individuals until the project was successfully completed. At Ivy, the extended albeit temporary project period clearly separated the team from the rest of the University; in this in-between space they experience the liminal condition.

The movement into phase two – transition – where one experiences the liminal condition was dependent upon the divestiture of old ways and the entrance into a more ambiguous space. The ambiguity of the project space is exemplified through the naming of the project initiative. Project X was initially used simply because no name was immediately forthcoming. The change manager of the project describes this:

The project at this point still didn’t have a nameyWe were running naming contests, trying to get a nameythere were some wild names that people voted on at a meeting to see if there was a preferred name but we couldn’t reach consensusySo the developer of the web site didn’t even have a name to give the project, and so she picked project ‘x’ as the front-page logo – ‘x’ was the placeholder until we decided on a name. Then eventually people just threw up their hands about six months later and said that’s the name! Well the name ‘Project X’ is odd enough that it got across several different feelings. One [feeling] is that this [project] is new, that it is difficult to understand, and this is something that is not meant to be institutional. It’s meant to be here and then go away. It’s a temporary thing! So it said – this is a temporary project – essentially. Not that – you know – the initiatives and the underpinnings of the project are temporary, but that the project itself is meant to not become institutional in nature. (Summer 1999)

The name stuck and became associated with the X-Files TV drama (popular at the time of the data collection) as it denoted mystery and something slightly unreal and imaginary/super-natural.

The ambiguity experienced during this transitional phase exists because of the lack of social and cultural norms. This gave team members the freedom to experiment and explore options, unfettered by the ongoing daily operations. At the same time, however, the secretive nature of the name suggested that highly confidential work was going on behind closed doors, the details of which were not discussed and this proved alienating to those external to the project team once the ES was rolled-out:

We’re not aware of any other major research university monitoring sponsored agreements and grants with a timed-phased budgeting approach, or of any institutional advantage that might be gained by making such a profound change. We agree that grants are a huge source of operating revenue that needs to be managed well. And monitoring and managing any huge revenue stream in an integrated system is a good thing. We agreed here, this was our common ground – but then [the project team] went and developed a system that isn’t fully incorporated, supported, and built with the end users in mind, and so it falls short of this ‘integrated’ goal. (End-user/manager, follow-up email 2002)

The naming and sequestering of the project not only influenced the ambiguity experienced, but also affected the extent to which the project team identified itself as a separated community as articulated by a project manager:

The people who are involved in [the project] implementation are a very cohesive group. The other thing that I really like to think about is group kinship because I think when you look at groups like that, it is really is like an initiation into a family, you’ve seen all our project paraphernalia [hats, shirts, baseball team, web site, etc] and we talk in a new way that most of [Ivy] doesn’t understand yet [laughs]. Our family has all the different characters types there are the people who are always reluctant to take a risk, and then you’ve got the risk takers and I think they balance each other out. And you’ve got the peace makers and I think that whole, there’s a whole structure behind the project that’s partly group kinship. (Summer 1999)

Thus, at Ivy, the project team members were creating their own environment – one that existed outside the cultural norms of the University – where they were given additional freedoms and the expectations of job skills and responsibilities was more in line with the corporate project management world than the 37.5-h Ivy administrative work week. The team took advantage of this to ‘think outside the box’ and create a new administrative environment for Ivy. Freedom from structural and institutional constraints was demonstrated through the creation of a dedicated, wellresourced project building that was used for the duration of the project. However, this isolated the team members from their organizational colleagues who were not involved. This freedom from the physical constraints of ‘the office’ in Ivy appeared to give team members a sense that they did not need to be tied to existing practices but rather had the freedom to experiment with new ways of working. Ivy data indicate that ‘kinship’ was an important part of the freedom of liminal space.

Those involved in Project X did not just separate themselves from the wider University, they created a subculture – an identity with a particular language – one that ‘outsider(s) wouldn’t understand because they hadn’t been part of it’. This sub-culture actually is juxtaposed against the broader Ivy culture, which historically has trouble making decisions because of its consensus orientation. As noted by one functional leader, in spite of a large number of people ‘the project was able to get in a room and make important decisions efficiently’. This in-group sub-culture was further enhanced by the way that project members were selected. That is, in Ivy, members were selected explicitly because they were ‘the brightest and the best’. This gave the team a feeling that they were all regarded as equally, highly talented. This caused some resentment from those not chosen, who were left to pick-up and continue ‘business as usual’. Furthermore, those alienated in this way felt that their work practices were being ordained by individuals who may never have done the job for which they were mandating practices. Thus, others in the organization saw the Ivy team as being part of the ‘out-group’.

As the scope of Project X broadened and the team expanded, inter-group dynamics surfaced. As the senior Vision consultant pointed out, in the last year of the project, the teamwork was different from what he was used to when implementing ES, and his employees who had been working on-site at Ivy for the previous year had adopted the work approaches of the Ivy team. This speaks to the strength of the liminal space to create an ethos and indoctrinate new entrants:

Ya know, we did have user participation in the project – we held meetings and asked for input but it was hard to get users to focus because they just weren’t deep into the project like we were. I like to tell the story, I lived out in Charlottesville, Virginia for a while. I lived out in the country right on the edge of the Blue Ridge Mountains. So there is a lot of wildlife out there. One night I was driving home and I came over the crest of the hill and in the road in front of me was a mama skunk and her five little babies. These are back roads, there’s not much traffic there, so I just put my car in park and I just watched. Soythe mama skunk used her nose to kind of get them all in a line and then she went to the head of the line and took about three steps forward across the road. And the baby skunks would follow a couple of steps and then they’d get interested in something else and all discombobulated and they’d be all milling around, and the mama skunk would look back over her shoulder and she would see what had happened, and she would go back and get them all lined up in a row. And she’d go back to the head of the line and she’d take about four more steps. And the skunks would take a few steps and then they’d get all discombobulated again. So it took about 20 minutes to get across the road. But I often thought over the course of project, that that was the kind of work the University entrusted us [the project team] with, what we do is, we get all the ducks in a row and then we head off marching and then we’d have to notice that they were getting kind of confused back there. Then we’d have to go back to get them all in a row, and start over again. So I think that’s really the story of this projectyis it really was pushed forward by a small group of people and while we listened to the [Ivy] community we couldn’t listen too much. (A project manager, winter 1999)

Through this analysis, we identified mechanisms that support a liminal space where particular characteristics (transitory, ambiguous, freedom and community) were used that facilitated the project work to be undertaken in isolation from the rest of the organization. In this section, we consider how this impacted the incorporation of the IS project team members and its technological solution as they moved back into the main organization. In looking at Table 4, it should be noted that in reality there is overlap between the characteristics of liminal space and the mechanisms employed. For example, naming the project not only influenced the ambiguity experienced, but also affected the extent to which the project team identified itself as a separated community. Nevertheless, we have matched the mechanisms with that aspect of liminality, which appeared from our analysis to be strongest, given our case data.

Our analysis suggests that the liminal space supported behaviors that led to ‘thinking outside the box’ and thus creating a transformational solution around the ES. However, the extreme liminal experience created in Ivy during the transition phase made it difficult during the subsequent incorporation, when users were unwilling to accept the solutions presented. Thus, although the team members had sought to engage end-users at the outset of the project, they became insular in the way they worked and prided themselves on the ‘family’ and ‘kinship’ that had been created among the team, enabling them to be more creative about the transformational potential of the ES. This divestiture was created through the way in which the liminal mechanisms were used in the Ivy case, for example in the naming of the project, the allocation of significant resources, the selection of the ‘best and the brightest’, the back-filling of jobs, and the isolation of the project team. In this way, Ivy maximized liminality in their project but at the expense of isolating it from the broader user community. In the next section, we analyze the subsequent reconciliation efforts that the team engaged in to overcome the resistance that they encountered as a result of this extreme liminality during the transition phase.

## Repairing the knowledge boundary during incorporation

The incorporation process was much more complicated than expected because IS project leaders underestimated faculty determination to maintain its preferred style of financial management. The IS project team entered into an unexpected back-and-forth process of system modification with faculty. More specifically, faculty rejected the grants accounting module that had been developed and demanded new functionality to support traditional practices used to manage grants. These traditional practices, described as ‘unprofessional’ by the Finance Director, had been explicitly designed out of the ES solution during the transitory project phase.

Given the rejection by end-users and the importance of meeting their needs, significant time and effort was spent in post-implementation negotiations, leading to cost and time overruns. Nevertheless, a new way of working did ultimately become stabilized through the creation of a working ES, this included making customizations to the ES to enable faculty to continue with its traditional work practices. At Ivy, the incorporation phase was extended beyond the initial installation of the ES. In fact, it was only after an extended period of use that the rite of passage was finalized through acceptance of the ES, thus confirming the importance of adopting a longitudinal perspective on ES success (Markus et al., 2000).

Our focus in this section is to explore how the IS project team created a solution that would satisfy both academic and administrative university missions, given its initial rejection, during the incorporation phase. Analyzing the interconnectivity of narrative accounts over time highlights not only the problematic events that constitute an IS project but also provides insight into repair work that helps restore order to the organized chaos of project work. We provide examples below of how this was achieved.

The FM sought to change administrative practice through a narrative around ‘professionalizing practice’. He was able to enroll the project team around this narrative. However, this meant that they designed grant accounting functionality based on corporate budgeting techniques that were directly at odds with faculty accounting practices. It was only during the ‘incorporation’ phase of the project that faculty interests surfaced through stories of feeling betrayed and demands for design modifications. Although initially these confrontational narratives were dismissed by the IS project team as ‘amateur complaints’, eventually it was recognized that ignoring the demands embedded in these narratives would not help in creating the working ES that all were seeking.

We became appeasement oriented and so when people began hurting, we said ‘yes’. And so we started building these things that could [mimic legacy practices]yeven though it was against our original ideas about what was the way forward for [Ivy]. (A project team manager, summer 2000)

The result was that the team worked with faculty to develop a hybrid ES involving post-installation customization. This hybrid was negotiated once the decision-makers began recognizing that using power to impose a single vision (i.e., the Financial Management (FM’s) vision) of the ES had failed and that repair was necessary. Rich examples of the shift to repair work are given below by following the series of quotes showing the progression over time of narratives from sense-making, through persuasion, to attempts at being conciliatory through the exposition of multiple perspectives – laying out different viewpoints:

[Ivy] has always been known for its excellence in research and teaching and this partnershipygives us the opportunity to update our infrastructure and create an admin istrative center of excellence that others can emulate. (Central administrator, winter 1999)

This statement is designed to tell the field researcher why the project was begun in order to help her develop understanding about the project’s inception.

This final quote is a narrative from a senior accountant who was part of the project team. Her story explains attempts to neutralize faculty recalcitrance before they reject the ES she helped develop:

We’re trying to make things as simple and straightforward as possible and we’ve failed miserably so far to be honest with you. The [faculty] grant reports – are the best example of the difficulties we haveywe are redesigning the reports and asking faculty what they want instead of assuming we know it all, and I hope it works.

In addition, reflective narratives emerged as IS project team members began reintegrating into Ivy’s daily operations. These narratives indicated there was palpable tension within the broad Ivy community toward IS project team members who were returning to the pre-project jobs but rather than letting the project separate them, we saw evidence of how the ‘fractious phenomena rather than continue to separate, was neutralized, forgiven, excused (Bruner, 1990):

I think the hardest part is that we [the project team] are not located in one place anymore and it can be lonely to be around people who don’t get your life for the past 3 years. I think that makes it much more difficult for us because the rest of the community doesn’t understand what we have been through – like coming back from a war or something – so that there’s a tendency to kind of fall back to the old way of thinking where we knew everything that [Ivy] needed, the us and them, and so there’s, there’s a real need for good communication between the beneficiaries of the system and the people who spent years building it. A big part of it for me has been accepting that we made mistakes, but we did our best and I know I am still trying to learn from the user community what they thought about the project. (A project manager, follow-up email 2002)

Our analysis suggests that narratives of repair that focus on reconciliation in the face of resistance are of fundamental importance in this process of conflict resolution, where alternative perspectives are exposed within a single story. The main goal of such narratives is explication rather than sense-making or persuasion. This is an effective way for researchers to gain access to the repair work involved in creating a working information system – one that is accepted and used throughout the organization as a goodenough solution (Wagner and Newell, 2006). The examples of narratives of repair emphasize the retelling of different perspectives in order to try and build operational integration and community coherence during times of conflict. The accountant’s story is an attempt to bring university members closer to the forgiveness and compromise of which Bruner (1990) speaks. Her motivation is to manage, despite conflict, to achieve a working solution. She communicates this goal to the researcher and in the process illuminates the intertwining of disparate perspectives in pursuit of this goal. Foregrounding the interconnectivity of narratives highlights the multiple histories involved in crafting organizational reality and provides an opportunity for us to understand the complexity of computer-mediated change efforts before this multiplicity becomes silenced beneath the one, official record of the initiative. By doing so, we gain insight into how actors traverse uncertain project landscapes, and the way in which choices made during one moment in time impact later opportunities. Focusing on narratives of repair, or what might be described as peacemaking narratives, presents IS researchers with an opportunity to analyze the flow of events differently and explore the characteristics of project work through the lens of liminality.

Our lens of liminality suggests that narratives of repair, which attempt to make peace between the IS project solution and user preferences may be less important where the project team has not isolated themselves so dramatically as in Ivy. As our original review of liminality suggested, there are different ways to create a liminal space – either as a series of small spaces that can gradually build up the change that is envisioned as with the adolescent process in the US or as a single big space where the change happens in one go as with the Massai adolescents. Indeed, Howard-Grenville et al. (2011) consider how the creation of multiple small liminal spaces can be effective in stimulating cultural change across organizations. We are not arguing that the approach adopted at Ivy is an example of some kind of best practice and involving users more throughout the process is certainly an option that is often advocated in the IS literature (Livari and Livari, 2011). Nevertheless, the extreme liminal approach adopted was ultimately effective in creating a working IS that continues to be used today. The point is that where a project is organized in a similar way to Ivy (as seems to be common, Engwall, 2003), the learning boundary that Scarbrough et al. (2004) describe can be overcome if those involved adopt a peacemaking orientation and become conciliatory toward those resisting in the post-roll-out environment.

Moreover, where an IS solution is more consistent with existing organizational practices so that the envisioned change is less potentially transformative it may also be the case that even with an isolationist approach to the project less reconciliation is needed during incorporation. In this case, the ES (and in turn Vision’s) way of managing financial budgets was very different to those existing at Ivy. Nevertheless, it was exactly this different (and in the FM’s view more professional) approach to financial management that was being sought by the senior decision-makers at Ivy. Given that organizational transformation is often an expected outcome from IS-enabled change, our analysis suggests that the need for reconciliation during incorporation may not be unique. Future research can usefully focus on when and how peacemaking can be successfully used during IS projects.

## Conclusions

The liminality lens has helped us examine and explain both IS project members’ experiences and project dynamics and outcomes. Project team members, especially employees, may not be barred from information channels and locales, but as indicated in the introduction projects can be a ‘lonely phenomenon’ (Engwall, 2003) since the project organization encourages them to separate and eventually lose touch, especially if they are assigned to the project full-time and if the project is of long duration. This fragile connection to others in the organization enhances a sense of community with those in the same liminal space (Turner, 1969) that can help to foster a strong collective and egalitarian identity, stripping away differences in rank and status. This can be both positive and negative.

During the IS project, liminality appeared to facilitate ‘thinking outside the box’ and thus creating a transformational solution around the IS. Liminality also, more broadly, changed the individuals who had experienced this transitory state. At the same time, the liminal, transitory phase influenced interactions between the IS project team and the rest of the organization and, consequently, the acceptance of the solution presented at the time of roll-out. For project team members, the IS project enabled them to gain new skills that improved several individuals’ personal career trajectory, even though prior to this they had not intended to change. For some, these career changes were within Ivy, but there were others who moved on so that the organization itself lost valuable human resources and knowledge related to ES-enabled operating practices. Career development was thus wholly beneficial from the point of view of the individuals involved, even though this was not the case from the organizational perspective. Such impressive career development may not always be the outcome of such a liminal experience, however. For example, liminality for the temporary workers discussed by Garsten (1999) was not necessarily related to significant career advancement.

From the perspective of the IS project team as a distinct organizational entity, liminality offered a space where members were freed from existing institutional constraints and this helped them to produce creative solutions. As we saw, IS project team members developed a strong sense of kinship with each other and embraced the project as an opportunity to redirect the organization’s future. All Ivy members were initially completely unfamiliar with ES software but they developed solutions that were very different to the existing practices. Although team members initially felt overwhelmed and lost, they eventually developed a conceptual schema for what they considered would be a ‘more professional’ administration system for Ivy. Therefore, the IS project team developed a strong sense of community and through this ‘collective mind’ they were able to envision how the ES could transform work practices. Again, this strong sense of community may not always accompany a liminal experience. For example, the high value placed on adaptability of some temporary workers – displaying the right attitude and competencies – can contribute to an individualized experience that is more strongly felt than the notion of sharing and belonging (Garsten, 1999: 615). However, in Ivy it was community rather than individualism that was more strongly pronounced.

During the incorporation phase, however, users evaluate the extent to which their local work practices are of greater value than those mandated by the IS. In the Ivy case, this evaluation was initially negative and resulted in an extended period of negotiation that ultimately led to its acceptance into the local community. We observed how narratives of repair were fundamentally important to this successful negotiation, confirming how language influences organizational outcomes (Shepherd et al., 2009). Using language as a tool for peacemaking and reconciliation has been investigated in multiple areas, including diplomacy/peace studies and marriage and family therapy. Future research might investigate how particular types of narratives are used to resolve conflict within IS projects. This study points to the link between peacemaking language and liminality, where in the liminal space itself, conflicts are avoided to some extent because the project cuts itself off from the rest of the organization. It seems to be in the final phase, at the boundary between the liminal space and new state of being, that peacemaking language comes to the fore. The incorporation phase involves the use of language that exposes multiple perspectives in an effort to provide insight rather than valorize any one particular perspective over another.

For this reason, an analytical understanding of negotiating through a uniquely liminal phase to a reordering of work life should be a part of any IS project management strategy. As Hammer said at the 2002 Strategy World Conference (Said Business School, 2002) in contemporary society, ‘execution is strategy’. It is not merely the strategic plan – the grand vision – that creates organizational success but rather the ability to negotiate end-to-end operational processes that is key for maximizing potential. Liminal spaces can be reflexive spaces for future strategy development at corporate, business or functional level. The findings also illustrate that fostering a strategic vision requires translating the vision into purposeful action and that this may be achieved both during the transition and incorporation phases.

For practicing professionals, this suggests that while control of IS is illusory during project management, the cultivation of relationships between a grand strategic vision and small daily stories can foster empathy during times of uncertainty and change. Similarly, this suggests for IS research that IS project work within organizations is as much about embracing the in-between time as unique and important as it is about maintaining a hold on the daily operations of the business. We encourage fully embracing the rite of passage as a precursor to creating an integrated enterprise-wide infrastructure.
