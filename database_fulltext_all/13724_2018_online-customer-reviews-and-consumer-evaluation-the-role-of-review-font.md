# Online customer reviews and consumer evaluation: The role of review font<sup>☆</sup>

Yunhui Huang, Changxin Li, Jiang Wu, Zhijie Lin

School of Business, Nanjing University, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Processing ease Review valence Online customer reviews Credibility Need for cognition Accountability

## A B S T R A C T

Inspired by the literature on processing <sup>fl</sup>uency and the practical question of how to choose fonts for website design, we investigated the impact of customer reviews on consumer evaluation under an easy-to-read (vs. di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read) review font. We found that the feeling of ease in reading led consumers to judge the reviewers as more credible, thus increasing the impact of the reviews. Moreover, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of font diminished when consumers elaborated information more thoroughly (i.e., when high in need for cognition, or in an accountable situation) and thus did not use simple heuristics (reading ease) to make judgment.

## 1. Introduction

In electronic commerce (e-commerce), after a purchase has been completed, e-commerce platforms usually allow consumers to provide numerical ratings of the product or service and to write textual descriptions that serve as a justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for the ratings [1–3]. These online reviews function as a free “sales assistant” [4] which in<sup>fl</sup>uences sub sequent consumer purchase decisions and product sales [5,6].

As online reviews are becoming increasingly popular, researchers have focused extensively on analyzing their impacts and the determinants of these impacts (e.g., [7–12,6]. The number of customer reviews [13 16] and the average and variance of the numerical ratings [17,7,18] appear to in<sup>fl</sup>uence online consumer behaviors. A more comprehensive understanding of the impact of online reviews can be found from text mining, sentiment analysis, and experimental examination of review texts [5,19–21]. Previous research has documented well that multiple textual features, including valence [22–24], twosidedness (highlighting positive and negative aspects of a product) [25], text length [26,20], semantic characteristics [27], and the inclusion of elements of a good story [28], all alter the impact of online reviews. More recently, greater e<sup>f</sup>ort has been made to investigate how the e<sup>f</sup>ect of such review features can be moderated by contingent factors such as brand equity [24], consumption goals (prevention vs. promotion) [29], and product category (experience vs. search products) [30].

However, most prior research has focused on contents generated by users and thus has ignored contextual factors that do not change the review contents but nevertheless may alter the impact of customer reviews. One exception is a study by Huang et al. [31] which found that ordering reviews into di<sup>f</sup>erent types may a<sup>f</sup>ect their perceived diag nosticity. The present research focuses on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of another indispensable feature of customer reviews, namely font.

One issue that online retailers usually face when designing their websites is the choice of font. In practice, online retailers choose par ticular fonts to present their website information, including customer reviews. For example, Amazon uses Amazon Ember (13px, black), PetSmart uses Proxima Nova A Regular (11px, dark blue), and GameStop uses Solido Book (16px, black) to present customer reviews; Toys “R” Us uses Avenir Roman (16px, dark grey) for the most positive reviews and most helpful reviews. In addition, top online store designers, such as BigCommerce, WIX, and Woocommerce, provide support for online retailers to customize the appearance of texts when designing online stores. Unfamiliar typefaces, small fonts, and low-contrast colors are apparently more di<sup>fi</sup>cult to read. Yet despite extensive discussions of this issue, there has been limited empirical examination of the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of font, and it remains unknown whether or not font chosen by online retailers alters the impact of customer reviews—and if so, how.

Inspired by the <sup>fl</sup>uency literature in social cognition [32,33], the present research suggests that reviews with a font di<sup>fi</sup>cult to read may have only a small impact on customers, less than that of reviews with an easy-to-read font. This decrease in impact may occur because processing ease serves as a heuristic cue for consumers to assess credibility [34,35]. Increased processing di<sup>fi</sup>culty lowers perceived reviewer credibility, decreasing the impact of customer reviews.

Persuasive positive (or negative) reviews should lead other potential consumers to evaluate a product or service as more favorable (or unfavorable). Thus, in the present research, we measured consumers product evaluations after exposure to positive and negative reviews. We inferred a greater impact of customer reviews from more positive consumer evaluation following positive reviews and more negative consumer evaluation following negative reviews. We found that reviews are likely to be less impactful on consumer evaluation when online retailers choose a di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read (vs. easy-to-read) font for customer reviews.

This research makes several important contributions to the literature. First, we provide empirical tests of whether and how font has an e<sup>f</sup>ect on perceived credibility and thus on the impact of customer reviews, deepening the understanding of the contextual factors altering the impact of customer reviews [31]. Second, we extend processing <sup>fl</sup>uency to the context of online reviews, providing empirical support for the dual processing model of credibility assessment [35]. Third, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings help online retailers to understand the possible e<sup>f</sup>ects of their choice of fonts for their websites.

This paper is organized as follows. First, we review the literatures on the visual elements of website design and on processing <sup>fl</sup>uency. Hypothesis 1 is based on these theories. Then, from the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) [36,37] and the dual processing model of credibility assessment [35], we propose the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of elaboration motivation, which we operationalize through measuring people’s need for cognition (Hypothesis 2a) or manipulating accountability for the judgment (Hypothesis 2b). After that, we report four experiments that compare consumers’ product evaluations after reading either positive or negative reviews written in either easy-to-read or di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read fonts. In Study 4, the underlying mechanism of perceived reviewer credibility is tested, while alternative mediators of review diagnosticity, self-reviewer similarity, and ease of comprehending the reviews are ruled out. Finally, we discuss the contributions of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 2.2. The efect of processing fluency

The impact of information depends not only on its content but also on the metacognitive experience of processing [32]. Processing <sup>fl</sup>uency, or the subjective experience of ease, is one such metacognitive experience. It may derive from incidental sources, such as perceived ease or di<sup>fi</sup>culty of reading information presented in certain fonts, and then be misattributed to aspects of a product or a brand, a<sup>f</sup>ecting perception and judgment [45,46]. For example, performing a novel behavior seems more di<sup>fi</sup>cult if the instructions are written in a di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read (vs. easy-to-read) font [47], and perceived choice di<sup>fi</sup>culty increases when options are presented in a di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read (vs. easy-to-read) font [46,48].

Interpretation of processing <sup>fl</sup>uency depends strongly on context; to inform their judgment, people utilize their naïve theories about how their <sup>fl</sup>uent processing experience relates to aspects of stimuli or to properties of their own knowledge [49]. Because it should be easy to make a categorization when an exemplar represents a category well, people tend to judge an exemplar to be a worse category member if it is presented in a smaller typeface, and thus, the experience of processing is less <sup>fl</sup>uent [50]. Because textbooks contain profound concepts and knowledge and are di<sup>fi</sup>cult to read, dis<sup>fl</sup>uent content is judged to be of higher quality when it is thought to come from a source that focuses on conveying information [51]. Similarly, experienced <sup>fl</sup>uency increases people’s perception of liking [52], intelligence of the text writer [53], con<sup>fi</sup>dence [54], and moral correctness of moral violations [55] but decreases the perceived importance of decisions [56] and the specialty of a product [57].

The present research proposes that processing <sup>fl</sup>uency (ease of processing) strengthens the impact of customer reviews because it increases reviewer credibility. Information credibility refers to the believability of information and/or its source [58]. While these two aspects intertwine and function concurrently, the believability is heavily in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by its source credibility [59]. As customer reviews describe subjective experiences rather than knowledge or facts, the incredibility of a review is most likely to originate from its source. Moreover, fake reviews of ostensible consumers on online review platforms also gain more attention [60], which makes the source credibility more salient in people’s mind. Therefore, we focus on source credibility. Previous research has well documented that source credibility increases consumers’ information adoption [35,61–63]. If processing <sup>fl</sup>uency increases perceived reviewer credibility, it should strengthen the impact of customer reviews.

Easy-to-process material is usually perceived as more familiar [49,64–66] because people hold a naïve (and usually correct) theory that familiar material is easier to process than unfamiliar material [33]. Hence, <sup>fl</sup>uently processed stimuli seem more familiar than dis<sup>fl</sup>uently processed stimuli, even when the <sup>fl</sup>uency experience is due solely to incidental factors, such as visual features of the material (e.g., [67]. Therefore, reviewers in <sup>fl</sup>uent reviews might be judged as credible because they seem familiar, and it is relatively easy to trust those with whom we are more familiar [68–70].

At the same time, although there is lack of direct evidence that <sup>fl</sup>uency increases perceived source credibility, previous research has provided support for the <sup>fl</sup>uency-truth association. For example, people are more likely to judge repeated statements as true. They do so because they hold the theory that well-accepted and accurate statements are usually familiar. The ease of processing repeated statements leads to the perception of familiarity, which in turn increases truth judgment [71,72]. Thus, statements having strong (vs. weak) contrasts to back ground [67] or aphorisms presented in rhyming (vs. non-rhyming) forms [73] are judged to be true more frequently. Skurnik et al. [74] even showed that repeated warnings of a false claim can lead old people to judge the claim to be true because it looks familiar and <sup>fl</sup>uent after multiple exposures. Similarly, Rennekamp [75] found that a more readable disclosure increases, through the feelings of processing <sup>fl</sup>u ency, investors’ beliefs that they could rely on the disclosure. The <sup>fl</sup>uency-truth association is likely to take e<sup>f</sup>ect when review readers are judging reviewer credibility. On the other hand, audiences are likely to infer speakers’ opinion strength and con<sup>fi</sup>dence through contextual cues, such as empty space [76]. For instance, in our context, processing <sup>fl</sup>uency might convey a feeling of reviewers’ con<sup>fi</sup>dence of their reviews and make the reviewers’ opinion seem more credible. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect <sup>fl</sup>uency to increase reviewer credibility which consequently will strengthen the impact of customer reviews.

Previous research has documented that processing <sup>fl</sup>uency can be operationalized in many forms, ranging from presentation characteristics, such as <sup>fi</sup>gure-ground contrast [52], print font [46], and rhyming vs. non-rhyming form [73], to the semantic relatedness of the material and the frequency and recency of previous exposure [77,78]. Following previous research [49,79,80], we presented customer reviews in an easy-to-read or di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read font to manipulate processing <sup>fl</sup>uency. As discussed above, we predict that processing <sup>fl</sup>uency would lead consumers to trust the reviewers more, and thus, customer reviews would become more impactful on consumer evaluation. Note that in the current research, we measured the impact of customer reviews in directly through consumer evaluation. That is, either more positive evaluation following positive customer reviews or more negative evaluation following negative reviews indicates the greater impact of customer reviews. Thus, we propose that:

Hypothesis 1. The impact of customer reviews on consumer evaluation is greater when customer reviews are written in an easy-to-read (vs. di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read) font.

## 2.3. ELM predicts the moderating role of elaboration motivation

The ELM [36,37] suggests that motivation and cognitive ability play an important role in guiding information processing. In brief, when message recipients lack the motivation or are too cognitively busy to process message content thoroughly, they rely more on heuristics. As a result, peripheral cues, such as the title or the attractiveness of the message communicator, a<sup>f</sup>ect message persuasiveness. In contrast, when people are motivated to process a message more thoroughly, such as having a high need for accurate information or having a personal stake in understanding an issue, they are likely to pay more attention to the message and think about the message content. A central route of information processing is used, and thus, message content, such as the strength of the argument, a<sup>f</sup>ects the message persuasiveness [36,37].

Metzger [35] applied the ELM to people’s website credibility assessment and suggested a dual processing model of credibility assessment. This model suggests that website users who are less motivated to <sup>fi</sup>nd credible information may assess credibility by using heuristic cues, such as the visual features of a website. Highly motivated users, however, are more likely to take a more rigorous, systematic approach to credibility assessment and would be less likely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by heuristic cues.

As suggested above, processing <sup>fl</sup>uency could have an e<sup>f</sup>ect on judgment when people rely on heuristics. Previous research has also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that people are likely to rely on their experience of processing <sup>fl</sup>uency when their processing motivation or ability is low, but they turn to accessing content when their processing ability and motivation are high [49,33,81]. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that, in the current research, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of processing <sup>fl</sup>uency, manipulated through font, should vanish when people are motivated to process the reviews thor oughly.

In this research, we examined two variables which have been demonstrated to alter people’s elaboration motivation, namely people’s need for cognition [82,83] and the accountability of people’s own judgment [84,85]. Need for cognition measures chronic tendencies of individuals to thoroughly consider information. Research has revealed that individuals high in need for cognition intrinsically enjoy elabora tion [82]. Thus, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of font in the current research should diminish when people are high in need for cognition. Formally, we propose:

Hypothesis 2a. Need for cognition weakens the e<sup>f</sup>ect of font on the impact of customer reviews on consumer evaluation.

Likewise, previous research has shown that individuals who feel responsible for their evaluation are more likely to process information provided to them more thoroughly, compared to those who believe that they are just part of a collective group [86,87]. Other research has suggested that individuals who feel accountable for a choice are more likely to make that choice thoroughly, compared to those who do not feel accountable [88], because they try to avoid post-decisional regret. In this case, when people need to account for their own judgment, they are more motivated to process the reviews thoroughly and are thus less likely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the heuristic cues of processing ease. Formally, we propose:

Hypothesis 2b. Accountability for an evaluation weakens the e<sup>f</sup>ect of font on the impact of customer reviews on consumer evaluation.

## 2.4. Alternative mediators

Although we predict that fonts alter the impact of customer reviews through perceived reviewer credibility, there are possible alternative mediators. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, under the context of online customer review processing, there are three main elements: (1) reviewers, (2) reviews, and (3) review readers. These three elements motivate us to explore three other possible mediators: (1) ease of comprehension (which is related to review readers), (2) information diagnosticity (which is related to reviews), and (3) self-reviewer similarity (which refers to reviewer-reader relationship). We next discuss these three alternative mediators.

First, processing <sup>fl</sup>uency by nature indicates that it is easy to process the information, and hence, the meaning of reviews is understood easily [31]. Better-understood reviews should be more persuasive when the argument quality is high [82,36] and thus increase the impact of reviews.

Second, the experience of processing <sup>fl</sup>uency may evoke a positive a<sup>f</sup>ective response and enhance liking [34,80]. Thus, an easy-to-read (vs. di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read) font might lead people to react more favorably to the reviews and judge the reviews to be more diagnostic in general. Information diagnosticity refers to people’s perceptions of the extent to which information conveys relevant content that can be of use in understanding and evaluating product quality and performance [89]. It is often conceptualized as the degree of information helpfulness [61,21]. Processing <sup>fl</sup>uency could alter people’s reliance on customer reviews by in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the perceived information diagnosticity.

Third, <sup>fl</sup>uency might also increase the perceived similarity between reviewers and review readers and thus strengthen the impact of customer reviews. On the one hand, people may intuitively hold the naïve theory that reviews generated by similar others should be easier to process. Then, if reviews are easy to process, they are likely to infer that these reviews are generated by reviewers who are similar to themselves. On the other hand, extensive research has shown that perceived simi larity between information senders and recipients increases the perceived value of the communication in both o<sup>fl</sup>ine [90–92] and online contexts [93,94]. Therefore, the existence of the association between <sup>fl</sup>uency and self-reviewer similarity is possible.

## 3. Study 1: the e<sup>f</sup>ect of review font

Study 1 aimed to show that a di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read (vs. easy-to-read) font would reduce the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the valence of online reviews, such that a product with positive reviews would be rated as less positive and a product with negative reviews would be rated as less negative when the reviews were written in a di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read font.

## 3.1. Research method of Study 1

## 3.1.1. Experimental design

The study adopted a 2 (font: easy-to-read vs. di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read) $\times ~ 2$ (review valence: negative vs. positive) full-factorial between-subjects design to validate H1. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four treatment conditions (see Table 1).

## 3.1.2. Materials and pretest

The target product was hotel accommodation. Participants partici pated in a survey about “online hotel reservation” and were asked to look at a screenshot of an online hotel reservation webpage (please see Appendix A for details) and answer questions. The webpage was designed according to the webpage of a hotel on Qunar.com, a popular Chinese online hotel reservation website. The name and address of the hotel were masked. The treatment material had two parts: (1) general information about a hotel, including pictures, averaged rating (i.e., 4.4 out of 5), number of reviews (i.e., 768), and price (¥271, about \$40) and (2) eight product reviews. Considering that in real situations, positive reviews and negative reviews are likely to coexist, the contents of the <sup>fi</sup>rst four reviews were identical across all conditions, with two positive and two negative. The remaining four reviews were either positive or negative depending on the manipulation of review valence. All the reviews were adapted from actual customer reviews of the hotel. These reviews were presented in either an easy-to-read font (regula Chinese style, non-italic, black; i.e., the <sup>fl</sup>uent condition) or a di<sup>fi</sup>cultto-read font (handwriting Chinese type, italic, grey; i.e., the dis<sup>fl</sup>uent condition).

## 3.1.3. Participants

Two hundred and eleven participants (103 females and 108 males) were recruited from a Chinese online panel (i.e., www.sojump.com). Their mean age was 33.56 (SD = 7.43) years old.

## 3.1.4. Dependent and control variable

We used the following items to measure participants’ hotel evaluation: (1) compared with other hotels of the same type, what is your evaluation of this hotel (1 = much worse than other hotels, 7 = much better than other hotels); (2) compared with other hotels of the same type, what is your impression of this hotel (1 = very positive, 7 = very negative; reverse coded); (3) generally speaking, how much do you like this hotel (1 = dislike it very much, 7 = like it very much). We also measured the frequency of participants’ online hotel booking (1 = very seldom, 7 = very often) and collected demographic data (i.e., gender and age) as the control variables.

The demographic data of the participants, such as age, gender, and usage frequency, were recorded to check the heterogeneity between di<sup>f</sup>erent groups. ## 3.3. Discussion of Study 1

Study 1 demonstrated that the di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read font reduces the impact of online customer reviews, con<sup>fi</sup>rming H1. Previous research has suggested that people are more likely to rely on their feelings (e.g., processing <sup>fl</sup>uency) as cues for a judgment when they do not have motivation to process information comprehensively [49,33]. In Study $^ { 2 , }$ we measured participants’ need for cognition as an index of elaboration motivation and tested H2a.

## 4. Study 2: need for cognition as a boundary condition

Study 2 aimed at replicating the e<sup>f</sup>ect of Study 1 using a di<sup>f</sup>erent target product, namely a pair of mini-computer speakers. More importantly, we examined the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of need for cognition. We expected that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of processing <sup>fl</sup>uency manipulated through font would diminish among participants that were high in need for cogni tion.

## 4.1. Research method of Study 2

## 4.1.1. Experimental design

As in Study 1, we adopted a 2 (font: easy-to-read vs. di<sup>fi</sup>cult-toread) × 2 (review valence: negative vs. positive) full-factorial between subjects design for Study 2. Before participants were randomly assigned to one of the same four treatment conditions as those in Study 1, they completed an 18-item need for cognition scale [95]. A sample item is “I like to have the responsibility of handling a situation that requires a lot of thinking” (1 = extremely uncharacteristic of me; 7 = extremely characteristic of me).

## 4.1.2. Materials and pretest

The target product was a pair of mini-computer speakers sold on line. Participants responded to a survey about “how people think and feel when they are shopping online,” and they were asked to look at the screenshot of a webpage of the speakers (please see Appendix B for details) and answer some questions about it. The screenshot was cap tured from the webpage of mini speakers sold on Amazon.com. The treatment material had two parts: (1) general information about the speakers, including a picture, product description, brand name, and price and (2) seven product reviews. Similar to Study 1, the content of the <sup>fi</sup>rst three reviews was identical across all conditions, with two positive and one negative. The remaining four reviews were either positive or negative depending on the manipulation of review valence. All the reviews were adapted from actual customer reviews of the speakers. The reviews were presented in either an easy-to-read font (Lucida Bright; i.e., the <sup>fl</sup>uent condition) or a di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read font (Lucida Calligraphy; i.e., the dis<sup>fl</sup>uent condition).

Before the main study, we conducted a pretest to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the va lidity of the manipulation of processing <sup>fl</sup>uency and review valence. ## 5. Study 3: accountability as a boundary condition

Considering that the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings were mirrored in the positive and negative review conditions, we included only the positive review conditions in Study 3. ## 7. General discussion

The present research found that customer reviews, positive or negative, are often more in<sup>fl</sup>uential when written in an easy-to-read (vs. di<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-read) font (Studies 1–4). This e<sup>f</sup>ect occurs because the processing <sup>fl</sup>uency induced by fonts serves as a cue indicating the credibility of the reviewers (Study 4). Moreover, because consumers are less likely to refer to their processing experiences when they are highly motivated to elaborate information [49,33], the e<sup>f</sup>ect of processing <sup>fl</sup>uency diminishes when consumers are high in need for cognition (Studies 2 and 4) or in an accountable situation (Study 3).

Noteworthy, the <sup>fi</sup>nding that ease of comprehension, review diagnosticity, and self-reviewer similarity fail to mediate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of font does not necessarily mean that font could not have an e<sup>f</sup>ect on them under any circumstances. As we discussed in the literature review, <sup>fl</sup>uency usually in<sup>fl</sup>uences judgment through a misattribution process. Thus, if the feeling of <sup>fl</sup>uency is misattributed to one aspect, such as credibility, it could not be misattributed to another aspect simultaneously [49,33]. As fake reviews on online review platforms have attracted more attention [60], consumers are likely to judge whether the reviews are from real and credible reviewers before they adopt the opinions. Thus, when people feel that the reviews are di<sup>fi</sup>cult to process, source incredibility might be a more accessible reason than di<sup>fi</sup>- culty of comprehension, low review diagnosticity, and self-reviewer dissimilarity because the latter three are not called into question for most online reviews.

## 7.1. Theoretical and practical contributions

First, the present research extends the literature about visual design of Internet interface. Although visual elements of commercial websites have attracted the attention of many researchers [43,42,44], most existing research has focused on the visual presentation of product in formation rather than that of consumer reviews. In fact, the psycholo gical impact of visual features of online reviews has been ignored. The present research shows the importance of taking the visual features of customer reviews into consideration.

Second, this research contributes to the understanding of the determinants of the impact of online reviews. Previous research has shown that factors altering the impact of online reviews may embed in the review content [5,19–21], in the target product [30,24], or in the review recipients [29]. This research identi<sup>fi</sup>es a new factor, namely processing <sup>fl</sup>uency, which can be examined when exploring the impact of user-generated contents. It is worth noting that although the present research uses review font to manipulate processing <sup>fl</sup>uency, other fac tors, such as linguistic characteristics [53], may also in<sup>fl</sup>uence processing <sup>fl</sup>uency and could be included and examined in future research.

Third, this research shows that source credibility leads customer reviews to be more impactful, especially when people are not motivated to process the review contents. It con<sup>fi</sup>rms that credibility could be a<sup>f</sup>ected by heuristic cues (e.g., <sup>fl</sup>uency), and thus, the assessment of information credibility is not necessarily derived by an intentional process. This <sup>fi</sup>nding provides empirical support for the dual processing model of credibility assessment [35].

Finally, this research contributes to the literature on processing <sup>fl</sup>uency by showing that <sup>fl</sup>uency has an e<sup>f</sup>ect on perceived source credibility. Previous research has found that processing <sup>fl</sup>uency makes people more likely to judge a statement to be true [73,67]. The current research further shows that, besides such content credibility, processing <sup>fl</sup>uency also a<sup>f</sup>ects another dimension of information credibility, namely source credibility, a <sup>fi</sup>nding which enriches our understanding of the processing-truth association.

Our research also o<sup>f</sup>ers implications for e-commerce practices. First, this research highlights the possible psychological consequences of online retailers’ font customization for their websites. Although online retailers carefully choose fonts for their websites, they usually focus on the aesthetic pleasure of fonts. The current <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that it is also necessary for online retailers to consider the possible e<sup>f</sup>ects of their font choices in altering the impact of customer reviews. Online store designers may also consider including information about the possible e<sup>f</sup>ects of fonts when they are providing font customization services for online retailers.

## 7.2. Limitations and future research

Although this research highlights several notable <sup>fi</sup>ndings and contributions, we acknowledge some limitations. First, although we designed our experiments using actual customer reviews of products, we did not control for product features mentioned in the positive and negative conditions. We also did not control for the extremity of the reviews across the positive and negative conditions because our purpose was to show that review fonts a<sup>f</sup>ect the impact of both positive reviews and negative reviews on consumer evaluation, rather than to compare the positive and negative conditions. However, both product features mentioned [101] and extremity of reviews [3,21] are important characteristics of customer reviews, and it is likely that they exert some moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects. Future research could examine these possibilities.

Second, we measured reviewer credibility but not review content credibility to represent information credibility. It is quite possible that these two aspects of information credibility are undistinguishable under the current research context. It means that <sup>fl</sup>uency does not have to go through a perception of reviewer characteristics to a<sup>f</sup>ect evaluation. The perceived credibility of the review content could also be one of the possible underlying mechanisms. Future research should examine which path is more fundamental and when.

Third, the present <sup>fi</sup>ndings may exist only within a certain range of processing di<sup>fi</sup>culty. When fonts are very di<sup>fi</sup>cult to read, some consumers may feel reluctant to read details at all, especially those people that tend to elaborate less. Moreover, in that case, consumers may be aware that their di<sup>fi</sup>culty is from the font. If they correctly attribute the di<sup>fi</sup>culty to the font rather than use it as a heuristic cue to judge source credibility, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of <sup>fl</sup>uency might diminish [49,33]. The boundary where processing <sup>fl</sup>uency exerts an e<sup>f</sup>ect could be further explored in future research.
