# Open to Your Rival: Competition between Open Source and Proprietary Software under Indirect Network Efects

Yu Wang<sup>a</sup>, Yu Chen<sup>b</sup>, and Bonwoo Koo<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Nanjing University, School of Business, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China; <sup>b</sup>University of Graz, Department of Economics, Graz, Austria; <sup>c</sup>University of Waterloo, Department of Management Sciences, Waterloo, Ontario, Canada

## ABSTRACT

With the popularity of open source software (OSS) as an alternative to proprietary software (PS), proprietary-software firms such as IBM and Microsoft started to embrace this new paradigm during the past decades. We analyze how firms choose the software development strategy between OSS and PS, by constructing a duopoly model in which consumers sequentially purchase software and complementary services in a market that exhibits an indirect network efect. We show that a PS firm may benefit from the presence of an OSS firm, and the software market can be dominated by a single OSS if the indirect network efect is weak and the cost saving efect of OSS is negligible. We also show that the market can support two OSS if the cost saving efect of OSS is sizeable, and two PS if firms can provide fully compatible services to competitor’s PS. Building upon the existing works that investigate the competition between PS and OSS, this study improves our understanding of the role of OSS in firm’s software development strategy and market equilibrium.

## KEYWORDS

open source software; indirect network efect; software development; complementary services; Hotelling model

## Introduction

As the examples of Linux, Apache, and Android illustrate, numerous open source software (OSS) applications have met with great success in recent decades and become an important alternative to proprietary software (PS). Free access to source code of OSS can help improve the quality of OSS through voluntary contributions of code by OSS communities and achieve a large consumer base. However, the traditional business model of selling software does not work, and OSS firms have to make profits from selling complementary products, support, and services [21, 27]. Red Hat, for example, made \$3.4 billion in 2019 fiscal year by selling complementary products (security patches, application packages) and services (development support, certification, and training). Having observed the business successes of OSS and its potentials, several PS firms support OSS projects (e.g., IBM’s financial support to Linux and Apache) and even launch their own OSS projects, such as Java from SUN and Android from Google.

The shift of Microsoft’s business model towards an open source company during the past decade provides a stark example of changing environments in the software industry. Having called Linux “a cancer” and open source “un-American” in 2001, Microsoft since then has gradually embraced Linux in its development process and promoted the Linuxbased open source environment in Microsoft Azure, its cloud platform that competes with Amazon Web Services. After Microsoft’s \$7.5 billion purchase of the leading open source hosting platform GitHub in June 2018, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella announced “We are all in on open source.”<sup>2</sup> In December 2018, Microsoft abandoned its proprietary EdgeHTML engine for its Edge browser in favor of the Blink browser engine which is a part of Google’s open source browser project called Chromium.<sup>3</sup>

Although there may be debates whether it was prudent for Microsoft to give up its own proprietary EdgeHTML engine and switch to the OSS-based Blink engine, Microsoft might have believed it had more to gain by embracing the OSS project. Microsoft’s foray into open source during the last decade illustrates where the software development process and market structure are heading in the future. As the industry shifts to cloud computing, many development tools to support this new model are OSS and the value of cloud business is largely tied to complementary services.<sup>4</sup> Many firms such as Google and Facebook make profits by selling complementary services based on OSS, not the software itself. Realizing this trend, Microsoft increasingly sells services built on top of open source projects like Apache Spark and hosts others’ OSS applications in Microsoft Azure.

Several interesting research questions arise from the changing attitudes toward OSS. First, how does the presence of OSS influence a PS firm’s profit and its software development strategy? There are debates whether firms can benefit from competition with OSS firms, and this study provides alternative explanations on this issue. Second, what are the factors that afect firm’s decision to develop OSS instead of PS and how do these factors afect the market equilibrium and social welfare? One of the main benefits of developing OSS is the cost saving from voluntary contributions, but this study points out other factors such as the indirect network efect and the dependency of software on complementary services that can influence the market equilibrium. Third, how does the availability of fully compatible services to other software afect the market equilibrium and social welfare? Consideration of these extensions under diferent market environments can help explain recent developments in open source platform.

By building a three-stage duopoly model in which firms can sell both software and its complementary services, this study analyzes firm’s software development choice between OSS and PS and how its strategy is afected by the indirect network efect and the dependency of software on complementary services [21, 28]. Under the OSS strategy, the software development cost is reduced, but a rival firm can provide services that work with the software [5]. On the other hand, the firm with a PS strategy bears the software development cost but enjoys a full monopoly over the provision of services for its software users. This analysis of firm’s strategy can help explain how the internet browser market has evolved over the past few decades and why Microsoft has embraced an open source business model in the cloud computing.

The modeling framework of this study difers significantly from that of existing studies in several aspects. First, instead of assuming an OSS firm as a passive competitor to a PS firm [10, 29, 36], this study considers symmetric firms that can develop software and sell complementary services on an equal footing. Second, while most studies focus on the direct network efect in the software market itself [10, 14, 38], this study examines the indirect network efect in which the installed base of software helps firms appropriate profits from the services market [19, 39]. Third, this study allows firms to sell software and its services separately such that firms can compete in both software and services markets, which contrasts to several existing studies in which bundled products of software and services are sold as a single unit by a single firm [3, 32] or firms are not allowed to compete in the services market for rival firms’ OSS [26].

This study finds that firm’s decision to develop OSS critically depends on the size of the indirect network efect and the degree of service dependency of software. The presence of OSS can make a PS firm better of if software is less dependent on complementary services and even increase social welfare if the network efect is not as strong. If the cost saving efect of OSS is negligible, either a proprietary equilibrium with two PS firms or a hybrid equilibrium with a PS firm and an OSS firm is plausible, depending on the network efect and the service dependency of software. If the cost saving efect of OSS is sizeable, an open source equilibrium with two OSS firms can be another possible equilibrium and may improve social welfare if the cost saving efect of OSS is large. If another firm can provide fully compatible services to PS, the market is more likely to be in a proprietary equilibrium with two PS firms but society is always better of with a hybrid equilibrium, which may justify external interventions in the market.

The results of this study can help explain the evolution of the internet browser market during the past few decades. In the early 1990s, the internet browser market was dominated by two proprietary firms, Microsoft and Netscape [9]. Both firms made sizeable profits from complementary services with a strong indirect network efect and consumers could use browser without complementary services as a stand-alone product, which had led to a proprietary equilibrium. As the market matures into the 2000s, internet browser became a platform in which consumers derive utility from complementary services and became more service-dependent. Microsoft gave up its proprietary browser software MS Edge by switching to the OSS-based Chrome browser engine in 2018 and is competing in the services market, which has led to a hybrid equilibrium. This study can also explain the current evolution of the cloud computing market. Microsoft’s recent decision of embracing the Linux-based open source environment in its Azure is likely to lead to a hybrid equilibrium in which Microsoft gives up service-dependent software and focuses on the services market which exhibits a strong indirect network efect.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. After reviewing related literature, we discuss the basic assumptions and main features of the duopoly model. Then, we examine the software and service provision game between the two firms under diferent combinations of software openness. The main results analyze firm’s software development strategy by deriving subgame perfect market equilibria and discuss social welfare by assuming the software development cost of PS is zero. We then extend the baseline model by considering of a positive development cost of PS and fully compatible services. After a discussion of the limitations and managerial implications, we end with the summary of our results.

## Literature Review

The success of OSS and its unique innovation process have attracted many researchers to study this new “private-collective” innovation approach in markets dominated by proprietary innovation systems [31, 44, 45]. Early literature discusses individual programmers’ incentives to participate in OSS projects, which include ideological or altruistic motivations and enhanced reputation through peer recognition [20, 44]. Lerner and Tirole [30] summarize two main benefits from OSS: direct user benefits for their own use and reputational signaling benefits for future job. This line of studies on individual incentives is the basis for later literature on the motivations of for-profit firms such as IBM to initiate and participate in OSS projects.

Andersen-Gott et al. [2] summarize three main motivations for firms to contribute to OSS projects: building better innovative capability, reducing software development costs, and selling complementary services. Revealing innovation and knowledge can encourage OSS communities to undertake intellectually challenging tasks and increase firms’ innovative capacity [44]. OSS communities also perform many routine tasks such as code testing and bug fixing, which help firms achieve high quality products at low costs [1]. By giving away software, firms can also obtain the know-how needed for providing better complementary services and enjoy competitive advantage over proprietary rivals [18, 26]. This stream of research summarizes that firms are motivated by economic and technological aspects of OSS and the OSS development process can be attractive to small firms with limited R&D resources [8].

There exists a large body of research which examines the efects of the presence of OSS on market competition with a duopoly model in which a for-profit firm is competing with a non-profit open source firm or organization. The price and quality of PS products are afected by the presence of OSS. While Lanzi [29] and Economides and Katsamakas [17] show that the presence of OSS increases the quality and price of PS, Jaisingh et al. [22] and Athey and Ellison [5] argue that PS firms may strategically reduce quality and price to slow the growth of OSS. In terms of the market equilibrium, some studies show the possibility of coexistence of an OSS firm and a PS firm because each type of software caters to a diferent segment of consumers [36, 37], while other studies show the dominance of a PS firm [10, 17] or of an OSS firm [41, 42].

Recently, an increasing number of studies analyze firm’s strategic decision on whether to develop OSS or PS. Caulkins et al. [12] and Haruvy et al. [21] consider a monopoly firm’s decision of adopting an OSS strategy, and Casadesus-Masanell and Llanes [11] analyze the same issue under a duopoly model between a PS firm and a nonprofit OSS firm. Many of these duopoly models consider asymmetric firms; for example, August et al. [6] consider a model where one firm develops a software product and the other firm only contributes to improve its quality, and Jaisingh et al. [22] assume only one firm can produce PS. On the other hand, this study analyzes the software development strategy of symmetric firms which can develop and sell both software and services.

When firms compete in both softwares and services markets, the network efect between software and complementary services critically afects firms’ strategy. Most existing studies consider the direct network efect of the size of the software market on users utility. Sen [38] finds that a PS firm can have a larger market share than an OSS firm under a weak direct network efect, while Cheng et al. [14] derive similar results when the direct network efect is strong. On the other hand, several other studies point out the importance of the indirect network efect in which the quality and variety of complementary services depend on the number of software users [15, 23].<sup>5</sup> Stremersch et al. [39] define two types of indirect network efect: “demand-side indirect network efects” in which software availability afects hardware utility of consumers, and “supply-side indirect network efects” in which the hardware installed base afects the software provision by software manufacturers. This study considers the supply-side indirect network efect to explain how firms’ consumer base in the software market can afect a competitive advantage in the services market.

Due to durable good nature of software, consumers tend to acquire complementary services after the initial purchase of software [34]. Provision of complementary services is tied to compatibility issues, and several studies examine the efects of compatibility between OSS and PS on firms’ software development strategy and market structure [11, 14]. While PS firms can have easy access to the source code of OSS, OSS firms may have dificulty in providing services to PS, and one-way compatibility is typically observed [13, 33]. By considering both incompatible services and fully compatible services to rival firm’s PS, this study can provide new insights on the role of software compatibility on the market equilibrium and social welfare.

## Model

## Firm’s Decision

Consider an environment in which consumers derive utility by sequentially consuming software and its complementary services. The software and services are characterized by “one-way essential complements” in that consumers can derive utility from the software alone but not from the services alone [13]. Two symmetric firms, A and B, can each develop software and provide its complementary services, and they are located at 0 and 1, respectively, on a Hotelling line. Each firm determines the prices of software and complementary services given its and the rival firm’s software development strategy.

On the one hand, if a firm develops PS without disclosing key source code, it incurs the software development cost $C _ { i } ~ > ~ 0$ where $i ~ \in ~ \{ A , ~ B \}$ . The other firm cannot provide complementary services for the software due to technical and legal barriers, and only the PS developer can provide complementary services to its software users. If, on the other hand, a firm adopts an OSS strategy, the firm can save the development cost, but the other firm can provide services for the software and compete with the OSS developer in the services market. If both firms develop OSS, each firm can provide two versions of services: one for its own software users and the other for the rival firm’s software users.

This study considers a three-stage competition game between two firms. In Stage 0, Firm A and Firm B simultaneously decide their software development strategies. In Stage 1, after observing the software development strategy of the other firm, each firm simultaneously sets its software price, $p _ { i } \geq 0$ , and consumers purchase software from either firm in anticipation of the availability and price of complementary services for the software. In Stage 2, after observing consumers’ purchase of software, each firm simultaneously sets the corresponding services price, $r _ { i } \geq 0$ . The marginal cost of providing services is assumed to be normalized at zero.

## Demand

Consider a group of heterogeneous consumers who are uniformly distributed over [0, 1] on the Hotelling line, and the total mass of consumers is one. From the purchase of software in Stage 1, the utility of a consumer located at $x \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ is

$$
u _ {1} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \beta - x - p _ {A} & \text { if   she   purchases   software   from   Firm   A } \\ \beta - (1 - x) - p _ {B} & \text { if   she   purchases   software   from   Firm   B }, \end{array} \right.
$$

where $\beta$ denotes the consumer’s valuation of the software and $ { \boldsymbol { p } } _ { i } \geq 0$ is the price of the software developed by Firm i. We assume $\beta$ is suficiently large $( \beta \geq 2 )$ such that all consumers purchase one software product from one of the two firms. We also assume that consumers are located at the same location in both stages. Several studies classify consumers’ preference either as brand-based or attributes-based [16, 40], and emphasize the importance of brand-based preference in consumers’ software purchase decision [7, 25]. Our model assumes consumers have brand-based preference for software, such that their preference location is unchanged in both stages.<sup>6</sup>

Consumer y who has purchased Firm A’s software in Stage 1 is called an A-user. If Firm B can provide complementary services for Firm $\boldsymbol { A } ^ { * } \boldsymbol { s }$ software, A-user’s utility from purchasing services in Stage 2 is

$$
u _ {A 2} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c l} \delta N _ {A} - y - r _ {A} & \text { if   she   purchases   services   from   Firm   A } \\ \delta N _ {A} - (1 - y) - \tilde {r} _ {A} & \text { if   she   purchases   services   from   Firm   B } \\ 0 & \text { if   she   does   not   purchase   any   services } \end{array} \right.
$$

where $\delta > 0$ is the indirect network efect of the installed base of software users on consumer’s utility from complementary services, $N _ { A }$ is the number of consumers who purchased Firm $\boldsymbol { A } ^ { * } \boldsymbol { s }$ software, $r _ { A } \ge 0$ is the price of Firm $\boldsymbol { A } ^ { * } \boldsymbol { s }$ services, and $\tilde { r } _ { A } \geq 0$ is the price of Firm B’s services for Firm A’s software. $\delta N _ { A }$ indicates the total benefit of the indirect network efect from using Firm A’s software.<sup>7</sup> Similarly, the utility of B-user who purchases services in Stage 2 is

$$
u _ {B 2} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c l} \delta N _ {B} - (1 - y) - r _ {B} & \text { if   she   purchases   services   from   Firm } B \\ \delta N _ {B} - y - \tilde {r} _ {B} & \text { if   she   purchases   services   from   Firm } A \\ 0 & \text { if   she   does   not   purchase   any   services } \end{array} \right.
$$

Consumers who have purchased software in Stage 1 have the option of not purchasing any services in Stage 2. If we assume $\delta \ge 2$ , however, all consumers end up purchasing services in equilibrium and the market is fully covered. Consumers’ purchase decisions of software in Stage 1 depend on the total utility obtained from both stages: $U = u _ { 1 } + \lambda u _ { i 2 } .$ The service dependency parameter, $0 \leq \lambda \leq 1$ , can be interpreted as the degree of service dependency of software. If λ is close to zero, the software is likely to be serviceindependent such that consumers get most utility from the software itself. If λ approaches one, on the other hand, the software is more service-dependent and consumers receive utilities from both software and services. For example, Microsoft Azure is considered more service-dependent than Microsoft Ofice.

## Competition in Software and Services Markets

Given a software development strategy made in Stage 0, this section considers the software-services provision game in Stage 1 and 2. With backwards induction, it first solves the services prices set by each firm in Stage 2, and then solves the software prices in Stage 1.

## Two PS Firms

Consider an environment in which both firms have developed PS and compete in the software and services markets. Due to the closed nature of PS, each firm cannot provide services for its rival firm’s software. Firm i in Stage 2 is faced with $N _ { i }$ users who have purchased its software in Stage 1, and sets a monopoly price $r _ { i }$ for its services. Among i-users, consumer y is indiferent to purchasing services from Firm i or not purchasing at all if $\delta N _ { i } - y - r _ { i } = 0$ . Thus, the demand for the services provided by Firm i in Stage 1 is $n _ { i } = \delta N _ { i } - r _ { i }$

In Stage 2, each firm is the monopoly provider of its services, and consumers are locked in one firm’s software. Firm i faces with a profit maximization problem as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {r _ {i}} \pi_ {i 2} = r _ {i} n _ {i} \\ \text {s.t.} \quad n _ {i} <   N _ {i} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

Solving Equation (1) for Firm A and Firm B leads to the following solutions in Stage 2.

$$
\begin{array}{l} r _ {A} ^ {*} = (\delta - 1) N _ {A}, \quad r _ {B} ^ {*} = (\delta - 1) N _ {B}, \quad n _ {A} ^ {*} = N _ {A}, \quad n _ {B} ^ {*} = N _ {B}, \\ \pi_ {A 2} ^ {*} = (\delta - 1) N _ {A} ^ {2}, \quad \pi_ {B 2} ^ {*} = (\delta - 1) N _ {B} ^ {2}. \end{array}
$$

Each firm’s prices for its services depend on the number of consumers who have purchased its software in Stage 1 $( N _ { i } )$ as well as the indirect network efect (δ).

In Stage 1, the consumer located at x is indiferent to buying software from either firm if the utility from both stages satisfies the following equation.

$$
[ \beta - x - p _ {A} ] + \lambda [ \delta x - x - r _ {A} ] = [ \beta - (1 - x) - p _ {B} ] + \lambda [ \delta (1 - x) - (1 - x) - r _ {B} ]
$$

With two PS, the demand for Firm $\boldsymbol { A } ^ { * } \boldsymbol { s }$ services in Stage 2 is simply x and the marginal consumer x receives zero utility in Stage 2. Thus, the demand for the software developed by Firm i competing with Firm j in Stage 1 is $N _ { i } = ( 1 + p _ { j } - p _ { i } ) / 2$ . In Stage 1, Firm i sets the price of software $ { \boldsymbol { p } } _ { i }$ to maximize the profit.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {p _ {i}} \pi_ {i} = p _ {i} N _ {i} + \pi_ {i 2} ^ {*} \\ \text {s.t.} \qquad N _ {i} <   1. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Solving Equation (2) for Firm A and Firm B leads to the following equilibrium.

$$
\begin{array}{l l} p _ {A} ^ {*} = p _ {B} ^ {*} = 0, & r _ {A} ^ {*} = r _ {B} ^ {*} = (\delta - 1) / 2, \\ N _ {A} ^ {*} = N _ {B} ^ {*} = n _ {A} ^ {*} = n _ {B} ^ {*} = 1 / 2, & \pi_ {A} ^ {*} = \pi_ {B} ^ {*} = (\delta - 1) / 4. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Lemma 1. If both firms compete with PS, they give away their software and set the same price for complementary services by evenly sharing the market. The services price and firms’ profit increase with the indirect network efect δ.

Proof. See Appendix.

When both firms compete with PS, the competition is concentrated in Stage 1 because each firm becomes a monopoly provider of services to its software users in Stage 2 [24]. Competitive pressure to take advantage of the indirect network efect forces each firm to provide its software for free in Stage 1 (i.e., $p _ { i } ~ = ~ 0 )$ and make profits from selling complementary services in Stage 2. In addition, when the network efect is strong, consumers are willing to pay more for services and each firm can charge a high price for its services. In the desktop operating system market with a strong network efect, for example, two PS products (Windows and MacOS) account for about 95 percent of the market share as of February 2020. Competitive pressure makes firms lower the price of the operating system to make profits from complementary products such as expensive hardware for Mac and MS Ofice for Microsoft.

## A PS Firm and an OSS Firm

Now consider an environment in which Firm A has developed PS and Firm B has developed OSS. Due the nature of free distribution, the price of Firm B’s OSS is zero. In addition, the source code of OSS is publicly available so Firm A can provide services for both users in Stage 2, while Firm B provides services only to its users. B-user z is indiferent to purchasing services from either firm if $\delta N _ { B } - z - \tilde { r } _ { B } = \delta N _ { B } - ( 1 - z ) - r _ { B } .$ where $z \in [ 1 - N _ { B } , 1 ]$ . The demands by B-users for the services provided by Firm A and Firm B are, respectively,

$$
\tilde {n} _ {B} = \frac {r _ {B} - \tilde {r} _ {B} - 1}{2} + N _ {B} \quad \text { and } \quad n _ {B} = \frac {1 - r _ {B} + \tilde {r} _ {B}}{2}
$$

In Stage 2, Firm A’s profit comes from selling services to A-users $( r _ { A } n _ { A } )$ and to B-users $( \tilde { r } _ { A } \tilde { n } _ { A } )$ . Firm A is the monopoly provider of services to A-users, and its profit maximization problem for A-users in Stage 2 is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {r _ {A}} \pi_ {A 2} = r _ {A} (\delta N _ {A} - r _ {A}) \\ \text { s.t. } \quad n _ {A} \leq N _ {A}. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

Solving Equation (4) leads to the following solutions for the services market for A-users.

$$
r _ {A} ^ {*} = (\delta - 1) N _ {A}, \quad n _ {A} ^ {*} = N _ {A}, \quad \pi_ {A 2} ^ {*} = (\delta - 1) N _ {A} ^ {2}.
$$

For B-users, both Firm A and Firm B compete to sell services in Stage 2. The profit maximization problems of Firm A and Firm B for B-users are, respectively,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {r _ {B}} \tilde {\pi} _ {A 2} = \tilde {r} _ {B} \tilde {n} _ {B} = \tilde {r} _ {B} \left(\frac {r _ {B} - \tilde {r} _ {B} - 1}{2} + N _ {B}\right) \\ \text { s.t. } \quad \tilde {n} _ {B} \leq N _ {B} \end{array}\tag{5a}
$$

and

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {r _ {B}} \pi_ {B 2} = r _ {B} n _ {B} = r _ {B} \left(\frac {1 - r _ {B} + \tilde {r} _ {B}}{2}\right) \\ \text { s.t. } \quad n _ {B} \leq N _ {B}. \end{array}\tag{5b}
$$

Solving Equations (5a) and (5b) leads to the following solutions for the services market for B-users.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \tilde {r} _ {B} ^ {*} = \frac {4 N _ {B} - 1}{3}, r _ {B} ^ {*} = \frac {2 N _ {B} + 1}{3}, \qquad \tilde {n} _ {B} ^ {*} = \frac {4 N _ {B} - 1}{6}, \\ n _ {B} ^ {*} = \frac {2 N _ {B} + 1}{6}, \qquad \pi_ {A 2} ^ {*} = \frac {(4 N _ {B} - 1) ^ {2}}{1 8}, \quad \pi_ {B 2} ^ {*} = \frac {(2 N _ {B} + 1) ^ {2}}{1 8}. \end{array}
$$

In Stage 1, the marginal consumer x is indiferent to buying a software product from either firm if the following equation is satisfied.

$$
[ \beta - x - p _ {A} ] + \lambda [ \delta x - x - r _ {A} ^ {*} ] = [ \beta - (1 - x) ] + \lambda [ \delta (1 - x) - x - \tilde {r} _ {B} ^ {*} ]\tag{6}
$$

If consumer x adopts PS, she can purchase services only from the PS firm. If she adopts OSS, on the other hand, she can purchase services from either firm. The left-hand side of Equation (6) represents the marginal consumer $x ^ { \prime } s$ total utility from buying PS, and her utility from Stage 2 is zero. The right-hand side represents her total utility from buying OSS in Stage 1, and the second term is the utility from Stage 2, which depends on the price of services provided by Firm A. The demands for software from Firm A and Firm B in Stage 1 are $\begin{array} { r } { N _ { A } = \frac { 3 ( \lambda \delta - \lambda - 1 + p _ { A } ) } { 3 \lambda \delta - \lambda - 6 } } \end{array}$ and $\begin{array} { r } { N _ { B } = \frac { 2 \lambda - 3 - p _ { A } } { 3 \lambda \delta - \lambda - 6 } } \end{array}$ , respectively. Firm A sets ${ \mathit { P } } _ { A }$ to maximize the total profit from selling its software products and services to both A-users and B-users.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {p _ {A}} \pi_ {A} = p _ {A} N _ {A} + \pi_ {A 2} ^ {*} + \tilde {\pi} _ {A 2} ^ {*} \\ \text { s.t. } \quad 0 \leq N _ {A} \leq 1. \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

Solving Equation (7) leads to the equilibrium that is summarized in Lemma 2.

Lemma 2. Suppose a PS firm competes with an OSS firm.

(i) If the network efect is strong $( \delta \ge \delta ^ { * } )$ and software is service-independent $( 0 \leq \lambda \leq \lambda ^ { * } )$ both firms compete in both software and services markets, where $\delta ^ { * } \equiv 2 5 / 9$ and $\lambda ^ { * } \equiv$ $( 9 \delta - 2 5 ) / ( 9 \delta ^ { 2 } - 2 2 \delta + 5 )$ . The PS firm makes a higher profit than the OSS firm does.

(ii) If the network efect is weak $( 2 \leq \delta < \delta ^ { * } )$ or software is service-dependent $( \lambda ^ { * } < \lambda$ ≤ 1), the OSS firm dominates the software market and both firms compete in the services market, making the same profit.

Proof. See Appendix.

If the network efect is strong, consumers are willing to pay more for services and the PS firm uses an aggressive pricing strategy to expand its market share by setting $p _ { A } = 0$ for its PS. If software is more service-independent, consumers’ purchase decision is mainly driven by the utility from software itself but firms can still make profits from services as long as the network efect is strong. In the services market, the PS firm can charge a high price to its users and a low price to OSS users to compete with the OSS firm, making a higher profit than the OSS firm does.

If the network efect is weak or software is more service-dependent, the software market is completely dominated by OSS and the PS firm competes with the OSS firm only in the services market. Since the profit from a fragmented software market with two softwares is too small, the PS firm is willing to give up its software to achieve a unified market. This result illustrates that the market environment forces firms to voluntarily coordinate software development to avoid a fragmented market.

The presence of OSS can benefit the PS firm if software is service-independent (0 $\leq \lambda \leq \lambda _ { 1 } )$ , where $\begin{array} { r } { \lambda _ { 1 } = \frac { 1 8 \delta ^ { 2 } - 5 2 \delta + ( 1 0 - 6 \delta ) \sqrt { ( \delta - 1 ) ( 9 \delta - 1 9 ) } + 4 2 } { 2 7 \delta ^ { 3 } - 9 1 \delta ^ { 2 } + 8 9 \delta - 1 7 } \ a n d \ \lambda _ { 1 } < \lambda ^ { * } . } \end{array}$

## Proof. See Appendix.

Lemma 3 illustrates that the PS firm can be better of with the presence of OSS under a certain condition, a result that contrasts with several studies which argue that the presence of OSS reduces a PS firm’s profit due to intense competition [10, 42]. A few exceptions include Sen [38] who show that a PS firm improves the quality of PS to compete with an OSS firm and Atal and Shankar [4] who show that reduced price competition with OSS can make a PS firm better of. Unlike these papers, however, Lemma 3 emphasizes the role of asymmetric access to the rival firm’s OSS services market by the PS firm.

## Two OSS Firms

Consider another environment in which two OSS firms compete in the market. Each firm can provide two versions of services: one for its software users and another for the rival’s software users. In Stage 1, the price of each firm’s OSS is zero by assumption. In Stage 2, both A-users and B-users can purchase services from either firm, and they are indiferent to purchasing services from either firm if

$$
\begin{array}{l} \delta N _ {A} - y - r _ {A} = \delta N _ {A} - (1 - y) - \tilde {r} _ {A} \\ \delta N _ {B} - z - \tilde {r} _ {B} = \delta N _ {B} - (1 - z) - r _ {B} \end{array}
$$

The demands for services of each software product are

$$
\begin{array}{l} n _ {A} = \frac {\tilde {r} _ {A} - r _ {A} + 1}{2}, \tilde {n} _ {B} = (1 - N _ {A}) - \frac {\tilde {r} _ {B} - r _ {B} + 1}{2}, \quad \tilde {n} _ {A} = N _ {A} - \frac {\tilde {r} _ {A} - r _ {A} + 1}{2}, \\ n _ {B} = \frac {\tilde {r} _ {B} - r _ {B} + 1}{2} \end{array}
$$

where $\tilde { n } _ { i }$ is the demand by i-users for the services provided by Firm j. The profit maximization problem of Firm i in the services market is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {r _ {i}, \tilde {r} _ {j}} \pi_ {i 2} = r _ {i} n _ {i} + \tilde {r} _ {j} \tilde {n} _ {j} \\ \text { s.t. } \quad 0 \leq n _ {i} \leq 1 \text { and } 0 \leq \tilde {n} _ {j} \leq 1. \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

Solving Equation (8) for both firms leads to the following solutions in Stage 2.

$$
\begin{array}{l l} r _ {A} ^ {*} = (2 N _ {A} + 1) / 3, & \tilde {r} _ {A} ^ {*} = (4 N _ {A} - 1) / 3, \\ n _ {A} ^ {*} = (2 N _ {A} + 1) / 6, & \tilde {n} _ {A} ^ {*} = (4 N _ {A} - 1) / 6, \end{array} \quad \begin{array}{l l} r _ {B} ^ {*} = (3 - 2 N _ {A}) / 3, & \tilde {r} _ {B} ^ {*} = (3 - 4 N _ {A}) / 3, \\ n _ {B} ^ {*} = (3 - 2 N _ {A}) / 6, & \tilde {n} _ {B} ^ {*} = (3 - 4 N _ {A}) / 6. \end{array}
$$

In Stage 1, the marginal consumer x is indiferent to choosing OSS from either firm if the following equation is satisfied.

$$
[ \beta - x ] + \lambda [ \delta x - (1 - x) - \tilde {r} _ {A} ^ {*} ] = [ \beta - (1 - x) ] + \lambda [ \delta (1 - x) - x - \tilde {r} _ {B} ^ {*} ].
$$

With two OSS, each firm can provide services to its rival firm’s users and consumers can purchase services from either firm. The marginal consumer needs to balance between the two options: adopting OSS from one firm and buying services from the other, or vice versa.<sup>8</sup> The equilibrium is summarized in Equation (9).

$$
\begin{array}{l} r _ {A} ^ {*} = r _ {B} ^ {*} = 2 / 3, \quad \tilde {r} _ {A} ^ {*} = \tilde {r} _ {B} ^ {*} = 1 / 3, \quad n _ {A} ^ {*} = n _ {B} ^ {*} = 1 / 3, \quad \tilde {n} _ {A} ^ {*} = \tilde {n} _ {B} ^ {*} = 1 / 6, \\ \pi_ {A} ^ {*} = \pi_ {B} ^ {*} = 5 / 1 8. \end{array}\tag{9}
$$

With two OSS, both firms compete in both software and services markets, but their profits are diferent compared with other market structures, as shown in Lemma 4.

Lemma 4. Suppose two OSS firms compete in the market.

(i) If the network efect is relatively strong $( \delta \ge 1 9 / 9 )$ , both firms are worse of compared with the case of two PS firms.

(ii) If the network efect is very weak $( 2 \leq \delta < 1 9 / 9 )$ , both firms are better of compared with the case of two PS firms, because they can efectively implement price discrimination.

The market outcome with two OSS firms is similar to the outcome with two PS firms, in that two diferent softwares compete with zero price and firms make profits only from services by evenly sharing the market. Unlike the case with two PS firms, however, each firm can provide services to the rival firm’s software users as well as its own software users. While a firm cannot provide diferentiated services to its software users, each firm can implement de facto price discrimination: a high price $( r _ { i } = 2 / 3 )$ to its own users and a low price $( r _ { j } = 1 / 3 )$ to the rival firm’s users. On the one hand, as long as the network efect is weak enough $( 2 \leq \delta < 1 9 / 9 )$ , this price diferentiation leads to higher profits for both firms than the case with two PS firms. If the network efect is relatively strong, on the other hand, both OSS firms are worse of because the benefit as a PS firm is larger than the benefit from implementing price discrimination as an OSS firm.

## Software Development Strategy and Social Welfare

This section analyzes firm’s software development strategy in Stage 0 by deriving a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium. It first examines the baseline case in which the development cost of PS is zero or the cost saving efect of OSS is negligible, and then examines the case of a positive development cost of PS with a sizeable cost saving efect of OSS. It also extends the baseline model to the case where a firm can provide fully compatible services to a rival firm’s PS.

## Equilibrium with Zero Development Cost of PS

As a baseline model, we first analyze the case of zero development costs of PS and OSS to illustrate the factors other than the cost that favor the OSS strategy. While the development cost of OSS is considered small relative to the cost of PS, some OSS projects incur substantial development cost. This baseline model can be applicable to the market where the cost saving efect of OSS is negligible. By comparing the profits under diferent software development strategies, we can identify two possible subgame perfect Nash equilibria: a proprietary equilibrium with two PS firms competing in both the software and services markets and a hybrid equilibrium with an OSS firm dominating the software market and an OSS firm and a PS firm competing in the services market. If the network efect is very strong $\left( \delta > 3 \right)$ , both firms always choose to develop PS and the market reaches a proprietary equilibrium. The following propositions focus on the case of $2 \leq \delta \leq 3$ to analyze more interesting cases.

## Proposition 1. Equilibrium with zero development cost of PS

(i) If the network efect is strong $( \delta \ge \delta ^ { * } )$ , the market reaches a proprietary equilibrium for service-independent software $( 0 \leq \lambda \leq \lambda ^ { * } )$ ) and a hybrid equilibrium for service-dependent software $( \lambda ^ { * } < \lambda \leq 1 )$ .

(ii) If the network efect is weak $( 2 \leq \delta < \delta ^ { * } )$ , the market reaches a hybrid equilibrium in which a single OSS dominates the software market and both firms compete in the services market.

(iii) A hybrid equilibrium leads to a higher social welfare than a proprietary equilibrium if the network efect is not as strong $( 2 \leq \delta \leq 5 / 2 )$ or software is service-dependent $( \lambda _ { 2 } \leq \lambda$ $\leq 1 )$ , where $\lambda _ { 2 } \equiv ( 2 \delta - 5 ) / ( 4 \delta - 6 ) \geq \lambda ^ { * }$

As summarized in Figure 1, Proposition 1 illustrates the role of the indirect network efect and software dependency on services in firm’s software development strategy. If the network efect is strong, a single OSS is likely to dominate the software market if the software is service-dependent, and two PS products are likely to compete if the software is service-independent. For service-dependent software, the demand for services is as large that a PS firm is willing to give up its PS to expand the consumer base in the services market. The early internet browser market could be characterized as a proprietary equilibrium in which browsers were more service-independent as a stand-alone software, while the current browser market is likely to be a hybrid equilibrium in which browsers become more service-dependent with many complementary services and applications.

If the network efect is weak, the market reaches a hybrid equilibrium in which a single OSS dominates the software market and both PS and OSS firms compete in the services market (Lemma 2). In the emerging big data market which shows a relatively weak network efect, Hadoop is the leading de facto industry standard open source platform. Microsoft abandoned its own proprietary big data processing platform, Dryad, in 2011, and switched to the Hadoop OSS platform to attract more consumers in the services market.<sup>9</sup>

While producer surplus (i.e., total industry profit) is larger under a hybrid equilibrium than under a proprietary equilibrium, consumer surplus depends on the network efect and software dependency. On the one hand, if software is service-dependent, a unified market under a hybrid equilibrium leads to a larger consumer surplus than a fragmented market under a proprietary equilibrium. On the other hand, if the network efect is strong and software is service-independent, PS firms can make more profits from a fragmented software market and consumers gain less from the services market, leading to a higher social welfare under a proprietary equilibrium. von Engelhardt and Maurer [43] argue that an OSS business model helps avoid duplication of investment and encourages firms’ R&D investment, leading to improved social welfare. This study provides alternative explanation of this argument in that social welfare can be maximized in the presence of OSS due to firm’s incentive to achieve a unified software market for higher profits in the services market.

## Equilibrium with a Positive Development Cost of PS

The previous discussion shows that if the development cost of PS is zero, the market equilibrium can be either a proprietary equilibrium or a hybrid equilibrium and the presence of OSS in a hybrid equilibrium can improve social welfare. One of the important advantages of developing OSS is the cost saving from voluntary contributions, an advantage that can be quite attractive to small firms with capital constraints [12]. This section considers a positive development cost of PS (but still zero development cost of OSS), and examines how the cost saving efect of OSS afects the market equilibrium and social welfare. Proposition 2 summarizes that an open source equilibrium with two OSS firms competing in both software and services markets can be another possible equilibrium if the development cost of PS is high (Figure 2).

## Proposition 2. Equilibrium with a positive development cost of PS

(i) If the network efect is strong $( \delta \ge \delta ^ { * } )$ and software is service-independent $( 0 \leq \lambda \leq \lambda ^ { * } )$ , the market reaches a proprietary equilibrium for $C \leq C _ { 3 }$ and an open source equilibrium for $C \geq C _ { 2 }$

(ii) If the network efect is weak $( 2 \leq \delta \leq \delta ^ { * } )$ or software is service-dependent $( \lambda ^ { * } < \lambda$ $\leq 1 )$ , the market reaches a hybrid equilibrium for $C < C _ { 1 }$ and an open source equilibrium for $C \geq C _ { 1 }$

(iii) An open source equilibrium leads to the highest social welfare as long as the development cost is larger than $( 1 8 \lambda \delta \mathrm { ~  ~ { ~ - ~ } ~ } 1 4 \lambda \mathrm { ~  ~ { ~ + ~ } ~ } 7 ) / 3 6 .$ , where $\begin{array} { r l r } { C _ { 1 } } & { { } \equiv } & { 2 / 9 } \end{array}$ $\begin{array} { r } { C _ { 2 } \equiv \frac { 1 8 \lambda ^ { 2 } \delta ^ { 3 } - 5 3 \lambda ^ { 2 } \delta ^ { 2 } + 4 8 \lambda ^ { 2 } \delta - 9 \lambda ^ { 2 } - 3 6 \lambda \delta ^ { 2 } + 7 6 \lambda \delta - 4 8 \lambda + 1 8 \delta - 1 4 } { 2 \left( 3 \lambda \delta - \lambda - 6 \right) ^ { 2 } } - \frac { 5 } { 1 8 } } \end{array}$ and $\begin{array} { r } { C _ { 3 } \equiv \frac { \delta - 1 } { 4 } - \frac { \left( 2 \lambda + 4 - \lambda \delta \right) ^ { 2 } } { 2 \left( 4 \lambda + 6 - 3 \lambda \delta \right) ^ { 2 } } } \end{array}$ , with $C _ { 1 } \leq$ $C _ { 2 } \leq C _ { 3 } ,$

![](/api/attachments/CCBPVGJH/fulltext/images/3d86a95efdf4ea1eab95fa0a68576d3f9edf07ec61046597f92d8e6f80ba2a96.jpg)  
Figure 2. Market equilibrium with positive software development cost.

## Proof. See Appendix.

A positive development cost of PS can make OSS more appealing due to its cost saving efect, and an open source equilibrium is another possible equilibrium. On the one hand, if the network efect is strong and the development cost of PS is not as high $\left( C \leq C _ { 3 } \right)$ , firms are more likely to develop PS to take advantage of consumers’ high willingness to pay for services as a monopoly provider. If the development cost of PS is higher than $C _ { 2 } ,$ on the other hand, an open source equilibrium can be reached due to the large cost saving efect of OSS. For the development cost is in the intermediate range $\left( C _ { 2 } < C \le C _ { 3 } \right)$ , the OSS firm has a strong incentive to switch to PS for a high monopoly profit from the services market, while the PS firm may want to switch to OSS to capture the significant cost saving benefit, leading to multiple equilibria.

If the network efect is weak, the cost saving efect of OSS is likely to dominate the network efect, and an open source equilibrium is more likely for a sizeable development cost $( C > C _ { 1 } )$ . For a low development cost $( C \leq C _ { 1 } )$ , the cost saving from developing OSS is not as large and firms may want to develop PS. However, the PS firm still has an incentive to avoid a fragmented software market, leading to a hybrid equilibrium with a single OSS in the software market. Both OSS and PS firms obtain the same profit from the equally shared services market, but the cost saving efect of OSS makes the OSS firm enjoy a higher overall profit than its PS rival does.

On the one hand, an open source equilibrium with two OSS firms can lead to the highest social welfare as long as the development cost is high enough. If the development cost is low, on the other hand, the cost saving efect is not large enough to ofset the reduced size of the services market, and a hybrid equilibrium with a unified software market leads to the highest social welfare. Due to the low development cost, there was proliferation of multiple, incompatible versions of the Linux kernel in the early development of Linux in the 1990s, slowing down the adoption of Linux. Later, the Open Source Initiative was founded to coordinate a compatible kernel, and firms started to focus more on complementary services [35].

## Compatible Services to PS

The baseline model assumes that a firm cannot provide services to a rival firm’s PS due to the closed nature of PS. This section considers the case of fully compatible services in which a firm can provide services to the rival firm’s PS users. Consumers have more options to buy services and an i-user’s utility from purchasing services in Stage 2 can be summarized as

$$
u _ {i 2} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c l} \delta N _ {i} - y - r _ {i} & \text { if   she   purchases   services   from   Firm } i \\ \delta N _ {i} - (1 - y) - \tilde {r} _ {i} & \text { if   Firm } i \text { is   an   OSS   firm   and   she   purchases   services   from   Firm } j \\ \theta \delta N _ {i} - (1 - y) - \tilde {r} _ {i} & \text { if   Firm } i \text { is   a   PS   firm   and   she   purchases   services   from   Firm } j \\ 0 & \text { if   she   does   not   purchase   any   services } \end{array} \right.
$$

where $\theta \in \left[ 0 , 1 \right]$ indicates the level of compatibility if the services to PS can be provided by the rival firm. For $\theta = 0 ;$ , a firm cannot provide compatible services to a rival firm’s PS and the baseline model in Proposition 1 applies to this case. If θ = 1, a firm can provide fully compatible services to a rival firm’s PS and Proposition 3 summarizes the results.

## Proposition 3. Equilibrium with fully compatible services

(i) If the network efect is very weak $( 2 \leq \delta \leq 1 9 / 9 )$ and software is service-independent (0 $\leq \lambda \leq \lambda _ { 0 } )$ , the market reaches a hybrid equilibrium in which an OSS firm competes with a PS firm in both software and services markets, where $\lambda _ { 0 } \equiv ( 1 9 - 9 \delta ) / ( 9 \delta - 3 ) < \lambda ^ { * }$

(ii) Otherwise, the market reaches a proprietary equilibrium.

(iii) A hybrid equilibrium always leads to a higher social welfare than a proprietary equilibrium.

## Proof. See Appendix.

There are two main diferences between the baseline model of incompatibility and the model of full compatibility. First, a proprietary equilibrium is more likely to dominate if firms can provide fully compatible services (Figure 3). If consumers can purchase services from any firms in Stage 2, they would worry less about the lock-in problem of PS and are more likely to purchase PS in Stage 1. Thus, firms have a strong incentive to develop and sell PS, leading to a proprietary equilibrium. Second, if the market reaches a hybrid equilibrium, the software market is fragmented with both PS and OSS firms. Since consumers are more willing to buy PS in Stage 1, a PS firm would remain in the software market by charging a positive price to its PS even when competing with an OSS firm.

A hybrid equilibrium always leads to a higher social welfare than a proprietary equilibrium does. When services are fully compatible, the coexistence of OSS and PS in the software market leads to higher consumer surplus because consumers can choose any software that fits their preference. In addition, the large consumer base in the services market enables firms to capture more profits under a hybrid equilibrium. Proposition 3 implies that while the market is more likely to reach a proprietary equilibrium, society is always better of with a hybrid equilibrium. Llanes and de Elejalde [32] propose that government should subsidize open source projects to improve social welfare, and their insight is more relevant when services are fully compatible.

## Discussion and Conclusion

The findings from this study have several managerial implications in terms of firm’s software development strategy and market intervention. We also discuss a few limitations of this study, followed by conclusion.

## Managerial implications

This study provides a few managerial and policy insights. First, the OSS development strategy by potential entrants does not always guarantee success in the market. A common perception is that small firms with financial constraints are better of developing OSS to take advantage of its cost saving efect. If the indirect network efect is very strong and software is more service-independent, however, potential entrants would be better of by developing PS to take advantage of the benefits of the indirect network efect. Developing OSS is optimal only if the network efect is weak or software is service-independent because existing PS firms may cede the software market to avoid market fragmentation.

![](/api/attachments/CCBPVGJH/fulltext/images/653efde984cb0f9d63e087893347f759655dc174cce8869c8c8c27e84d322241.jpg)  
Figure 3. Market equilibrium with full compatibility and zero software development cost.

Second, PS firms may be better of when OSS firms enter the market. Competition between PS firms is likely to lead to a prisoner’s dilemma with a fragmented market, and the availability of OSS can present a feasible way to break the deadlock. Though PS firms may lose some market share with the presence of OSS firms, they can make more profits in the services market. Microsoft’s shift toward OSS internet browser engine may illustrate its strategic decision to take advantage of the potentially profitable opportunities in the services market.

Third, when firms can provide fully compatible services to rival firms’ PS, there exists a conflict between the market equilibrium and social welfare. Government or non-profit organizations may need to intervene to align the market equilibrium with social welfare. Market fragmentation under a proprietary equilibrium may slow down consumers’ adoption of software and firms may not obtain enough profits from complementary services. Government or organizations such as the Open Source Initiative (OSI) for Linux have a role to coordinate market development by providing standardized open source licenses and simplifying access to licenses.

## Limitations

For simplicity and clarity, this study assumes the price of software is non-negative, but the consideration of a negative price, or subsidy, of software can be a possible extension. Under a strong indirect network efect, firms have an incentive to provide subsidies to increase the installed base and make more profits in the services market. This would intensify competition in the software market, and a PS firm may end up having zero profits in equilibrium [46]. Another extension can be the sequential entry game. Our model assumes that two firms simultaneously decide the type of software without observing the other firm’s decision. If an entrant determines its software development strategy after observing the incumbent’s decision, the chance of market fragmentation can be reduced and an open source equilibrium is a more likely outcome. This study also considers only an extreme case of full compatibility of services to simplify the model, but a model with continuous level of compatibility can provide richer insights.

## Conclusion

With the popularity of open source software (OSS) as an alternative to proprietary software (PS), proprietary firms started to embrace OSS in their software development strategy. With a model in which consumers sequentially purchase software and complementary services, this study analyzes firm’s software development choice and its impact on the market equilibrium and social welfare. It shows the equilibrium critically depends on the level of the indirect network efect and service dependency of software. If the cost saving efect of OSS is negligible, the market is likely to lead to a hybrid equilibrium in which a PS firm gives up the software market and competes with an OSS firm in the services market. This unified software market can also maximize social welfare. If the cost saving efect of OSS is sizeable, an open source equilibrium with two OSS is another possible equilibrium and may improve social welfare if the cost saving efect of OSS is large. If a firm can provide fully compatible services to a rival firm’s PS users, the market is more likely to reach a proprietary equilibrium while society is always better of with a hybrid equilibrium, justifying market intervention. The findings of this study would improve our understanding of the role of OSS in firm’s software development strategy and market equilibrium, such as the evolution of internet browser market during the past few decades.
