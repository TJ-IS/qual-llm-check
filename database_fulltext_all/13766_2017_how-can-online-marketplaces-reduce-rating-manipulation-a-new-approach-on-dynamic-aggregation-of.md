# How can online marketplaces reduce rating manipulation? A new approach on dynamic aggregation of online ratings

Faculty of Business Administration and Economics, University of Passau, Innstr. 43, Passau 94032, Germany

Keywords: Electronic markets Fake ratings Rating aggregation method Online fraud Simulation

## A B S T R A C T

Retailers’ incentives to manipulate online ratings can undermine consumers’ trust in online marketplaces. Finding ways to avoid fake ratings has become a fundamental problem. Most marketplaces update product ratings immediately, i.e., display new ratings as soon as they are submitted. Some platforms have proposed to reduce the frequency of rating updates, as hiding ratings for a certain amount of time allows identifying and eliminating bursts of suspicious ratings. Reducing the update frequency also allows aggregating ratings and displaying only a summary statistic (e.g., mean of ratings). Although such aggregation helps to reduce the amount of fake ratings, as multiple fake ratings get represented by only one value, it might also distort legitimate ratings from real customers and hence have negative impact on honest retailers. In the present study, we propose and evaluate a novel method that instead of displaying every new rating immediately, aggregates a sequence of most recent ratings to k-values, with k determined dynamically based on the distribution of the recent ratings. In a simulation, we demonstrate that our proposed method outperforms state-of-the-art aggregation methods – it effectively reduces the impact of fake ratings on sales, and at the same time only marginally affects sales of honest retailers. Our proposed method can be easily integrated in online rating systems and can be especially used for designing fraud-resistant ranking algorithms.

## 1. Introduction

To improve consumer shopping experience, most online marketplaces introduced online rating systems, which provide consumers with the opportunity of exchanging their opinions on sellers and purchased products (e.g., Amazon.com, eBay.com, Epinions.com, TripAdvisor.com). Numerous research suggest that online ratings significantly affect consumer purchase decisions [1–3], and, therefore, a retailer’s sales and revenue [4–6], as they help to alleviate uncertainty involved in inferring product quality [7]. Prior research also advocates that consumers consider such user-generated content to be more credible and trustworthy than traditional sources of information, like product descriptions generated by retailers [8,9].

Given this popularity of online ratings, retailers clearly have a strong incentive to manipulate ratings to boost their sales. This is particularly possible as the anonymity of online environments makes it dificult for consumers to distinguish between honest and fake content. There is ample evidence that retailers use “reputation service companies” to inflate ratings of their products. For instance, for \$ 35, buyreallike.com will provide an online retailer with 5000 “thumb-up” ratings of a promotional video clip on YouTube.com. On bayreviews.net, online retailers can obtain a perfect 5-star Amazon review for only \$ 5. In an investigation of 395,832 Amazon reviews for 1797 products in 5 Amazon product categories we found – with the online text analysis tool Fakespot.com – that on average, only around 78% of the reviews are reliable. In other words, more than 20% of the analyzed Amazon reviews are likely to be fake. This is consistent with recent studies that estimated the percentage of fake ratings to be between 15 and 30% [10,11].

Previous research indicates that online retailers especially engage in rating fraud to increase sales of products of low quality [11–14]. But the presence of fake ratings might have disastrous consequences. Fake ratings lead consumers to make suboptimal purchase decisions, and, as a result, can undermine their trust in the rating systems’ fairness. This in turn, will have a negative effect on the reputation of retailers who do not manipulate ratings of their products.

Most online marketplaces nowadays are facing the challenge of developing effective mechanisms against fake ratings. For instance, Yelp applies a proprietary filter to identify fraudulent and other suspicious ratings and reviews [15]. In 2013, Reddit changed the design of its feedback system by giving moderators an option to hide the ratings on user comments for a predetermined amount of time after their submission. The goal was to “reduce the bandwagon voting, where if a comment gets a few initial downvotes, it often continues going negative, or vice versa” [16]. E-bay has introduced a similar rule, which allows to hide for one week the negative and neutral feedback that was submitted for the seller registered on the website for at least 12 months – all in an effort to stave off fake ratings for established retailers [17]. If consumer feedback posted on TripAdvisor [18] appears suspicious to platform moderators, it can be placed on hold pending examination and can be eliminated if the platform’s proprietary filter classifies it as fake.

Hiding ratings for a certain amount of time after their submission allows identifying and subsequently eliminating bursts of suspicious ratings. However, the detection of fake ratings might be inherently problematic, as it cannot be fully excluded that undetected fake ratings penetrate the platform’s defenses, or that legitimate ratings get falsely classified as fraud. Hiding ratings for a certain amount of time, on the other hand, also allows aggregating ratings and displaying only a summary statistic (e.g., mean of ratings). Although such aggregation helps to reduce the amount of fake ratings, as multiple fake ratings get represented by only one value, it might also have the undesired effect on retailers who don’t manipulate ratings (hereafter referred to as honest retailers). It results in a decrease in the overall rating volume, which in turn might negatively affect product sales.

In the present study, we propose and evaluate a novel method that doesn’t display every rating as soon as it is posted, but rather aggregates a sequence of recently submitted ratings to k-values, with k determined dynamically based on the statistical distribution of the most recent ratings. It reduces the information loss compared to aggregating to only one value and hence doesn’t significantly distort sales of honest retailers.

Based on a simulation study, we arrive at several important results. (1) We confirm the findings of previous research [11–14] by showing that especially retailers selling products of low quality benefit from rating manipulation. However, the effectiveness of online rating manipulation varies significantly depending on the value and number of the product’s initial ratings. (2) Aggregation of several recent ratings to only one value (i.e., by calculating the median) significantly reduces sales of manipulating retailers and hence discourages them from manipulation, but at the same time, sales of honest retailers are also substantially reduced. (3) If the influence of the ratings is additionaly weighted by their age (i.e., the newer ratings are assigned more importance than others), the sales of honest retailers are reduced even more dramatically. (4) We demonstrate that our proposed method of dynamic rating aggregation outperforms state-of-the-art methods in most cases, as it substantially reduces the impact of fake ratings on sales, without significantly affecting sales of honest retailers.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on existing approaches against fake ratings and explains how our study complements existing research. Section 3 introduces current rating aggregation methods and discusses their effectiveness against fake ratings. Section 4 introduces our novel dynamic aggregation method. Section 5 describes the simulation study which investigates the effectiveness of the proposed dynamic aggregation and state-of-the-art aggregation methods by analyzing their impact on sales. Section 7 presents the results of the simulation study. The paper concludes with a discussion of the practical and research implications and outlines some directions for future research.

## 2. Related work

To deal with fake ratings, previous research has proposed various mechanisms that can be broadly grouped into two categories: first, detective mechanisms that directly detect and eliminate fake ratings, and second, mechanisms that consider different ways of aggregating ratings and re-weighting their influence.

In this study, we focus on binary online rating systems (i.e., systems that allow users to submit either a positive or a negative rating). Binary rating systems are very widespread in practice: YouTube uses “Thumbs Up” and “Thumbs Down” labels to enable users voting on videos, eBay displays merchant ratings as the total number of positive and negative transactions, Reddit allows to vote up or down on other user comments, on slickdeals.com the deal hotness reflects the difference between hot and cold votes, i.e., users who liked this specific deal and those who didn’t. Unlike textual reviews that contain detailed product information that can be considered by the consumer in their quality inferences, binary ratings generally contain little information and mostly no information about who contributed them and why. Despite its limited information content, prior research has shown that binary ratings can have a substantial influence on consumer decision-making [e.g., 19,20]. Consumers might especially rely on online ratings during the initial screening of products when they are faced with the abundance of information and seek to narrow down their search to products with an acceptable level of quality [21,22].

## 2.1. Detective mechanisms

Previous research in the domain of rating manipulation has mainly attempted to detect fake ratings by analyzing the behavior of raters. Such methods aim at identifying anomalies in raters’ behavior based on statistical analysis of past ratings (for an overview see [23]). One of such methods is the binomial Beta Reputation Defensive Mechanism (BRDM) [24,25] which is considered as state-of-the-art mechanism for detecting fake ratings. Past ratings are used to estimate the probability of how well a product is expected to be rated in the future. BRDM assumes that honest ratings follow a beta distribution, and verifies if the rater’s ratings for a given item fall between the c (lower) and 1−c (upper) percentile of a beta probability distribution of the past ratings on that item. If it is not the case, this rater is considered dishonest and is removed. Liu et al. [26] proposed a fuzzy logic mechanism which analyzes the time when a user submits a rating, the number of submitted ratings and the similarity of the user’s ratings to other existing ratings for a given item to identify how reliable this user is. Lim et al. [27] utilize scoring methods to measure the extent to which raters can be fraudulent by looking at how their ratings deviate from the ratings of others. These methods attempt to identify fraudulent raters by comparing individual ratings with the overall rating trend from other raters. Although these methods provide a statistically sound basis for detecting outlier ratings, they are only accurate when a high number of honest ratings is available. If the fake ratings are submitted before any honest ratings are available (i.e., if manipulating raters are early raters), they also won’t be detected with these methods.

A considerable amount of previous research has focused on identifying anomalous bursts in the number of ratings submitted for a single item at the same time interval [28] or by a single rater [29,30]. For instance, Wessel et al. [28] propose to detect rating fraud by locating the time intervals when an item receives more than a threefold standard deviation of the amount of ratings of a single day. Dellarocas [31] proposes a clustering algorithm to detect fraudulent raters. This method first determines the nearest “neighborhood” for each rater by including other raters who provided similar ratings on the same set of items. The raters in the neighborhood are then divided into two groups with the Macnaughton-Smith et al. [32] clustering algorithm: an upper cluster containing honest raters and a lower cluster comprising raters with fake ratings. This algorithm computes dissimilarities between the raters based on the average of their ratings for a given item or the value of their most recent ratings for that item. Although experiments have shown that this method has a good detection accuracy, it is vulnerable when manipulating raters take the similarity filtering mechanism into account and mimic the tastes of other raters in everything, except the target item.

To sum up, although detective mechanisms have been shown to accurately detect and remove some fake ratings, they don’t achieve a perfect recall (i.e., many fake ratings are not identified as such). The proposed methods are only effective as long as the ratio of manipulating raters to total raters is rather low. Last, there still exists a probability that some honest ratings can be erroneously identified as fake and get excluded.

## 2.2. Rating aggregation mechanisms

Another stream of research, particularly relevant for our study, considers different methods of rating aggregation and their robustness against fake ratings (e.g., [33–36]). Aggregation of ratings allows re-weighting the influence of each single rating, so that “unwanted” ratings get discounted. While most ratings are commonly aggregated using arithmetic mean, it is quite sensitive to extreme ratings and outliers, and might fail to convey an accurate measure of a product’s underlying quality. Existing research hence proposes calculating the median or the mode to aggregate ratings [35–37]. Other studies use a weighted average, where the weight either represents raters’ reputation based on their prior rating behavior [37–39] or the time when the rating was submitted [40]. Shapiro [41] advocates that the quality of an item might change over time, improve or deteriorate, and so the most economically eficient way to estimate sellers’ reputation is to take a time discounted average of their ratings. The time decay rate, which controls the rate at which older ratings are discounted, has therefore been widely applied in previous research. For instance, Leberknight et al. [40] propose to account for the volatility present in online ratings to better reflect the trend emerging in the latest ratings. If the average volatility of ratings is low (i.e., similar ratings across several time periods), then the discounting factor should be kept large so that more weight is put on recent ratings and a deviation in the trend $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ change in a product quality) get quickly reflected. If, however, the average volatility is high, the discounting factor should be kept low to average out any noise or sudden fluctuations.

Dellarocas [33,34] proposed instead of displaying every single rating immediately after it was submitted, rather accumulate the m most recent ratings and display only an aggregated value, i.e., sum or median of the most recent positive and negative ratings submitted over the last m transactions. This mechanism was shown to decrease the probability of fake ratings and induce higher cooperation between sellers and buyers on eBay. For instance, to avoid that sellers receive unfairly low ratings, the proposed mechanism only displays a negative rating if all ratings received over the past m transactions are also negative (i.e., all negative ratings are aggregated to one negative rating).

Lappas et al. [42] proposed to aggregate ratings into groups according to the date of their submission (i.e., by a week or month) and calculate for each group the average score and the sum of the submitted ratings. This approach then assigns a higher weight to the average ratings of the second most recent group, assuming that fake ratings are submitted in the present and hence shouldn’t be assigned the highest possible influence. Such method helps to reduce rating fraud, as multiple fake ratings in a batch get represented by one value which is then discounted at a time decay rate. However, aggregating batches of ratings and subsequently discounting older ratings significantly reduces the overall rating score, and hence makes it more vulnerable to manipulation. So, for instance, to boost the average rating value of a product with 10 positive and 10 negative ratings to 75% positivity, just 20 fake positive ratings will be suficient.

It is clearly beneficial, that aggregation mechanisms, in contrast to detection mechanisms, do not rely on the assumption that several honest ratings must be available. This makes these mechanisms robust and effective also if a product’s initial ratings are fake. There is no need to collect and analyze any further consumer or transaction data, as is, for instance, necessary when proving a consumer’s permission to submit a rating. Further, such aggregation of recently submitted ratings can be easily automated. We hence focus on the process of aggregating new ratings after their submission.

We propose a novel method that aggregates sequences of recent ratings to k-values, with k determined dynamically based on the distribution of the recent ratings. By aggregating ratings to multiple values our method overcomes the problem of a high information loss associated with an aggregation. Further, it helps to effectively reduce the negative impact of fake ratings – regardless of the time of their submission and the initial amount. In the next section, we discuss possible aggregation methods in a greater detail and fall back on their advantages and drawbacks, before we introduce our proposed dynamic aggregation method.

## 3. Aggregation of ratings

Let $R _ { j } ( t ) = \{ r _ { j t } , r _ { j t - 1 } , \ldots , r _ { j 0 } \}$ represent product j<sup>-</sup>s rating profile as a sequence $\operatorname { o f } j ^ { \prime } s$ ratings ordered according to their submission time t. As mentioned before, we focus on binary rating systems that allow rating a product either positively or negatively. Let $r _ { j } ^ { + } ( t )$ denote the number of positive ratings and $r _ { i } ^ { - } ( t )$ denote the number of negative ratings submitted till t. An exemplary rating profile that consists of 10 binary ratings with $r _ { j } ^ { + } ( t ) = 7$ and $r _ { j } ^ { - } ( t ) = 3$ is represented in Fig. 1.

New customer ratings for product j are appended to the end of $R _ { j }$ with a time stamp. Online marketplaces determine when and how new ratings are integrated to the rating profile $R _ { j } ,$ , or, in other words, when $R _ { j }$ gets updated and the new ratings get displayed. New ratings can be either displayed immediately after they were submitted (hereafter referred to as immediate update), or first collected in a cache and displayed at a later time point. If new ratings are first stored in a cache, they can be aggregated in batch before being displayed. Ratings can also be discounted, given that the quality of a product might change over time and that only recent ratings should be considered reliable for inferring quality [41]. If ratings are displayed immediately after their submission, the effect of fake ratings will be also immediate. If recently submitted ratings are first stored in a cache and displayed at a later time point, the effect of fake ratings will be quite the same, but lagged. However, storing ratings in a cache allows aggregating ratings before displaying them.

Any aggregation aims at representing a sequence of data by a single value [43]. The data sequence is in our case the sequence of cached ratings CR for product j, with $C R _ { j } ( l ) = \{ c r _ { j l } , c r _ { j l - 1 } , . . . , c r _ { j 0 } \} .$ The size l of the cache determines how long new ratings are stored in cache before being integrated into $R _ { j }$ . Note that $C R _ { j }$ might consist of positive and negative ratings but also of empty entries, as we define the cache size l in terms of time units rather than the amount of submitted ratings. The larger the size of the cache, the longer the time interval between a rating’s submission and the update of a product’s rating profile $R _ { j } .$ . To derive a single value that best represents a sequence of cached ratings, we propose using median because a sequence of ratings is best approximated by the rating type being in majority [35–37]. The aggregation of ratings decreases the adverse effect of fake ratings, as many fake ratings become represented in $R _ { j }$ by only one rating value. At the same time, such aggregation also increases the probability that honest positive (negative) ratings will get neglected, especially if the probability for a certain rating type is rather low and the size l of the cache is rather high. We will demonstrate different rating aggregation methods on an exemplary sequence $C R _ { j }$ consisting of 12 entries, with 7 positive and 3 negative ratings, and 2 empty entries as shown in Fig. 2. This sequence is best represented by 1 positive rating because the majority of ratings are positive and so is also the median. If we assume that, in our example, 7 positive ratings are fake, calculating the median appears effective, as 7 fake ratings will be represented by 1 fake rating only. But at the same time, it completely neglects 3 negative ratings that might be submitted by real customers.

Reducing the size of the cache and aggregating smaller rating sequences might help to avoid the information loss and hence overcome the problem of completely neglecting one type of ratings. However, if we aggregate sequences of 4 ratings, as also shown in Fig. 2, all negative ratings will still get “lost” if a negative rating appears only once in a sequence of 4 ratings. So, aggregation of ratings by calculating median hence reduces the negative effect of fake ratings but also results in a significant information loss.

Another possibility is to re-weight the influence of ratings by introducing a time decay factor d at which ratings are discounted $r _ { i t } ^ { \prime } = r _ { j t } \delta ^ { t }$ , with $0 \leq \delta \leq 1$ . Past ratings have almost no effect with a decay factor of $\delta \approx 0 ,$ while the effect of past ratings increases when $\delta \to 1$

Fake ratings will become less visible, the more honest ratings are submitted right after them. However, such discounting by a decay rate also decreases the influence of honest ratings that were submitted earlier. It is further necessary that consumers make use of the discounted values instead of the raw ratings. Interpreting discounted values can, however, lead to misinterpretations, as consumers might wonder why some product ratings might “disappear” over time. The rating sequence in the example above gets discounted to 4 positive (sum of positive ratings is 3.79) and 1 negative rating (sum of negative ratings is 1.27). Thus, consumers might have a misconception on how many ratings were really submitted.

## 4. New approach: dynamic rating aggregation

To reduce the probability of neglecting a certain type of ratings (i.e., positive or negative), we propose a k-value aggregation, with a sequence of l cached ratings $C R _ { j }$ represented by k values with $k < l .$ A k-value aggregation is implemented in three steps: (1) sorting all entries in a data sequence according to their value<sup>2</sup>; (2) dividing the sorted data sequence into k parts; and (3) calculating the median of each part. All median values are finally integrated into a product’s rating profile $R _ { j } .$

Suppose $k \ = \ 3 ,$ i.e., the ratings are aggregated to 3 values. The result of a three-value aggregation of our exemplary rating sequence is shown in Fig. 4. The 12 entries in the rating sequence are aggregated to a sequence with 2 positive ratings and 1 negative rating.

The probability of neglecting any type of ratings is reduced with a k-value aggregation in comparison to a one-value aggregation described in the previous section, also if the one-value aggregation is performed on rather short rating sequences (see Fig. 2). The probability that a negative rating occurs c times in a rating sequence with l entries and a probability of $q _ { j }$ that a rating is positive is given as<sup>3</sup>:

The probability that 2 out of 4 ratings are negative is $P \left( r _ { j } ^ { - } | q _ { j } \right) = 0 . 2 1 0 9$ . The lower the k the higher is the ratio of ratings of a sequence of size kl that will be neglected. A k-value aggregation might hence reduce the effect of fake positive or negative ratings and is well suited to keep more information of a rating sequence than a one-value aggregation. Nevertheless, positive (negative) ratings might also get neglected with a k-value aggregation, if the probability for a certain type of rating to occur is rather low. The probability that all negative ratings will be neglected is depicted in Table 1 for some exemplary product qualities $q _ { j }$ and different aggregation methods.

The higher the probability that a product will be rated positively (negatively), the larger the size k of an aggregation must be so that negative (positive) ratings that occur with a low probability don’t get neglected (see Figs. 2 and 4 for an example).

However, at the same time, if the majority of ratings in the rating sequence is fake, setting a larger k might also have a reverse effect, as the fake ratings are then represented by multiple values. If we again assume that 7 positive ratings in the sequence illustrated in Fig. 2 are fake, setting $k = 4$ will result in representation of the sequence by 3 fake positive ratings. Thus, it is critical to define the proper k to sustain a trade-off between reducing the amount of fake ratings and retaining as much honest content as possible.

Thus, we propose that k is determined dynamically for each product j according to the distribution of positive and negative ratings in $C R _ { j } : k _ { j } = f ( r _ { i } ^ { + } , r _ { i } ^ { - } )$ with $k \in \{ 1 , 2 , 3 \}$ . We suggest that function f should set $k _ { j } = 3 ^ { ^ { \prime } } ( \mathrm { i } . \mathsf { e } .$ , three-value aggregation), if both types of ratings are approximately uniformly distributed $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , r _ { i } ^ { + } / r _ { i } ^ { - } \approx 1 )$ . In other words, the ratings should be aggregated with a small information loss, which is achieved by $k _ { j } = 3$ in the case that a huge amount of positive or negative ratings might get lost. If there are, however, substantially more positive (negative) than negative (positive) ratings in $C R _ { j } ,$ , we suggest that function f should set $k _ { j } = 1$ because (1) only a small amount of negative (positive) ratings will get lost in this case and (2) recent research indicates that fake ratings often are submitted in a bulk [28], so that fake ratings will more likely occur in a sequence with substantially more positive (negative) than negative (positive) ratings. As function f we hence propose:

$$
k _ {j} = \lfloor 3 - \left(| 2 \bar {C R} _ {j} |\right) \rfloor\tag{5}
$$

Here, $\bar { C R _ { j } }$ describes the expected value or mean of the ratings in $C R _ { j }$ . This value is a real number in [−1, 1], so that $3 - \left( | 2 C \bar { \cal R } _ { j } | \right)$ is an integer in [1, 3]. The higher the absolute mean of ratings in $\acute { C } R _ { j }$ , the higher the probability that a certain type of ratings will predominate in $C R _ { j } ,$ , and hence the lower the aggregation parameter $k _ { j }$ should be. Fig. 5 depicts the value of $k _ { j }$ in dependence of ${ \bar { C R } } _ { j } .$ Since an aggregation to only 1 value is subject to a large information loss, we set $k = 1$ if the expected absolute value of $C R _ { j }$ is high and thus also the probability that $C R _ { j }$ represents a series of solicited ratings. The aggregation parameter is set $k _ { j } = 3$ if the expected value is between −0.25 and 0.25. In all other case, $k _ { j } = 2$ , as it is rather unlikely that the sequence CR includes fake ratings, but there is still a chance that some ratings might be fake.

Our proposed dynamic aggregation method provides three major advantages: First, the probability that especially ratings occurring very rarely will get neglected is low compared to a one-value aggregation. Second, whereas the number of aggregation values is product-specific (i.e., based on a product’s rating distribution), the displaying frequency is kept constant over all products. Last, our approach can be easily implemented in online rating systems and doesn’t require any additional customer or product information.

## 5. Simulation

In a simulation, we analyze the effectiveness of the aggregation methods introduced in Sections 3 and 4 against fake ratings. We turn to simulation because of the following reasons. First, the proposed methods are dificult to represent analytically, however, they can be easily implemented in a simulation. Second, in a real setting, it is hard to identify which ratings are fake and which are honest, making an analysis based on empirical data dificult. Although a considerable amount of previous research has established different proxies for identifying fake ratings [11,13,28,42], the estimation task is very problematic, as it cannot be fully excluded that fake ratings will not be detected or that honest ratings might be falsely classified as fake. Third, we have to investigate a sequence of interdependent purchase decisions that consumers make observing products’ ratings, and with it changes in products’ sales over time, which will be dificult using randomized experiments. And fourth, in a simulation, the effect of factors other than customer ratings (e.g., product pictures, product experience) on sales can be kept constant across all consumers. As a consequence differences in terms of the number of sales between different simulation scenarios and between different aggregation methods are merely a result of the customer ratings and the applied aggregation methods.

We simulate an online marketplace in which we analyze the effects of different rating aggregation methods on product sales. The proposed methods are only effective if they reduce additional sales generated as a result of soliciting fake ratings, without significantly distorting sales of retailers that do not manipulate ratings. It is hence also important to assess the effectiveness of the proposed methods if none or just one retailer solicits fake ratings. In the following subsections, we describe the simulation design and provide an overview of the simulated scenarios.

## 5.1. Simulation design

## 5.1.1. Rating aggregation methods

We compare our proposed dynamic k-aggregation method with the methods discussed in Sections 3 and 4: immediate update, discounting of ratings, one-value aggregation and three-value aggregation. All methods operate on binary ratings: consumers can either submit a positive or a negative rating or refuse to rate a purchased product<sup>6</sup>.

An immediate update of ratings (i.e., ratings are displayed immediately after being submitted) is the most widespread method currently used in practice. It acts as a benchmark in our simulation. The second method discounts older ratings at a rate of $\delta = 0 . 9 9 . \mathrm { A }$ positive rating (i.e., a rating with a value of $r _ { j t } = 1 )$ is devalued to 0.904 after 10 and to 0.366 after 100 simulation rounds. Ratings are discounted at a rather low rate of 1% to avoid an overweighting of recent ratings in comparison to older ratings.

The third algorithm aggregates ratings every $l \ = \ 1 2$ rounds (independently of the number of ratings submitted within these 12 rounds) to exactly one value by calculating the median. In practice, $l = 1 2$ can refer to 12 time units (e.g., 12 days or 12 h depending on the product characteristics and its popularity, as well as specifics of the platform). This method is referred to as one-value aggregation thereafter. It represents the simplest method for aggregating ratings before integrating them into the rating profile $R _ { j }$ of product $j ^ { 7 }$ . We choose $l = 1 2$ , as the sequence of 12 simulation rounds can be divided by 2 or 3 values without a remainder. We additionally tested a one-value aggregation with $l = 4$ in order to compare a three-value aggregation with a one-value aggregation of the same aggregation rate (12 values will be aggregated to 3). We also tested other values of l to examine if the proposed aggregation methods are still effective when the ratings are aggregated more (or less) frequently. The results are presented in Section 7.3.

As discussed above, a one-value aggregation is subject to a high information loss, especially if a certain rating type (i.e., positive or negative rating) occurs with a rather low probability. Our fourth method is a k-value aggregation that aggregates ratings every l = 12 simulation rounds to $k \ = \ 3$ values, i.e., three-value aggregation. Finally, we aggregate ratings every l = 12 simulation rounds applying a dynamic aggregation method where k is determined based on Eq. (5).

## 5.1.2. Purchase process in an online marketplace

To examine the effects of different rating aggregation methods on product sales, we model $I = 1 2 0 0$ consumers making purchase decisions in an online marketplace that applies one of the methods described in Section 5.1.1. There are hence 100 sequences with size $l \ = \ 1 2$ that are aggregated using a one-value, a three-value or a dynamic aggregation method. We analyze a market with two competing retailers that sell substitutable products $j \in \{ A , B \}$ at compara ble prices. To avoid confounding effects of price and product features, we assume that the differences in the products’ prices and features are negligible, and consumers don’t incorporate this information in their purchase decisions. The products are, however, different with respect to their quality (e.g., their failure rate). We assume that one of the retailers engages in rating manipulation, while the other only displays ratings from real customers.

![](/api/attachments/CKW9CTM9/fulltext/images/be708a07ac4c42d11e964e8267d1d53fb71058396a782fc0be00ca2e7f70a39d.jpg)  
Fig. 5. Function f.

We model a burn-in phase in which a specific number of ratings (depending on the simulated scenario) is submitted to the marketplace and aggregated using one of the aggregation methods. The simulation starts when one of the two retailers solicits fake ratings before each of the 1200 consumers makes her decision. Each of the $T = 1 2 0 0$ rounds models the purchase decision of exactly one of the $I = 1 2 0 0$ consumers. In each round $t \in ( 1 , \ldots , T )$ a consumer i either purchases one of the two competing products A or B, or makes no purchase at all. A consumer’s choice (in round t) is solely based on the products’ ratings, $r _ { A }$ and $r _ { B }$ respectively, submitted prior to t. If consumer i decides to purchase one of the products, she submits a rating for the product with a certain probability. The value of the rating depends on the product’s quality. When and how a rating is integrated into a product’s rating profile $R _ { j }$ is determined by the online marketplace. Ratings that have been effectively integrated into $R _ { j }$ will affect subsequent purchases of product j. At the end of each simulation round, a consumer has made a purchase decision, and if she has bought one of the two products, also a decision whether to submit a rating. We compare how often the products were purchased under given aggregation methods to analyze (i) the effectiveness of each method against fake ratings (ii) the effect on an honest retailer (given that the other retailer solicits fake ratings), and (iii) the effect on retailers if none of them solicits fake ratings. We now discuss each actor (retailers, consumers, and online marketplace) in more detail and describe the parameters characterizing the actors in the simulation.

5.1.2.1. Retailers. The competing retailers offer products that are characterized by three parameters: price, quality and online ratings. As mentioned above, for the sake of tractability, we assume that the retailers set homogeneous prices for their products. We further assume that the underlying quality of each product is not observable prior to a product’s purchase and can only be determined upon the product’s consumption. The product’s quality can be, however, inferred from its corresponding ratings provided that all ratings are honest and not fake. Each honest rating is a Bernoulli trial with a probability to be positive that is equal to the products true quality $q _ { j }$ with $q _ { j } \in [ 0 ,$ 1]. However, a retailer soliciting fake ratings for the own or the competitor’s product aims at soliciting ratings strategically to maximize sales. It means that a retailer will give the highest possible number of positive ratings to its own product or the highest possible number of negative ratings to the competitor’s product. The sales will, however, decline, as soon as the information about the retailer inflating ratings of products becomes public.

We assume a scenario where retailer A solicits positive ratings for the own product to boost additional sales<sup>8</sup>. Retailer $B ,$ in contrast, doesn’t engage in rating manipulation. As a result, the rating distribution of retailer B<sup>-</sup>s product converges to the real product quality $q _ { B } ,$ whereas the rating distribution of retailer A<sup>-</sup>s product is systematically biased by fake positive ratings. We assume that retailer $A$ solicits for its product $s _ { A }$ fake ratings, with $s _ { A } \in$ $\{ 0 { \% } , 5 0 { \% } , 1 5 0 { \% } , 2 0 0 \% , 2 5 0 \% , 3 0 0 \% \}$ of the product’s initial ratings. This means that $\mathrm { i } \mathbf { f } s _ { A } = 5 0 \%$ , for instance, a product with $r _ { A } ^ { + } = 1$ and $r _ { A } ^ { - } = 9$ will additionally get $0 . 5 r _ { A } \ = \ 5$ positive ratings. The impact of fake ratings $s _ { A }$ depends on the amount of ratings product A initially had before the fake ratings were solicited. This allows comparing the effectiveness of each aggregation method across a variety of settings where products are described by the distinct number and value of ratings.

5.1.2.2. Consumers. At each purchase round t a consumer i makes a purchase decision. Consumer i<sup>-</sup>s decision includes whether to buy and which product to buy. The probability of choosing a product j at time t is determined based on its expected quality $\hat { q } _ { j t } ,$ which is assumed to be a vertically differentiated characteristic [44] – that is, the better the expected quality of a product, the higher its value to a consumer. Since consumers are not able to assess a product’s true quality $q _ { j }$ prior to purchase, they infer its expected quality $\hat { \boldsymbol { q } } _ { j t }$ from the corresponding ratings $r _ { j t }$ displayed in product $j ^ { \prime } s$ rating profile $R _ { j }$ at time t. It is assumed that consumers consider all ratings in a product’s rating profile but they are not able to distinguish between the honest and fake content. A product with a more positive rating profile (i.e., higher average rating) is more likely to be purchased than a product with a lower average rating, as the former signals a higher underlying quality [45]. Previous research provides evidence that consumers also consider the total number of a product’s ratings when making quality inferences [12,46]. So, we assume that the probability for each product j to be chosen by consumer i is determined by its expected quality $\hat { q } _ { j t }$ at time t that is inferred based on the average rating value and rating count of the product’s rating profile $R _ { j } .$ Several studies provide evidence that human decision makers are typically risk-minimizers [47]. This means they have more confidence in the quality of a product that has received 100 ratings than in the quality of a product with a single positive rating. Thus, we propose that the lower bound on 95% confidence interval for a product’s rating profile best approximates the product’s expected quality. Since, we model binary ratings $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ sequences of positive and negative ratings), we need to compute the binomial confidence interval, also known as Wilson Score $\left[ 4 7 \right]$ for a product’s rating profile $R _ { j } ^ { 9 }$ . It is then straightforward to model the selection among products during the purchase decision process: the product with the higher average rating and rating count, and hence higher Wilson score, will have a higher probability to be selected by consumers.

In order to model rationally bounded consumers [48], we assume that there is also a probability that a consumer will purchase a product of an inferior quality, as conveyed by an inferior rating profile and lower Wilson score. So, each product is purchased with a probability of $\hat { q } _ { j } ( t ) / 2$ at time t. More precisely, the product’s expected quality $\hat { q } _ { j } ( t )$ at time t is divided by 2, since we model two products. If both products have an ideal expected quality of $\hat { q } _ { j } ( t ) = 1$ , then each is purchased with a probability of 50%. Because consumers in our simulation might purchase the product with the worse rating, we model a correlation between sales and ratings, but not a perfect correlation.

5.1.2.3. Online marketplace. The online marketplace manages the rating profiles of all products. It controls when and how ratings are aggregated, when and how a product’s rating profile is updated (i.e., ratings are displayed publicly) and implements the rating system’s cache for each product. The online marketplace further decides which rating aggregation method should be applied. In our simulation, we investigate the effectiveness of several rating aggregation methods and hence adjust the implementation of the online marketplace accordingly. Fig. 6 summarizes the simulation procedure.

## 5.1.3. Simulation scenarios

To test the effectiveness of the proposed dynamic aggregation method and to compare it with other methods, we examine how much additional sales a manipulating retailer can generate under a particular method. The less additional sales (relative to the scenario with honest ratings) are generated, the more effective is the given method against fake ratings, leading to a waste of manipulation effort.

The effectiveness of an aggregation method might vary significantly depending on the monetary benefit of a manipulation. Fake ratings might boost sales of a product with mainly negative ratings to a greater extent than sales of a product with mainly positive ratings. In the latter case, there is already enough evidence that the product has an outstanding quality, so that adding fake positive ratings might only have a limited impact on its sales. We hence examine the effectiveness of different aggregation methods by simulating scenarios in which retailers sell products at a different quality (10% or 90% positivity) and with distinct rating counts in the burn-in phase (10 ratings and 100 ratings). The simulation scenarios are illustrated in Table 2 with respect to the number of positive and negative ratings in the burn-in phase.

In all scenarios, retailer A solicits fake positive ratings to compete against honest retailer B whose products are characterized by the same average rating value and rating count. In the absence of fake ratings and aggregation mechanisms, both retailers will generate the same amount of sales. This scenario enables us to directly analyze the benefit of rating manipulation by comparing the amount of sales of a manipulating retailer with the sales of an honest retailer.

## 6. Analysis and results

To ensure robust results, we ran 1000 replications of the simulation for each rating aggregation method, while varying the amount of fake ratings (i.e., solicitation level) and initial rating distribution. The results are plotted in Fig. 7. The plots depict sales of the two competing products; with A representing the product for which additional positive ratings were solicited, and B representing the product with honest ratings. Such alongside graphical representation allows to analyze the effects of different aggregation methods on sales of retailer B who doesn’t engage in manipulation – that is, if sales of retailer B are also affected and in the worst case reduced. Finally, the consideration of a solicitation level of $s _ { A } = 0$ (no solicitation) allows analyzing the effect of different aggregation methods when none of the retailers solicits fake ratings.

First, we analyze scenarios in which both retailers sell products characterized by the same quality and hence average rating value and the same rating count. In the absence of fake positive ratings, both products will yield the same amount of sales (see the dashed line in the plot) if the ratings are updated immediately by an online marketplace (i.e., if none of the aggregation methods is applied). As Fig. 7 shows, fake ratings will especially increase sales of products of low quality. These products will be sold very rarely, unless additional positive ratings are solicited for them. Given that sales will monotonically increase with the average rating value and rating count, the optimal strategy would be to give the highest possible amount of positive ratings, as long as the costs of solicitation of each additional rating do not exceed the marginal profit. Fig. 7a shows that adding just 10 fake positive ratings (i.e., s = 100%) increases retailer A<sup>-</sup>s sales by more than 750% if both products have initially 10 ratings, 9 of which are negative and ratings are aggregated immediately (aggregation method “Immediate”)<sup>10</sup>. However, each additional fake positive rating boosts sales at a decreasing rate. So, adding 20 fake positive ratings $( \mathrm { i . e . , } s _ { A } = 2 0 0 \% )$ increases retailer A<sup>-</sup>s sales by only 1150% if both products have initially 10 ratings, 9 of which are negative. After purchasing the product, consumers are able to experience its true quality. After realizing that they were fooled by fake positive ratings, they will start to submit negative ratings to express their dissatisfaction, which in turn will affect the consequent product sales.

The effect of fake ratings is not that pronounced if both retailers are selling products of high quality. Adding 10 fake positive ratings increases retailer A<sup>-</sup>s sales by only 3.5%, if both retailers are selling a product with 9 positive and 1 negative ratings (Fig. 7b, method “Immediate”). Adding fake ratings for products with an initially large amount of negative ratings also has a limited impact on sales. So, adding 100 positive ratings increases sales by about 600% if both retailers start with 10 positive and 90 negative ratings, which is significantly lower than in the case where retailers start with 1 positive and 9 negative ratings (Fig. 7c, method “Immediate”). At the same time, we find an increase in sales by nearly 6% in the case where both retailers start with 90 positive and 10 negative ratings and 100 positive ratings are added (Fig. 7d, method “Immediate”). Clearly, both the amount and average value of existing ratings affect manipulation effectiveness. For products with a large amount of positive ratings, adding fake ratings doesn’t substantially improve sales, leading to a waste of manipulation effort.

The sales of honest retailer B do not change if retailer A solicits fake positive ratings and they are immediately updated after their submission (i.e., none of the aggregation methods is applied). Given that the expected quality of retailer B<sup>-</sup>s product doesn’t change with the manipulation level of retailer A, its purchase probability will also be the same.

Fig. 7 demonstrates that discounting by rating age significantly diminishes the effect of fake ratings (Fig. 7), method “Decay”. An increase in sales caused by adding fake positive ratings is not as pronounced as when ratings are updated immediately after their submission. For instance, adding 100 positive ratings for a product with 10 negative and 90 positive ratings will increase sales by only

92% if the decay rate is d = 0.99, which is substantially lower than in case of an immediate update (Fig. 7c), “Decay”). Discounting ratings by their age is thus an effective instrument for reducing manipulation in settings where all retailers in the market are offering products of low quality. However, such discounting of ratings results in a general reduction of sales – that is, sales of retailers who don’t inflate ratings of their products also decrease. For instance, sales of product B with 90 positive and 10 negative ratings decrease by nearly 18%. By reducing the importance of older ratings, this method reduces the overall number of visible ratings that contribute to a consumer’s decision which product to buy. Online marketplaces should be cautious when discounting users’ ratings by their age, and especially choosing the appropriate decay rate.

A one-value aggregation significantly diminishes the effect of fake ratings, especially if the product for which fake ratings are solicited is characterized by low average rating values (Fig. 7, method “One-Value Aggregation (l = 12)”). Fake positive ratings are likely to get neglected during the aggregation, i.e., get represented by only one rating. However, one-value aggregation is not as effective as discounting by rating age when many fake ratings are solicited for a product with a large amount of negative ratings. So, adding 100 positive ratings for a product with 10 positive and 90 negative ratings will increase sales by 225% if a one-value aggregation is applied, which is far below the percentage increase in case of an immediate update, but about 240% higher than in case of discounting by rating age (Fig. 7c, method “One-Value aggregation (l = 12)”). Similar to discounting by age (i.e., overall reduction of the amount of visible ratings), one-value aggregation leads to a significant decrease of sales of honest retailers, especially when products are characterized by a high number of ratings. Sales of an honest retailer B offering a product with 90 positive ratings and a total of 100 ratings fall by nearly 25%.

Aggregation of ratings to three values helps to partially alleviate the negative effect that one-value aggregation exhibits on sales of retailers who don’t engage in manipulation. Aggregating ratings to more values helps to reduce the information loss and hence overcome the problem of reducing the number of honest ratings. So, when a three-value aggregation is applied, the sales reduction of retailer B offering a product with 90 positive and 10 negative ratings will be less than 5%, far below the percentage decrease in case of discounting by rating age and one-value aggregation of l = 12 values (Fig. 7c, method “Three-value aggregation”). However, it is not as effective as the latter two in mitigating the effect of fake ratings.

Reducing the size of cache and aggregating rating sequences every 4 rounds (l = 4 values) result in a lower reduction of retailer A<sup>-</sup>s sales as compared to a three-value aggregation. The retailer B<sup>-</sup>s sales are only marginally affected. So, at a solicitation level of $s _ { A } = 3$ retailer A generates approximately 10% more sales with a one-value aggregation of l = 4 values compared to a three-value aggregation of l = 12 values when products are characterized by both low average rating value and low rating count (see Fig. 7a). If both products are characterized by high average rating value and high rating count, retailer B’s sales decrease by only 3% when implementing a onevalue aggregation of l = 4 values, but they stay at the same level when using a three-value aggregation (see Fig. 7d). The lower information loss of a three-value compared to a one-value aggregation (see Section 4) is the reason for both effects.

In summary, aggregating sequences of ratings to one value helps reducing the negative impact of fake ratings on sales. Such onevalue aggregation, however, has the disadvantage that the sales of honest retailers will be also affected, as the amount of visible ratings decreases. Aggregating ratings to multiple k values effectively reduces additional sales generated as a result of rating manipulation while only marginally affecting sales of honest retailers. This is due to the lower information loss of a k-compared to a one-value aggregation. Finally, a dynamic determination of k further helps to reduce the impact of fake ratings on sales. Although sales of manipulating retailers are reduced most significantly if older ratings are discounted at a rate $\delta = 0 . 9 9$ . However, the sales of honest retailers in that case also dramatically decline, especially if their products are of high quality.

In the next section, we will conduct several robustness checks in order to show that the aforementioned findings are robust to changes in (i) the behavior of manipulating retailers (i.e., submission of fake negative ratings for a competitor’s product and frequent submission of fake ratings), (ii) the implementation of a rating aggregation method by the marketplace (i.e., varying number of values that will be aggregated), and (iii) the consumers’ behavior (i.e., different value function).

## 7. Robustness checks

## 7.1. The impact of fake negative ratings

Soliciting fake positive ratings for the own product is economically beneficial for a retailer, as it helps to increase sales. However, self-boosting manipulation of products might not always yield the highest monetary benefit. Especially, if manipulating retailers are offering products of a rather high quality, as conveyed by a large amount of positive ratings. In that case adding fake positive ratings to the own product might have a limited impact on sales. Instead, it might be more beneficial to solicit fake negative ratings for the competing product. In fact, this manipulation strategy (also known as bad-mouthing of competitor products) is very widespread in practice and has received considerable attention in previous research (e.g., [12,13,33,42]). In the following, we examine whether and to what extent the proposed aggregation methods are effective against such a bad-mouthing strategy. We exemplarily analyze two scenarios, where the competing products are characterized by a high quality of $q _ { j } = 0 . 9$ and either a low (10 ratings) or high rating count (100 ratings).

Fig. 8a demonstrates that adding 10 fake negative ratings for a competing product B significantly reduces its sales (about 18%) and this decrease is even higher, if B features a total of 100 ratings (about 35%). A decreasing monotonic relationship exists between the number of fake negative ratings and sales. Adding many fake ratings for a competitor’s product can benefit a retailer by both increasing its sales and driving the competitor out of the market, as long as the retailer’s products are characterized by high ratings.

As Fig. 8 shows, one-value aggregation becomes increasingly ineffective in mitigating the impact of fake negative ratings. The reason is that due to a high aggregation level (i.e., multiple ratings are aggregated to one value), it reduces the overall number of ratings that contribute to the average score making it more vulnerable to manipulation. So, to reduce the average score to 0.5, it would be suficient to add 65 fake negative ratings to a product with initially 100 ratings, 90 of which are positive. If the same product has only 50 ratings, it would be suficient to add only 30 negative ratings to reduce its average score.

Dynamic k-value aggregation helps to alleviate the problem of information loss, however, its impact is limited as well, especially if the product features plenty of negative ratings. So, adding 100 fake negative ratings to a product with 90 positive and 10 negative ratings will decrease its sales by almost 30% when a dynamic aggregation is applied, which is only by 12% lower than in case when ratings are updated immediately (Fig. 8b, method “Dynamic Aggregation”). The decrease is even more dramatic, when 300 negative ratings are added. The sales decrease by more than 60%. On the contrary, discounting ratings by their age effectively reduces the adverse effect of bad-mouthing. However, this method results in an overall reduction of sales, regardless of the solicitation strategy of a competing retailer.

## 7.2. Increasing the frequency of fake ratings

We assumed that a retailer solicits fake ratings only once. This might be somewhat unrealistic, since soliciting fake ratings more frequently might be more effective for increasing sales or driving competitors out of the market. We therefore additionally examined the effectiveness of the aggregation methods, if retailer A frequently solicits positive ratings. Fig. 9a shows the results for the case where retailer A solicits positive ratings every 400 rounds (three times in the simulation). And Fig. 9b shows the results for the case where retailer A solicits fake ratings every 120 rounds (ten times in the simulation). Both plots are generated for the case that retailer A and B offer a product with low quality and already got a few ratings.

The higher the frequency with which retailer A solicits fake positive ratings, the higher the percentage increase in sales. Adding 10 positive ratings for a product with 1 positive and 9 negative ratings every 120 rounds increases sales by nearly 1600% (Fig. 9b, method “Immediate”), which is considerably higher than the sales increase of approximately 1200% in a scenario where ratings are solicited three times (Fig. 9a, method “Immediate”), and much higher than the sales increase of 750% in a scenario where ratings are solicited only once in the simulation (Fig. 7a, method “Immediate”). Discounting by rating age becomes extremely ineffective, when the frequency with which fake ratings are solicited increases. Given that retailer A solicits ratings more frequently, product A<sup>-</sup>s most recent ratings will be positive, and therefore the ratings displayed to consumers will also be mostly positive, which in turn will result in a greater increase of sales. Fig. 9 also demonstrates the ineffectiveness of both a one-value of l = 4 values (sales reduction of retailer B) and a three-value aggregation method (weak compensation of the negative effect of fake ratings). A dynamic k-value rating aggregation is, in contrast, the most effective method in mitigating manipulation.

## 7.3. Varying number of rounds for aggregation of ratings

The effectiveness of a dynamic aggregation might depend on the frequency at which ratings are aggregated (i.e., whether the interval between the two aggregations is long or rather short). We therefore also analyzed the effects of different aggregation methods by varying size l of the cache. Fig. 10a shows the results for the case where ratings are aggregated every l = 6, and Fig. 10b shows the results for the case where ratings are aggregated every l = 24 rounds. Both plots depict the scenario in which both retailers offer products with a low quality and start with only a few ratings (i.e., 1 positive and 9 negative ratings).

As Fig. 10 shows, increasing the size l of the cache (i.e., reducing the frequency with which ratings are aggregated) reduces the effect of fake ratings on sales. Our proposed method outperforms other rating aggregation methods, as it effectively mitigates the impact of fake ratings (the sales almost reach the initial level at which no manipulation occurs) and at the same time the sales of the honest retailer are only moderately reduced. However, online marketplaces should be cautious when choosing the appropriate size of the cache, as too long intervals between the updates of a product’s rating profile lead to a decrease in the overall amount of ratings, so that honest negative and positive ratings from real customers might get neglected. Sales of retailer B, for example, decrease by nearly 15% when its product features 9 positive and 1 negative ratings, and ratings are aggregated every $l = 2 4$ rounds using a dynamic k-value aggregation. This in turn has especially negative consequences for retailers that do not engage in rating manipulation. As no ratings means no sales, and no sales means no ratings from honest customers.

## 7.4. Alternative value function for consumer’s purchase probabilities

We additionally tested an alternative value function for modeling consumers’ purchase probabilities. Specifically, we assume that the probability for each product j to be chosen by consumer i is determined by its expected quality $\hat { q } _ { j } ( t )$ at time t that is inferred based on the product j<sup>-</sup>s rating profile $R _ { j } ( t )$ The higher the expected quality qˆ , the higher the purchase probability for the respective product. Following previous works, we express that a product’s expected quality is inferred based on a product’s average rating and the log of a product’s rating count [5,49,50]. Formally, $\hat { q } _ { j t } = \log ( z / ( 1 - z ) )$ with $z = \bar { R _ { j t } } \log ( | R _ { j t } | )$ , where $\bar { R _ { j t } }$ denotes the average score of the product’s rating profile at time t, and $| R _ { j t } |$ the number of ratings in a product’s rating profile at time t.

In this study, we show that existing methods of rating aggregation that are currently considered state-of-the-art [33–36], are subject to at least one of the following two problems: First, they are not effective in mitigating the deleterious effects of fake ratings. And second, since they reduce the overall rating count, they have negative consequences for retailers that do not engage in rating manipulation. In particular, discounting of older ratings results in a reduction of the total amount of visible ratings that contribute to consumers’ evaluation of product quality. Further, such practice of assigning greater importance to the most recent batches of ratings, might favor fake ratings, especially if they are solicited rather frequently. In line with [35], we show that aggregating ratings to one value significantly reduces the negative impact of fake ratings, as multiple fake ratings get represented by only one value (i.e., median rating). In addition our study also demonstrates that a one-value aggregation negatively affects honest retailers, as a decrease in total amount of ratings inhibits a decrease in sales. Our study also shows that aggregating ratings to k values instead of one value – as being state-of-the-art [33–36] – helps to reduce the information loss by improving the probability that the type of ratings that occurs very rarely (i.e., positive (negative) rating in case of low (high) product quality) will get neglected. However, such k-value aggregation, on the other hand, also boosts the effect of fake ratings – as fake ratings get represented by multiple values. The similar effect is obtained when ratings are aggregated more frequently, i.e., reducing the size l of the cache also favors fake ratings. We thus propose an aggregation to k values, with k determined dynamically based on whether the probability for a certain type of ratings to get neglected is high or rather low.

Our method has valuable implications for retailers and online rating platform operators. First, our method can be easily integrated in online rating systems to enhance their reliability and provide a more trustworthy measure of how a product was evaluated by previous customers. The proposed method on dynamic rating aggregation can be especially applied for the implementation of fraud-resistant ranking algorithms. At the core of each ranking algorithm is the aggregation of new ratings into a single score which is used to establish the ordering of products. Adding multiple fake ratings can easily alter the ranking position of products and, consequently, significantly distort a retailer’s sales and revenue. So, to improve ranking functions and obtain more robust ranking scores, online platforms can adopt our method on dynamic rating aggregation. Honest retailers will benefit from our proposed method as the impact of fake ratings of competitors will be reduced and their own sales will be only marginally affected. Further, our method will help consumers to avoid purchasing products of low quality.

From a methodical perspective, we provide a simulation model for analyzing the effects of fake ratings on retailers’ revenue. In addition to informing online marketplace about the effectiveness of different aggregation mechanisms against fake ratings, our analysis can also inform retailers about the benefits of different manipulation strategies. Our simulation model can be extended to analyze more complex market scenarios in which multiple retailers compete against each other.

Our research is in line with the concept of value co-destruction that emerged recently as an important way to conceptualize negative outcomes of actor-to-actor interactions [11,51]. We show that fake ratings destroy value for several actors: they jeopardize the credibility and competitiveness of online marketplaces, they mislead consumers’ decision making and they endanger the reputation, image and sales potential of retailers. The value of ratings is thus reduced in the presence of fake ratings. Our study shows that this value reduction is especially pronounced if (1) the manipulating retailer offers a product at low quality, (2) ratings are immediately added to a product’s rating score, and (3) fake ratings are solicited frequently.

Our study is subject to some limitations. First, we did not take the product price into account and assumed substitutable products with comparable prices. Consumers’ purchase decisions are strongly affected by price – that is, a high product price can attenuate the effect of fake ratings, whereas a low price, on the contrary, might boost it. We, however, do not expect that the differences found between the examined aggregation methods will significantly change. Second, we compared the effects of different aggregation methods, but did not examine the absolute effect of any method on sales. The amount of absolute sales presented in the study is based on the assumptions of the simulation. Since the assumptions are kept constant over the examined scenarios, we expect that the relative changes across the aggregation methods will be robust to absolute changes in sales. Third, we analyzed scenarios with only two retailers and assumed that consumers consider both products before making a purchase decision. The effectiveness of the proposed aggregation methods most likely depends on the ratio of retailers soliciting fake ratings. Examining the effects of fake ratings on sales in scenarios with multiple retailers engaging in rating manipulation hence provides an interesting avenue for future research. Fourth, as we used a simulation to evaluate our proposed method, consumers are modeled with a static behavior. They do not adapt their behavior with regard to situational factors, such as their mood or information from other persons. We further assumed that consumers are not able to distinguish between honest and manipulated ratings. Several platforms, such as Youtube or eBay, allow their customers to not only provide a rating for products or services, but also to provide a textual customer review. These customer reviews might help consumers to detect manipulated ratings, which are not considered in our simulation. As a consequence, the impact of manipulated ratings might be lower than expected, however, the differences between the investigated aggregation methods stay the same.

We simulated the way in which fake ratings were submitted. It would be meaningful to evaluate aggregation methods also on a set of true ratings in which fake and honest ratings are known with certainty. Although several studies use a data set of fake and honest ratings [27,52,53], none of them is based on a dataset of real customer ratings that are classified with certainty into fake and honest ratings.

Further, we found that our proposed method has a limited impact on reducing rating fraud when a retailer posts a large amount of negative ratings to its competitor. Interestingly, recent evidence suggests that it is easier and less risky to affect overall ratings by adding fake positive ratings for the own product than undermining competitors [54]. A possible explanation is that, businesses caught soliciting fake ratings for their competitors can face serious backlashes, such as lawsuits and long-term damage of their reputation. Further, the strategy of posting defamatory ratings for the rivals doesn’t necessarily promise to improve sales of the own products, especially if those have a large amount of negative ratings.

Using multiple methods and combining their outcomes is a meaningful approach in many application areas and typically called ensemble method. Although our proposed dynamic value aggregation also combines multiple aggregation approaches (i.e., a onevalue, a two-value, and a three-value aggregation), it does not combine their output, but rather selects an aggregation approach from a portfolio of approaches. This selection controls the tradeoff between a high information loss (i.e., low k) and a high effect on the rating profile and hence a consumer’s decision (i.e., high k). Alternative forms of combining multiple aggregation approaches might, however, provide an interesting avenue for further research.

And last, we assumed that fake ratings cannot be accurately detected by existing detection methods. Combining mechanisms for detecting fake ratings with rating aggregation mechanisms might be even a more effective way to reduce rating fraud. Rating profiles of products with suspicious ratings can be then updated less frequently or using a smaller k.

## Appendix A. Proof for Eqs.1 and 2

There are <sup>- l </sup> possible ordered sequences of length l that contain a certain element c times. Element c is either a positive or a negative honest rating in our case. A negative rating occurs with a probability of $1 - q _ { j } .$ The probability that c ratings are negative is hence given as $( 1 - q _ { j } ) ^ { \mathfrak { c } }$ <sup>c</sup>. If c ratings are negative in a sequence of l ratings, there are l − c non-negative ratings in this sequence . The probability that c ratings are negative whereas l − c are not is hence $( \dot { 1 } - q _ { j } ) ^ { c } q _ { i } ^ { ( l - \bar { c } ) }$ in an unordered sequence of l ratings. We finally get the probability that c ratings are negative whereas l − c are not in an ordered sequence of l ratings as:

$$
P \left(r _ {j} ^ {-} | q _ {j}\right) = \left(\frac {l}{c}\right) (1 - q _ {j}) ^ {c} q _ {j} ^ {(l - c)}
$$

When substituting $q _ { j }$ by $1 - q _ { j }$ we get the probability that a positive rating occurs c times in a rating sequence:

$$
P \left(r _ {j} ^ {+} | q _ {j}\right) = \left(\frac {l}{c}\right) q _ {j} ^ {c} (1 - q _ {j}) ^ {(l - c)}
$$
