# Formal work<sup>fl</sup>ow design analytics using data <sup>fl</sup>ow modeling

Sherry X. Sun ⁎, J. Leon Zhao

Department of Information Systems, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 24 August 2011 Received in revised form 25 September 2012 Accepted 21 January 2013 Available online 16 February 2013

Keywords: Work<sup>fl</sup>ow design Data dependency Activity dependency Activity relations Business process automation

## a b s t r a c t

Work<sup>fl</sup>ow design has become a critical function in enterprise information management. However, only scant research attention has been paid to formal work<sup>fl</sup>ow design methodologies. As a result, existing design methods in business process management remain a manual and experiential effort and result in inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in design tasks and potential errors in work<sup>fl</sup>ow models. Considering that there are hundreds and thousands of business processes in organizations worldwide, overcoming this de<sup>fi</sup>ciency will have an enormous technical and economic impact on enterprise information management. In this paper, we investigate the possibility of incorporating formal analytics into work<sup>fl</sup>ow design, thus alleviating the intellectual challenge faced by business analysts when creating work<sup>fl</sup>ow models. The work<sup>fl</sup>ow design analytics we propose helps construct a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model based on information about the relevant activities and their associated data. In addition, our work<sup>fl</sup>ow design approach also helps determine whether the given information is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for generating a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model and ensures the avoidance of certain work<sup>fl</sup>ow anomalies. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of our study is to enable the transformation of work<sup>fl</sup>ow design from a manual and experiential effort into a more systematic and rigorous approach.

## 1. Introduction

Over the last decade during the drive towards e-business, many organizations have realized the importance of Internet-based business process automation as they face the challenge of obtaining competitiveness in the global marketplace. To achieve high levels of business process ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency, many organizations resort to work<sup>fl</sup>ow management systems to integrate business activities that span multiple functional units, such as human resources, marketing, and manufacturing [31,33]. As a prerequisite for effective work<sup>fl</sup>ow management, a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model is required to specify the execution sequence of activities needed to support certain business functions, such as producing a product or offering a service. Thus, it is critical to design correct work<sup>fl</sup>ow models ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently according to speci<sup>fi</sup>c business requirements.

The existing work<sup>fl</sup>ow design approaches, such as the participative approach widely used in practice, mainly focus on collecting business requirements rather than providing a rigorous procedure for generating work<sup>fl</sup>ow models. The lack of formal design approaches often causes design problems. Recent empirical studies have shown an error rate of 10%–20% from over 2000 process models used in different industry practice, which include the widely used SAP reference model, the models used in a process reengineering project in the service sector in Germany, the models documented in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry in Austria, the models used by consulting <sup>fi</sup>rms, and the models from IBM projects [20–22].

With the empirical evidence of the design problems, the importance of formal design methods starts to be recognized.

The process of designing work<sup>fl</sup>ow models can be partitioned into three stages. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage is business analysis consisting of design activities to understand business objectives, system environment, domain knowledge, and process management rules [37,38]. The second stage is process analysis that deals with the issues of activity identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and data<sup>fl</sup>ow speci<sup>fi</sup>cation [14,32]. Activities can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by workers and managers in various business units since they generally know what they do on the daily basis. Input and output data for each task, i.e. data<sup>fl</sup>ow, can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from documents, forms, and databases used in the business process [15,27,35].

The third stage, namely model construction to derive the correct activity sequencing, is an intellectually challenging step. First, no clear guidelines exist to help determine whether a given set of activities and related data are suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for designing a work<sup>fl</sup>ow. Moreover, due to the human cognitive limit, great effort is required to <sup>fi</sup>gure out all interrelations of different steps in a complex process with no guarantee for the resulting model to be error-free [21,22]. These challenges are compounded by the fact that a work<sup>fl</sup>ow can involve multiple business units and different organizations located in different geographic locations.

The common wisdom for dealing with potential errors in the stage of model construction has been to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the work<sup>fl</sup>ow models through simulation and veri<sup>fi</sup>cation before deploying them in practice [2,7,17]. However, it is a fundamental principle in software engineering that design errors should be prevented as early as possible [21,23]. The later the errors are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, the more cost and effort are needed to <sup>fi</sup>x the errors. This principle also holds for designing and automating business processes [5]. Consequently, we believe that once a set of activities and their input and output data have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the stage of process analysis, it is possible to derive a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model through analyzing the data dependencies among activities, namely data<sup>fl</sup>ow analysis, so as to avoid certain potential design errors in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place. Of course, this assumes the knowledge of all the activities and their input and output data can be obtained in the stage of process analysis given that the necessary information can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from interviews, discussion sessions, and documents by using the existing design methods [13,15,27,35]. This simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation makes it manageable to develop a <sup>fi</sup>rst-cut work<sup>fl</sup>ow design method.

The scope of our research is that starting from a data<sup>fl</sup>ow to derive a corresponding acyclic work<sup>fl</sup>ow consisting of <sup>fi</sup>ve basic constructs [1,2,28]. A data<sup>fl</sup>ow is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed with a set of business activities, the related routing constraints de<sup>fi</sup>ned on the given business activities, and input and output data items for each business activity. In this paper, we focus on determining relations between business activities from the data<sup>fl</sup>ow and then developing a control <sup>fl</sup>ow consistent with the data<sup>fl</sup>ow. The basic idea is to decide how activities should be sequenced in a work<sup>fl</sup>ow through examining the data<sup>fl</sup>ow in a process, i.e., the transformation from input data to output data via a sequence of activities. First, we de-<sup>fi</sup>ne certain data<sup>fl</sup>ow properties, including well-connectedness, completeness, and conciseness, to verify if a data<sup>fl</sup>ow speci<sup>fi</sup>cation provides correct and suf<sup>fi</sup>cient information for constructing a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model. Moreover, we develop design principles for deriving potential activity execution steps, referred to as “activity relations”, from data dependency analysis. Third, we develop an analytical method to enable the creation of complete work<sup>fl</sup>ow models from activity relations. Fourth, we simplify work<sup>fl</sup>ow design by applying the concept of inline block and decoupling the issue of model correctness from the issue of work<sup>fl</sup>ow ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Comparing with the existing work<sup>fl</sup>ow design methods that involve largely manual and experiential efforts, our method converts the design of complex work<sup>fl</sup>ow models from a task that depends on the knowledge and experience of work<sup>fl</sup>ow developers to a task that is supported with a systematic and mathematically rigorous approach. Therefore, we refer to such an analytical approach as work<sup>fl</sup>ow design analytics.

The impact of work<sup>fl</sup>ow design analytics is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. First, work<sup>fl</sup>ow design analytics builds the foundation for a formal approach to deal with the complexity of work<sup>fl</sup>ow model construction, thus alleviating the intellectual challenge faced by business analysts. Second, analytical work<sup>fl</sup>ow design can help verify the suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the given process information, including activities and associated data, for work<sup>fl</sup>ow model construction. Moreover, our approach can help eliminate certain potential work<sup>fl</sup>ow <sup>fl</sup>aws caused by violation of data dependencies [30] and inappropriate model structures [7]. This feature of our approach will reduce the costs of generating error-free work<sup>fl</sup>ow models. As our research goal is to enable the transformation of work<sup>fl</sup>ow design from a manual and experiential effort into a more systematic and rigorous approach, the contribution of our research goes well beyond the computational formalism presented in this paper. Successful application of our approach will have considerable economic implications for companies with complex business processes.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature. Section 3 presents the data dependency concepts needed for work<sup>fl</sup>ow design. Section 4 de<sup>fi</sup>nes the principles for deriving basic work<sup>fl</sup>ow structures from analyzing data dependencies. Section 5 demonstrates how to generate complex work<sup>fl</sup>ow models step by step. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper by outlining our contributions and future directions.

## 2. Literature review

Most work in the area of work<sup>fl</sup>ow management focuses on representing activity sequences through various graph-oriented modeling languages. For example, both Event-driven Process Chains (EPC), used to describe the SAP reference model [9], and Business Process Modeling Notation (BPMN), developed by the Business Process

Management Initiative (BPMI), are notations intended not only for technical developers but also for business analysts. As efforts to make the two graphic notations formal, different formalisms have been added to both EPC and BPMN [11,20]. Another popular graphic tool, UML activity diagram, plays an important role in work<sup>fl</sup>ow modeling [12]. Due to the lack of formal foundation [19] in UML activity diagrams, propositional logic has been applied to verifying UML activity diagrams [7].

As a formal modeling language, Petri nets [1,2] can help determine if an activity sequence contains errors, such as deadlock, activities without termination or activation, and in<sup>fi</sup>nite cycles, due to its abundance of analysis techniques. Based on Petri nets, a new work<sup>fl</sup>ow modeling language, Yet Another Work<sup>fl</sup>ow Language (YAWL), has recently been developed with some extended expressive power for specifying different work<sup>fl</sup>ow structures [4]. Metagraphs, a graphic structure extended from directed graphs and hypergraphs, has also been used as a formal work<sup>fl</sup>ow modeling approach to supports analysis of the data transformation through a sequence of activities [6].

While a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of research in the work<sup>fl</sup>ow area has focused on modeling, veri<sup>fi</sup>cation, and analysis issues [2,7,17], formal methods to generate work<sup>fl</sup>ow models, namely the work<sup>fl</sup>ow design approaches, have not received enough attention in the literature [31]. The most well-known work<sup>fl</sup>ow design approach is the participative approach [13], which adopts the principle of joint application design (JAD) consisting of a variety of methods for conducting workshops where users and technical developers work together to de<sup>fi</sup>ne requirements of information systems [10]. This approach is useful for collecting information needed for work<sup>fl</sup>ow design, such as business documents, business rules, relevant business activities, and data elements critical to execute the activities. However, the participative approach does not offer formalism for generating work<sup>fl</sup>ow models in a rigorous manner.

The method of Product-Based Work<sup>fl</sup>ow Design uses the relationship among data elements derived from product speci<sup>fi</sup>cations as a starting point for work<sup>fl</sup>ow design [27,35]. Moreover, cost and <sup>fl</sup>ow time are considered as criteria for selection of work<sup>fl</sup>ow models. The policy-driven work<sup>fl</sup>ow mining method [15] focuses on deriving a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model from documented business policies. However, formal procedures are still needed on how to determine activity sequences and how to use parallelism and conditional routing on the basis of analyzing relationships among data.

As an important step in work<sup>fl</sup>ow management, data<sup>fl</sup>ow analysis can help discover data<sup>fl</sup>ow errors hidden in a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model after the model has been created [25,29,32,34]. However, it should be more ef<sup>fi</sup>- cient to prevent data<sup>fl</sup>ow errors by taking data<sup>fl</sup>ow into consideration at the time the work<sup>fl</sup>ow model is created. Therefore, in this paper, we propose to start work<sup>fl</sup>ow design with analyzing data dependency, a type of <sup>fl</sup>ow dependency that arises whenever one activity uses a resource produced by another activity [18]. Moreover, we propose a new concept called “activity relations” as a foundation for a formal work<sup>fl</sup>ow design method. The concept of activity relations is inspired by the concept of “log-based ordering relations” used in [3]. However, the concept of “activity relations” is different from “log-based ordering relations” in that the former describes the structures of a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model and the latter describes the event sequences found in an event log.

The detection and veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of errors have also been investigated in the area of rule-based systems [24,26,30,37,38]. Validation of rule-based systems requires the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of anomalies such as redundancy, ambivalence, circularity, and de<sup>fi</sup>ciency at a large-scale since a rule-based system can have thousands of rules. However, work<sup>fl</sup>ow design faces a different challenge given that each step in a business process typically produces a data output while a rule in a rule-based system may or may not produce any data. Further, consideration of the two intertwined aspects, i.e., data<sup>fl</sup>ow and control <sup>fl</sup>ow, is not explicit in rule-based systems but critical for preventing structural errors in work<sup>fl</sup>ow systems.

As shown above, the number of publications related to work<sup>fl</sup>ow design is still quite small, particularly in comparison with the area of database design. However, work<sup>fl</sup>ow design is becoming increasingly important given that many organizations are rushing to automate their business processes via work<sup>fl</sup>ow systems, indicating that more research is needed in this area.

## 3. Data and activity dependencies analysis

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst extend the concepts of data dependency analysis, originally developed for verifying data<sup>fl</sup>ow anomalies in a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model [32], for the purpose of work<sup>fl</sup>ow design. We then introduce an example of order processing and show how data dependencies can be decided using this example. Further, we explain how to derive activity dependencies from data dependencies and brie<sup>fl</sup>y discuss how to determine whether a given set of data dependencies is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for deriving a proper work<sup>fl</sup>ow model.

## 3.1. Concepts of data dependencies

This section classi<sup>fi</sup>es data dependencies into three types, unconditional, conditional, and execution data dependencies. Following the convention in relational databases [34], we consider a data item to be an atomic data element that can be modeled as an attribute in a relational database. An activity v can perform read/write operations on a data item d which is an input/output of v [32]. To handle changes in a work<sup>fl</sup>ow, multiple data items can be used to capture a change rather than simply overwriting the existing data because the history of how a process is conducted usually needs to be kept. For example, we may need to track when the order is placed, ful<sup>fi</sup>lled, and shipped. The order status can be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed using “order\_placed”, “order \_ful<sup>fi</sup>lled”, and “order\_shipped”, each of which can be set “Yes” or “No”. To track all states of the same data object, a Structure data type $d ^ { \prime } \{ d _ { 1 } .$ $d _ { i } , d _ { i + 1 } , . . . , d _ { n } \} \left[ 3 4 \right]$ should be used where $d _ { i }$ is a data item capturing a state of $d \mathbf { \prime }$ . An example of Structure is Order{order\_placed, order\_ful<sup>fi</sup>lled, order\_shipped}. Therefore, we do not consider the operation of “update” in this paper.

Next, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the concept of routing constraint because it is useful for analyzing data dependencies.

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 1. (Routing Constraint) A routing constraint c is denoted as $c { = } f ( D ) { : } E x e c u t e ( V )$ , where D is a set of data items and $f \left( D \right)$ is a logic expression of D.

For example, when the condition “travel expense d is greater than \$5000” is true, the activity v approve the travel application will be executed. This is written as $c = ( d > 5 0 0 0 )$ : Execute(v).

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 2. (Data Dependency) In general, each activity v takes in a set of input data items $I _ { \nu }$ and produces a set of output data items $O _ { \nu } .$ This is called a data dependency for v, denoted as $\lambda _ { \nu } ( I _ { \nu } , O _ { \nu } )$

## 4.2. Identification of sequential execution

This section discusses how to identify immediate precedence relations from data and activity dependencies. The basic idea is straightforward. ## 5.5. Generality of workflow design analytics

We have shown above how to derive a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model from data and activity dependencies. The ingenuity of our approach lies in the idea of breaking down a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult task through a re<sup>fi</sup>ning process, which enables us to consider sequential structures, conditional routings, and parallel routings separately. In doing so, we are able to limit the focus <sup>fi</sup>rst on deciding the application of XOR-Splits and creating a correct model. Then the correct model is re<sup>fi</sup>ned into an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient model by adding back the AND-parallel relations previously ignored, and <sup>fi</sup>nally Joins are added to <sup>fi</sup>nalize the model. Nevertheless, the use of design instruments, such as “activity relation matrices”, “graph simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation with inline blocks”, and “emphasizing correctness before ef-<sup>fi</sup>ciency”, does not alter any critical design decisions when determining activity relations.

Nevertheless, the essence of our approach is to derive a control <sup>fl</sup>ow from a data<sup>fl</sup>ow through a two-phase procedure by applying Propositions 1–6 as shown in Fig. 12. Note that Propositions 1 through 3 transform, in Phase 1, a data<sup>fl</sup>ow Λ and the set of associated activities A into a set of activity relations Φ along with a set of intermediate arcs L’ consistently, and then in Phase 2, Propositions 4 through 6 derive the set of routing activities R along with the <sup>fi</sup>nal set of arcs L from the activity relations Φ and the intermediate arc L’. In such a way, the control <sup>fl</sup>ow derived is consistent with the data<sup>fl</sup>ow as formally speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in Proposition 7.

Proposition 7. (Verification of Flow-Consistency) Starting with a dataflow Λ and the related set of activities A, application of Propositions 1–6 leads to a control flow, defined with R and L, that satisfies the flow-consistency property defined in Definition 11A.

Note that since Propositions 3 and 5 help identify all possible parallel branches, a control <sup>fl</sup>ow obtained through applying these two propositions are ef<sup>fi</sup>cient, i.e., no more parallelism can be added without violating any data<sup>fl</sup>ow dependencies.

Next, we discuss that all six propositions cover the necessary instruments needed for the transformation from data<sup>fl</sup>ow to control <sup>fl</sup>ow.

1. Theoretical generality of a naive approach: Given any well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned data<sup>fl</sup>ow that is acyclic, well-connected, complete, and concise, applying Proposition 1 alone will derive all linear sequences of activities in the control <sup>fl</sup>ow. Of course, the resulting work<sup>fl</sup>ow model is not yet fully connected because execution dependencies have not been considered. Next, we assume that all execution activity dependencies are mandatory dependencies; the effect of this naive assumption is for a work<sup>fl</sup>ow to do more work than necessary by ignoring routing constraints but will de<sup>fi</sup>nitely derive all necessary output data. Under this naive assumption, we can apply Proposition 1 further. This will lead to a control <sup>fl</sup>ow that satis<sup>fi</sup>es the data<sup>fl</sup>ow requirements although it does so improperly because of (1) wasteful execution of activities due to the ignorance of routing rules and (2) a potential lack of synchronization caused by not using parallelism correctly. This naive approach de<sup>fi</sup>nitely satis<sup>fi</sup>es any data<sup>fl</sup>ow that is acyclic, well-connected, complete, and concise and is generic since it can derive a naive control <sup>fl</sup>ow that satis<sup>fi</sup>es the given data<sup>fl</sup>ow.

2. Practical generality of our approach: In our approach, Proposition 2 and Proposition 4 combined together help avoid wasteful execution of paths that need only be executed when a routing condition is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. This makes our approach more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than the naive approach but does not reduce its generality because Proposition 2 and Proposition 4 simply help reduce the workload of the naive control <sup>fl</sup>ow by applying routing constraints to trigger certain activities only when necessary via XOR-Split. Further, Proposition 3 and Proposition 5 combined together make the work<sup>fl</sup>ow more ef-<sup>fi</sup>cient by explicitly applying parallelism via AND-Split without changing the overall workload of the work<sup>fl</sup>ow. Proposition 6 simply re<sup>fi</sup>nes the look of control <sup>fl</sup>ow by adding appropriate join activities.

Therefore, our approach is general to help design a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model from a data <sup>fl</sup>ow that is acyclic, well-connected, complete, and concise.

## 5.6. How workflow design analytics prevent structural anomalies

We now discuss how the work<sup>fl</sup>ow design analytics can help prevent not only data<sup>fl</sup>ow anomalies as discussed in Section 3.4, but also some structural anomalies, thus reducing the burden for veri<sup>fi</sup>cation. We argue about the validity of this claim informally.

1) The property of well-connectedness given in Section 3 ensures that some simple types of structural errors are avoided. For instance, dangling activities and disconnected work<sup>fl</sup>ows occur when some links in a work<sup>fl</sup>ow are broken [7]. Given a well connected data<sup>fl</sup>ow, all activities will be linked to the work<sup>fl</sup>ow designed with our approach. Thus, dangling activities and disconnected work<sup>fl</sup>ows are avoided.

## 6. Conclusions

Designing work<sup>fl</sup>ow models is a critical step in business process management. However, existing work<sup>fl</sup>ow design methods remain a largely manual and experiential effort, and the quality of work<sup>fl</sup>ow design depends mainly on the skills of the work<sup>fl</sup>ow developers, thus resulting in inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in work<sup>fl</sup>ow design tasks and potential errors in work<sup>fl</sup>ow models. In this paper, we have demonstrated the feasibility of injecting analytics into work<sup>fl</sup>ow design by mathematically representing data and activity dependencies. We have shown that work<sup>fl</sup>ow design analytics is nontrivial because of the complex interplay of data, activity, and routing constraints. In our research, we succeeded in formulating a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model via an two-phase procedure by means of several novel techniques, including the concept of activity relations in work<sup>fl</sup>ow modeling, inline blocks analysis for design simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation, and decoupling model correctness from model ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency when creating the control <sup>fl</sup>ow. Our research is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant because it is the <sup>fi</sup>rst work<sup>fl</sup>ow design approach that incorporates a formal procedure starting with data<sup>fl</sup>ow speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. Moreover, certain potential work<sup>fl</sup>ow errors can be avoided at the time of work<sup>fl</sup>ow design, thus alleviating the burden of work<sup>fl</sup>ow veri<sup>fi</sup>cation to a great extent.

Our approach is applicable when a data<sup>fl</sup>ow speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is wellconnected, complete, concise, and acyclic. We limit our focus on the <sup>fi</sup>ve basic work<sup>fl</sup>ow constructs, i.e., sequence, XOR-Split, XOR-Join, AND-Split, and AND-Join [36]; other work<sup>fl</sup>ow patterns [28] may be added as extension to our research. Thus, we do not claim completeness in this paper. Since advanced work<sup>fl</sup>ow constructs such as the OR vertex (either Join or Split) can be simulated by a combination of AND and XOR [7], the design approach we propose is extendable to handle more advanced work<sup>fl</sup>ow constructs. In order to design work<sup>fl</sup>ows with loops, one approach is to <sup>fi</sup>rst identify the main path and the feedback path [8]. We are currently in the process of investigating design mechanisms for cyclic work<sup>fl</sup>ows with OR-nodes.

It is worth noting that there are a few factors that can constrain the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of our work<sup>fl</sup>ow design approach. First, our approach relies on the quality of data<sup>fl</sup>ow speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. Even though our approach can be used to determine if a set of activities along with their input and output data is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for generating a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model, we do not address how to collect data<sup>fl</sup>ow relevant information, how to determine if a data<sup>fl</sup>ow speci<sup>fi</sup>cation truthfully represents the data transformation of a process, and how to deal with incomplete information of activities and data<sup>fl</sup>ow. Therefore, when the data<sup>fl</sup>ow is not well-documented, the usage of our approach may be restricted. Second, when other considerations, such as resource limitations and cost optimization, also play an signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly important role in determining the structure of a work<sup>fl</sup>ow model, those considerations may con<sup>fi</sup>ne the use of the proposed method and require some modi<sup>fi</sup>- cation with the work<sup>fl</sup>ow model resulting from our approach.

In future research, we intend to extend our work mainly in two directions. First, we will extend our initial research results to more complex situations and develop practical guidelines on work<sup>fl</sup>ow design. Second, in order to evaluate the effectiveness of the work<sup>fl</sup>ow design analytics, it is important to either conduct laboratory experiments or examine the proposed method though real-world applications.
