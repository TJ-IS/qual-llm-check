# Theoretical principles for knowledge management system design: Application to pediatric bipolar disorder

Sandra M. Richardson <sup>a,1</sup>, James F. Courtney <sup>b,\*</sup>, John D. Haynes <sup>b,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Management Information Systems, Fogelman College of Business & Economics, The University of Memphis, Memphis, TN 38152-3120?

<sup>b</sup> Management Information Systems Department, University of Central Florida, Orlando, FL 32816-1400, United States

Received 24 February 2004; received in revised form 27 October 2005; accepted 1 November 2005 Available online 19 December 2005

## Abstract

Knowledge management systems (KMS) are systems designed to support organizational knowledge processes. Knowledgeoriented theories of the firm are emerging, as theories of KMS design continue to evolve. This paper adopts Habermas’ theory of communicative action and discourse ethics [Drake, B, Yuthas, K, and Dillard, J F., <sup>b</sup>It<sup>T</sup>s Only Words – Impacts on Information Technology on Moral Dialogue<sup>Q</sup>, Journal of Business Ethics 23: 41–59, 2000, Habermas, J., The Theory of Communicative Action: Reason and the Rationalization of Society,<sup>Q</sup> Beacon Press Books, Boston, MA., 1984, Habermas, J., "Reconciliation through the Public Use of Reason: Remarks on John Rawls’s Political Liberalism," Journal of Philosophy, Vol. 92, No. 3, March, 1995, 1109– 131.], and Churchman’s [Churchman, C.W., The Design of Inquiring Systems: Basic Concepts of Systems and Organizations, Basic Books, New York, NY, 1971.] Singerian inquiring system as kernel theories [Van Gigch, J.P., <sup>b</sup>The Paradigm of the Science of Management: In Celebration of C.W. Churchman’s 80th Birthday,<sup>Q</sup> Interfaces, 25 (2), March – April 1995, 81–88.] on which to base KMS design principles. Specifically, we build on Churchman’s nine requirements for an object to constitute a system and his Singerian inquiring system, and Habermas’ critical theory to develop the notion of Churchmanian–Habermasian knowledge management systems (CHKMS) and principles for their design. We define a CHKMS as a purposeful, ethical and adaptable system that creates exoteric knowledge (relevant for solving social and managerial problems) through communicative action, providing a link between knowledge and action in an organization or community of practice. The design principles involve a strong emphasis on ethical behavior, the development of success measures for CHKMS, the need to ensure that the CHKMS enhances the dignity of humankind, a highly participatory design process, the desire to unify the designers, the decision makers and clients, the need to shape CHKMS components in relation to the system as a whole, and the need for a <sup>b</sup>guarantor<sup>Q</sup> to validate knowledge through discursive communication and the <sup>b</sup>cashing in<sup>Q</sup> of validity claims. The principles are illustrated by applying them to management of knowledge concerning pediatric bipolar disorder. The implications of the design theory for KMS research are discussed. <sup>D</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Knowledge management; Inquiring systems; Critical theory; Communicative action; Discourse ethics

## 1. Introduction

Today we live in what is clearly manifesting as an ever increasing <sup>b</sup>knowledge society<sup>Q</sup> [6,27,77,78]. Correspondingly, organizations currently engage in knowledge management in order to create knowledge for strategic decisions [37,40,42,46,59,75,77,79]. Knowledge management (KM) involves the creation of value from an organization’s intangible assets [37,74]. Many organizations are developing computer-based systems designed to facilitate the sharing and integration of knowledge. In doing so it is necessary to incorporate the notion that knowledge management involves more than just technology for facilitating knowledge sharing. Practitioners realize that people and the culture of the workplace are driving factors that ultimately determine the success or failure of knowledge management initiatives [73].

Despite the importance of KM to strategic decision processes, few design theories exist for how computerbased systems supporting KM should be developed. For example, Alavi and Leidner [2] reviewed the knowledge management literature and found that <sup>b</sup>. . . the preponderance of knowledge management theory stems from strategy and organizational theory re search,<sup>Q</sup> but that in practice KM tends to rely on information technology and the theories do not relate to design and use of information technology.

At least four attempts at developing KMS design theory have been undertaken. Boland et al. [10] developed design principles for distributed cognition systems by integrating hermeneutics, inquiring systems and cognitive mapping. Similarly, Markus et al. [53] proposed a design theory and design guidelines for systems supporting what they refer to as emergent knowledge processes (EKPs). Following Walls et al. [83] who suggest that design theories should be based on a <sup>b</sup>kernel theory,<sup>Q</sup> include design meta-requirements, a meta-design, and testable design hypotheses, Markus et al. [53] develop a set of design principles, use those principles in an action research project and generate a set of revised principles as a result. In discussing future research, they pose the question <sup>b</sup>Are there alternative sets of requirements that also fit the kernel theory of EKPs?<sup>Q</sup> In a third approach to KMS development, Hall and colleagues [34–37] also follow Walls et al. [83] and rely on inquiring systems theory to develop an architecture for the major components of an integrated KMS that supports organizational learning. Lastly, Holsapple and Joshi [41] offer a KM ontology in order to provide a well-integrated framework that unifies the concepts of KM and lays a foundation for the future of KM research, practice and education. Their ontology is a first attempt to provide a common vocabulary in order to facilitate communication and sharing of ideas.

This paper takes an approach to design theory similar to that of Holsapple and Whinston [42], Boland et al. [10] and Hall and colleagues [34–37], building on

Churchman’s [16] inquiring systems theory, but adding elements of critical theory [26,50,51,65]. Specifically, our kernel theory is based upon Churchman’s Singerian inquirer which is integrated with Habermas’ theory of communicative action [32] and discourse ethics [27,33] to derive a set of design principles which emphasize ethical issues. Communication and ethical behavior are critical elements of Habermasian theories and we show that the works of Habermas and Churchman complement one another by deriving a broadly based set of design principles for knowledge management systems. Interestingly, even though we have in common the use of inquiring systems, our principles are much more general than those of Boland and Hall, and relate more to the climate and context of KMS rather than specific components of the KMS itself, more like the design ideals of Klein and Hirschheim [50].

Using communicative action, discourse ethics and inquiring systems as kernel theories has several advantages. First, inquiring systems are based on a long history of epistemology, the theory of knowledge, building upon some of the most well known philosophers of the ages. Second, our approach contributes to Keen’s [48] goal of continuity in computer-based systems research, as inquiring systems theory has been part of this literature since early on [55]. Third, Churchman has done a large part of the work for us, as he has already viewed the epistemologies of five philosophers through the lens of systems theory. Fourth, communicative action can be used to generate what Churchman calls <sup>b</sup>exoteric<sup>Q</sup> knowledge, or that which is suitable for solving social and managerial problems. And fifth, the theory of communicative action and discourse ethics can be used to create an environment for the open discourse required for Singerian inquiry, and as the basis of a <sup>b</sup>guarantor<sup>Q</sup> for validating knowledge that is created by the system. In addition, we believe that our design principles are suitable for well defined organizations such as business firms, but also for more loosely coupled entities such as communities of practice [34].

This paper first describes Habermas’ theory of communicative action and discourse ethics, then explores Churchman’s nine requirements that define systems in general. The manner in which Churchman applies those nine requirements to Singerian inquirers is described next, and theoretical design guidelines for Churchmanian–Habermasian KMS are derived. As both Singerian systems and the theory of communicative action place great emphasis on ethical concerns, many of the design principles relate not simply to the system itself, but to the behavior of those involved in the design process. We illustrate these principles by showing how they relate to systems for managing knowledge about pediatric bipolar disorder, which is described later in the paper. Ethical issues are especially important in medical applications such as this [8], and illustrate the principles well. Finally, the implications of the design principles for KMS research and KMS researchers are considered.

## 2. Habermas’ theory of communicative action and discourse ethics

The roots of Habermas’ theory of communicative action and discourse ethics are found in the school of <sup>b</sup>critical theory;<sup>Q</sup> a community of scholars that originally sought to study Kantian conditions for reason and knowledge within a Marxist environment. Members of this school saw distortions of thought emerge from social inequities that simultaneously concealed the inequities; and concluded that modern society was becoming increasingly passive and depersonalized by ignoring inequities [7]. Habermas’ agenda seeks to reveal, through discursive communication, the domination of positivistic, scientific, instrumental categories and structures that shape our current world [12]. He articulates a complex political, social, and epistemological critical theory to study the problems of the nature of the causation of social action [6]. Habermas proposes that people have a <sup>b</sup>technical interest in knowing and controlling the world around them, an interest in removing distortions in our understanding of ourselves, and an interest in being able to understand each other and join in common activity<sup>Q</sup> [7, p. 442]. In particular his development of discourse ethics provides a basis for participants to fully express their opinions and honor consensual agreements within a focus on rational discourse in solving problems with moral content [27].

Habermas views people as social beings; and although his focus was on society as a whole his views can be applied to the organizational context [25]. He identifies a communicative element in knowledge, affected by the relationships among people. Rather than articulating precise observable behaviors he collects these forces and behaviors of individuals in society into a <sup>b</sup>lifeworld.<sup>Q</sup> A lifeworld holds a society or organization’s cultural norms, identifies forms of communication and patterns of interpretation for use in interpretive efforts [7]. There are three cultural spheres within the lifeworld in which learning, cognition and institutional practice take place; the objective sphere, the inter-subjective sphere and the subjective sphere [7]. The objective sphere is where facts that are independent of human thought reside. The intersubjective or social sphere includes the combination of personal experiences and those facts that are independent of the person. The subjective sphere is where purely personal or private experiences reside. The goal for any person in a communicative process is to differentiate between the three spheres, to step outside of their perspective and achieve a <sup>b</sup>decentered<sup>Q</sup> understanding of the lifeworld. It is here, where one can transcend personal needs and societal norms, where one can consider moral problems abstractly, and distinguish matters of truth, justice, and taste according to the objective, social, and subjective views [7].

Habermas defines an environment, the <sup>b</sup>ideal speech situation,<sup>Q</sup> for discursive action. Discursive action is action that tries to discover and assess the arguments proposed for or against a message in terms of its clarity, truthfulness, correctness, and appropriateness. These four criteria define <sup>b</sup>validity claims<sup>Q</sup> and discursive action is aimed at justifying any or all of these claims [51]. Anyone at anytime can <sup>b</sup>cash in<sup>Q</sup> on these validity claims, speakers are free to investigate the claims of others [7]. Participants engaging in discursive action must agree to some ground rules before the discourse begins; that anyone who wishes may participate in the discourse to the extent desired; that the intent is a cooperative search for the <sup>b</sup>truth;<sup>Q</sup> to accept the force of the better argument; and to respond to the redemption of validity claims.

The ideal speech situation is meant to be one that <sup>b</sup>motivates participants in their own emancipation and through which different evaluative criteria can be used by the decentered speaker-hearer, depending on the type of claim;<sup>Q</sup> it is an environment of mutual understanding, where distortions are minimized [7, p. 460]. A problem arises when one component of the lifeworld takes over, resulting in an unequal shift in power. <sup>b</sup>When speakers, embedded in a lifeworld, attempt to speak about, or otherwise share, knowledge that is scientific in the usual sense, then they must filter out themselves in order to create an objective, normatively neutral attitude creating a filtered or objectified knowledge in relation to the interwoven view of life<sup>Q</sup> [7, p. 461]. When this cannot be achieved then one lifeworld can take over (<sup>b</sup>colonize<sup>Q</sup>) another.

For example, when science and technology become the leading productive forces institutionalized within society, the institutions take on a life of their own, and the development of the social system will be determined by the logic of the scientific-technical process. As a result the culturally defined self-understanding of a social life world is replaced by the self-reification of people under the categories of purposive-rational action and adaptive behavior [32]. The public realm loses its function as a political force for dissemination of different ideas and a place to discuss social issues and their impact, and instead becomes a place for alternative sets of leaders of administrative personnel. This is a poor replacement for communicative action in the public realm. Habermas also discusses the emancipatory potential of discursive action in which individuals can bring ideas into the discourse and combat the domination of technical interests. The focus here is on a subjective, voluntary empowering action of individual members of society in bringing out individual, and thus social, emancipation.

Drake et al. [27] operationalize Habermas’ notions on discourse ethics and the ideal speech situation by drawing on Kettner [49] who articulates five propositions, that, if met would allow moral discourse (discourse with a moral content) to take place effectively. These propositions are as follows:

1. Power neutrality: the discussion must be free from coercion;

2. Generality: the discussion should be open and accessible to all interested parties;

3. Autonomous evaluation: participants must have the opportunity to fully express ideas;

4. Role taking: participants must attempt to view the situation from others’ perspectives;

5. Transparency: participants must reveal their goals and intentions relevant to the issue.

The above 5 propositions are an attempt to provide the enabling circumstances for an ideal speech situation, particularly in an organizational context.

Thus, Habermas’ theory is designed to define an environment in which participants in a discursive communication situation have agreed to seek the <sup>b</sup>truth,<sup>Q</sup> accept the force of the better argument, and respond to cashing in of validity claims. This environment is designed to allow balancing of lifeworlds, preventing the dominance of one over another. The process of discursive action should therefore allow the truth to emerge, at least the truth as viewed by the participants involved.

As shown below, the environment for Churchman’s Singerian inquiring system is consistent with that of communicative action. The elements of the Singerian inquirer are described next, the Singerian–Habermasian KMS is defined and principles for their design are developed based on an integration of the two theories.

## 3. The Singerian inquirer, systems and KMS design principles

Churchman [16, p.18], in his influential book <sup>b</sup>The Design of Inquiring Systems,<sup>Q</sup> defined inquiry as the process of creating knowledge, and recast the epistemologies of Leibniz, Locke, Kant, Hegel and Singer <sup>b</sup>in the language and design of inquiring systems,<sup>Q</sup> providing <sup>b</sup>a description of how learning can be designed, and how the design can be justified.<sup>Q</sup> The Churchmanian inquirers have been used to form the theoretical basis of a type of learning organization referred to as an <sup>b</sup>inquiring organization<sup>Q</sup> [18,21,19,20,38,66,67]. Knowledge management in inquiring organizations has been described [17,18,52] and a new decision-making paradigm for Singerian organizations has been proposed [18]. Furthermore, a generalized architecture for learning-oriented KMS for inquiring organizations has been developed [35–37].

This paper extends the principles of Churchman’s inquirers even deeper into the KM realm by proposing principles for KMS design based on the Singerian model. Of the five models, the Singerian approach was chosen because it is the model most suited to complex, <sup>b</sup>wicked<sup>Q</sup> [18,68] environments such as those of knowledge management processes and it stresses the fact that the direction and style of inquiry can change frequently and dramatically [10]. While convergence of measurements in other models may indicate progress, Singerian systems operate by moving back and forth between refining measurement and <sup>b</sup>sweeping in<sup>Q</sup> change from outside forces to further extend knowledge. Finding an answer to the current question becomes less important than finding a better question [10]. Singerian systems are open to multiple perspectives, are innovative and adaptive and best in complex situations [18,22]. They are also well suited to management problems, as they generate <sup>b</sup>exoteric<sup>Q</sup> knowledge, or knowledge for broad social problems, as opposed to esoteric, scientific knowledge that applies to smaller and smaller domains as it is refined [16]. For example, the germ theory of infection may be considered exoteric knowledge even though it was scientifically derived, because it has <sup>b</sup>gone out<sup>Q</sup> to all societies resulting in the widespread use of soap and disinfectants that inhibit the spread of disease. Finally the Singerian model incorporates any of the other inquirers as necessary to solve a problem, so it is the most comprehensive of the five. In Chapter 3, Churchman lays out nine elements of systems in general and in Chapter 9, he describes the nine corresponding attributes of Singerian inquiring systems. In general, for something to be conceived of as a system (S), it must satisfy the nine conditions described next [16, p. 43]. Also described are the corresponding elements of Singerian inquirers [16, pp. 200–201].

First, S is teleological, that is, goal seeking or purposeful. A Singerian inquirer is above all teleological, a <sup>b</sup>grand teleology with an ethical base.<sup>Q</sup> The purpose is to create exoteric knowledge.

Second, S has a measure of performance. The measure of performance of a Singerian inquirer is the level of scientific and educational excellence in all of society.

Third, there exists a client whose interests (values) are served by S in such a manner that the higher the measure of performance, the better the interests are served. The client of the Singerian inquirer is humankind, all human teleological beings.

Fourth, S has teleological components that co-produce the measure of performance of S. In Churchman’s view, the components have been the academic disciplines, but that design has not worked well in producing exoteric knowledge useful to many people in many societies.

Fifth, S has an environment (defined either teleologically or ateleologically), which also co-produces the measure of performance of S. The environment is a critical aspect of the design and must be one in which there is open communication and human behavior is highly cooperative.

Sixth, there exists a decision maker who can produce changes in the measure of performance, but in a Singerian system, everyone is a designer in the ideal.

Seventh, there exists a designer who conceptualizes the nature of S in such a manner that the designer’s concepts potentially produce actions in the decision maker. Again, in the ideal, everyone is a designer.

Eighth, the designer’s intention is to change S so as to maximize S’s value to the client. Progress can be measured in the Singerian system in terms of the degree to which client, decision maker and designer become as one.

Finally S is <sup>b</sup>stable<sup>Q</sup> with respect to the designer, in the sense that there is a built-in guarantee that the designer’s intention is ultimately realizable. In Singerian inquirers, given that all else is realized, there is a built-in sense of optimism that progress is being made in the creation and validation of exoteric knowledge.

In the ensuing discussion, we describe these nine elements, developing a definition of what we call <sup>b</sup>Churchmanian–Habermasian knowledge management systems,<sup>Q</sup> and design principles for CHKMS design (summarized in Table 1). Discussion of each of the nine elements themselves could constitute an entire paper, hence given the space limitations here, we can only provide the essential highlights of each.

The design principles are illustrated by showing their relevance to the management of knowledge related to pediatric bipolar disorder. Bipolar disorder, previously referred to as manic-depressive disorder, is an affective condition in which a person cycles between extremes of depression and excitability (mania or hypomania). Once considered to be rare in children and adolescents, bipolar disorder is increasingly being diagnosed at younger and younger ages [30,60]. It is not clear whether this may be the result of social conditions (e.g., dietary changes), environmental factors (e.g., toxic elements in the food supply) or improved knowledge of the disorder and better diagnostic procedures. Whatever the reason, a diagnosis of pediatric bipolar disorder carries with it very serious ramifications for the child and his or her family, and must be made with extreme care.

Because pediatric bipolar disorder (PBD) has only recently been recognized in a significant number of cases, a body of knowledge about the disorder, its manifestations, diagnosis, and treatment is just beginning to emerge. Diagnosis of the disorder is complicated by the finding that mood disorders such as BPD may be co-morbid (co-occur) with several other disorders with similar symptoms, including obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), anxiety disorder, and disruptive behavior disorder. Fig. 1 is a Venn diagram developed by Papolos and Papolos [61] showing the percentage of cases in which the various co-morbidities occur. As we develop the various design principles, we will show how they apply to the management of knowledge concerning PBD.

## 3.1. Teleological and ethical behavior

The first of Churchman’s [16] nine necessary requirements for an object to constitute a <sup>b</sup>system<sup>Q</sup> is that it be teleological, or goal seeking; a collection of interrelated components working together toward a common goal. A purposeful system has <sup>b</sup>will;<sup>Q</sup> it is one that can change its goals and it selects ends as well as means and thus is willful [1]. Under this notion, systems are purposeful entities shaped by design efforts. The implications of this notion should not be understated; it is here that Churchman puts the designer of a system directly on the hook, the designer cannot distance himself or herself from the system conceived [77].

The notion of purposefulness and responsibility are carried through and incorporated into the Singerian inquiring system [82]. The goal of the Singerian system is to create exoteric knowledge, thus making it suitable for the resolution of complex managerial, social and public problems [18], as opposed to the goal of most sciences of solving limited-domain problems. Ethical concerns are paramount in Singerian systems, discourse ethics and communicative action, and are receiving increasing attention in the information systems and knowledge management literatures (see, for example, Refs. [8,13,14,23,54,56,62–64,80]. With regard to ethical issues, Habermas’ theory of communicative action plays a strong role in CHKMS. By providing a forum for honest and truthful examination and communication in the ideal speech situation, everyone involved can bring to the table concerns from all levels of system design, development and use. The ability to truthfully and openly communicate concerns will bring ethical issues to the forefront of system design in an interactive way throughout the life of a system. This supports the development of a purposeful and ethical system, and creates an environment with a focus on maintaining equality for all involved. Ethical issues arise in a number of different ways in the context of CHKMS development. For example, there are ethical issues regarding the behavior of the CHKMS designers themselves, the behavior of the organization (or organizations) for which the CHKMS is being built, the behavior of the decision makers involved, and in the protection of any sensitive or private information that may be contained within the system. Ethical concerns will be discussed as they emerge during the discussion of other elements of CHKMS that follows. For now, we provide the following design principle:

Design principle 1. The CHKMS designers, the design process and the CHKMS itself should adhere stringently to ethical and moral principles, for example, those espoused by professional organizations, such as the Association for Computing Machinery, in their codes of ethics and professional conduct.

Ethical issues are paramount in any medical application, such as that of pediatric bipolar disorder, and, unlike other sets of design principles, the Churchmanian–Habermasian KMS (CHKMS) approach emphasizes the need for ethical behavior of all parties involved, including the system designers. Thus Design principle 1 deals with ethical issues and suggests that the behavior of the system designers should conform to codes of conduct of professional organizations, such as the Association for Computing Machinery [3]. While too extensive to discuss in detail here, this code encourages computer professionals to contribute to social and human well being, respect the dignity, privacy and confidentiality of others, to avoid harm to others (see Design principle 6 below), and to respect intellectual property.

Codes of conduct are formal statements that encompass the values and practices of all parties involved. With regard to codes of conduct, communicative action plays a strong role. Codes of conduct are necessary because of a power differential between the parties involved. Communicative action plays a vital role in establishing what inequities in a relationship are, bringing them to the forefront for discussion and consideration, including them in everyday practice, and providing an avenue for recognition of a breech of conduct and the process for dealing with it.

While we believe that a knowledge management system for pediatric bipolar disorder can contribute to social and human well being, it must also be designed to respect the dignity of bipolar children and their families, and to provide access to information about them only to appropriate parties, and then only when release has been authorized. Because one element of an integrated system for PBD knowledge management may be an expert system for diagnosis of the disorder, respect must also be given for the intellectual property of expert psychiatrists and psychologists on the basis of whose knowledge the system is built. Ethical issues are pervasive throughout the design of CHKMS for bipolar disorder, or any clinical system for that matter [8]. Additional ethical concerns are addressed as they arise in the discussion that follows.

A key aspect of the concept of will is the will to survive. Thus, one goal of CHKMS is sustainability. In developing his theory of vulnerability, Boulding [11] shows that the more flexible or adaptable a system, the more sustainable it becomes. That is, the CHKMS must itself learn to adapt to changing circumstances. For example, the TOP Modeler KBS described by Markus et al. [53] went through 70 significantly different versions in only 18 months, exhibiting a rather extreme need for adaptability. Thus, our second design principle is:

Design principle 2. A CHKMS should be a learning system itself and exhibit sustainability by being easily adaptable to changing environmental conditions.

As more and more US children are diagnosed with bipolar disorder, knowledge concerning its diagnosis and treatment is emerging rapidly. It is important that the CHKMS be able to incorporate new knowledge and propagate that knowledge to those with a need to know. New knowledge gained by exchanging ideas in an ideal speech situation can be incorporated into the system development process. Moreover, as technology continues to develop the system should be designed to adopt appropriate innovations to enhance usability and effectiveness.

For example, a new way in which technology is being used in therapy is via the development of animated computer games and virtual reality technology [39]. Animated games have been used in conjunction with biofeedback technology to help people learn relaxation and stress reduction techniques [29]. Virtual reality simulations have already been shown to be helpful in diagnosing and treating attention deficit, hyperactivity disorder [70], and controlled experiments have been conducted on the use of VR in treating various phobias, including acrophobia [71], agoraphobia [60], posttraumatic stress disorders with Vietnam veterans [72], arachnophobia [39], and eating disorders [69]. It seems logical that games or VR simulations could be developed to diagnose and treat pediatric bipolar disorder as well. The VR approach to therapy has developed to the point that at least one company, Virtually Better, has been founded to treat various phobias with VR technology (http://www.virtuallybetter.com).

## 3.2. The measure of performance

Churchman’s second requirement of a system is the existence of a measure of performance, which <sup>b</sup>puts teeth<sup>Q</sup> into the system [77]. Measurement allows one to know how well the system is working and meeting the needs of its client or clients. For the Singerian inquirer, the measure of performance is the level of scientific and educational excellence of all of society, a measure yet to be developed [16, p. 200].

The measure of performance in a CHKMS then is the level of knowledge attained in the targeted user community as a result of system usage. A related measure would be how meaningfully the system processes data into information and knowledge, and communicates that knowledge to the members of the organization or community of practice. While the main goal of an inquiring system is to create knowledge, the sweeping in process described below may end up bringing in knowledge from outside the system, or in combining knowledge as in the process described in Nonaka’s [59] spiral model.

How well the system links knowledge to organizational action, and how ethically it behaves in achieving its other objectives are also important metrics. Identification of the levels of each of these can be established through discursive action as participants are free to express their beliefs, encouraged to consider ethical issues in their actions, and may come to understand the beliefs of others through open communication and by cashing in validity claims.

It is difficult indeed to measure the factors suggested above. Even though measuring information system success has proven difficult in research and practice, success has emerged as the dependent variable of choice in information systems (IS) research in general [24]. Multi-dimensional instruments for measuring IS success have been developed and a rather extensive literature has arisen on user evaluations of information systems as a surrogate measure for IS success (see [43] for a meta-analysis of this research). System usage is being employed more widely as a measure of success in many KM studies [81].

Jennex et al. [45] adapted the DeLone and McLean [24] IS success model to a knowledge management environment (see Fig. 2) and this model has been used as the basis for measuring success of knowledge management and organizational memory information systems (OMIS) [44,45]. As OMIS are closely related to KMS, we propose that an instrument could be developed based on the model of Jennex et al. In specific applications, the measures would be defined by the organization or community and should be related to the mission and culture, and would accurately inform managers of how well the system is working to meet the needs of its users.

Design principle 3. CHKMS success measures should be developed for specific applications, based on information systems and organizational memory success measures existing in the literature and in the organization.

The basic measure of success for the PBD system is, of course, how well it performs in distributing knowledge to relevant parties, how successful it is in supporting diagnosis and prognosis, and how many children are treated successfully as a result of the system. Berner et al. [9] describe the performance of four similar systems and discuss difficulties in measuring success. One anticipated difficulty is that it won’t be possible to determine whether a child might not have been diagnosed and treated successfully in the absence of the CHKMS, but it will be possible to at least keep track of the number that are diagnosed and the frequency of system use. Other success measures include the amount of system usage, and the number of physicians, clinicians, parents and children that use it.

## 3.3. The client, the designer and the decision maker

While one goal of a Singerian inquirer is for the client, designer and decision maker to be one and the same, we will first consider each individually, and then consider them collectively as one. Communicative action plays a vital role in bringing the concerns of each of the parties in this relationship to the forefront for inclusion and consideration in the system. Through the ideal speech situation and its iterative nature, the client, designer, and decision maker in the PBD relationship can voice their concerns for inclusion in the design, development, and lifecycle (adaptation) of the system. Through the ideal speech situation concerned parties are considered equally and inequalities between the parties are removed so that each plays a balanced role. The role of communicative action is included in the following design principles for the decision maker, designer and his/her intentions, and for the relationship between each as one. In addition, 5–9 are all related and we shall consider their relevance to PBD after all are presented.

The client of the Singerian inquirer is all of humankind, anyone that can benefit from exoteric knowledge. Perhaps the United Nations’ humanitarian agencies, despite their flaws, might be the best example of a Singerian organization. In most instances, the client served by a CHKMS would be the organization and relevant stakeholders. Stakeholders are <sup>b</sup>any group or individual who can affect or be affected by the achievement of the organization’s objectives.<sup>Q</sup> [31, p. 25] Stakeholders may be divided into primary and secondary groups. Primary stakeholders are those whose cooperation the organization needs for survival, such as customers, employees and investors. Secondary groups are those who are not essential to the organization for survival. Because stakeholder behavior affects the interests of their stakeholders whose behavior affects their stakeholders, there is a cascading effect that may <sup>b</sup>sweep in<sup>Q</sup> [16] all of humanity. Of course, it is absurd for an organization to consider every human being in its decisions, thus the bounds of the problem (see Section 3.8 below) must be set to include only the most salient groups [57].

The client is served by the system in such a manner that the higher the measure of performance, the better the client’s interests are served. If an organization is truly serving social needs, and using a CHKMS to do so, then it is clearly creating exoteric knowledge if it is improving its ability to service public needs. Philanthropic organizations, for example, the Carnegie Foundation, or organizations such as the Red Cross or some United Nations agencies would be others. Governmental agencies also qualify. Business firms producing socially desirable goods would be others, but some businesses and business practices are clearly questionable.

Design principle 4. The client of the CHKMS should include all salient stakeholders.

## 3.4. The decision maker

The decision maker controls and allocates resources so as to maximize the performance measure of the system, and the designer seeks a system in which the client’s values are best served by the decision maker’s actions [77]. In the context of a CHKMS, the decision makers would be managers in the organization or members of a community of practice who have authority and responsibility to use the system for knowledge creation and sharing. Managers in a business firm should be directed toward balancing their duty to seek profits and maximizing returns for its shareholders, with ethical duties such as honesty and avoidance of any ostensible harm to its other stakeholders, including those contributing knowledge to the system [76]. The designer should motivate the system toward this type of action on the part of the decision maker.

In the Singerian inquirer the sixth requirement states that the decision makers are everyone in the ideal and participation should be extended to as many interested parties as possible, a concept consistent with the goal of free and equal participation in discursive action. Examples would be management within the organization or members of a team assembled to share in the design, development, and maintenance of the system. The decision makers can make changes to the measures of performance of the system to the benefit of the client. It is unsure if the decision maker’s intentions are <sup>b</sup>good<sup>Q</sup> with respect to the client. For instance, managers may use knowledge for their own individual purposes that might be counter to those of the organization. Issues such as control of information or other resources in order to secure the decision maker’s power within a company, may restrict the ability of the system to perform as required. If all parties agree to the covenants of the ideal speech situation and discourse ethics, then it is much less likely that unethical control of information or resources will occur, as the openness of the environment would discourage such action.

Design principle 5. The decision maker should be expected to manage the system and be aware of its impact in such a way as to increase the measure of performance to the client.

## 3.5. The system designer

In a limited sense, the CHKMS designers can be viewed as members of the technical staff who have the skills to produce a design for the system. The designer receives input from the stakeholders, possibly through the client, external constituencies, potential users of the system, the decision makers, and the designers themselves. The designer will produce a system that will meet the knowledge management needs of the organization and make changes in the performance of the organizational KMS, which will be captured in the measurement instruments of the system.

The designer must remain aware of the impact the system has, not only on the organization or community as a whole but on its individual members, and stakeholders, as well. The building of individual intellectual capital is vulnerable in many ways and therefore a system designed for knowledge management must take this into account. For example, people’s intellectual capital can be impaired whenever they lose their personal information without being compensated for it, when they are precluded access to information which is of value to them, when they have revealed information that they hold intimate, or when they find out that the information upon which their living depends is in error [54]. Above all, a CHKMS should conform to the ethical principle of nonmaleficence [5] and avoid doing harm; the designers should not lose sight of the potential use of a computer or information technology to inflict harm on other people, directly or indirectly [76] and should design systems to motivate away from such potential use.

Design principle 6. The CHKMS should avoid doing harm.

## 3.6. The designer’s intentions

The designer who develops a Churchmanian–Habermasian KMS should have the intention of meeting the knowledge management needs of the organization or community under the terms agreed upon with the decision makers, clients and other stakeholders. The design of the system will be compromised if it is not ethically sound, and take into account and reinforce the over all ethical foundation and goals of the organization, and contribute to the learning and productivity of the organization. In order to operationalize these tenants the following 5 points (as mentioned in Section 2) ideally need to be put in place: (1) power neutrality, (2) open and accessible discussion, (3) autonomous evaluation (4) viewing the situation from other’s perspectives (5) transparency.

Moreover, the system should be designed to ensure that the decision makers using the system do so in such a manner as to maximize the value to the client, noting that the value is measured in broader terms than just profit. Hence:

Design principle 7. The designer should seek a design that encourages the decision maker to use the system in a way that maximizes the value to the client, broadly defined.

Thus, the attitude of the designers of a CHKMS, with respect to the client and decision maker, are assumed to be <sup>b</sup>good,<sup>Q</sup> that the knowledge produced and communicated by the system will serve the needs of the organization and its relevant stakeholders, and support an ethical foundation of business practices. If the decision maker’s influence on the design and creation of the system negatively impacts the role of the designer to produce such a system, it is the obligation of the designer to try to change the views of the decision maker in order to meet those requirements. The designer should only chose clients with which he can achieve those goals. In a Kantian sense [5,47,54], the moral imperative of the designer is clear:

Design principle 8. The designer must ensure that the CHKMS and the knowledge it handles are used to enhance the dignity of humankind, and choose only those clients and decision makers who also abide by this imperative.

3.7. The client, the decision maker and the designer as one

Concerning the Singerian inquirer, Churchman writes, <sup>b</sup>progress can be measured in terms of the degree to which the client, the decision maker, and the designer are the same,<sup>Q</sup> [16, p. 201). This is indeed, another measure of performance of the system. As the desire is to have client, designer and decision maker be the same, then appropriate members of the organization and its stakeholders must be swept into the design process. Each salient stakeholder group deserves respect in its own right, and the organization and its knowledge management system have certain moral obligations to their diverse stakeholders. One must consider the goals of each stakeholder in the design of the system [76]. There are many participatory design methodologies available in the literature that may be used to support this objective (see, for example, Refs.

[4,15,28,58]). We believe that this is a perfect situation for Habermas’ ideal speech situation, especially as exemplified by his notion of discourse ethics. Involvement here is more than simply participation, rather all parties involved must come to a deep level of mutual understanding and commitment to the design process and system success. The force of discourse ethics is to assert that such commitment and deep understanding results in inter-subjective knowledge, or the awareness that each participant has jointly reached an agreement with others regarding their participatory relationship. According to Drake et al. [27] that kind of joint participatory awareness is precisely what gives these shared understandings their moral force, thereby highlighting the critical importance of discourse ethics. Thus,

Design principle 9. Design in a CHKMS environment is more than simply participatory, rather the client, the decision maker and relevant stakeholders are all swept into the design process along with the CHKMS design staff members themselves, who use the elements of the ideal speech situation and discourse ethics to create a situation in which designers, clients and decision makers act as one.

Because this principle represents a rather lofty goal that may not be fully realizable in practice (as indeed do many of the others), the designers will likely have to exercise ethical judgment concerning whether the degree of mutual compatibility, communication and cooperation is sufficient to achieve the design of an acceptable system and operating environment. If not, then perhaps the endeavor should not be undertaken.

## 3.8. The system’s boundaries and environment

Systems in general have an environment that also co-produces the measure of performance. Where the system’s boundaries are set determines what is inside the system and what is outside, in the environment. The boundaries should be constructed in such a way as to include all relevant parties, and to encourage activities that produce exoteric knowledge. The proper environment is crucial to a Singerian inquirer. Churchman states:

. . . the environment which the [Singerian] system critically needs is a cooperative environment, where A wants that goal which will aid B in attaining his goals. One sees how fuzzy the boundaries of the inquiring system become because inquiry is evidently needed to create cooperation and cooperation to create inquiry. That is why the design of a Singerian inquiring system eventually becomes design of the whole social system [16, p. 200].

The question, of course, is how to create such an environment, and unfortunately, a question about which Singer and Churchman have little to say. But Habermas, on the other hand, has provided a theory of communicative action and discourse ethics, which we believe is most relevant of all in helping to create such an environment. Compare the quote from Churchman above with Habermas’ brief description of discourse ethics:

Under the pragmatic presuppositions of an inclusive and non-coercive rational discourse among free and equal participants, everyone is required to take the perspective of everyone else, and thus project herself into the understandings of self and world of all others; from this interlocking of perspectives there emerges an ideally extended we-perspective from which all can test in common whether they wish to make a controversial norm the basis of their shared practice; and this should include mutual criticism of the appropriateness of the languages in terms of which situations and needs are interpreted [33, p. 117].

The assistance that Habermas gives through these theories is to recast perspectives in the light of an ideal speech situation. In one important sense the ground of these theories is their provision for the enabling circumstances for such an environment. For example, if the environment is conducive to learning then learning will likely take place. Similarly if cooperation is effectively encouraged and rewarded then cooperation will likely take place. <sup>b</sup>The ground<sup>Q</sup> in this sense is very much an aesthetic understanding of the need for <sup>b</sup>enabling circumstances.<sup>Q</sup> Lyytinen and Hirschheim [51] and Benoit [7] have applied this theory to information systems development, and have shown that it may be used to create such a situation. Thus:

Design principle 10. The theory of communicative action and discourse ethics should be employed in the design of CHKMS to create an environment in which goals of participants are mutually compatible.

It seems noteworthy however, that organizations seem to be expanding their boundaries rapidly through the use of information and communications technology, especially the Internet and world wide web. Applications such as customer relationship management have the potential to provide better customer service, but also have the potential for abuse if not deployed in an ethical manner. Likewise, supply chain management and interorganizational systems are redefining what is within the <sup>b</sup>organization<sup>Q</sup> and what is outside.

The most important clients in the bipolar disorder application are the child and his or her family. Yet other users are clients also, in the sense that they may rely on knowledge provided by the system. The parents are the most important decision makers in that they must decide to seek treatment, choose a physician and therapist, if appropriate, as well as manage the daily life of the child. Of course, the attending physician is a vital decision maker as well, as he or she makes the diagnosis and recommends the treatment.

The designers of the CHKMS will be the research team, but certainly many stakeholders must be involved in the design process. Principle 7 indicates that the designer’s intentions should be to create a design which encourages decision makers, in this case clinicians and parents, to act so as to maximize the value to the client, the child. Principle 6 is related in the sense that the CHKMS should do no harm, or at least do minimal harm, as it is probably not possible to take any kind of action without harming something or someone. Obviously, this is consistent with the Hippocratic Oath sworn to by physicians.

Design principles 8 and 9 relate to the design process, indicating that it should go beyond being simply participatory and that the desire is for the designers, clients and decision makers to act as one. Papolos and Papolos [61] recognize this, citing Dr. Nancy Austin, a highly regarded therapist in this field:

Dr. Austin feels strongly that the entire team—the parents, the prescribing physician, the school personnel and the therapist—should be collaborating, and the therapist can facilitate ongoing discussion [61, p. 17].

Thus the design team and all other parties must work together well and strive to create a cooperative, mutually reinforcing culture that will benefit all concerned.

## 3.9. The CHKMS components

A system is comprised of teleological components that co-produce the measure of performance. The theme that emerges from Churchman’s discussion here is that all systems and their components are inseparable or <sup>b</sup>design non-specific.<sup>Q</sup> As Swanson [77] puts it, a sound design is one in which each system component is shaped in relation to the other components and to the system as a whole. Originally, computer-based systems were highly segmented and related to perhaps one or a few organizational units. Over time, systems have become more integrated, leading to such things as massive data warehouses, and the enterprise resource planning systems of today.

The components of a CHKMS should reflect the established institutional practices of the organization, but at the same time aspire to create exoteric knowledge, knowledge relevant to both the organization and its external stakeholders [21]. The knowledge produced and communicated through communicative action by the CHKMS should be relevant and useful to as many members of the organization as possible, incorporate cross-divisional views within the organization into the CHKMS, and be communicated freely and accurately to the members of the organization or community that need the knowledge with the four validity claims enforced. The components and communication of knowledge among the individuals should provide a measure of success of how the system is operating to meet the needs of the organization. Unique and constructive combinations of knowledge should be freely created and shared among members when needed in ways that would incorporate the cumulative experience of the organization within the CHKMS, serve to produce more informed, more comprehensive, and more knowledgeable decisions and aid in future learning within the organization. Boundaries within the CHKMS should not be restricted to the traditional <sup>b</sup>units<sup>Q</sup> within an organization; rather the freedom to combine information and create new knowledge paths should be encouraged. The boundaries should be clearly defined yet be flexible and encourage cooperation among organizational units. A detailed specification of the specific components is beyond the scope of this paper, however, Hall and her colleagues [35–37] have identified the general components of a learning-oriented KMS that may serve as a starting point.

Design principle 11. Each system component should shaped in relation to the other components and to the system as a whole, so as to co-produce the measure of performance (that is, contribute to the creation of exoteric knowledge) and should not be constrained by organizational boundaries in doing so.

We have identified four ways in which technology may be useful in managing PBD knowledge and forming the basis of the CHKMS itself. First, a system can be developed for helping parents chart the behavior of their children. Charting is used to track the mood swings of the child and can be useful in making a proper diagnosis, as well as in evaluating the efficacy of treatment.

Second, expert systems or clinical decision support systems [8] can be developed to assist in the diagnosis of PBD and related disorders. However, as already illustrated in Fig. 1, an accurate diagnosis is exceedingly difficult due to the co-morbidity of several different disorders. Moreover, the elements of Fig. 1 are general classes of disorder, not specific types. Specific disorders that may share symptoms of bipolar disorder include attention-deficit disorder with hyperactivity (ADHD), obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), Tourette’s syndrome, oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), conduct disorder (CD), and Asperger’s syndrome [61]. Complicating matters even further, all these disorders may be co-morbid or occur alone. Specific criteria for diagnosing them are given in the DSM-IV, as are guidelines for differentiating one from another (a differential diagnosis). Yet an accurate diagnosis is critical, because the treatment is dependent on it. A good diagnostic system would thus not be easy to develop.

And third, the world wide web can be used to collect information and knowledge from the various parties involved, as well as to disseminate exoteric knowledge suitable for parents and the general public. Several websites already exist containing knowledge about pediatric bipolar disorder, http://www.bipolarchild.com provides links to many of these.

The fourth major component could be an animated game or perhaps a suite of games that could be included to help in the treatment of bipolar children. The detailed development of the elements of the various components is itself a large undertaking and is beyond the scope of this paper.

## 3.10. The guarantor

The last requirement of an inquiring system relates to the validity of the knowledge it generates. Here the system is said to be stable with respect to the designer in the sense that there is a built-in guarantee that the designer’s intention is ultimately realizable. In other words, if you act on the basis of a design, you must have some confidence that it is right—right in the sense that the design accomplishes what you set out to do. In a Singerian inquirer, the guarantor gives a sense of optimism that if you follow the design, you are producing knowledge relevant to a broad segment of society. The resulting guarantor is one that provides internal consistency, consensus, a fit between data and model, an objective over-observer, and allows for replicability [20]. The implication for a CHKMS then, is that it should be designed with some sort of validation mechanism in mind to guarantee that the knowledge it produces is <sup>b</sup>true<sup>Q</sup> in some sense. In practice, this is a non-trivial matter. Nevertheless, the output of the system should be knowledge, <sup>b</sup>justified true belief,<sup>Q</sup> or at least knowledge that is believed not to be false and that the necessary mechanisms are implemented in order to guarantee that only valid knowledge is produced [17].

When there is disagreement among individuals within an organization, new ideas would be <sup>b</sup>swept in<sup>Q</sup> in order to make the knowledge more accurate or meaningful, and those measures of agreement would be incorporated into the overall measures of performance within the system. We believe this process could take place through Habermas’ ideal speech situation and discourse ethics such that knowledge generated by participants would be <sup>b</sup>validated<sup>Q</sup> through the communication process and the enforcement of validity claims. One such validation is the opening of each participant’s awareness arising from the sharing of understanding that takes place when the conditions for discourse ethics within an ideal speech situation are achieved. Such an awareness opens the participant. This <sup>b</sup>opening<sup>Q</sup> thus makes participants more receptive to acquiring additional knowledge that before the ideal speech situation may have been blocked.

Design principle 12. The CHKMS should include a mechanism for supporting Habermas’ ideal speech situation and discourse ethics for guaranteeing the validity of the knowledge created in the CHKMS process.

If the first three of the elements discussed in the preceding section, charting, diagnostic systems and games, are linked to the WWW, then a valuable database of shared knowledge can be developed to support data mining and analysis by researchers. This resource could prove to be invaluable in improving diagnoses and understanding the efficacy of various therapies, as well as a general tool for creating and sharing knowledge about pediatric bipolar disorder. This can support Design principle 12, that the knowledge in the system should be validated.

Of course, medical records are among the most private kinds of information and their use is highly regulated. It would be imperative, and in the spirit of the principles discussed herein, to obtain all the necessary releases and to protect the data from improper or unauthorized use. Security measures would thus be paramount in any kind of shared environment.

## 4. Discussion

This paper has taken Churchman’s [16] inquiring systems theory and Habermas’ theory of communicative action and discourse ethics [32,33] as the kernel theories for developing twelve design principles for knowledge management systems. These principles stand on the shoulders of centuries of work in epistemology and suggest the basic goal of creating exoteric knowledge, but emphasize ethical behavior and the inclusion of all salient stakeholders throughout the design process. The CHKMS should do no harm, and enhance the dignity of those people that it affects. CHKMS designers should behave in an ethical manner and choose only those clients and decision makers who behave likewise. Indeed, ideally the client, the decision maker and the designer become as one.

While it seems that the nine conditions for a Singerian inquiring system can be integrated with elements of critical theory to form the foundation on which the design principles for KMS can be based, much remains to be done. The design principles stated herein must be tested and refined, preferably in action research projects in which the principles are put to actual use. It is hoped that many KMS researchers will pursue this effort in their future endeavors. There is a need for increased validation and open communication within the context of CHKMS design; Churchman and Singer are silent on these points. We believe that Habermas’ theory of communicative action and discourse ethics can be applied in the CHKMS context, resulting in an environment that is more open to communication, which is essential throughout the design process. In addition, discursive communication provides a mechanism for validating knowledge through the concept of validity claims.
