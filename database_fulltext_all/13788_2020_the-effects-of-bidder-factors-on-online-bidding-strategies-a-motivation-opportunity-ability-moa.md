## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online auctions Bidding strategy Risk preference E-commerce Motivation-opportunity-ability (MOA)

## A B S T R A C T

The use and popularity of online auctions is growing all over the world. Bidding strategies are important because they are related to an auction's final price and ultimately its revenue. This study investigates the bidding strategies adopted by online bidders and the factors of the bidders, including bidding motivations, time availability, bidding experience, and risk aversion. We use the data from China to test the model and identify three bidding strategies in single-unit auctions: agent bidding, snipe bidding, and ratchet bidding. By running logistic regression, we find that hedonic motivations, utilitarian motivations, time availability, bidding experience, and risk preference all influence online bidding strategies. We also conduct pairwise comparisons of bidding strategies based on these factors and a simulation experiment to compare the benefits brought by diferent bidding strategies. We conclude by discussing the implications for both research and practice.

## 1. Introduction

Online auctions have attracted individuals' attention due to the dynamic and flexible pricing mechanisms [28] since the first launch of eBay in 1995. As the Internet has increased its market force, online auctions have become increasingly popular. For example, Taobao.com, the biggest online auction platform in China achieving 0.57 billion users in 2018 and making revenue USD 0.431 billion between November 2017 to October 2018, provides auctions for all kinds of products and attracts numerous bidders. Using online bidding platform, people could go beyond domestic platforms and bid for products from overseas websites [93] with less cost and less geographic limitation. From a micro-perspective, the revenue of a given auction is dramatically afected by the bidding strategies of the bidders in the auction, such as using or not the bidding agent, an information technology provided by the online auction sites [78]. Hence, it is necessary for researchers and practitioners to investigate bidders' behaviors regarding their bidding strategies. Formally, a ‘bidding strategy’ refers to ‘a series of interrelated types of bidding behaviors that a bidder adopts with a certain purpose or tactic in mind across diferent phases of an online auction’ 25, p. 3 [15].

Several studies have examined the factors influencing online bidding strategies, for example the designed bidding mechanism [7], the types of products [6], the evolution of technology [9], the size of a bid [33], and the ending timing of a bid [16], which sheds light on our understanding of the determinants of bidding strategies. Besides these product- or technology-related factors, we posit that bidders' personal factors also have an influence due to the determination of personal factors on consequent behaviors [79]. However, the existing literature lacks investigations specifically on the bidder-factor antecedents of the bidding strategies. Although some studies have begun to focus on the efects of personal factors in online auctions, such as gender diferences [45] and trait impulsiveness [76], the limited studies from this perspective hinder understanding of online bidding strategies. Therefore, our research aims to address this research gap and help solve the associated practical problem by investigating bidders' personal factors and their relationships with the bidders' bidding strategies.

Several theories have been adopted in the online auction context to understand the bidders' behaviors. For example, signalling theory has been adopted to investigate how signals, such as sellers' reputation (feedback scores) and their location, influence bidders' behaviors [32]; transaction cost theory has been used to explain why a bidder favors a particular form of bidding behavior under the uncertainty of auction outcomes from a cost saving perspective [96]; social network theory is to describe how information difusion in online auction afects the bidders' behaviors [44]. These theories deepen our understanding of the bidders' behaviors, although they are not developed for personal factors. We then introduce the motivation-opportunity-ability (MOA) fra mework that fits to the bidder-factor perspective to build our research model and supplement the online auction literature. The MOA specifies the efects of individuals' motivations, opportunities, and abilities on their behaviors [65,66]. It has been widely adopted in prior literature on knowledge sharing [83], technology adoption [59], decision making [46], and so on. Thus, we believe that the factors related to bidders' motivations, opportunities, and abilities also afect their choice of bidding strategies. Based on MOA, we develop the antecedents of bidding strategies in our research model, including bidding motivations (including hedonic and utilitarian motivations), time availability, and bidding experience.

In addition, online auctions are associated with risk. Participating in online auctions is similar to making investment decisions where in dividuals' risk attitudes are pivotal [56,97]. Actually, in the online auction literature, individuals' risk attitudes have also been identified as a key personal factor determining their bidding strategies [9,48,49]. In this paper, risk preference (aversion) is defined as the (negative) attitude an individual holds toward risky decisions (e.g., to bid for an auction o not) [94]. To better understand the efects of bidders' personal factors on their bidding strategies, we introduce risk preference as another antecedent of bidding strategies in our research model.

Accordingly, we further develop the detailed bidder-factor-antecedents of online bidding strategies: bidding motivations (including hedonic and utilitarian motivations), time availability, bidding experience, and risk aversion. The proposed research model is tested using survey data collected from 425 online bidders who participated in the biggest Chinese online auction platform, Taobao.com. The results sug gest that the proposed four personal factors influence bidding strategies diferently. We also conduct post-hoc analyses to compare the efect of each antecedent on each pair of bidding strategies and the bidding outcomes among the bidding strategies.

This study contributes to the existing online auction literature by exploring the efects of bidders' personal factors on their bidding strategies. Second, we validate the application of the MOA model for bidding strategies and also confirm that risk preference influences the choice of bidding strategies. In addition, the post-hoc analyses in this study further enriches the literature with not only more research find ings, but also diversified research methods. Finally, the cross-sectional survey we use in this study to identify bidding strategies provides a complementary perspective to previous studies that primarily use secondary data. Thus, our research findings provide some insightful implications for practitioners that we further address in the discussion section.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Typical bidding strategies used in online auctions

Researchers find that online bidders are not homogeneous in their bidding behaviors. They choose diferent bidding strategies for multi unit [9] or single-unit auctions [25], or auctions for small loans [78]. Several common bidding strategies identified in previous studies in clude agent, snipe, ratchet, and jump bidding.

Agent bidding refers to the use of a software-bidding agent to help with bidding in online auctions [29]. This strategy enables bidders to place bids when they are unavailable online. The software-bidding agent automatically places bids by the minimal bid increment until there is no one left to bid, or a preset reservation price is reached. This strategy is generally considered eficient and can thus provide competitive advantages over other bidders who are not using software agents for bidding [78,87]. Agent bidding can also raise the final price and generate more revenue for sellers and auctioneers [78]. Snipe bidding (aka late bidding) refers to the strategy that bidders wait and bid only near the end of an auction [16,80]. This approach can help to avoid a price war and allows a bidder to outdo rival bidders by leaving them no time to respond [68]. Bidders using this strategy are more likely to achieve success at a lower price [40]. Therefore, sellers and auctioneers try to discourage bidders from using this method [70]. Ratchet bidding (or nibbling) is the strategy of outbidding others by minimal bid increments no matter what stage the auction is in $[ 9 , 1 8 , 2 2 , 3 9 ]$ ]. This strategy is typically used when bidders are unsure of the true value of the product being auctioned and are trying to deduce this information from the bidding process [39]. Jump bidding occurs when bids are made at a price that is higher than necessary; that is, they increase the current bid by an amount that is higher than the minimal bid increment [33]. Both inexperienced and experienced bidders adopt this strategy. Inexperienced bidders often follow it to evaluate a product's price, whereas experienced bidders use it to signal to their rivals that they have a strong interest and afordability to force the rivals to drop out $[ 9 , 1 6 , 3 3 ]$

## 2.2. The antecedents of online bidding strategies

Some study provides a contingency framework for the antecedents of online bidding strategies with bidding mechanisms, product categories, bidder characteristics and bidder motivations [28]. Our literature review is based on these four categories, as detailed in Table 1. First, some studies investigate the efects of bidding mechanisms. For example, researchers find that hard-close auctions may encourage snipe bidding whereas soft-close auctions can prevent sniping and yield higher revenues for sellers [70]. One study designs a comprehensive auction format to discourage sniping [87]. Agent bidding is also found to be positively influenced by the efectiveness of the bidding agent, and this efect is strengthened when more agent bidders are present [29]. One study finds that the auction duration can influence the bidding strategies of the winners because snipe bidding brings more chances to win in auctions with longer durations [67]. Other studies investigate the product categories in online bidding. Snipe bidding is found to be a dominant strategy for any common value product auction whereas for private value product auctions, it only dominates in those with first-price and hard closings [30]. Feng, Fay and Sivakumar [38] find that the products with high face value usually lead to less overbidding among online bidders.

Regarding the bidder characteristics, three main research tracks are identified, namely, bidders' experience, their time, and risk preference. The previous studies find that experienced bidders tend to use bidding agents [9], bid late [16] or use sniping tools [10], indicating that bidding experience may have some influence on the choice of a bidding strategy. Regarding bidders' time, Bapna, Goes, Gupta and Jin [9] emphasise that the value that online bidders place on time may influence their bidding strategies. For example, those who value time much may choose to use software agent to bid for them, instead of observing the auctions and bidding manually by themselves. Another study argues that time pressure can increase bidding utilitarian motivations [21], which may also influence the bidding strategies eventually. However, the relationship between time-related factors and bidding strategy is still not empirically confirmed, which needs a further investigation. Regarding risk preference, risk aversion is found to influence a bidder's choices among diferent types of auctions. Risk-averse bidders prefer ascending auctions to sealed bid auctions [48,49]. But the efect of risk aversion on bidding strategies still needs empirical confirmation although Bapna, Goes, Gupta and Jin [9] have discussed bidders' risk preferences conceptually

The relationship between bidder motivations and bidding strategies has also been investigated. Hedonic motivations are found to increase impulse bidding, whereas utilitarian motivations decrease it [21], indicating the possible relationship between bidding motivations and bidding strategies. Researchers also find that bidders' perceived bidding enjoyment can increase.

Although these studies shed some light on the efects of bidders' characteristics and motivations, the underlying relationships between bidders' personal factors and bidding strategies still remain unclear. ## 2.3. Motivation, opportunity and ability model (MOA)

The MOA model is first gradually developed in marketing fields. It posits that an individual's actions are driven by motivation, opportunity and ability [65,66]. Motivation is often viewed as a force that directs individuals toward their goals [42]. It encompasses individuals' readiness, willingness, interest and desire to engage in a given behavior [66]. Opportunity captures the situational factors that facilitate or hinder a particular behavior [53,66], such as the attention paid or the time available [65]. Ability refers to the necessary skills, abilities and profi ciencies that an individual needs to be able to undertake an action. When someone has the motivation, opportunity and ability to make a certain choice, the chance of such a choice to be selected by him/her is increasing. The MOA model is broadly applied to explain a wide variety of behaviors in diferent areas. For instance, Siemsen, Roth and Balasubramanian [83] examine how the ‘bottleneck’ or constraining factor of MOA influences knowledge sharing behavior. Binney, Oppenheim and Hall [13] explore the use of MOA model as a basis for developing efective strategies aimed at achieving responsible environmental behaviors among rural land managers. Rothschild [81] designs a framework with the combinations of MOA to investigate how to obtain ap propriate strategies inherent to education, marketing and law. In addition to its application to marketing and management research, MOA is gradually adopted in e-commerce research as the information and communication technology being developed. For example, re searchers integrate MOA with elaboration likelihood model and constraint-based and dedication-based relationships to study electronic word-of-mouth intentions in online forums [82]. Other research applies MOA to study travellers' social media involvement [59]. Given its broad utility, we believe MOA can also be applied into online auction studies, particularly because selecting a bidding strategy is a sophisticated de cision-making behavior. A mature theoretical model can help to examine such behavior and unveil the underlying relationships between bidder factors and their bidding strategies.

## 3. Theoretical model and hypotheses development

We develop our research model based on the MOA model for un derstanding online bidding strategies. Motivation is the first construct that reflects the dynamic, personal energy with which an action is performed. In our study, a bidder's motivation to bid is defined as his or her inner drive to choose specific bidding strategy to participate in an online auction. Research indicates that hedonic and utilitarian moti vations are the most relevant ‘motivations’ in the bidding process [21]. Therefore, we have both included in the model.

Opportunity refers to the circumstances that allow for or facilitate people to perform a behavior. MacInnis and Jaworski [65] outline several situational factors, among which time availability is often considered as the most important proxy for opportunity in MOA model [65,72]. For example, Siemen [83] discuss that individuals' knowledge sharing behavior is influenced by their suficient slack time available and only if they have the available time. Specifically, in online auctions, when bidders are available to carry out a particular bidding strategy in an auction, the likelihood of using such a strategy increases. Therefore, we adopt time availability in our research model. Because most online auctions last for days and they are hard-closed, the time near the end of an auction usually is more important than other stage. Some researchers do find that bidders are more active in this stage [50]. We define a bidder's time availability as the availability of his/her time near the end of the auction to place bids.

Regarding ability, it is also determinant to people's behaviors. A person must possess the appropriate ability in the relevant domain of a certain behavior to be able to perform the given behavior. Ability is believed to influence people's action [65,66], and has been also found crucial to building online trust [47,73]. In the online bidding context, bidders' ability of bidding is mainly gained through their bidding ex perience. As the experience is accumulated, the bidders' bidding ability is increased. Usually, experienced bidders generally have higher ability than inexperienced ones. Bidding experience, as a key factor in online auctions, has also been investigated in diferent studies [10,16]. Therefore, we include it as one of the bidder-factor antecedents of bidding strategies in our research model.

Aside from the three dimensions of bidder factors, risk preference is also important to online bidding strategies [9,49]. Bidders are facing uncertainty when bidding and their decisions can be influenced by their risk preferences. Bidders in online auctions are not homogenous in their risk preferences [9]. It is necessary to include bidders' risk preferences in the model. Finally, in the research model, bidding motivations (in cluding hedonic and utilitarian motivations), time availability, bidding experience and risk preference are all hypothesised to influence bidding strategies. We also include the control variables in the model, namely the bidders' demographic information (i.e., age, gender, income level, and education) and the product characteristics (i.e., product common value) to control their efects on bidding strategies.

## 3.1. Bidding motivations

Motivation is believed to influence people's certain behavior consistent with the motivation [65,66]. In our research model to influence online bidding strategies, the most relevant motivations, hedonic and utilitarian motivations [21], are chosen. Hedonic motivations refer to feelings of pleasure and arousal from some activity [61,89], whereas utilitarian or extrinsic motivations refer to the economic and functional aspects of the activity [5]. Based on Vroom's expectancy model (that is, people choose a specific behavior because they anticipate it will lead to desired outcomes) [91], online bidders may prefer some bidding strategies to others when they are motivated in a hedonic or utilitarian way.

Previous studies find that hedonic motivations positively influence the adoption of information technology or systems [86,89,92]. The finding that hedonic motivations influence people's systems-related behavior may be generalisable to online bidding behavior. Online bidders' bidding strategy may be influenced by their hedonic motivations. For example, people with high hedonic motivations are unlikely to make bids using software agents because doing so lacks interactivity, engagement, participation and control that are the key components of hedonic motivations [61,62]. In contrast, researchers have observed that intrinsic motivations are positively associated with people's will ingness to spend time on a job [90], suggesting hedonic motivations may drive online bidders to adopt ratchet bidding because it is fun and requires more involvement. This is consistent with the result of one study that bidders with high hedonic bidding values are more likely to take a ratchet bidding approach [12]. In addition to ratchet bidding strategy, some online bidders in an in-depth interview study report that they are having fun when they choose to snipe at the end of an auction [28], which also indicates hedonic motivations may afect online bid ders' bidding strategies. Therefore, we propose:

## H1. Bidders' hedonic motivations influence their bidding strategies.

Utilitarian motivations are widely applied in predicting consumers purchase behaviors. One study finds that the utilitarian value of a product is positively associated with buyers' repeat purchasing intentions [23]. Another study on a travel agency's Facebook page reveals that consumers' utilitarian motivations lead to more browsing of the agency's page and higher intentions to buy or refer it to other people [75]. The utilitarian motivations make people more realistic and ra tional, focusing on the utility of the products rather than anything else. Therefore, utilitarian motivations are found to decrease impulse bid ding in online auction [21]. It can help bidders to hold on their impulse to bid that is consistent with snipe bidding strategy. Therefore, bidders' utilitarian motivations may encourage them to wait to take actions until the end of the auctions, rather than bid during the whole auction duration. When bidders have the utilitarian motivations, such as pursuing eficiency or winning an auction, they may choose to snipe [28]. In another study of an experiment to compare manual bidding and agent bidding, researchers found that bidders with high utilitarian bidding values are more likely to use the agent bidding approach over manual bidding [12]. It also indicates that the utilitarian motivations may afect online bidders' bidding strategies. Thus, we propose:

H2. Bidders' utilitarian motivations influence their bidding strategies.

## 3.2. Time availability

Time-related factors have been found as important factors for people's behavior. For example, people who are experiencing time pressure are found to respond well to time-saving appeals [2]. Time pressure influences decision making [11,71] or consumers' commercial activities [60]. In online auctions, time pressure is also identified as a significant predictor of online bidders' utilitarian motivations [21]. Therefore, the time-related factors are crucial to online bidding behavior. We then believe time availability can influence the online bidders' bidding strategies. Having time provides a useful opportunity for performing some bidding strategies. However, bidders may not always be available online. Bidders who spare no time to wait for an auction to end are more inclined to adopt agent or ratchet bidding. By contrast, having the time availability increases the likelihood of bidding by themselves. Bidders who experience no time constraints or are available to observe an auction's ending report their tendency toward snipe bidding [28]. Thus, we propose:

H3. Bidders' time availability influences their bidding strategies.

## 3.3. Bidding experience

In online auctions, bidders are heterogeneous in terms of their bidding experience. Compared with inexperienced bidders, experience bidders have learnt to avoid herd behavior [39], decrease risk [95], reduce ratchet bidding [3], bid late [16], use a bidding agent [9], use sniping tools [10], or choose a type of bidding strategy that leads to a better economic outcome [34]. These research findings have indicated that bidding experience has some efect on bidding strategies. One study of in-depth interviews with online bidders also suggests that consumers normally adopt ratchet bidding when they start learning how to bid; however, the application of this strategy decreases as they gain more ability through experience, being replaced by ‘more mature or efective methods’ 28, p. [61]. Therefore, less experienced bidders will tend to enjoy the bidding process and choose ratchet bidding. Otherwise, the more efective bidding strategies, such as agent or snipe bidding, will be leveraged. Thus, we propose:

H4. Bidders' bidding experience influences their bidding strategies.

## 3.4. Risk preference

The last factor in the research model is risk preference. Researchers have found that consumers' risk preferences afect their purchasing behavior [95]. Bapna, Goes, Gupta and Jin [9] have suggested that risk preference may be a key factor in leading bidding behavior. For example, risk-averse bidders do not prefer sealed bid auctions because it is risky: winners may pay considerably more than the second highest bid [48]. This is similar to agent bidding of which one high bid cannot be reversed, which is riskier than ratchet and snipe bidding. Bidding strategies have diferent levels of risk [25]. Then, bidders with diferent risk preferences may choose diferent bidding strategies. For example, those who are risk seeking or sometimes risk seeking may be highly stimulated by competition [28] and are willing to choose agent bidding to set a high reservation price to show others their determination to win the auction. Meanwhile, bidders who are risk averse to high final prices may be rarely afected by other bidders. They tend to observe and bid by minimal increments or snipe the auction. Thus, we propose:

H5. Bidders' risk preferences influence their bidding strategies.

## 4. Methodology

This study uses a survey for the research model and hypotheses testing, which complements the previous studies on bidding behavior that use archival data captured from auction sites [7,9,16,39,80]. The purpose of using survey is to catch some psychological or personal factors such as bidding motivations, time availability, bidding experience, and risk preference that cannot be obtained from archival data. The survey was conducted on Taobao.com,<sup>1</sup> the biggest online auction website in China. All auctions were single-unit auctions with hard closings. Because there were many auctions daily, we used a convenience sampling method to select the auctions that had just ended and invited all the bidders in the auctions to participate in our study by filling in an online questionnaire. They were asked to answer the questions based on their behaviors in the auctions they have just completed. The questions include all the measures used in this study. After several rounds of reminder, we got a valid sample with 425 online bidders.

We reviewed the relevant literature for the proper measures to be adopted for reliability assurance. Most of the measures are composed of multiple statements to which the respondents were asked to respond from ‘1’ (‘strongly disagree’) to ‘7’ (‘strongly agree’) on a 7-point Likerttype scale. Table 2 details these measurement items. The measure for hedonic motivations in this study is mainly adapted from the success motivations measure of Simmons's [84]. It is was measured by the importance and preference for enjoyment, the comparison with other motivations, and the satisfaction obtained after the hedonic motivations was fulfilled. The measures for utilitarian motivations include three dimensions: possession, economic and eficiency motivations [19]. The measure of possession motivations has four items: the importance of and the preference for possessing the auctioned product, the comparison with other motivations and the pleasant feeling of having a chance to win the product [35]. Economic motivations is determined by the im portance of and the preference for saving money, the comparison with other motivations and the happiness experienced when finding a bargain [5,54,84]. Eficiency motivations is measured by items that describe the bidding with ‘least time spent’ and ‘least bidding efort’ representing the preference for time saving and the feeling of accomplishment that came along with saving time [5,54,84]. The summation of the three dimensions is used to measure utilitarian motivations.

Kaufman and Wang [50] identify an ‘ending efect’ in Internet auctions that more orders were placed during the last three-hour period of the auction cycles—indicating that the time near the end of auctions is a key transition period. Therefore, we measured time availability di chotomously by asking the respondents one question whether they were available online when the auction is near its end. In addition, the control variable, product common value, is measured by four items that describe the extent that the common values of the product are known [37,98].

The measures of bidding strategies were adapted from previous studies [7,9,16,39,80]. To capture the information processing and behavior rationale of the bidders and complement to the previous studies that used archival data, this study attempted to measure online bidding strategies using subjective data. Instead of asking one question about which bidding strategy they have used directly, we asked four questions in the survey to describe the extent of bidding frequency, the frequency of using software agent, late entry and bid increments in the auction in which they had just participated. The reason is that we found in an interview beforehand that most online bidders did not exactly know the concept of ‘bidding strategies’ or the diferent types of bidding strategies although they were using one of them. Therefore, asking more specific questions to describe their bidding strategies rather than one general question is more appropriate in this study. In addition, the Likert-type scale was applied to the four questions to capture more information rather than a dichotomous scale. The data generated from these four questions served as proxies to profile the bidders' bidding strategies and as the basis for the cluster analysis of online bidding strategies. Such a design also makes it possible to profile the bidders bidding strategies even when bidders shift bidding strategies throughout the auction.

After the variables specific to the auctions the bidders have parti cipated in, we measured the other two variables in general situation. The measure of bidding experience is modified based on the ‘skill’ mea sure [69]. Only the first three items were retained, including experi ence, knowledgeableness and frequency of online auction participation; others could not be fit into the online auction context. The measure of risk aversion is adapted from the work of Dorn and Huberman [31]. The importance of safe investments and guaranteed returns, dislike of risky investments, insurance of safe investments and willingness to bear risk were used to measure the variable.

The questionnaire was initially developed in English and subsequently translated into Chinese and then back into English to ensure its content validity. In addition, the questionnaire was pretested by four business professors and twenty experienced online bidders. Based on their feedback and comments, changes were applied accordingly. Finally, a pilot study was administered to 25 online bidders randomly chosen from Taobao.com. Cronbach's alpha values for all items in this pilot test were above 0.70, suggesting the adequate reliability of the questionnaire. The pilot test also resulted in a few changes in wording and sentence structure, which improved the survey's content validity.

## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Respondents profile and measurement assessment

The sample contains 425 bidders who completed the questionnaire. Among them, 67.3% are female and 94.7% range in age from 19 to 38 years. Respondents are mostly college or university educated (81.2%) and their occupations are widely distributed. Common method bias is first assessed by Harman's single-factor test. All items with Likert scales are included in an exploratory factor analysis. Seven factors are generated and the first unrotated factor accounts for only 19.32% of the variance, thereby indicating that common method bias is not a concern in this study. However, because this test is increasingly criticised, we also evaluated the correlations between our variables (see Table 3) and found that all these are far below the standard threshold of 0.700, which indicates common method bias is not a problem in this study.

Next, we tested the reliability of the measures. All the Cronbach's alpha values are above 0.7, indicating that the reliability of the measurements is acceptable. The measurement model generates excellent goodness-of-fit indices: Chi-square/df = 2.339 (708.614/303), RMSEA = 0.056, NNFI = 0.913, CFI = 0.925 and SRMR = 0.058. As indicated in Table 3 (measurement model statistics), the square root of the AVE values of all the variables (along the diagonal cells) are higher than or near 0.7, and thus the convergent validity of the measures is established. They are also higher than their corresponding correlation values (in the of-diagonal cells) in the table, indicating good discriminant validity.

## 5.2. Model and hypothesis testing

The model and hypothesis testing follows a two-step process. First, the bidding strategies adopted by sample bidders in online single-unit auctions were identified by cluster analysis. Second, multinomial logistic regression was performed to validate the research model and test the hypotheses. The detailed results are as follows.

## 5.2.1. Cluster analysis of bidding strategies

We used a two-step cluster analysis method [52] to identify bidding strategies in single-unit online auctions with four proxies: bidding fre quency, agent bidding frequency, bidding entry and bid increment. In the first step, hierarchical cluster analysis was performed to generate a hierarchical side view and an agglomeration schedule table. The result show that the percentage of change in the agglomeration coeficients from four to three (2.2%) exhibit a dramatic decrease, suggesting a three-cluster solution. K-mean cluster analysis was then conducted with the results presented in Table 4. We interpret the results of the cluster analysis using the most obvious features of the bidding strategies. Namely, we identify the dominant bidding strategy(ies) used, including the hybrid bidding strategy when bidders used two bidding strategies equally in their auctions. The preliminary examination of the results does not find obvious hybrid bidding strategies used in our sample. Cluster 1 has the lowest bid increment and frequency of using agent software. This indicates that these bidders bid manually and as low as possible, suggesting ratchet bidding. Cluster 2, using the agent bidding strategy, has a high frequency of agent bids and bid increment, suggesting that these bidders typically set a high reservation price with the bidding agent and allow the latter to conduct the bidding for them. Meanwhile, Cluster 3 is characterised by late bidding, with low bid frequency and bid increment, indicating that the bidders enter auctions very late and place few bids during the auction process. This is identified as snipe bidding. Among the three bidding strategies, agent bidding is adopted the most, followed by snipe bidding.

## 5.2.2. Testing of H1–5 with the direct efect

We used the classified bidding strategies as the dependent variable (DV). The model with a categorical dependent variable needs to be analysed by logistic regression or discriminant analysis. We choose logistic regression because it imposes fewer requirements on the independent variables (IVs) and is less afected by inequalities of variance-covariance across groups than discriminant analysis [55]. We ran a multinomial logistic regression model with three bidding strategies identified in the cluster analysis. The independent variables include hedonic motivations, utilitarian motivations, time availability, bidding experience and risk preference.

The −2 log likelihood of the full model is 480.121 with significant improvement to the intercept model (Chi-square change/df 440.294/ 296, $p = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ , indicating that the full model fits well. The Cox and Snell, Nagelkerke and McFadden are 0.645, 0.729 and 0.478, respectively, also indicating a good fit for the full model. The multinomial logistic regression model tests hypotheses by comparing the full and reduced models (each antecedent was removed from the full model, one at a time). The significant change of −2 log likelihood means omitting the corresponding variable from the full model causes a significant degradation, indicating that the variable contributes significantly to the full model and the hypothesis is supported. The diferences in −2 loglikelihood between our full and reduced models were calculated, shown as Chi-square in Table 5. The results indicate that the efects of hedonic motivations, utilitarian motivations, time availability, bidding experience and risk preference are all significant. Therefore, H1–H5 are all supported.

## 5.3. Post-hoc study

## 5.3.1. Pairwise comparison

Although we find that the five bidder-factors are all significan antecedents of bidders' bidding strategies, whether they have efects on the selection between each pair of the bidding strategies remains unclear. Therefore, we conducted a further analysis by using binary logistic regression analyses to compare each pair of bidding strategies once at a time. In the binary logistic regression, the significance level of Wald 0.1 was set as a cutof and a 90% confidence interval (C.I.) was used for the odds ratio. Wald is the statistic to measure the significance of each variable in the model after accounting for its error [43]. The odds ratio is the measure of association between the DV and IV. It re presents an increase in the likelihood of the DV corresponding to one unit of increase in the IV [51]. If the value is higher than ‘1,’ the DV increases along with the increasing IV, indicating a positive relationship. In contrast, an odds ratio value lower than ‘1’ indicates that the likelihood of an increase in the DV is less than ‘1.’ Thus, it decreases along with the increasing IV, indicating a negative relationship between them. Researchers usually examine whether the C.I. for odds contains a turning point, the number ‘1,’ to determine whether the efect of the IV is significant or not [51].

5.3.1.1. Agent bidding versus ratchet bidding. In comparing the agent and ratchet bidding strategies, two dichotomous values were set: ‘1’ for agent bidding and ‘0’ for ratchet bidding. The binary logistic regression indicates the −2 log likelihood of the model is 323.262 and the standardised Nagelkerke R Square is 0.226. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test also indicates a marginal fit, which means that the variables in the model are significantly better at determining the bidding strategies between agent and ratchet bidding than random chance. The significant variables are retained to examine the Wald and C.I.s for the odds ratio (as summarised in Table 6).

Bidding experience and risk preference (aversion) reach the significance level of 0.1 for Wald and their 90% C.I. odds ratio contains the number ‘1,’ indicating that they are the antecedents in diferentiating the selection between ratchet and agent bidding. The odds ratio of bidding experience is higher than ‘1,’ indicating that bidding experience enhances agent bidding over ratchet bidding. The odds ratio of risk preference (aversion) is less than ‘1,’ indicating that risk-averse bidders tend to select ratchet bidding over agent bidding.

5.3.1.2. Snipe bidding versus agent bidding. In this comparison, we used ‘1’ to represent snipe bidding and ‘0’ to represent agent bidding. The −2 log likelihood of the binary logistic model is 344.524 and the standardised Nagelkerke R Square is 0.328. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test also indicates that the model fits well. The significant variables in diferentiating between the snipe and agent bidding retained include utilitarian motivations, time availability, bidding experience and risk preference, as indicated in Table 6. The odds ratio of utilitarian motivations, time availability and risk preference (aversion) are higher than ‘1,’ indicating that these variables are positively related to snipe bidding compared with agent bidding. The increase in bidding experience enhances the adoption of agent bidding over snipe bidding because its odds ratio is less than ‘1.’

5.3.1.3. Snipe bidding versus ratchet bidding. The comparison between snipe and ratchet bidding uses ‘0’ for ratchet bidding and ‘1’ for snipe bidding. The −2 log likelihood of the binary logistic model is 321.743 and the standardised Nagelkerke R Square is 0.125. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicates that the model fits well. The significant variables in diferentiating ratchet from snipe bidding include time availability and bidding experience, as indicated in Table 6. Both of their odds ratios are higher than ‘1,’ indicating that the increase in time availability and bidding experience may enhance the adoption of snipe bidding over ratchet bidding.

5.3.1.4. Likelihood of use for each bidding strategy. We summarise the results of the likelihood of use for each bidding strategy with diferent bidder-factor antecedents. Citing the result of ‘bidding experience’ for agent bidding over ratchet bidding in Table 6 as an example, the odds ratio is 1.806, which means that the chances of adopting an agent bidding strategy are higher than those of adopting a ratchet bidding strategy when bidding experience increases. All such comparison analyses are summarised in Table 7.

## 5.3.2. Simulation experiment

The results of the pairwise comparison indicate that bidders may choose a specific bidding strategy based on the benefits it brought. To better understand the benefits of the three bidding strategies identified in this study, we conducted a simulation experiment, in which three bidders with diferent bidding strategies compete in an auction. The starting price and the bid increment of the auction are set to 1 dollar. The reservation prices of the three bidders for the product being auctioned are randomly generated from 0 to 100 dollars. Bidder 1 with snipe bidding only bids by the end of the auction if the current price is lower than his/her reservation price. Bidder 2 with agent bidding bids by software agents whenever the bids submitted by others is lower than his/her reservation price. The behavior of bidder 3 with ratchet bidding is similar as that of bidder 2's, except that bidder 3 bids manually which leads to two diferent situations. First, bidder 3 may lose the competi tion with bidder 1 because he/she may not have enough time to respond to the sudden snipe before the auction ends while software agents, as computer programs, always have the chance to defeat the human speed. Second, bidder 3 may become irrational when competing with other bidders in person. He/she may be determined to win the auction by continuously bidding even the current price is higher than his/her reservation price. A previous study reports such irrational be havior among bidders [27]. Therefore, we considered the situation that bidder 3 continuously bids over his/her reservation price until winning and tried diferent levels of the probability of such an irrationality. The results are similar and the following example is given based on the probability as 5%, as shown in Table 8.

The replication of the simulation experiment is 5000 and we calculated the winning outcome and the average of winners' consumer surplus of bidder 1, 2, 3, respectively, as the proxies of the benefits of the corresponding strategy. The winners' consumer surplus = (reservation price-final price)/ final price, based on one previous study [25]. The winning times for each bidding strategy are 177, 2354, and 929, respectively and the ANOVA indicates significant diferences among them. Namely, using agent bidding can gain the highest chance of winning, followed by snipe bidding, and then ratchet bidding. The means of their average winners' consumer surplus are 3.085, 2.112, and 0.714, respectively. The ANOVA result indicates snipe bidding is the most beneficial bidding strategy considering the consumer surplus, followed by agent bidding and ratchet bidding. We also compared the sellers' economic profit based on the winning strategy of the bidders. We assumed the average of the reservation prices of the three bidders for each product can indicate the actual price of the product. Therefore, we used half of the value as the proxy of the cost of the product and its diference from the final price was calculated as the seller's economic profit. The ANOVA result indicates that bidders' snipe bidding brings the least economic profit for the sellers (3.262) whereas agent bidding is in the middle (10.191), and ratchet bidding is the most profitable (27.204).

The results of the simulation experiment are consistent with the research findings in the literature that sniping can help bidders to win the auction at a lower cost [40] although it is not a desirable behavior from the perspective of the sellers and auctioneers due to the lowest economic profit it brings to them [70].

## 6. Discussions

## 6.1. Discussions of the results

This study applies the MOA model [65,66] for online bidding strategies to investigate the bidder-factor antecedents. The empirical study confirms all the hypotheses. However, there are still some unexpected results to be discussed. First. the bidding strategies identified in this study include only agent, snipe and ratchet bidding. The absence of a jump bidding strategy may be explained by the specific mechanisms of Taobao.com. When a bidder attempts to jump in a single-unit auction, Taobao's bidding agent is automatically triggered to bid step-by-step until the price is raised to the jumped price or no other bids are submitted. This built-in mechanism assists bidders through the efective use of a bidding agent to save as much money as possible. However, it also restricts the use of real ‘jump behavior’ in the bidding process.

In addition, although we confirm the proposed relationship between hedonic motivations and bidding strategies, the efect of hedonic motivations is not significant in the post-hoc analysis for pairwise comparison of bidding strategies. This may be explained by the online bidding system, which is mainly a utilitarian system. In such a system, the efects of utilitarian motivations dominate. Previous studies have reported that bidders with utilitarian bidding motivations are more likely to win auctions [12] and have higher bidding satisfaction [21] than those with hedonic motivations. Therefore, the efects of hedonic motivations on bidding strategy are not as strong as those of utilitarian motivations.

Finally, the result that bidding experience increases agent bidding more than snipe bidding is not fully consistent with one of the previous research findings that experienced bidders tend to be more active at sniping compared with inexperienced bidders [16]. It can be explained by the development of bidding agent and people's acceptance of the technology in the past years. Previous studies have actually found a learning efect with the growth of agent bidders from 1999 to 2000 [9]. After more than two decades of development of online bidding, bidders now are more familiar with bidding agents. Some study finds that bidders with more experience build more institutional-based trust to the online auction sites [74]. Such trust encourages them to adopt the bidding agents provided by the online auction sites more, increasing more agent bidding than other forms of bidding. Agent bidding is becoming increasingly mature and popular as time progresses.

## 6.2. Implications for research

In terms of research implications, this study first provides a new perspective on the current auction research. Although there are some literature focusing on exploring the antecedents of bidders' bidding strategies $[ 4 , 6 - 9 , 1 6 , 3 3 , 8 0 ]$ , previous research still lacks a specific investigation on the bidder-factor antecedents of online bidding strategies. This study fills in the research gap by not only investigating bidders' personal factors, but also examining all the identified bidding strategies adopted by online bidders in this study.

Second, this study expands the application of the MOA model into the online auction arena. Previous studies that have applied MOA model mainly exist in consumer choice [65,66], knowledge management [53,83,88], and human resource management [13,17] areas. Recently, researchers in e-commerce also begin to adopt MOA in their studies [59,82]. This study follows the trend and adopts the MOA in online auction research. The results confirm all the antecedents of on line bidders' bidding strategies developed from MOA, including bidding motivations, time availability and bidding experience, are significant, validating the application of MOA in this area. Researchers are also encouraged to apply MOA model based on the specific situations of their research in other area.

Third, this study further compares each pair of bidding strategies identified regarding the efect of each antecedent on them and their bidding outcomes in the post-hoc study. The research findings deepen our understanding of online bidding strategies, thereby enriching the current literature along this line. In addition, this study applies multiple methods, including survey and simulation experiment. The combina tion of subjective data and simulation data analyses further validates the research findings and also enriches the literature in online auctions.

Last, although this study is cross-sectional, it indicates the dynamism of bidding strategies through the efect of the time-related factor: bidding experience. Online bidders may not always adopt one strategy as time progresses. With their experience increasing, their choices of bidding strategies may also evolve. Our research findings further prove the existence of a learning efect among bidders [9]. The dynamism indicated in this study provides more research opportunities for future researchers in this area.

## 6.3. Implications for practice

Although some previous studies [e.g., 70, 87] provide feasible general solutions to encourage more bids as sellers and auctioneers prefer, our study makes a further contribution in this area with its unique research focus. Nowadays, when online auction sites are trying to provide more customised services to bidders, the investigation of bidders' personal factors and their bidding strategies is necessary and meaningful. As bidders are heterogeneous regarding not only their bidding strategies [9], but also their personal factors, with the application of big data and artificial intelligence, the online auction sites can profile the bidders based on the historical data easily [1,36]. The online auction platforms are suggested providing tailor-made services for the bidders to manipulate some factors to afect their bidding strategies to obtain more revenue. In addition, the results in the main and post-hoc studies indicate snipe bidding is favorite among bidders especially when it is the least beneficial one for the sellers. Therefore, the online auction platforms and sellers need to encourage online bidders to avoid using snipe bidding strategy in several ways.

First, online bidders' time availability can be leveraged. The results of this study indicate that online bidders tend to snipe in an auction if they are available online when the auction ends. The first option for sellers is to set the ending time to avoid nights or weekends, which can decrease the snipe behavior in some certain way. However, auctioneers may also need to avoid letting bidders wait too long because it can harm the online experience and reduce the revenue of the sellers [58]. The second and also efective option for sellers is to encourage the use of agent bidding and ratchet bidding with special promotions even when bidders are available near the end of the auctions to enhance sellers' economic profit.

Second, we observe that as the bidding experience increases, bidders go through three stages: (1) ratchet bidding, (2) snipe bidding, and (3) agent bidding. To increase revenues, the online auction platforms and sellers should keep attracting new customers to their auctions as much as possible. These bidders tend to use ratchet bidding, which is expected to increase the economic profit of the sellers. They can also retain the most experienced bidders with virtual money, virtual titles, special discounts or VIP status on the website to increase their preference for it. These bidders tend to use software agents to bid, which also increases the revenues. At the same time, the auction platforms and sellers can expedite the medium experienced bidders' learning process to shorten their stay on snipe bidding stage. Training videos, FAQ sections, and simulated bidding exercise can be provided to them especially for this purpose. The growing of their bidding experience can thus be accelerated.

Third, this study shows that some risk-averse bidders are likely to use snipe bidding more than agent bidding. Platforms are suggested to alleviate their concerns by providing smart designs of bidding agents to make them appear less risky. For example, platforms can consider developing options for risk-averse bidders to ‘save’ their reservation price before they finally ‘submit’ it, setting the range of the number of rival bidders they are competing with, and sending them reminders of ab normal competition in the auction. These functions can make the bid ding process less risky and easier for risk-averse bidders to accept.

Last, although online auctions are usually considered to be utilitarian information systems, they can also be designed to include some of the intrinsic motivations of bidders: fun, learning, challenges, engagement and so on [61,62]. In that way, bidders can feel noticed, respected or even cared for, which should encourage them to revisit a website or auction house with high customer satisfaction and loyalty. Site owners and auctioneers may obtain higher revenues in this manner.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

Several limitations of this study provide compelling opportunities for future research. First, this study considered only hedonic and utilitarian motivations. Some studies provide a more comprehensive taxonomy by classifying the traditional intrinsic motivations into hedonic and intrinsic categories [62]. Intrinsic motivations for seeking satisfaction through accomplishment, learning, enlightenment or socialization [14,85] can be included in online bidding studies. In addition, data were collected from only one auction site in this study. Although this specific site is the biggest online auction platform in Chine, it may restrict the generalization of our research findings to other overseas platforms. It is also necessary to scrutinise whether research finding from single-unit auctions are the same as those from multi-unit auctions. Therefore, data collected from websites in diferent countrie or using diferent auction mechanisms can help to generalise the re search findings.

The research model in this study mainly includes four dimensions of personal factors: bidding motivations, time availability, bidding experience, and risk preference of the bidders. Future research could consider other personal factors, such as mood, which has been identi fied as a key factor in online auctions [20]. In addition, future investigations of online bidding strategies can be expanded to include interpersonal factors, such as trust [63]. The trust between sellers and buyers is believed to have a significant efect on online bidding beha vior [77]. In addition, auctions are like gameplay with multiple parties. Seller strategies [15], third-party seller rating systems [24], and social reference systems [57] all influence online buyers' behavior. Therefore, devoting more resources to investigating the efects of interpersonal factors on online bidding strategies is a worthwhile endeavor.

Finally, we do not consider the fraud that is common in online auctions. Certain special bidding strategies in online auctions, such as bid shielding [64] or shill bidding [68], may surface for personal gain. How to detect such behavior, how it afects others' bidding and how it can be prevented [41] require further examination.

## 6.5. Conclusion

This study investigates the influence of bidders' personal factors of online bidding strategies based on the MOA model. In accordance with the model, we develop five bidder-factor antecedents of bidding strategies: hedonic motivations, utilitarian motivations, time availability, bidding experience and risk preference. These are all hypothesised to influence online bidding strategies. We identify three bidding strategies, ratchet, agent and snipe bidding, in the single-unit auctions using the data collected from the biggest online auction platform in China. The efects of the bidder-factor antecedents on the respective strategie are examined by performing a multinomial logistic regression with the identified bidding strategies. The results suggest that hedonic media tions, utilitarian motivations, time availability, bidding experience and risk preference all influence online bidders' bidding strategies. We also conduct post-hoc analyses to examine the efects of these five antecedents on each pair of bidding strategies and compare the benefits of these bidding strategies. The research findings provide several academic and practical implications. This study not only contributes to the literature on online auctions, but also provides insights for practitioners to leverage customization and manipulate diferent bidding strategies to obtain more revenue.
