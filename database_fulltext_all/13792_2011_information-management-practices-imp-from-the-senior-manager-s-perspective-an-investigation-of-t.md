# Information management practices (IMP) from the senior manager’s perspective: an investigation of the IMP construct and its measurement

William J. Kettinger\* & Donald A. Marchand<sup>†</sup>

\*Management Information Systems Department, Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, Memphis, USA, email: bill.kettinger@memphis.edu, and <sup>†</sup>Strategy Execution and Information Management, IMD – International Institute of Management Development, Lausanne, Switzerland, email: marchand@imd.ch

Abstract. While many scholars have discussed what a company should look like when it has reached an advanced state in using its information, there has been little empirical research to develop a valid effectiveness measure to determine whether a company is doing a good job of managing its information. Based on a theoretically derived model of an information life cycle, this study empirically tests a measure of information management practices (IMP) that can be part of future business measurement approaches. To ensure that we maintain an enterprisewide perspective, we focus on senior managers’ perceptions of how well their companies manage information. We discuss IMP as a ‘process’ or life cycle that involves sensing, collecting, organising, processing and maintaining information to enhance its use for decision-making. We first give an overview of the research on life cycle views of information management. Next, we present our information management life cycle framework and its dimensions. We then statistically determine the extent to which these ideas exist in the minds of senior managers as reflected in the second-order construct IMP. Finally, we discuss the implications for evaluating and measuring IMP in companies. The contributions have scholarly value as a building block in a theory of knowledge management, and practical value as a validated measure of the effectiveness of IMP.

Keywords: information management, life cycle, senior management

## INTRODUCTION

You are an Executive Club frequent traveller. You are tired of the standard meals so you ask for a vegetarian meal, hoping it will offer some variety. After sampling the bland ‘veggie’ option, you mention to the flight attendant that the veggie meal does not look good and tastes worse

The flight attendant agrees, and nearby passengers say their meal is not much better. Yet, after several more flights, nothing changes: the same meals, the same agreement from the flight attendants that they are awful, the same comments from other passengers. What you may not know is that the senior managers of the airline catering service are also frustrated. Here is how the senior vice president of one of the world’s largest airline catering services views the problem of managing information about customers’ reactions to their meals: ‘In the past, the catering service was a fully integrated part of the entire airline structure. When the catering service was separated from other operations as a stand-alone company, a new type of customer–vendor relationship developed. While we now consider the airline as our customer, we are struggling with how to get closer to our customer’s customers – the passengers who consume our products. This is tricky. In order for our company to grow, we need to know how the customers of our customers like our meals and how we can improve them. But the knowledge that resides in the airline about the customer is not transferred to us, so there is an information gap’. The result of this information gap is that the company’s information management life cycle is broken, resulting in inadequate organising and processing of customer information because of poor information sensing and collecting processes between the airline and the catering service. The result? The catering service is unaware of the customer reactions to the veggie meal.

This case is but one example of the need for a company to develop effective information management practices (IMP) throughout its information management life cycle. The catering company should create an information management process with its partner airlines to systematically sense, collect and organise customer reactions to meal options. It should consider a campaign to persuade flight attendants to record and report customer comments about meals or to examine what customers have left on their meal trays. This probably requires that airline personnel are trained and rewarded for collecting customer reactions and complaints and passing them on to the catering service and will require changes in contractual requirements between the airlines and catering service to include an information management component. The two companies must restore a working life cycle of information managemen if they are to use their knowledge to achieve mutual goals and ultimately improve their performance.

There have been many attempts over the last 25 years to describe companies that manage information and knowledge well. Peter Drucker (1988) introduced the notion of the ‘information-based organization’ as an advanced development stage of companies that employ information effectively. Nonaka & Takeuchi (1995) have written about the ‘knowledgecreating company’, which excels at converting information into usable knowledge for innovation in products and services. Thomas Davenport (1997) has proposed ‘information ecology’ as a metaphor for a ‘business model for information management’. Chun Wei Choo (1998, p. 3) offers the concept of the ‘knowing organization’, which he defines as an ‘organization that is able to integrate sense making, knowledge creation, and decision making effectively’. Seltsikas & Currie (2002) propose a company-wide process-oriented approach to information management and present support for an overarching capability of information management that supports business practices. While each of these authors has painted a picture of what a company looks like when it has reached an advanced state in managing and using its information, none of these authors has empirically confirmed these ideas or developed a valid measure to determine whether a company is doing a good job of managing its information. Based on the contribution of these authors and others, this paper will develop an empirical measure of ‘information management practices’ – a term that describes the overall capabilities of an organisation to manage information effectively over the life cycle of information use.

To ensure that we maintain an enterprise-wide performance perspective, we focus on senior managers’ perceptions of how well their companies manage information. We discuss IMP as a ‘process’ or life cycle that involves sensing, collecting, organising, processing and maintaining information to make it more useful for decision-making. While some may propose that there are other components of the IMP construct, we conclude from our overview of the literature and pilot testing with senior mangers that these five stages of the life cycle reflect senior managers conception of IMP. In the following sections, we first give a brief overview of the research on life cycle views of information management. Then, we present our information management life cycle framework and its dimensions. Next, we statistically determine the extent to which these ideas exist in the minds of managers as reflected in IMP, a second-order construct. Finally, we discuss the implications for evaluating and measuring IMP in companies.

## A THEORETICAL PERSPECTIVE ON THE LIFE CYCLE VIEW OF INFORMATION MANAGEMENT

Life cycle approaches have been found in multiple business-related disciplines such as organisational behaviour, marketing and information science. Information life cycle approaches are heavily influenced by Simon (1957), who views human organisation as a computation system. Carlile & Rebentisch (2003) recognised that early research on organisational information processing considered the information management processes as a ‘black box’, a hidden unit between input and output units. Increasingly, authors attempted to delve into this black box describing information management as a set of activities that moves through a logical succession of phases, each dependent on the other (e.g. Simon, 1977; Marchand & Horton, 1986; Moorman, 1995; Seltsikas, 1999; Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Butler & Murphy, 2007). Table 1 outlines selected life cycle approaches. Although the number of life cycle phases and the labelling of information management processes differ slightly in the literature, there are relatively few discrepancies in terms of the underlying concepts, which consistently include the four phases of information management – collecting, organising, processing and maintaining information (Ashby, 1956; Taylor, 1968).

Sense-making has been recognised as an important management activity in the organisa tional literature for some time. For example, Karl Weick observed: ‘The goal of organizations, viewed as sense-making systems, is to create and identify events that recur to stabilize thei environments and make them more predictable’ (Weick, 1995, p. 170). Managers as ‘monitors ‘. . . perpetually scans his environment for information, interrogates his liaison contacts and his subordinates, and receives unsolicited information’ (Mintzberg, 1975, p. 56). Sensing has also been identified by marketing scholars in the context of information needs associated with sensing and responding to customer and market changes (Moorman, 1995; Haeckel, 1999; Srivastava et al., 1999). Grover et al. (1993) also recognise sensing information through information systems as critical for environmental scanning. Increasingly, information management scholars such as Kuhlthau (1991; 2004), Dervin (1992) and Choo (1998) have suggested that sensing should be recognised as an additional fifth information management life cycle phase involving the active seeking and scanning of information in external environments. For example, Choo (1998) identified the sensing phase of an information management life cycle as involving activities related to scanning, noticing and interpreting information in the external environment. Taylor & Van Every (2000) note that sensing is socially anchored and is concerned with emergent aspects of organisation – information sensed about the external competitive environment helps define information usage needs and thereby dictates and drives changes to what information would be collected, organised, maintained and processed. They go on to point out that a network view of organisation (i.e. connectionism) is emerging, whereby organisations – like connectionist nets – learn and develop distinctive organisational configurations of sense-making. In this way, sensing may influence all IMP phases since humans wil rework their information use assumptions based on new signals and views of their environment. It is for these theoretical reasons that we included ‘Sensing’ as a fifth distinct phase o an information management life cycle.

## Our IMP model

We use an information life cycle with the five phases discussed above as the basis for our IMP model. The purpose of this life cycle is to improve the ways information is used in organisations for decision-making. According to the path dependency theory (David, 1985; 1994), decisions regarding current and future choices are influenced and constrained by past decisions. Similarly, the informational choices regarding sensing, collecting, organising, processing and maintaining are influenced and constrained by past decisions regarding these phases. Therefore, the IMP model treats each phase as independent in concept but dependent on the previous phase.

Another important assumption of our IMP model is the recognition that ‘there is no time that information does not exist in a company’ – it is present in the knowledge of its people. In essence, organisations do not start from totally blank information slates, creating knowledge from nothing; even a start-up company has information residing in the minds of its entrepreneurs. According to Nonaka & Takeuchi (1995, pp. 59–61), tacit knowledge is ‘personal, context-specific, and therefore hard to formalize’. Explicit knowledge is ‘knowledge that is transmittable in formal systematic language’. People possess both types of knowledge simultaneously. Authors have noted that identifying knowledge sources in a company is critical to making use of the company’s collective expertise (von Krogh & Roos, 1996; Prusak, 1997). The opportunity costs of not identifying and managing available information means that people collect information that others have already collected before, or they cannot use information that has been collected because they cannot locate it (Myers, 1997; Stewart, 1997). To tap this knowledge, organisations face the challenge of formalising (making explicit) personal (tacit) information, using it where it is needed and then maintaining it for future organisational use. Hence, the IMP model manifests the organisational desire to move information to more formal, useful and maintainable forms.

## The context of information usage

To manage information effectively, we recognise three important properties of information use. First, information use is constructed in the minds of people; people define meaning and value relative to information. Second, information use is situational. The company’s business context defines norms and practices that constitute the information usage behaviours through which people may value information as useful. People’s information valuation judgments are determined within this context. Third, information use is dynamic: determining what information is needed, i.e. sensing information, then collecting, organising, processing and maintaining it all happen in repeated cycles that are dictated by business situations and people’s abilities and desires to use information in those unique contexts. Thus, effective IMP represents the company’s efforts to execute this information life cycle within an information usage context that is personal, situational and dynamic.

## THE FIVE PHASES OF AN INFORMATION LIFE CYCLE IN PRACTICE

At each phase of an information life cycle, we believe there is continuous evaluation of information, both at the individual and at the company level, and this ultimately determines how far any piece of information will progress, if at all. At the sensing phase, people use cognition and affective responses to the external environment to make a valuation judgment about whether potentially collectible information will satisfy a new or unanswered problem or decision. At the collection phase, people decide whether the decision-making benefits resulting from collecting the new information are worth the associated cost. At the organising phase, decisions are made about whether appropriate data structures, standards and routings can be established to help ensure the information can be used to make decisions. At the processing stage, people decide whether the information collected and organised actually satisfies analytical and decision-making needs. At the maintaining phase, a value decision is made about whether information should continue to be stored and updated in anticipation of future use.

In light of this evaluation, it seems to be a moot point to ask where an information life cycle really begins. However, we do propose that two phases of an information life cycle seem to be particularly important valuation points – processing and sensing. Processing is probably the most critical valuation point of the life cycle phases because it is at this stage that a decisionmaker determines whether the information sensed, collected and organised really meets the problem solving needs of the business context. In essence, this is the point where the payof of an information life cycle occurs. A valuation judgment determining that the information is deficient at the processing phase can immediately create the need for further sensing, better collection, improved organisation or enhanced updating of the information to be used in processing.

Sensing also appears to be a particularly crucial valuation phase. To effectively anticipate and begin to define new information needs, sensing requires perceptual skills to recognise both changes in competitive environments and which business stimuli are relevant to a particular business context. If these skills and judgments are weak, then new information requirements are not fully anticipated and new sources of information are poorly identified. Ultimately, filtering information judgments at the sensing phase determines whether a company will be able to effectively develop competitive strategies to address changing business demands or respond to changes in customer needs or to competitors’ product innovations that are influenced by social, economic, regulatory and cultural shifts in the company environment.

We will next define and explore in more depth each phases of our information managemen life cycle. But for the purposes of the discussion, we will follow a notional cyclical movemen of sensing, collecting, organising, processing and maintaining.

## Sensing information

We define sensing information as the life cycle phase in which information is detected and identified concerning:

economic, social and political changes affecting the business;

• competitors’ innovations that might impact the business;

market shifts and customer demands for new products; and

anticipated problems with the company’s suppliers and partners.

Information workers’ jobs are closely tied to decisions about what to sense, and how to sif through the overabundance of signals from inside and outside the organisation, as well as interpretations of the perceptions of other organisational members. It is through sensing that a company’s members and managers observe (1) changes between their current perceptions and the ways they collect and use information; and (2) changes in environmental conditions that require a reinterpretation of opportunities, risks and the ways information is collected and used.

Failure to appropriately sense changes in business conditions can result in not collecting the right information to respond to these changes, as well as in misreading the significance of changes for decision-making, which can result in inappropriate strategies and actions. To ensure that accurate information is being sensed, thus influencing the next phase of collecting information, people should be ready to learn to interpret changes or errors within their working environment and have the flexibility to act on what they are learning. This is important to allow for changes in information that might affect future business decisions and strategic direction.

## Collecting information

The next phase of the information management life cycle is the practice of systematically collecting relevant information. It includes:

• profiling the information needs of employees to ensure the right information is delivered to them at the right time;

• filtering information for managers and employees to prevent information overload;

• identifying key knowledge sources so that employees can make use of the company’s collective expertise; and

• training and rewarding employees for accurately and completely collecting information fo which they are responsible

While information technology (IT) has advanced the capability of organisations to collect more data about their customers, operations and processes, and to share the data more broadly across networks, a critical information management concern relates to profiling and filtering information to improve decision-making performance and prevent overload. In this regard, most companies experience two problems simultaneously. First, largely because of the internet and tools such as data mining and warehousing, the information that is potentially available often exceeds the abilities of organisational members to absorb and use it effectively. Second, many people in companies do not have the appropriate information to execute their duties because even carefully designed information systems quickly become outdated. As a result, these systems continue to produce the same volumes of information on a routine basis, even if it is out of date or no longer relevant. Conversely, the absence of relevant information often goes undetected, so companies do not collect information that they can use. These two problems affect not only employees in carrying out their operational tasks, but also managers in their strategic role.

People are generally motivated to collect information that will be of immediate use. The problem arises when the act of collecting information is either separated from the acts of organising, maintaining, and processing information or is carried out by different people, processes and information systems. Training people to collect accurately and completely the information for which they are responsible is an important step in making sure that the information is properly organised and processed for decision-making.

## Organising information

The organising phase of our information management life cycle focuses on:

• indexing and classifying information for appropriate availability;

• linking databases across the business units and functions within an enterprise; and

• training and rewarding employees for accurately and completely organising the information for which they are responsible.

Organising information seems to involve several critical decisions that organisational members make on an ongoing basis. First. it is important to know what categories to use and whose purposes will be served through the categorisation scheme. In integrated business enterprises, departments, functions, suppliers and customers increasingly share databases across related business processes, which can result in differences over categories, terminology, language and indexing schemes. Many organisations have attempted to deal with these problems through a standardised data dictionary, data integrity guidelines, and formal database administration functions that oversee the corporate databases. Unfortunately, these data standards have tended to disregard personal data sources, including personal computer systems and, significantly, informal information sources. Furthermore, making information ‘available’ through networks and databases does not always make it usable. Second, although IT may provide the technical means for organising and connecting databases across a company, the challenges of organising information to share and use it across functions, professional domains and different business units are essentially human activities involving choices. Third, like other work responsibilities, organising information requires the relevant skills, expertise and work habits; these can be developed by training employees. Because sensing and collecting are precursors to organising, people should possess a rich enough understanding of one another’s work to be able to make valuation judgments about whether some information they have access to might be organised to offer value to someone else. Such behavioural proactiveness toward sensing, collecting and organising is probably the result of formal training, as well as the institutionalisation of information sharing incentives and recognition as part of the company’s overall control systems.

## Processing information

Just as processing information is critical to decision-making in organisations, so decisionmaking by managers and organisational members shapes the ongoing processing of information. Thus, as mentioned earlier, the processing phase of our information management life cycle requires that people can access and actively engage in analysing information sources to derive useful knowledge as inputs to decisions. Processing information includes:

• accessing appropriate information;

• analysing databases to drive reasonable decisions;

hiring people with analytical skills;

• training and rewarding employees on how to use information to arrive at decisions; and

• evaluating employees’ performance according to how they use information.

Analysis is critical in information processing to translate information that has been sensed, collected and organised into specific knowledge that can be used by managers and organisational members to achieve the purposes of the organisation. Analysis in most organisations is an ongoing responsibility of organisational members, and not just for special occasions or ‘big decisions’. Moreover, because most information work is difficult to ‘observe’ and measure, managers might pay special attention to the hiring, ongoing training and evaluation of employ ees, to assure that they have hired the right people to process information into knowledge in their companies.

## Maintaining information

The next phase of the information management life cycle brings us full circle. Maintaining information involves:

reusing existing information to avoid collecting the same information again;

updating information databases so they remain current; and

• refreshing data to ensure that people are using the best information possible.

Many organisations have developed sophisticated processes to make sure that their opera tional information systems and databases are updated continuously. Content management systems employ features such as ‘replication’ that permit team members to know when documents are modified, and by whom, each time they access the document. Moreover, internet and social media ‘push’ technologies permit people to receive their choice of streaming updates of news, financial quotes and social content all day.

While managers often advocate reusing information, there are several reasons why reusing is difficult to practice. First, organisational members may not be aware that information has already been collected in one part of the organisation. Second, managers may inadvertently encourage new searches for information by redefining the decisions or prob lems that they face enough so that previous efforts to collect, organise and process information ‘look different’, although they may be basically the same. Third, the information previously collected may not be easily accessible because of the ways it has been categorised and automated. Finally, people in a company may be reluctant to reuse information that they do not ‘own’.

## RESEARCH METHODS AND ANALYSIS

To operationalise and empirically test our theoretically derived construct – IMP – we chose a survey method to ensure the generalisability and stability of our findings in a large population. After initial rounds of pilot testing and refining the survey tool, we conducted a large-scale, multi-company survey to confirm the existence and relevance of this concept to business managers (Hurley et al., 1997). The steps and results of the research process are presented below.

## Theory development and construct identification

## Instrument development

Because the intention of this research was to empirically test, operationalise and confirm a theoretically derived construct in a generalisable manner, across companies, cultures and industries, we chose a multi-industry and multi-national survey method to conduct our research (Creswell, 2008). All the survey measures were developed from the literature review and integrated existing and previously tested constructs and variables in accordance with our theoretical framework. Multiple items were used to measure all proposed constructs.

## Questionnaire pretest

At the draft stage, the questionnaire was reviewed and tested by numerous faculty members and research associates at IMD, a leading European business school and executive development institute in Lausanne, Switzerland and at the University of South Carolina, a research university in the USA. The instrument was in Enalish. but it was tested for comprehension in culturally diverse managerial groups at IMD. The focus of this activity was two-fold: (1) to review the substance of the instruments and ensure content validity, and (2) to address presentation issues. This pretest effort resulted in feedback and observations regarding language interpretation (in English), the time to complete the survey, and unclear meaning and references by the very same types of international executives who would be participating in the project. This was invaluable in improving the clarity, format and length of the final survey instrument.

## Pilot testing

Next, the study questionnaire was pilot tested with 151 managers attending executive programmes at IMD. IMD offers over 200 executive education programmes per year covering over 6000 managers from all regions and most countries in the world. It has more than 40 000 alumni in over 5000 companies in its database, which served as the source for identifying an international, diverse, cross-industry and cross-cultural sample of senior managers to be involved in this research project. In accordance with our planned study sample, one-half of the respondents in the pilot testing groups were the chief executive officer (CEO), president or managing director of their companies. A key goal of this pilot testing was to verify both that practitioners clearly understood the statements in the questionnaire and that there was enough variation in the responses to detect relationships between variables. Again, the questionnaire was improved according to the respondents’ feedback.

In addition, the confirmatory factor models described later in the analysis were first tested with the pilot test sample, and the models showed acceptable model fit and factor loadings. Given these positive results and the theoretical basis that informed our definition of the IMP construct and its operationalisation, our next step consisted of creating a generalisable survey tool (Creswell, 2008) and conducting confirmatory structural equation modelling (SEM) (Jöreskog & Sörbom, 1989; Hurley et al., 1997) to confirm the existence of a perceived IMP concept in the minds of senior managers.

## Final survey instrument

Because we wanted to use a full set of parametric statistical techniques to analyse the data, with the aim of producing the most robust and meaningful inferences, we used continuous scales (1–7 scale, from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7); questions were framed in the format: Our company is extremely good at . . .). Based on the multi-phased development process described above, a final survey instrument was defined to measure the conceptual model’s constructs and subdimensions. ## Data collection and sample description

Although national and cultural differences exist, managers of international companies are challenged to address a common global competitive environment while facing a barrage of new information technologies. To allow us to better understand their common perceptions, a representative international sample of senior managers was used as survey respondents in this study, in accordance with the key informant principle (e.g. Hambrick & Mason, 1984; Huber & Power, 1985). To capture the individual perceptions of senior managers over a large sample of companies and industries, we focused on individual strategic business units (SBUs) as our unit of analysis. An SBU is defined as an autonomous division or organisational unit that is a strategic profit and loss entity, has its own independent leadership and management team, and that is large enough to exercise control over most of the factors affecting its long-term performance. By focusing on individual SBUs, we were able to better generalise our findings, because SBUs – even if part of the same company – are usually different enough to be representative of a unique company (Govindarajan, 1988).

## The role of an ‘organizational informant’ in data collection

In many empirical studies, the measurement of organisational characteristics has typically utilised ‘key informants’. In essence, this method of data collection relies on a selected set of members to provide information about a social setting. Such informants are not chosen at random; rather, they are chosen because they possess special qualifications, such as a particular status, level of experience or specialised knowledge. In survey research, targeted respondents assume the role of a key informant and provide information on an aggregated unit of analysis, by reporting on organisational properties rather than personal attitudes and behaviours (Venkatraman & Walker, 1989). We followed this approach by selecting senior executives as our key informants concerning the effective use of information within their companies.

In focusing on senior managers, we adopted Hambrick & Mason’s (1984) upper-echelons perspective that the organisation becomes a reflection of its top executives. They argue that top management characteristics and functions have a far greater potential for predicting business performance and strategic choices. This view is based on the premise that top managers structure decision situations to fit their view of the world. Other researchers who adopt this perspective include Child (1972), with his strategic choice perspective of organisa tional adaptation, as well as Cyert & March (1963) and Thompson (1967), who refer to ‘dominant coalition’. Studies by Bourgeois (1980), Hrebiniak & Joyce (1985), and Stubbart (1989) also suggest that choices made by top managers influence organisational design outcomes and firm performance.

## Sampling frame and sampling procedure

For this study, potential participant companies were randomly selected from a database of ove 5000 partners, business associates and other companies that had participated in executive programme activities at IMD over the previous 5 years. The study sample of 376 companies represented multiple industries, with varying company sizes, levels of business performance and years of existence. Of the companies invited by direct mail, 103 (27%), signed a Corporate Participation Agreement.

In order to properly capture the uniqueness of each SBU and to maintain the level of analysis at the organisation level, we identified 218 individual SBUs within these 103 companies. In concordance with the key informant principle explained above, we identified the most senior executive within each SBU as the respondent for that particular SBU. In this way, 218 respondents were selected and their answers were entered in the analysis. This sample size satisfies the requirements for SEM analysis and the number of constructs and items, as specified by Pedhazur & Pedhazur-Schmelkin (1991) and Hair et al. (1992).

## Sample profile

All of the 218 senior managers in the study sample held a CEO or a similar position in their respective SBU. The respondents represented 26 countries and 25 industry sectors. They worked for companies that ranged in size from relatively small (500 employees) to very large (+250 000 employees). The study sample included respondents who had held their existing position for less than 1 year to over 16 years, of which the majority (or 62%) had been in thei positions from 1 to 5 years and 55% had been associated with their companies for over 11 years. The sample covered respondents aged from under 35 to over 56. The majority of the respondents (67%) were aged between 41 and 55.

## Statistical data analysis

Our literature review and executive interviews allowed us to identify dimensions and associ ated indicators that provided the definitional context for the IMP construct. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to establish the validity and reliability of our pre-specified factors as well as to test the presence of higher-order factors (Jöreskog & Sörbom, 1989). CFA directly tests the viability of a pre-developed measurement model’s structure (theory) against the observed data (Hurley et al., 1997). Within this frame, a unique solution can be obtained and statistical metrics can be employed to determine the adequacy of the model in capturing the observed covariances. The outcome of this analysis suggested five dimensions that might be useful for defining IMP. Item measures were developed to provide an operational framework for assessing these unique dimensions. Scale reliability was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha values. Cronbach’s alpha values for constructs were 0.68 for sensing, 0.79 for collecting, 0.80 for organising, 0.84 for processing and 0.88 for maintaining, indicating that the scale reliabilities are satisfactory (Nunnally, 1978). ## DISCUSSION

Based on the theoretical development and attribute specification above, we found that senior managers perceive each of the five separate phases of our information management life cycle as distinct and valid measures. We also found that senior managers view these phases and associated practices as part of a higher-level idea that indicates how good they believe their company is in ‘information management practices’. As demonstrated in Figure 2, the study’s results indicate the existence and relative strengths of these ideas associated with $\mathsf { I M P }$ in a sample of senior managers in international companies. In this section, we discuss the findings in more detail to clarify how senior managers perceive IMP.

## The collecting, organising and processing dimensions

As can be seen in Figure 2, the three most ‘traditional’ phases (dimensions) of our factor mode were shown to be discrete ideas in the minds of senior managers. Figure 2 shows that collecting (0.94), organising (0.96) and processing (0.91) had very high factor loadings, indicating that our measures very accurately captured the ideas that managers have concerning each of these dimensions of information management practice. The $R ^ { 2 }$ for each individual construct were very high – 92% for organising, 88% for collecting and 82% for processing;

For the collecting dimension, as indicated in Figure 2, we can see that on the item level. senior managers have a clear understanding that identifving key knowledge sources and training and rewarding people result in better information collection. Companies are now actively experimenting with improved ways of understanding the information needs of employees as well as filtering information to prevent overload. When it comes to organising information, senior managers perceive indexing and classifying information for employees, as well as training and rewarding them for organising information for which they are responsible, more clearly than linking databases so that other business units can access the information they require. One explanation might be that linking customer, product and service databases together is a more recent development enabled by and distributed database management systems, cross-company enterprise resource planning solutions, virtualisation and the Internet. In processing information, each of the information practices identified seems to play a role in improving information use for decision-making, although the senior managers did not recognise hiring people with analytical skills as strongly as they did the other items. Accessing information before decisions, analysing databases to derive knowledge, evaluating, and training and rewarding were all found to be strongly associated with better information processing.

## The maintaining dimension

Figure 2 demonstrates maintaining (0.74) has a moderately high factor loading, but below that of collecting, organising and processing; with relatively strong $R ^ { 2 }$ of 55%. Knowledge management advocates emphasise the need for a company to protect its core knowledge and expertise against loss, degradation or lack of use. This school of thought is urging managers to develop improved capabilities to reuse, update and refresh the valuable explicit knowledge and formal information that the company develops through its operations and decision-making on an ongoing basis. It seems that managers do recognise that the time and financial resources expended in collecting quality information may be wasted if i is not maintained. In fact, if maintenance slips extensively, it may prove more effective to collect the information again than to cleanse badly neglected old data. Thus, while maintaining information is identified as important, a fundamental condition may always persists, namely, managers and organisational members will remain more interested in using information for current problems and decisions than maintaining it for probable future use.

## The sensing dimension

As can be seen in Figure 2, sensing, the relatively newer recognised phase of an information management life cycle, showed a factor loading of 0.42 with an $R ^ { 2 }$ of 17%. Sensing is acceptable, but lower factor loading than the other IMP dimensions, indicates that we have not completely captured all aspects of this less-developed measure of an information management life cycle, providing opportunity for future research to further refine this dimension.

We surmise that sensing information, as an activity, remains more tacit, informal and personal, and is closely tied to the ways people perceive and respond to changes in business conditions and in their personal environment. The strong cognitive component of sensing makes it somewhat more difficult to define as part of a company’s IMP. Of the four items under sensing information in Figure 2, senior managers seem to recognise sensing information on market shifts and customer demands for new products most clearly, followed by competitors’ innovations. Sensing of these information types maybe enabled through established activities of marketing departments and competitive intelligence units. Anticipating problems with suppliers and partners appears to be less understood; perhaps because the practices associated with sensing information from these sources are less transparent, with interorganisational boundaries limiting company employees’ ability to detect this information. Sensing on economic, social and political changes affecting the business seems to be the least developed, or formalised, information practice in the minds of senior managers. Despite the lower factor scores and $R ^ { 2 }$ for this dimension, we believe that sensing will be increasingly recognised as an essential part of the cognitive and perceptual processes influ encing information collection and the definition of new information requirements. Our beliefs our grounded on the fact that if people do not possess the cognitive skills or ability to sense competitive opportunities and threats, the organisation will lack the required information to address emerging strategic challenges. It appears from our findings that specific practices associated with good sensing capabilities are still evolving in management thinking and practice. Companies must learn how to better enable information workers’ about what to sense and how to sift through the overabundance of signals from inside and outside the organisation as well as to better interpret the perceptions of other organisational members.

## IMP

We operationalised IMP through the lens of an information life cycle that uses five salient constructs – sensing, collecting, organising, processing and maintaining information. As dem onstrated in Figure 2, the five phases of the proposed information life cycle represent one parsimonious indicator of critical information processes that exist in a company. The overal measurement model indicates that senior managers seem very comfortable in recognising the four phases (dimensions) of a traditional information management life cycle – collecting, organising, processing and maintaining. Probably based on years of personal experience in making information valuation judgments at each phase, these managers are able to clearly recognise whether their organisations are good at IMP that actually satisfies analytical and decision-making needs. Although somewhat less developed, the information sensing dimen sion seems to be a discrete and vital valuation point within our information management life cycle, which should be more carefully explored in future managerial and academic research. It is anticipated that the IMP measure can be integrated in future organisational level research linking to such factors as information culture and business performance. ${ | \mathsf { M P } ^ { \prime } \xi }$ s dimensions can be practically used as indicators of a company’s goodness in managing information from the senior manager’s perspective.

## CONCLUSIONS AND IMPLICATION OF THE RESEARCH

This research is the first to present an operationalised IMP construct, which was designed to identify and measure the perceptions that managers have regarding the capability of their SBUs in managing information. The key asset that individuals can leverage for their benefit and that of the organisation is their own knowledge (their expertise, experience and skills); they can do this through information processing (the way they express, convey and communicate their knowledge to others). Thus, an underlying issue affecting good corporate IMP is the extent to which managers can influence organisational members to formalise and share their knowledge. The IMP model indicates its dimensions are discrete and are essential parts of a company’s effective management of information. As a measurement tool, the IMP model can be used to check the pulse of an organisation’s effectiveness in using and managing information. Managers should encourage and provide incentives that motivate organisational members to contribute their knowledge as better information managers. If organisational members perceive that their contributions to the information management life cycle advance the purposes of the organisation and their own ‘enlightened’ self-interest, then the phases of the life cycle will be effectively implemented through formal and wellarticulated practices.

While the results of our research paint a clearer picture of good IMP than has previously been depicted, the canvas is not yet completely covered. Based on our research, future work can investigate in more depth the individual stages of the proposed life cycle (e.g. sensing) and how they influence one another in accordance with path dependency theory. Also, the actua outcomes or benefits of having a high capability in IMP can be investigated, and each individual component of the IMP model can be measured in relation to actual benefits such as improved software quality, better requirements definition, reduced costs and development time for projects, and ultimately better business performance. These issues provide a rich set of topics for future knowledge management researchers.
