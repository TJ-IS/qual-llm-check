# How Do Paternalistic Leaders Motivate Employees’ Information Security Compliance? Building a Climate and Applying Sanctions

Jiawen Zhu,<sup>1</sup> Gengzhong Feng,<sup>2</sup> Huigang Liang,<sup>3</sup> Kwok-Leung Tsui<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Xi’an Jiaotong University, China, zhujiawen@mail.xjtu.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>Xi’an Jiaotong University, China, gzfeng@mail.xjtu.edu.cn <sup>3</sup>University of Memphis, USA, hliang1@memphis.edu <sup>4</sup>Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University, USA, kltsui@vt.edu

## Abstract

This paper examines the underlying mechanisms through which paternalistic leadership (PL) motivates employees’ information systems policy (ISP) compliance. We propose that the three dimensions of PL—authoritarian leadership (AL), benevolent leadership (BL), and moral leadership (ML)—influence employees’ ISP compliance by affecting their perceptions of two information security control mechanisms: sanctions and the information security climate. Based on survey data from 760 participants, we found that the impact of AL is partially mediated by employees’ perceptions of sanctions, the impact of BL is partially mediated by employees’ perceptions of the information security climate, and the impact of ML is partially mediated by employees’ perceptions of both sanctions and the information security climate. Our research extends the existing literature by exploring the impact of specific leadership styles on employees’ perceptions of information security control mechanisms and by proposing that perceptions of information security control mechanisms play a mediating role between PL and ISP compliance. The findings suggest that in addition to choosing effective control mechanisms, it is also important for leaders to adjust their leadership style to ensure that employees perceive control mechanisms in the expected manner.

Keywords: Paternalistic Leadership, ISP Compliance, Information Security, Sanction, Information Security Climate

Patrick Y. K. Chau was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on June 13, 2021 and underwent two revisions. Huigang Liang is the corresponding author.

## 1 Introduction

Control mechanisms refer to attempts and efforts on the part of management to ensure that employees act in ways consistent with organizational goals (Ouchi & Maguire, 1975; Ouchi, 1977; Ouchi, 1979). In the IS literature, control mechanisms have been recognized to be crucial to the success of IT programs (Deak, 1978; Kling & Iacono, 1984; Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch et al., 2002; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 2004). ISP compliance research has also verified the fact that information security control mechanisms, such as sanctions, rewards, climate, and social control, all contribute to employees’ ISP compliance (e.g. Chan et al., 2005; Chen et al., 2012; Hsu et al., 2015; Johnston et al., 2015; Trang & Brendel, 2019). Since employees are often reluctant to comply with ISP requirements because of the work impediments caused by ISP compliance (Chen et al., 2012), it is common for organizations to use external forces to regulate employees’ ISP compliance, which explains the frequent adoption of information security control mechanisms.

Control mechanisms adopted to regulate or promote employees’ ISP compliance, such as human resources (HR) practices, may be perceived differently by various employees (Liao et al., 2009). Wright and Nishii (2007) posit that HR practices exist objectively but must be perceived and interpreted subjectively by employees in order to function. According to general deterrence theory (Gibbs, 1975), the function of these control mechanisms relies on employees’ subjective interpretations. That is, employees’ perception of these control mechanisms (e.g., perceptions regarding the severity of the consequences of sanctions) affects their behavior. Due to changing environments and individuals’ continuous social learning, these subjective perceptions are not static and may change over time (Thornberry, 1987). For example, employees’ appraisal of sanctions may be diminished every time they notice management neglecting deviant behaviors (Xue et al., 2011). However, the majority of existing ISP compliance literature has focused only on verifying the effectiveness of these control mechanisms (Moody et al., 2018), and less attention has been paid to how perceptions of control mechanisms are formed and how such formation can be influenced or maintained.

In the rare literature investigating the antecedents of employees’ perceptions of such control mechanisms, researchers have mostly focused on the influence of information security countermeasures, such as monitoring techniques and information security education, training, and awareness (SETA) programs (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Hovav and D’Arcy, 2012) and specific management acts (Chan et al., 2005) that primarily raise employees’ awareness of the existence of control mechanisms (Siponen et al., 2022). We argue that in addition to countermeasures, employees’ interpretation of control mechanisms can be affected by many other elements, such as the objective properties of control mechanisms (Ouchi, 1979; Johnston et al., 2015), the organizational context surrounding the individual (Ghoshal & Bartlett, 1994), and the individual’s direct and indirect experience of the implementation of control mechanisms (Warkentin et al., 2011; Mutchler & Warkentin, 2020).

Such a broad range of precursors indicates the potential impact of organizational leaders on employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms. Namely, leaders are the decision makers who decide which control mechanisms to adopt, i.e., the objective properties of such mechanisms and methods for facilitating and implementing them (Kankanhalli et al., 2003; Veiga & Eloff, 2007). In addition, the acts of such leaders nurture the organizational context that establishes a form of embedded work ethic to guide employees’ behavior (Ghoshal & Bartlett, 1994). Moreover, different leadership styles have different management philosophies and thus may prefer different ways of implementing leaders’ plans and motivating employees (Westwood, 1992; Zohar, 2002). Therefore, our research adopts a leadership-style lens to explain how employees perceive control mechanisms.

We focus on the paternalistic leadership (PL) style (Farh et al., 2000; Cheng et al., 2004) for the following reasons. First, the relationship between PL and employee compliance has long been recognized but is rarely examined in a specific context, and its mode of influence remains unexplored. Second, findings in the existing literature have shown that the three dimensions of PL—authoritarian leadership (AL), benevolent leadership (BL), and moral leadership (ML)—can affect employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms. For example, the strictness of AL, the kindness of BL, and the righteousness of ML may affect employees’ expectations regarding the consequences of control mechanisms (Xue et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2012). Third, PL may be more effective than other single-dimensional leadership styles in motivating ISP compliance. The three PL dimensions may affect the ways in which employees perceive formal and informal control mechanisms, both of which are essential to ensure the success of the ISP (Hsu et al., 2015; Guhr et al., 2019).

In this research, we mainly focus on two information security control mechanisms—sanctions and information security climate. Sanctions are one of the most frequently adopted formal control mechanisms (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2013; Johnston et al., 2015). Organizations need to clearly articulate the rules regarding sanctions for deviant behavior to create enough external pressure to motivate employees’ ISP compliance (Deci & Ryan, 1985). In addition, in the context of all formal control mechanisms, such as articulated behavioral rules and desired outcomes in the organization’s formal documents (Ouchi, 1977; Ouchi, 1979), sanctions are generally the next step when deviant behavior is detected. No policies or regulations take effect unless sanction mechanisms are appropriately articulated and implemented to complete the control feedback loop (Liang et al., 2013).

We also selected information security climate for our research because this organizational value can help employees judge whether their behavior is appropriate when formal instructions are not present (Ouchi & Maguire, 1975; Hsu et al., 2015). More importantly, the information security climate serves as the basis for the formation of other informal control mechanisms, such as culture, social norms, and personal norms. For example, researchers have pointed out that climate is a reflection of the current state of culture, which is less observable and more difficult to interpret (Mearns & Flin, 1999; Mearns et al., 2003). To reveal the underlying culture, the climate needs to be understood first (Denison, 1996). Based on norm activation theory,

Yazdanmehr and Wang (2016) argued that both social norms and personal norms need to be activated by corresponding organizational climates. Dang-Pham et al. (2016) also posited that the security climate helps to establish norms relating to security behavior.

Therefore, motivated by the theoretical and practical importance of leadership style, control mechanism, and security compliance, we constructed a research model to reveal how PL affects employees’ perceptions of sanctions and the information security climate and then further improves their ISP compliance. This research aims to contribute to the existing literature in the following ways. First, the findings enrich our understanding of how employees’ perceptions of information security control mechanisms are formed. Second, we adopt employees’ perceptions of sanctions and the information security climate to explain the influence of PL on ISP compliance, which offers us a new perspective revealing how leaders can motivate ISP compliance. Third, we expand the knowledge about the impact of PL.

The rest of our paper proceeds as follows. In the following section, we provide the theoretical basis for the paper. Then, we propose the research model and elaborate our hypotheses. Next, we describe the construct operationalization, survey design, and data collection. Subsequently, we present the model tests and discuss the findings, theoretical contributions, and managerial implications. Finally, we discuss limitations and future research avenues and then conclude the paper.

## 2 Theoretical Foundation

## 2.1 Information Security Control Mechanisms

In addition to enacting ISPs and directly requiring employees to comply with them, organizations also initiate measures to ensure employees’ ISP compliance. Ouchi and Maguire (1975) named these measures control mechanisms, which describe the attempts and efforts on the part of management to ensure that employees act in ways that lead to the achievement of organizational objectives. The core reason for the adoption of control mechanisms to motivate ISP compliance is that employees are often reluctant to comply with ISPs due to work impediments caused by ISP compliance (Herath & Rao, 2009b, Chen et al., 2012) and the non-valueadding nature of ISP compliance (Chan et al., 2005).

Currently, most of the literature assumes that the effectiveness of control mechanisms depends on employees’ subjective perceptions of control mechanisms, which then generate the constraining power to curb employees’ ISP violations (Gibbs, 1975). For instance, researchers have studied how employees’ perceptions of formal control mechanisms, such as the perceived severity of sanctions, certainty of sanctions, and swiftness of sanctions, affect their ISP compliance (Herath & Rao, 2009a, Herath & Rao, 2009b, Hu et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2012; Johnston et al., 2015). Other researchers have focused on investigating employees perceptions of informal control mechanisms, such as clan control mechanisms, which include perceptions of the information security climate and culture (Chan et al., 2005; Knapp et al., 2006; Jaafar & Ajis, 2013; Goo et al., 2014; Dang-Pham et al., 2015), and self-control mechanisms, which include moral beliefs, normative beliefs, shame and social bonds (Lee et al., 2004; Siponen & Vance, 2010; Hu et al., 2011; Cheng et al., 2013; Ifinedo, 2014). Some scholars have further examined the joint or interactive effects of formal and informal control mechanisms in motivating ISP compliance (Herath & Rao, 2009a, Hu et al., 2011; Hovav & D’Arcy, 2012; Johnston et al., 2015).

## 2.2 How Employees Perceive Control Mechanisms

Despite the extensive attention devoted to the impact of employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms on ISP compliance, little is known about the antecedents of these perceptions. Identifying the antecedents of employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms is important because such perceptions are subjective and thus subject to the influence of many processes and factors that evolve over time (Thornberry, 1987). Research also suggests that understanding the variability of perceptions of HR practices among employees offers an important perspective that can reveal the effects of focal HR practices (Liao et al., 2009; Li et al., 2018).

In this research, we draw on general deterrence theory (GDT) (Gibbs, 1975) to explain the formation of employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms. We argue that employees’ perceptions of a certain control mechanism are mainly generated by four aspects of their work settings: (1) the objective properties of the control mechanism, (2) the information security countermeasures established to facilitate the control mechanisms, (3) the organizational context in which employees interpret the control mechanism, and (4) employees’ own experience of the control mechanism.

Initially, employees’ perceptions of certain control mechanisms are affected by the objective properties of control mechanisms or the objective actions that constitute control mechanisms (Gibbs, 1975). For formal control mechanisms, objective properties can be the form and intensity of control mechanisms specified in organizational files, e.g., how deviant behavior is punished by oral reprimands, fines, demotion, or firing (Ouchi & Maguire, 1975). For informal control mechanisms, visible management acts can create constraining power over individuals (Hellriegel & Slocum, 1974; James & Jones, 1974). Indeed, it has been argued that the core and fundamental assumption of GDT is that the properties of control mechanisms can only be subjectively perceived (Gibbs, 1975; Paternoster, 2010).

Second, organizations utilize information security countermeasures, such as procedural and technical monitoring countermeasures (Hovav & D’Arcy, 2012), SETA programs (Yoo et al., 2018), peer monitoring (Yazdanmehr et al., 2020; Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2021), and other information security communications and activities (Cram et al., 2021) to help employees become aware of the existence of control mechanisms and understand how a control mechanism works. Namely, simply listing rules in a document does not automatically lead to change (Peace et al., 2003). Organizations need to ensure that employees are aware of the existence of control mechanisms and that they have knowledge concerning various permitted or forbidden actions (Gibbs, 1975). Jaeger et al. (2021) argue that employees’ awareness of the information security policy can affect how they perceive multilevel sanctions in their organization.

Third, employees also rely on organizational context to interpret control mechanisms. According to Ghoshal and Bartlett (1994), organizational context refers to the form of embedded work ethic in performance and social relationship management, which offers a rationale for employees to behave in a certain way. In our research, for example, employees may speculate on whether a claimed formal control mechanism will truly be implemented based on their perceptions of the organizational context regarding performance management, i.e., the degree to which their organization values discipline and the way in which their organization generally deals with deviant behaviors (Gibbs, 1975; Ghoshal & Bartlett, 1994; Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). From the perspective of social relationship management, an organizational context characterized by trust and support, which cultivates employees’ willingness to cooperate and their commitment to collective interests (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004), may affect how employees perceive the constraining power of informal control mechanisms.

Fourth, after directly or indirectly experiencing the implementation of control mechanisms, employees become aware of the real power of control mechanisms. According to Gibbs (1975), by personally experiencing punishment or witnessing others being punished, employees develop clear knowledge about whether penalties will actually be enforced and how severe the actual consequences will be in the context of informal control mechanisms. Employees thus adjust their previously developed perceptions regarding the corresponding control mechanisms accordingly, and such perceptions continue to evolve as employees experience or witness more implementations of the control mechanisms (Siponen et al., 2022). The fact that employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms are dynamic and constantly evolving indicates the necessity of exploring the antecedents of control mechanism perceptions, which may guide managers in cultivating and maintaining employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms in order to regulate their behavior.

A few studies have investigated the antecedents of employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms. Hovav and D’Arcy (2012) examined the impact of procedural and technical countermeasures on employees perceptions of informal and formal sanctions. D’Arcy et al. (2009) found that user awareness of information security countermeasures, e.g., security policies, SETA programs, and computer monitoring, directly influence employees’ perceptions of certainty and the severity of the organizational sanctions. However, these researchers focused mainly on how information security countermeasures affect employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms but provided limited information regarding why employees have various interpretations of the same control mechanisms.

## 2.2.1 The Impact of Leaders

In this research, we argue that leaders play an important role in determining employees’ perceptions of information security control mechanisms because all four aspects mentioned above are influenced by leaders. According to Ouchi (1977), control mechanisms for IT projects can be categorized as formal or informal. Formal control refers to written and management-initiated mechanisms that prescribe the ways in which employees should behave. Informal control refers to unwritten and worker-initiated mechanisms that identify acceptable behaviors for employees but have little coercive power to ensure obedience (Ouchi, 1979). Both formal and informal control mechanisms have been investigated in IS studies (Kling & Iacono, 1984; Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1997; Kirsch et al., 2002; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 2004). Since different leadership styles relate differently to these four aspects, we propose that leadership style may affect employees’ perceptions of formal and informal information security control mechanisms. Specifically, we identify sanctions for ISP violations from the perspective of formal information security control mechanisms and information security climate from the perspective of informal information security control mechanisms to illustrate how leadership styles influence employees’ perceptions of different types of information security control mechanisms.

## 2.2.2 How Leaders Affect Perceptions of Sanctions

Research suggests that sanctions, i.e., punishments and penalties, are a primary and effective formal control mechanism for reducing employees’ IT misuse (Straub, 1990). Sanction perceptions include perceptions regarding sanction severity and sanction certainty. Sanction severity refers to the individual’s perceptions of the harshness of punishment for deviant behaviors. Sanction certainty refers to the individual’s perceived probability that deviant behaviors will be punished. According to general deterrence theory (GDT) (Gibbs, 1975), when high sanction severity and certainty are perceived, unacceptable behavior decreases. GDT is based on the notion that people make rational choices. Thus, when fear and anxiety regarding potential sanctions outweigh the potential benefits of misconduct, people modify their behavior to reduce misconduct. Numerous studies have confirmed the effectiveness of sanctions in motivating employees’ ISP compliance (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Herath & Rao, 2009a; Herath & Rao, 2009b; Siponen & Vance, 2010; Hu et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2012; Hovav & D’Arcy, 2012; Johnston et al., 2015). Researchers have also shown that organizations can utilize other measures, such as procedural countermeasures (security policies and SETA programs) and technical countermeasures (monitoring and auditing), to increase employees’ perceptions of sanction severity and certainty, which may further deter ISP violations (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Hovav & D’Arcy, 2012).

Leaders can affect how employees perceive sanctions in the following ways. First, leaders with different leadership styles may hold different attitudes regarding whether sanctions should be used, the proper form of sanctions, and how much loss should be faced by deviant employees (Kelman & Hong, 2016). For example, leaders who intend to do whatever is necessary to achieve desired outcomes may be more likely to use harsh punishment than leaders who care about the feelings of their employees (Wu & Tsai, 2012). As described by Truss et al. (1997), leaders who adopt a hard approach prefer to utilize tight control strategies to pressure employees into behaving properly, such as monitoring and punishment, whereas leaders who adopt a soft approach prefer not to compel their employees by using authority but to influence employees by nurturing their commitment to work (Truss et al., 1997; Kelman & Hong, 2016).

Second, leaders may also have different preferences in adopting information security countermeasures to facilitate the implementation of sanctions. For example, active and considerate leaders may frequently use emails, newsletters, and briefings to disseminate information regarding sanctions as appeals to fear to prevent noncompliance (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Johnston et al., 2015). Without these efforts, employees will be less informed about the sanctions they may face for ISP noncompliance. In addition, some leaders may believe that close supervision, such as monitoring employees’ internet activities and emails, is unfair to employees, while other leaders may consider such countermeasures to be acceptable (Falkenberg & Herremans, 1995; Brown & Trevino, 2006).

Third, the organizational context cultivated by different leaders can also affect how employees perceive sanctions. Even if employees have not experienced sanctions themselves, they develop expectations regarding the certainty and severity of sanctions (Xue et al., 2011). For example, according to Ghoshal and Bartlett (1994), in a disciplined organizational context in which management establishes clear standards and goals with fine-grained information, has consistent principles for punishing underperformance, and values swift feedback (Ghoshal & Bartlett, 1994; Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004), employees may perceive high certainty that sanctions will be implemented. Leaders’ commitment to maintaining justice within their organizations can also contribute to employees’ expectations regarding sanctions (Xue et al., 2011).

Fourth, leadership style may also affect how sanctions are implemented and therefore how employees perceive sanctions. According to Podsakoff et al. (2006), whether leaders implement punishment consistently for every deviant behavior and every deviant employee and whether all leaders within the organization behave consistently in punishing the same deviant behavior may greatly affect the sanction certainty perceptions of employees. Xu et al. (2021) argued that abusive supervision, which is often accompanied by strong emotions, will cause employees to perceive that punishment is likely and the consequences severe. Meanwhile, different leadership styles can trigger different emotions in employees in the workplace. For example, Chen et al. (2019) posited that leaders’ compulsive behavior can trigger negative feelings such as fear and anger in employees, which have been found to affect employees’ perceptions of sanctions (Xu et al., 2020).

## 2.2.3 How Leaders Affect Perceptions of the Information Security Climate

Climate is a control mechanism that is commonly used in organizations (James & Jones, 1974). Kankanhalli et al. (2003) posited that the organizational climate is different from any specific organizational attribute or effort applied to influence employees’ behavior, including deterrence, prevention, or enforcement. In contrast, organization climate is a perceived hidden value generated from all these organizational efforts and serves as an invisible hand guiding employees behavior. Information security climate perceptions refer to individuals’ perceptions of the value of information security via organizational policies, procedures, and practices in their work environments (Zohar, 1980; Flin et al., 2000; Chan et al., 2005; Goo et al., 2014).

The concept of information security climate was first proposed by Chan et al. (2005), based on the notion of safety climate (Zohar, 1980), which has been shown to be a strong predictor of safety outcomes (Clarke, 2006) such as employees’ safety compliance behavior (Neal et al., 2000; Dejoy et al., 2004; Clarke, 2006) and lower accident rates (Zohar, 2002; Zohar & Luria, 2004; Clarke, 2006). Inspired by the safety climate literature, IS researchers have examined and confirmed the role of the information security climate in motivating employees’ ISP compliance (Chan et al., 2005; Jaafar & Ajis, 2013; Goo et al., 2014; Dang-Pham et al., 2015; Dang-Pham et al., 2015; Shih & Liou, 2015). However, with few exceptions (Chan et al., 2005; Goo et al., 2014), past research has largely ignored the antecedents of the information security climate. Chan et al. (2005) found that employees’ observations of upper management practices, direct supervisory practices, and coworker socialization have significant impacts on the perceived information security climate. Goo et al. (2014) showed the impact of top management attention, security enforcement, security policy, and security awareness training on the formation of the perceived information security climate. Both studies highlighted the importance of top management and leaders in this context.

Leaders can affect how employees perceive the information security climate in the following ways. First, the intensity of the information security climate depends on leaders’ participation and commitment (Kessler et al., 2020). Goo et al. (2014) posited that top management attention is an essential contributor to the information security climate. This is because sufficient top management attention can lead to the allocation of more financial and technical resources (Kankanhalli et al., 2003). Chan et al. (2005) found that direct supervisory practices positively affect employees’ perceived information security climate. If employees repeatedly see their leaders emphasize performance over information security, they will perceive that information security is not valued in their organization (Chan et al., 2005). Evidence in the safety climate literature has also shown that supervisory actions (Zohar, 2002), management value (Neal et al., 2000), management support (Flin et al., 2000), and management commitment and attitude (Zohar, 1980) can significantly impact employees’ perceptions regarding the importance of security over other competing work demands (Clarke, 2010). Therefore, leaders are important icons that employees use to understand the attitude of their organization toward information security (Chan et al., 2005; Jaafar & Ajis, 2013). The strictness, devotion, activity, and tolerance of leaders demonstrated in information security campaigns may all serve as signals to employees concerning the degree to which their organizations value information security and may affect employees perceptions of the information security climate.

Second, different leaders may rely on different tactics to facilitate the formation of an information security climate. The ways in which leaders enforce ISP, design ISP, and organize ISP training may all impact the formation of an information security climate (Goo et al., 2014). For example, if a leader is close to employees, praises them in a timely manner for ISPcompliant behavior (Chan et al., 2005; Goo et al., 2014), demonstrates trust and confidence in their information security performance (Balozian et al., 2019), and regularly communicates with them about information security issues (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010), employees will be more likely to understand the importance of information security for their organization. The safety climate literature suggests that leaders who develop high-quality relationships with employees (Hofmann et al., 2003), clearly express role expectations and priorities (Clarke, 2013; Zohar & Polachek, 2014), design cooperative work environments (Grojean et al., 2004), and frequently communicate with employees regarding underlying organizational values (Kuenzi and Schminke, 2009; Clarke, 2013) contribute to employees’ perception of the safety climate of their organization. Therefore, In our research context, leaders’ willingness to support, guide, and initiate open communication concerning information security issues may also affect employees perceptions of the information security climate.

Third, leaders can also influence the information security climate via the organizational context they cultivate. According to Ghoshal and Bartlett (1994), management acts, such as inviting employees to participate in decision-making, staffing positions with competent people, and granting employees autonomy in work contexts, help form an organizational context of trust and support, which can endow employees with a sense of belonging and identification with the organization. In such work contexts, employees tend to act voluntarily to protect the information security of their organization as a means of support and reciprocation for the activities of their leaders and the organization (Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016). Feelings of uncertainty, complexity, and overload by employees during their ISP compliance are more likely to be positively addressed (D’Arcy et al., 2014; D’Arcy et al., 2018) due to mutual help and support among members of the organization (Hsu et al., 2015; Yoo et al., 2020). Thus, this type of work context contributes to the formation of a strong information security climate, and without such an organizational context, employees may feel less of a bond with their organization and may act in their own best interests rather than in the benefit of the organization (Ghoshal & Bartlett, 1994).

Fourth, the reactions of leaders when someone deviates from the information security climate also impact employees’ perceptions of the information security climate. Unlike articulated formal control mechanisms, there are no written rules concerning how to deal with employees who deviate from the organizational climate (Ouchi & Maguire, 1975). Leaders need to make their own decisions regarding how to rectify employees’ behavior, and different leadership styles may lead to different choices. For example, leaders who prefer management by exception may ignore such violations because no formal rules are violated (Ricketts & Nelson, 1987). In contrast, leaders who emphasize absolute control may still penalize the deviance even though the deviant behavior in question is not described in formal documents (Farh et al., 2000). Some leaders also prefer peaceful conversations intended to persuade employees not to act in ways that may harm the information security of the organization (Barlow et al., 2018). All these types of leader actions are important signals to employees regarding how their organization values information security, and employees’ perceptions of these signals thus collectively shape the information security climate.

## 2.3 Paternalistic Leadership

The paternalistic leadership style (Westwood, 1992) in the field of management is based on leaders acting as parental figures in exercising their power and authority to motivate subordinates to improve their personal and professional lives (Weber, 1968; Gelfand et al., 2007). In this framework, if employees then play the part of compliant children, their behavior becomes more controllable (Kerfoot & Knights, 1993; Zhang et al., 2015). Farh and Cheng (2000) define PL as a leadership style that combines strong discipline and authority, parental benevolence, and moral integrity and propose a triad model of PL comprising three dimensions: authoritarian leadership, benevolent leadership, and moral leadership (Farh et al., 2000). This triad model of PL has been widely adopted in the field of PL studies (Pellegrini & Scandura, 2008; Lee et al., 2017; Mansur et al., 2017). Authoritarian leadership stresses absolute control over subordinates and demands unquestioning obedience (Farh et al., 2000; Cheng et al., 2004). Benevolent leadership reflects individualized and holistic concern for subordinates’ well-being both within and outside of the work setting (Farh et al., 2000; Cheng et al., 2004). Moral leadership acts according to a code of ethics. Moral leaders demonstrate the virtues of integrity, altruism, prudence, unselfishness, and self-discipline (Farh et al., 2000; Cheng et al., 2004).

The relationship between PL and employee compliance has long been recognized in the PL literature (Aycan et al., 2000; Cheng et al., 2004; Aycan, 2006; Farh, 2006; Pellegrini & Scandura, 2006). Paternalistic leaders act like parental figures in their work roles (Cheng et al., 2004). Such leaders hold higher authority than employees, take care of employees, and set good examples for employees. Ideally, employees’ compliance with paternalistic leaders is similar to that of children obeying their parents. Weber (2009) argued that employees owe loyalty and compliance to leaders only by virtue of their status. Aycan et al. (1999) suggested that this natural willingness of employees to be obedient and loyal proceeds from leaders’ efforts to care for and protect their employees, which relieves employees’ stress, making them feel more relaxed at work and more willing to comply with leaders (Kerfoot & Knights, 1993).

However, existing findings concerning the relationship between PL dimensions and employee compliance have been mixed. For example, Cheng et al. (2004) argued that different PL dimensions elicit different psychological responses from employees. Compliance without dissent is the result of AL, BL fosters gratitude and repayment, and ML mainly induces employees identification with and imitation of their leaders. However, the results of this research have shown that all PL dimensions positively influence employees compliance. Niu et al. (2009) suggested that all PL dimensions contribute to employees’ compliance. However, they showed that BL and ML have a significant impact on employees’ compliance with leaders while AL does not. Moreover, the findings of general compliance studies may not be applicable to specific contexts (Aurigemma & Mattson, 2019). A high general compliance score does not entail high compliance in the context of ISP. Employees who are noncompliant regarding ISPs but compliant with most other policies will still report high scores in general compliance. Therefore, the findings of general compliance research have limited generalizability in the information security context. It should also be noted that existing findings have provided only limited explanations regarding how PL affects employees’ ISP compliance. Existing research has not considered the uniqueness of ISP compliance, such as work-security conflicts and the non-value-adding nature of ISP compliance (Chen et al., 2012; Feng et al., 2019). Likewise, the mode of influence between PL and ISP compliance remains unclear (Pellegrini & Scandura, 2008). Existing studies have generally assumed that employees’ compliance is primarily based on fear or respect for leaders’ authoritative power, reciprocation for leaders’ benevolence, or recognition of leaders morality (Pellegrini & Scandura, 2008; Niu et al., 2009). We argue that the impact of leaders may be broader than those factors. For example, leaders attitudes, efforts, and acts may affect employees’ cognitions, perceptions, or beliefs regarding ISP compliance and thus motivate ISP compliance.

While many leadership styles may affect employees’ ISP compliance, we focus on PL for two reasons. First, PL dimensions can influence employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms. For example, authoritarian leaders who emphasize absolute control over subordinates and require everything to be done in the best way according to their own vision (Cheng et al., 2004; Wu & Tsai, 2012) and moral leaders who value justice and live up to their words (Cheng et al., 2004; Niu et al., 2009) may be more likely to strictly implement the prescribed penalties for ISP violations, which may strengthen employees’ perceptions regarding the certainty/severity of sanctions for ISP violations. Likewise, benevolent leaders who develop high-quality interpersonal relationships with their employees (Farh et al., 2000; Wu et al., 2012) and moral leaders who are recognized by subordinates for their moral behavior (Cheng et al., 2004) may cultivate a sense of belonging and responsibility to the organization among employees (Chan et al., 2013), which may contribute to the formation of the information security climate (Chan et al., 2005). Second, we contend that PL is more effective than other single-dimensional leadership styles in motivating ISP compliance. Namely, ISPs cannot list every proper or improper action regarding information security protection (Son, 2011; Xue et al., 2011). Employees are expected not only to strictly follow the explicit rules of the ISP but also to act prudently in information security-related situations that are not specified by the ISP (Guhr et al., 2019). Hsu et al. (2015) demonstrated that to motivate extra-role ISP compliance, both formal and informal control mechanisms must be in place. PL, consisting of AL, BL, and ML, is a multifaceted leadership style that can influence employees’ perceptions of both types of control mechanisms, which makes PL an effective leadership style for motivating employees’ ISP compliance.

Employees’ perceptions of sanctions and the information security climate are adopted as mediators between paternalistic leadership dimensions and ISP compliance.

Authoritarian leaders emphasize authoritative power and control over subordinates and require unquestioning obedience from subordinates (Farh and Cheng, 2000; Farh et al., 2000). We argue that employees’ ISP compliance is similar to compliance with any other policy, for which absolute obedience is demanded by authoritarian leaders (Chen, 2013; Cheng & Wang, 2015). In addition to assuming authority and control, authoritarian leaders are characterized as powerfully overbearing, rigorous and doctrinal (Erben & Guneser, 2008), which may induce fear in employees and encourage them to behave as expected (Farh, 2006). However, information security practices specified by ISPs often conflict with or impede other operational demands (Zohar, 2010) and may be ignored to prioritize more profitable organizational objectives (Herath & Rao, 2009b). Although authoritarian leaders’ insistence on high levels of performance motivates employees to accomplish assigned tasks (Niu et al., 2009), the strict control associated with AL also signals the importance of ISP compliance, which would be expected to reduce employees’ hesitation and reluctance to comply with ISPs because of the time or effort required (Boss et al., 2009). Therefore, we propose the following:

## H1a: Authoritarian leadership positively influences employees’ ISP compliance intention.

Benevolent leaders show holistic concern for employees’ well-being both at work and in their personal lives (Farh & Cheng, 2000; Farh et al., 2000). Therefore, the main reason for employees’ compliance in this case would be gratitude and reciprocation of the kindness of benevolent leaders (Cheng et al., 2004). In addition, both within and outside of work settings, benevolent leaders seek to take good care of their subordinates, which helps them gain recognition and respect from subordinates (Cheng et al., 2004; Chan et al., 2013), leading employees to express agreement with their leaders’ decisions, support their policies, and comply as expected. In the IT security context, information security compliance may be driven by employees’ willingness to support organizational interests (Herath & Rao, 2009b). Chan et al. (2013) and Mussolino and Calabro (2014)) suggested that under benevolent leaders, employees feel that they are group members and feel a close connection to their organization. Since benevolent leaders can cultivate shifts from self-interested goals to collective goals among employees (Wu & Tsai, 2012), they may be willing to comply with the ISP to protect organizational interests.

Moreover, employees consider benevolent leaders to be trustworthy and are likely more willing to share difficulties they encounter at work with them, such as problems related to ISP compliance (Chan, 2014; Chen et al., 2015). Benevolent leaders are generally sensitive to employees’ needs and interested in offering help (Wu et al., 2012). For example, if employees report that they lack the knowledge and skill to deal with safety issues, benevolent leaders may provide them with training and education to enhance their capabilities, discover their potential, and increase their confidence, which may greatly reduce behavioral barriers to employees’ ISP compliance (Chan, 2014; Mussolino & Calabro, 2014). If employees report difficulties balancing ISP compliance with other work demands, benevolent leaders will likely show understanding and help them solve the problem (Cheng et al., 2004; Chan, 2014; Cheng & Wang, 2015)—for example, by revising task procedures to ensure that subordinates can comply with the ISP and also accomplish other assigned tasks. Therefore, we propose the following:

## H1b: Benevolent leadership positively influences employees’ ISP compliance intention.

Moral leaders align their behavior with moral standards and demonstrate integrity and self-discipline (Farh & Cheng, 2000; Farh et al., 2000). Employees thus often view such leaders as role models and seek to internalize their values and imitate their behaviors (Cheng et al., 2004; Wu et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2014). Since moral leaders comply with ISPs to protect organizational information security, employees learn from them and likewise comply (Cheng et al., 2004). Moral leaders work unselfishly to serve their organization, which cultivates an ethical work norm that encourages employees to pursue collective interests and fosters employees’ sense of duty and normative commitment to their organization (Erben & Guneser, 2008; Cheng & Wang, 2015). Thus, employees may voluntarily comply with the ISP because they feel morally obligated to protect organizational interests.

Moral leaders also initiate respectful interactions with employees (Zhang et al., 2015). They treat employees with sincerity, respect, and fairness; keep their promises; refuse to take advantage of employees; and are even willing to sacrifice their personal interests for those of employees (Niu et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2012). As a result, employees are aware of the virtuous intentions of their moral leaders, have confidence in their moral leaders’ judgment concerning information security issues, and will be likely to support the policies advocated by their leaders (Chan, 2014). Moreover, since they feel well-treated and valued by moral leaders, employees may be willing to spend more time on both in-role and extrarole tasks (Wu et al., 2012). Even though ISP compliance causes inconvenience and does not contribute to the assessment of work performance, employees under moral leadership are likely willing to comply anyway. Hence, we propose the following:

## H1c: Moral leadership positively influences employees’ ISP compliance intention.

According to general deterrence theory (Gibbs, 1975), the perceived severity and certainty of sanctions can deter undesired behaviors. The greater an individual perceives the severity and certainty of sanctions to be, the less likely they are to engage in deviant behavior. This theory has been widely adopted to predict criminal, antisocial, and deviant behavior in the workplace (D’Arcy et al., 2009). In the IT security context, Straub (1990) noted that deterrence measures are useful strategies to deter computer abuse. Peace et al. (2003) found that deterrence measures can reduce employees’ intention to copy illicit software. Based on this work, researchers have found that sanction perceptions are positively related to employees’ ISP compliance (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Herath & Rao, 2009b, Hu et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2012; Johnston et al., 2015). When employees perceive that the severity of punishment for noncompliance is high, involving demotion, loss of job, heavy fines, or other serious consequences, they are more likely to comply to avoid punishment (Herath & Rao, 2009a). Meanwhile, GDT suggests that employees need to be made aware of organizational efforts to control deviant behavior (Gibbs, 1975; Straub, 1990). When potential violators notice the monitoring and surveillance measures employed by their organization, such as the installation of detection software and the examination of computer history and internet logs, they perceive a high probability of being caught and punished (Kankanhalli et al., 2003; Herath & Rao, 2009a). As a result, their intention to violate the ISP likely decreases. Therefore, we propose the following:

## H2: Employees’ perceptions of sanctions positively influence their ISP compliance intention.

According to Campbell et al. (1970), climate is a perceptual medium through which the objective characteristics of the work environment are translated into employees’ behavior. This interpretation highlights the potential impact of climate on employees’ behavior. The concept of the information security climate originates from the safety climate literature and has been found to be a strong predictor of safety behavior and accident rates (Zohar, 1980; Zohar, 2002; Zohar, 2002; Zohar & Luria, 2004; Zohar & Tenne-Gazit, 2008; Zohar & Polachek, 2014). Safety climate is positively related to safety compliance (Griffin & Neal, 2000; Clarke, 2006; Kuenzi & Schminke, 2009; Niskanen et al., 2012) because safety climate reflects the degree to which safety is valued by the organization and thus informs employees regarding what behaviors are expected and permitted, especially when formal rules are absent (Zohar, 1980; Zohar, 2002; Zohar & Luria, 2003). In the context of a strong safety climate, to maintain their identity and gain supervisory approval, employees tend to engage in more safety behaviors (Zohar & Luria, 2003). In addition, Clarke (2006) argued that a safe climate can raise employees’ level of awareness of knowledge about the importance of safety and safety measures abd reduce their skepticism regarding the importance and efficacy of such measures. Employees with higher scores in safety climate perceptions are more likely to work safely (Mearns et al., 2003). Based on the same logic, the information security climate can have an impact on employees’ ISP compliance (Chan et al., 2005; Jaafar & Ajis, 2013; Goo et al., 2014; Dang-Pham et al., 2015; Dang-Pham et al., 2015; Shih & Liou, 2015; Dang-Pham et al., 2016). Goo et al. (2014) showed that an information security climate encourages employees to understand their responsibilities more clearly and develop dispositions toward information security actions. Shih and Liou (2015) demonstrated that an effective information security climate can facilitate the formation of employees’ security work habits and strengthen their awareness of information security, which can increase their intention to comply with ISPs. Therefore, we propose the following:

## H3: Employees’ perceptions of the information security climate positively influence their ISP compliance intention.

Punishment and monitoring are often applied by authoritarian leaders as major control mechanisms to ensure employee obedience (Erben & Guneser, 2008; Pellegrini & Scandura, 2008; Chan, 2014). According to Pellegrini and Scandura (2008), authoritarian leaders support Theory X (MacGregor, 1960), which posits that employees inherently dislike work and must be controlled because they would otherwise not work properly. Therefore, authoritarian leaders tend to adopt rigid control strategies such as rewards and punishments to ensure employee compliance (Farmer et al., 1997; Kelman & Hong, 2016; Zheng et al., 2021). Moreover, Farh (2006) found that due to their strict control and lack of attention to employees’ wellbeing, authoritarian leaders incur negative feelings among subordinates, which causes employees to be emotionally distant from authoritarian leaders and develop low organizational commitment (Pellegrini & Scandura, 2008; Wu & Tsai, 2012). However, authoritarian leaders are mostly concerned with getting things done and tend to ignore the feelings of their subordinates (Wu & Tsai, 2012). Hence, authoritarian leaders may employ harsh punishments to achieve their goals regardless of negative reactions among employees (Farh, 2006; Zhang et al., 2015). In such situations, employees often lack intrinsic motivation to protect their organization. This situation results in a vicious cycle in which authoritarian leaders apply ever more punishment to ensure compliance rather than relying on soft-control measures or employees’ selfcontrol (Chen et al., 2014). To strengthen their rigid control strategies, authoritarian leaders often deploy surveillance systems to closely monitor and assess the performance of their subordinates, which may increase employees’ perceptions regarding the certainty and severity of sanctions (Aryee et al., 2007). Hence, under AL, employees tend to believe that they are likely to be caught and harshly punished if they violate the ISP. Hence, we propose the following:

H4: Authoritarian leadership positively influences employees’ perceptions of sanctions.

Benevolent leaders, in contrast, typically do not employ rigid control mechanisms because they have their employees’ best interest at heart (Wu et al., 2012) and do not wish to see noncompliant employees face severe consequences such as loss of income or reputation. Benevolent leaders also tend to listen to employees to understand the difficulties they are facing (Farh & Cheng, 2000; Chan et al., 2013). If misconduct occurs because of understandable reasons or in other excusable situations, benevolent leaders are often willing to forgive such misbehavior (Wu & Tsai, 2012; Zhou et al., 2020). They may consider formal sanction mechanisms that severely punish noncompliant employees to be inappropriate regardless of the cause of noncompliance. Benevolent leaders seek to influence employees by demonstrating their appreciation for employees and showing concern for their welfare (Cheng et al., 2004). Employees are willing to comply with the wishes of benevolent leaders because they feel gratitude and want to repay their kindness (Chen & Kao, 2009). We argue that a primary way in which benevolent leaders can influence employees’ IT security behavior is by cultivating an information security climate. This climate influences employees in a soft way. Since employees are not constrained by compulsory requirements or supervision, they feel relaxed and trusted. Such employees are willing to comply with ISPs as a means of reciprocating the kindness of their benevolent leader (Chan et al., 2013). Thus, for benevolent leaders, adopting climate as a means of control not only demonstrates care for employees’ feelings but also helps to achieve managerial purposes.

Moreover, benevolent leaders nurture an open environment that permits communication and socialization, which helps employees better perceive and understand the organizational vision regarding the improvement of information security (Grojean et al., 2004; Chan, 2014; Chen et al., 2015). For instance, employees may feel free to express their confusion and uncertainty at work to leaders who demonstrate kindness (Wu et al., 2012; Chan, 2014; Karakitapoğlu-Aygün et al., 2021), including uncertainties regarding how to balance ISP compliance with performancerelated goals. Benevolent leaders provide prompt and positive feedback to help employees eliminate ambiguity and develop an awareness of the hidden values underlying ISPs (Grojean et al., 2004; Chan, 2014). Furthermore, researchers have found that BL makes employees feel like group members (Cheng et al., 2002; Cheng et al., 2004; Cheng et al., 2006; Wu & Tsai, 2012), making them intrinsically motivated to contribute to group norms by learning, sharing, participating, and socializing (Chan et al., 2013). The more employees are exposed to such normative influences, the more likely they are to understand the degree to which their organization values information security and to share this understanding with peers (Zohar, 1980; Zohar, 2002; Zohar, 2010). As a result, we propose that BL fosters a strong information security climate and offer the following hypothesis:

H5: Benevolent leadership positively influences employees’ perceptions of the information security climate.

Finally, moral leaders not only embody their own moral values but also strive to motivate ethical behavior from their employees via decision-making, two-way communication, and sanction-reward mechanisms (Brown et al., 2005; Walumbwa & Schaubroeck, 2009). ISP compliance can be considered a type of ethical behavior that is impacted by employees’ ethical awareness (Vance & Siponen, 2012; Al-Omari et al., 2013). ISP compliance requires employees to spend extra effort beyond their in-role tasks to protect the information security of the organization by, for example, helping new employees learn about the ISP and offering suggestions for ISP design, which are essential to collective interests but are not directly related to (or even obstacles for) their own job performance (Hsu et al., 2015). Therefore, to ensure that employees behave properly, moral leaders often have to apply sanctions to maintain moral standards and deter ISP violations (Farh et al., 2004; Cheng & Wang, 2015). Furthermore, moral leaders highly value fairness, an essential component of morality (Niu et al., 2009). If an ISP violation is not sanctioned, this situation is unfair to employees who strictly follow the ISP, especially those affected by the work impediments caused by ISP compliance (Herath & Rao, 2009b, Chen et al., 2012). Adopting a formal sanction-reward system signals to employees that the organization has established uniform standards for all employees and that everyone is treated equally. Therefore, moral leaders may adopt sanction mechanisms to maintain justice in the context of information security management.

In addition, moral leaders also make it clear that sanctions are likely to be imposed. For example, a salient characteristic of moral leaders is that they keep their promises and live up to their word (Niu et al., 2009). This integrity demonstrates that the organization strictly follows the sanction policy and that any violations are punished accordingly with no exception. Employees tend to trust moral leaders (Wu et al., 2012) and believe that sanction policies are strictly and fairly implemented. Moreover, according to Erben and

Guneser (2008), moral leaders cultivate employees’ sense of duty and obligation to their organization. Thus, they create a working environment in which employees tend to engage in organizational citizenship behaviors (Cheng et al., 2002; Wu et al., 2012), such as voluntarily supervising each other and reporting misconduct to ensure the information security of their organization. In such cases, employees may perceive a high probability of being caught and punished if they commit ISP violations. Hence, we argue that ML facilitates employees’ perceptions of sanctions for ISP violations, and propose the following:

H6: Moral leadership positively influences employees’ perceptions of sanctions.

ML influences ISP compliance not only by implementing sanctions but also by encouraging twoway communication and decision-making as a means of shaping employees’ ethical beliefs (Brown et al., 2005; Bedi et al., 2016). These efforts on the part of moral leaders can contribute to shaping an information security climate that improves employees’ ISP compliance because moral leaders can help employees better understand the values embraced by their organization. For instance, through top-down, bottomup, and lateral communication, employees can become well-informed regarding the information security threats faced by the organization and the significance of information security to the organization’s business success (Balozian et al., 2019). By inviting employees to engage in decision-making regarding information security and express confusion and concerns (Niu et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2012; He et al., 2021), ML demonstrates the organizational importance of information security to employees. Thus, moral leaders can help employees recognize the information security climate and the importance of information security for the organization.

Moral leaders tend to be self-disciplined and act as examples for employees (Brown et al., 2005; Chen et al., 2014). In addition to promoting ISP compliance among employees, moral leaders also strictly follow the ISP and express disapproval for ISP violations (Banks et al., 2021). This signals to employees that their leaders pay close attention to information security, which fosters a prevailing climate in the organization (Davison et al., 2020). Further, moral leaders facilitate employees’ understanding of the organizational climate because moral leaders seek to endow employees with a sense of obligation and duty to the organization (Erben & Guneser, 2008; Wu & Tsai, 2012; Davison et al., 2020; Lau et al., 2020). Hence, moral leaders cultivate concern among employees regarding how the organization expects them to behave (Bouckenooghe et al., 2015). This concern then motivates employees to learn about the underlying values of the organization and act in accordance with them. Thus, we propose that moral leaders motivate employees to gain an understanding of the climate of their organization, including the information security climate, and offer the following hypothesis:

H7: Moral leadership positively influences employees’ perceptions of the information security climate.

According to Crossler and Bélanger (2014), measuring actual security behavior is important since the ultimate goal of information security research is behavioral change. Therefore, we include ISP compliance behavior in our study. Behavioral intention describes one’s willingness to exert effort to perform a behavior (Ajzen, 1991). If one is willing to spend time and energy on a behavior, then this behavior is very likely to take place. Although there could be obstacles that might prevent behavioral intention from leading to actual behaviors, the results of several meta-analyses have shown that compared to other cognitive or personality factors, behavioral intention has superior predictive power regarding the performance of actual behaviors (Sheeran, 2002). Therefore, behavioral intention is a vital predictor of one’s behavior (Sheeran & Webb, 2016). Prior research has already confirmed the positive relationship between ISP compliance intention and ISP compliance behavior (Pahnila et al., 2007; Crossler & Bélanger, 2014; Boss et al., 2015; Sommestad et al., 2015). Hence, in the current research, we propose that employees with a high ISP compliance intention are likely to demonstrate compliance behaviors:

H8: Employees’ ISP compliance intention positively influences their ISP compliance behaviors.

## 4 Method

## 4.1 Construct Operationalization

We conducted a survey to collect our data and used mature scales adopted from the existing literature to measure all constructs. Each item was measured using a 7-point Likert scale. Adopted items are shown in Appendix A (items with low factor loadings and highly correlated error terms were deleted). Minor revisions were made to the wording to make the items consistent with our research context.

To measure PL, we adopted the scale developed by Cheng et al. (2004) and Farh et al. (2000), which measures PL in terms of its three dimensions, i.e., AL, BL, and ML. This scale has been widely adopted in the PL literature (e.g. Erben & Guneser, 2008; Niu et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2012; Wu & Tsai, 2012; Chan, 2014; Chen et al., 2014; Cheng et al., 2014). We asked respondents to rate their leaders’ behavior according to the descriptions of AL, BL, and ML items. The reason that PL is not considered to be a unified or higher-order construct is that there are negative interdimensional correlations among PL dimensions (as evident from previous PL literature and Table 1 in the current study) and these PL dimensions describe different leadership features and can lead to distinct outcomes. Thus, using a unified construct may overlook the impact of each PL dimension (Pellegrini & Scandura, 2008; Polites et al., 2012).

The information security climate scale was adopted from Chan et al. (2005), who first proposed this construct based on work concerning safety climate (Zohar, 1980; Zohar, 2002; Zohar & Luria, 2004). This construct measures individual perceptions regarding how information security is valued in the organization. In our research, the sanction construct reflects both employees’ perceptions of sanction severity and sanction certainty for ISP violations. We measured sanctions by cross-multiplying <sup>1</sup> the two items of sanction severity and the two items of sanction certainty (Nagin & Paternoster, 1993). Our reasoning for this choice was that “even highly certain sanction cannot be expected to affect one’s decision to offend unless they are also perceived to entail some cost” (Andenaes, 1974; Bailey & Lott, 1976; Grasmick & Bryjak, 1980; Grasmick & Bursik, 1990). That is, only when the severity and certainty of sanctions are both high can the sanction mechanism truly deter one’s decision to offend. Many information security scholars have also adopted this approach to measure sanctions (e.g., Siponen et al., 2012; Vance et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2020). Items regarding sanction severity and sanction certainty were adopted from Peace et al. (2003) and D’Arcy et al. (2009) and have been widely adopted in the IT security literature (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2009; Herath & Rao, 2009a). Each item was centralized before multiplication to reduce multicollinearity (Aiken et al., 1991). The ISP compliance intention scale was adopted from Bulgurcu et al. (2010) to measure employees’ intentions regarding ISP compliance.

Following Chan et al. (2005) and Yazdanmehr and Wang (2016), we asked respondents to report the frequency of their ISP violations and reverse-coded the ratings as the measure of their ISP compliance behavior. We used self-reported data because ISP compliance behavior is often difficult to monitor (Herath & Rao, 2009a) and only employees themselves know the exact degree to which they complied with the ISP. For example, if an employee forgets to lock their computer screen when leaving the desk but this lapse remains undiscovered, then only the employee would know that they violated the ISP. Many existing ISP compliance researchers have adopted this self-report method to measure ISP compliance behavior (e.g. Chan et al., 2005; Son, 2011; Hsu et al., 2015; Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016; Gwebu et al., 2020). Hence, we consider it appropriate to use self-reported data to measure employees’ ISP compliance behavior.

For CMB analysis, we also measured employees social desirability (SD) (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The SD scale was adopted from Reynolds (1982). For control variables, we measured age, gender, education, and organization size.

## 5 Results

## 5.1 Common Method Bias

Following the suggestions of Podsakoff et al. (2003), we applied both procedural control and statistical control measures to mitigate any potential CMB in our research. We adopted two procedural control measures. First, we divided the questionnaire into two parts so that the independent variables and dependent variables were separated into two parts. We administered each part to the respondents at different times to reduce method-induced correlations (Cote & Buckley, 1987). The second part of the questionnaire was sent to respondents after the first part was completed. The length of time between the two parts was at least 30 minutes. This time interval reduced the possibility of respondents guessing the research purpose by employing their short-term memory and revising their answers accordingly. Collecting data from two time points also reduced the effects of respondents’ mood or environmental conditions, which can also lead to potential bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Second, to avoid the single method effect (Podsakoff et al., 2003), we collected our data from two different sources. Of the 760 questionnaires, 222 were collected from MBA or EDP classes, while 538 were collected from government agencies and companies. As shown in Appendix F, the measurement invariance test of these two sample groups demonstrates that the constructs were equally measured regardless of the location from which they were collected. Therefore, we were able to pool the data for later analysis.

For statistical control, we used the single-method-factor approach proposed by Podsakoff et al. (2003) to control for the effect of a potential omitted variable, social desirability. Social desirability describes one’s desire for social approval and the belief that such approval can be attained by performing socially acceptable behaviors (Crowne & Marlowe, 1964). This factor measures individuals’ tendency to present themselves in a favorable way regardless of their real feelings. In our research context, respondents’ social desirability could have been a major source of bias because respondents, wishing to maintain a socially desirable image, may have been likely to overrate sensitive variables such as leadership styles and ISP compliance intention and behavior, which may not have reflected their real perceptions or experience. Respondents with high social desirability would be less likely to report low ISP compliance intention or actual ISP violations. In particular, our survey was conducted in China, where cultural values, such as collectivism, have been found to cause higher social desirability tendencies (Johnson & van de Vijver, 2003).

To test for social desirability bias, we included the social desirability factor in our research model. Following the single-method-factor approach, social desirability was measured using its own items and all the items of the principal constructs in our model (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Then, we calculated and compared the variances of principal construct items explained by their assigned construct and social desirability. As shown in Appendix C, Table C1, the average variance explained by the assigned factor is 0.753, while the average variance explained by social desirability is 0.303. The average factor loading on the principal constructs is 0.861, while the average factor loading on social desirability is -0.371. This result suggests that the principal constructs explain most of the variance of their items. However, considering the fact that social desirability bias cannot be overlooked, the model test results after controlling for social desirability bias should be more reliable (Podsakoff et al., 2003). We compared the research model with and without controlling for social desirability and found that the first model has a better fit (see Table 2 in Appendix C). Therefore, all the results we report hereafter are based on the research model in which social desirability is controlled.

## 5.2 Measurement Model

First, we conducted an EFA using SPSS. The items with low factor loadings were removed. Then, we conducted CFA using AMOS. Our measurement model showed acceptable model fit (CMIN/df = 3.345, GFI = 0.896, CFI = 0.965, TLI = 0.959, RMSEA = 0.056, SRMR = 0.046). To determine whether correlated error terms affected the overall fit, we examined the modification indices. Two pairs of error terms from the items of BL were found to be highly correlated $( \gamma _ { 1 } = 0 . 4 8 9 , \gamma _ { 2 } = 0 . 5 3 1 )$ . Since eliminating the correlated error terms and their associated item will make the model more parsimonious, following the suggestion of Fuller et al. (2006) and Hermida (2015), for each pair of correlated error terms, we dropped the item with less theoretical meaning. Items that are used in the measurement model are summarized in Appendix A.

Second, we examined the reliability and validity of all measured constructs in our model (Carmines & Zeller, 1979). As shown in Appendix D, all composite reliability coefficients are above 0.7. The AVE of each construct is at least 0.526 (above 0.5). This result means that all the constructs explain more than half of the variances of their items. Table 1 shows that, for each construct, the squared root of its AVE is larger than its correlation with other constructs, suggesting good discriminate validity (Chin, 1998). The crossloading table (Appendix E) shows that each item has higher loading on its assigned construct than other constructs, which shows sufficient convergent validity and discriminate validity of our measurement instruments (Gefen et al., 2000).

## 5.3 Hypothesis Testing

Our model was tested by structural equation modeling using AMOS 22.0. ## 6 Discussion

This research aims to explore the role of leaders in ISP compliance management by investigating the impact of a specific leadership style, PL, on ISP compliance. We propose that PL can influence employees’ perceptions of information security control mechanisms in their organization (regarding sanctions and the information security climate) and then further affect employees’ ISP compliance intention and behavior. The findings of our research show that employees’ perceptions of sanctions and the information security climate have significant and positive relationships with IP compliance intention, which is consistent with the existing literature (e.g., Chan et al., 2005; D’Arcy et al., 2009; Herath & Rao, 2009a). More importantly, we found that different PL dimensions affect employees’ ISP compliance intention through different information security control mechanisms. The positive impact of AL can be partially mediated by employees’ perception of the sanction mechanism, while the positive impact of BL can be partially mediated by employees’ perception of the information security climate. The positive impact of ML can be partially mediated by both sanctions and the information security climate. This finding suggests that different leadership styles influence employees’ ISP compliance by affecting different control mechanism perceptions.

## 6.1 Theoretical Contribution

Our research contributes to the literature in the following ways. First, our findings enrich the understanding of the formation of employees’ perceptions of information security control mechanisms. Previous studies have mostly focused on examining how perceived control mechanisms motivate ISP compliance (e.g., Chan et al., 2005; Chen et al., 2012; Hsu et al., 2015; Johnston et al., 2015). Indeed, these studies have rarely examined the factors that can affect the formation of employees’ subjective perceptions of these control mechanisms. Although a few studies have examined the antecedents of control mechanism perceptions, these studies have only focused on the impact of information security countermeasures (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Hovav & D’Arcy, 2012) or specific management acts (Chan et al., 2005). Drawing on general deterrence theory (Gibbs, 1975), our research shows that in addition to information security countermeasures, employees’ perception of control mechanisms can be shaped by many factors in the organization, such as the objective properties of control mechanisms chosen by management, organizational contexts, and employees’ direct and indirect experience of control mechanisms, which may all depend on the decisions of leaders. Therefore, considering the differences between leaders, our research adopts the perspective of leadership style and proposes that PL can influence employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms. Our findings show that AL and ML positively influence perceptions of sanctions, and BL and ML positively influence perceptions of the information security climate. These findings help to explain why certain information security control mechanisms are preferred by certain leaders.

Second, our research reveals the influence pathways from PL dimensions to ISP compliance intention via the mediation of perceived control mechanisms. Previous researchers have mostly examined employees’ cognitive factors, such as attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and self-efficacy, as influencing mechanisms that explain how leaders can impact employees’ ISP compliance (Hu et al., 2012; Humaidi & Balakrishnan, 2018). Although Chan et al. (2005) adopted the perceived information security climate to study the impact of leaders’ practices on ISP compliance, the style differences among leaders were not considered. Our research demonstrates that leaders’ impact can be explained by how it affects employees’ perceptions of information security control mechanisms; we thereby offer a new perspective to explain how leaders influence employees’ ISP compliance. Furthermore, in the extant ISP compliance literature, style differences among leaders are rarely considered and it is generally assumed that leaders employ the same mediating mechanisms to influence employees’ ISP compliance (Chan et al., 2005; Hu et al., 2012; Humaidi & Balakrishnan, 2018). Our research finds that the functioning paths of different PL dimensions on ISP compliance intention are different. AL can influence ISP compliance intention by enhancing perceptions of sanctions, BL functions by enhancing perceptions of the information security climate, and ML can affect ISP compliance via both mechanisms. Our study extends the existing ISP compliance research and calls for more research attention on the influencing mechanisms for different leadership styles.

Third, this study advances our knowledge about the impact of PL. Specifically, our results show that AL positively influences employees’ perceptions of sanctions and thus motivates employees’ ISP compliance intention. This positive impact of AL has been given little attention in the previous PL literature, which typically negatively links AL to employee outcomes such as organizational commitment, team identification, in-role performance, and teamwork (Wu et al., 2012; Chen, 2013; Cheng & Wang, 2015). The enhanced perception of sanction severity/certainty among employees imposed by AL offers a new explanation regarding the ways in which AL motivates ISP compliance, supplementing the literature that has conventionally maintained that employees’ compliance as a result of AL is mainly due to their fear of authority (Cheng et al., 2004; Pellegrini & Scandura, 2008). Our research also uncovers the fact that BL affects employees’ ISP compliance intention by positively influencing their perceptions of the information security climate. Employees’ compliance with BL has previously been explained by their appreciation and gratitude for leaders’ kindness (Cheng et al., 2004; Chen & Kao, 2009). The results of our research imply instead that employees are willing to comply with the ISP because benevolent leaders help them to understand the organization’s priorities and expected values, i.e., the climate, which is a new angle for understanding the impact of BL. Our results suggest that ML can affect employees’ ISP compliance intention via both perceptions of sanctions and the information security climate. Previous studies have also studied the impact of morality on employees’ ISP compliance and shown that moral leaders can influence employees’ ISP compliance by enhancing the moral climate and the moral obligation of employees (Al-Omari et al., 2013; Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016). Our research shows that moral leaders can affect ISP compliance intention by influencing the ways in which employees perceive control mechanisms. Thus, we extend knowledge on how the influencing pathways of ML impact employees compliance into the information security context.

## 6.2 Managerial Implications

Our research makes the following implications regarding leadership in information security management practices. First, leadership style matters in the context of information security management. The model test results show that all three dimensions of PL have a direct positive influence on employees’ ISP compliance intention and contribute to employees perceptions of the control mechanisms that create external forces to motivate employees’ ISP compliance. This finding implies that AL, BL, and ML are all beneficial for information security management in organizations. Organizations can design training programs to draw leaders’ attention to their leadership styles and encourage them to apply PL in the context of information security practices. Meanwhile, the impact of PL dimensions on perceived control mechanisms reminds leaders that, in addition to choosing effective controls to motivate ISP compliance, leaders also need to pay attention to their leadership style to ensure that employees perceive control mechanisms as strong and likely to be implemented.

Second, our research shows that AL and ML positively influence employees’ perceptions of the sanction mechanism. Therefore, AL and ML can be applied to affect employees’ perceptions regarding sanctions for ISP violations. For example, leaders can learn from AL to require unquestioning obedience from employees (Cheng & Wang, 2015), rigorously monitor employees’ behavior (Chen et al., 2014), enact punishments once violations are detected (Chan et al., 2013), express their desire for everything to be done in the best possible way according to their own judgment, and demonstrate their intolerance for deviant behavior (Niu et al., 2009; Wu & Tsai, 2012). Additionally, leaders can learn from ML to emphasize fairness and justice in the organization (Niu et al., 2009), which can signal to employees that anyone who violates the ISP will be punished appropriately with no exceptions. As a result, employees may perceive that ISP violations are inexcusable and that punitive consequences are very likely to follow.

Third, we find that BL and ML positively affect employees’ perceptions of the information security climate. Our results suggest two ways for leaders to cultivate an information security climate. Leaders can learn from BL to nurture the climate by offering supportive programs, such as security education, training, and awareness programs, and to encourage a work environment of open communication that can assist employees in making sense of how information security is valued by their organization. In addition, leaders can learn from ML to express the value of information security by displaying their own virtues and morality (Cheng et al., 2004). Employees who regard their moral leaders as role models will learn from their leaders to be concerned with collective interests and develop a sense of duty to their organization (Erben & Guneser, 2008), which will further motivate them to make sense of the values and priorities of their organization and protect their organization’s information assets.

Finally, adopting PL holistically by integrating all three dimensions may lead to better results than any single PL dimension alone in the context of information security management. Namely, employees’ ISP compliance requires the joint influence of formal and informal control mechanisms (Hsu et al., 2015). Our research shows that BL exerts no significant influence on employees’ perceptions of sanctions and that AL has no significant impact on employees’ perceptions of the information security climate. Although ML can affect both perceptions of sanctions and the information security climate, its overall influence is less than that of PL. Therefore, we suggest that leaders comprehensively apply the three PL dimensions instead of adopting one single dimension when motivating employees’ ISP compliance.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

Our research has four limitations. First, our research adopts only one representative control mechanism from the group of formal and informal control mechanisms. More control modes can be studied to examine the impact of leaders in the context of information security management. In the current study, we adopt the sanction mechanism as formal control, which can be further categorized into behavioral control and outcome control (Ouchi, 1979). In behavioral control, leaders use articulated rules and procedures to regulate employees’ behavioral processes. Punishment occurs when employees do not follow prescribed procedures. In outcome control, leaders use articulated goals and outcomes to guide employees’ behavior. Punishment occurs when the prescribed target is not achieved. Therefore, future work could consider how different leadership styles motivate ISP compliance via these formal control modes. Climate is a type of clan control category in the informal control category. Another type of informal control is self-control, which occurs when people establish goals and procedures on their own and use mechanisms of self-punishment or self-reward to control their own behavior (Ouchi, 1977; Ouchi, 1979). Self-control mechanisms could also be a potential perspective for understanding the impact of different leadership styles in motivating employees ISP compliance.

Second, our research assumes that all leaders are aware of the importance of information security and are actively involved in information security management, which may not always be the case. The impact of leadership styles on employees’ ISP compliance may be contingent on leaders’ priorities regarding information security and their involvement in information security practices. For example, regardless of leadership style, if leaders place low priority on information security or underestimate the importance of information security, they may not devote energy to deploying relevant control mechanisms or take other measures to promote ISP compliance. For example, if leaders are rarely involved in information security activities, employees will not be monitored, and the information security climate will become weaker, resulting in lower levels of ISP compliance. In other words, if leaders adopt PL but do not actively engage with information security management, then the benefits of PL would not accrue to information security management and would thus not impact employees’ perceptions of the corresponding control mechanisms. This situation suggests a possible moderated mediation effect of leadership styles on employees’ ISP compliance, in which the paths between leadership styles and ISP compliance through control mechanisms would be moderated by leaders’ information security awareness and involvement. This possibility could be an interesting research topic and deserves further exploration.

Third, we tested our research model using crosssectional data, which provide limited support for the causal relationship between PL dimensions and sanctions/information security climate. We call for longitudinal studies that use a time interval between the measurement of leadership style and the measurement of employees’ perceptions of control mechanisms or experiments that use manipulated leadership style training to validate the causal nature of the relationships discovered in our research.

Lastly, we used self-reported data to measure ISP compliance behavior, which is not always reliable (Podsakoff et al., 2003). For example, to protect their professional image, employees may intentionally overreport their ISP compliance behavior, and employees may not even know that they have violated an ISP. Future research might consider using multisource behavior data from employees, peers, leaders, and even computer records to validate the relationship between ISP compliance intention and behavior.

## 7 Conclusion

This study examines the impact of PL on employees’ ISP compliance. We propose that in addition to the direct impact, PL dimensions can influence ISP compliance intention by affecting employees’ perceptions of information security control mechanisms, such as sanctions and the information security climate. We tested our research model using survey data collected from 760 participants. Our findings suggest that all three PL dimensions have a positive direct impact on employees’ ISP compliance intention. However, the mediating control mechanisms of the different PL dimensions are not the same. The impact of AL is partially mediated by employees perception of sanctions, the impact of BL is partially mediated by employees’ perception of the information security climate, and the impact of ML is partially mediated by employees’ perceptions of both sanctions and the information security climate.

## Appendix F: Measurement Invariance Test

Measurement invariance tests were conducted to examine whether the constructs can be measured equally across different sample groups. Following the instructions of Steenkamp and Baumgartner (1998), we examined the configural, metric and scalar invariance across sample groups. Criteria were adopted from Cheung and Rensvold (2002). Configural invariance can be established by good model fit indices. Metric invariance can be ensured if the difference in CFI between the new constrained model and the baseline model is less than 0.01. Scalar invariance can be established if the difference in CFI between the new constrained model and the preceding model is less than 0.01. Based on our data collection process, we conducted two measurement invariance analyses. The results are summarized in Table F1. Overall, the results show that the measurement scales are comparable across different sample groups.

The ΔCFI of the configural model and metric model was less than 0.01 (0.9755-0.9751 = 0.0004). The ΔCFI of the metric model and scalar model was less than 0.01 (0.9751-0.9741 = 0.001). Therefore, both metric and scalar invariance was established. These results suggest that our constructs can be equally measured regardless of the context from which the data were collected.

In the second analysis, we tested the group of 314 employees whose leader also participated in our survey and reported the ISP compliance behavior of these employees (Group A) and the group of 446 employees without leader participation (Group B). The fit of the configural invariance model was good (with IFI, TLI, and CFI above 0.90 and RMSEA below 0.08), suggesting the establishment of configural invariance. The ΔCFI of the configural model and metric model was less than 0.01 (0.9791-0.9780 = 0.0011). The ΔCFI of the metric model and scalar model was less than 0.01 (0.9780-0.9758 = 0.0022). Therefore, both metric and scalar invariance was established. These results suggest that our constructs can be measured with or without leaders’ participation.

## Test 2: Examining the ISP Compliance Intention-Behavior Relationship Using 314 Leader-Employee Averaged Behavior Data.

According to Burton-Jones (2009), both self-reported and leader-reported data have potential biases. In our research, employees may have the potential to hide ISP noncompliance, while leaders may have exhibited knowledge bias because they cannot monitor their employees all the time. Therefore, the average score of self-reported data and leaderreported data may be more accurate as a reflection of an individual’s ISP compliance behavior (Burton-Jones, 2009).

Hence, we tested our model by 314 leader-employee averaged ISP compliance behavior data. The intention-behavior relationship was positive and significant ( = 0.347, p < 0.001). For the 314 self-reported data, the intention-behavior relationship was also positive and significant ( = 0.426, p < 0.001), which was consistent with the result based on average behavior scores. In fact, the correlation between averaged ISP compliance behavior and self-reported ISP compliance behavior was very high ( = 0.871, p < 0.001). These results suggested that self-reported ISP compliance behavior data were highly consistent with leader-employee averaged ISP compliance behavior data.

Moreover, the measurement invariance test for 314 employees with leaders’ participation and 446 employees without leaders’ participation showed that our constructs can be equally measured with or without leaders’ participation (Appendix F). This result indicated that our use of self-reported ISP compliance behavior data from 760 participants was acceptable. Although the 314 leader-employee paired data points provided a more accurate estimation of the intention-behavior relationship (Burton-Jones, 2009), the data from all 760 participants may provide more accurate estimations of all the other relationships in our research model with only a slight change in the intention-behavior relationship. Since our research focus was on how PL affects ISP compliance intention via sanction and information security climate, we used the data from all 760 participants to test our hypotheses.
