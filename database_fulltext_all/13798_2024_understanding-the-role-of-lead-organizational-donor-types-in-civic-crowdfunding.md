## Abstract

Civic crowdfunding is a subgenre of crowdfunding wherein the crowd finances civic projects of value to communities. A unique aspect of civic crowdfunding is that projects regularly attract a mix of individuals and organizations, spanning the private, public, and nonprofit sectors. This diversity of stakeholders raises novel questions and challenges for platform operators and fundraisers. Here, we focus on such challenges as they relate to third-party endorsements. A lengthy literature has examined the signaling value of endorsements and prominent lead contributions, typically treating their sources as homogeneous. However, the varied backgrounds, motivations, and objectives of stakeholders in civic crowdfunding suggest that endorsers’ signaling value is likely to vary. We theorize how onlookers’ (potential backers’ or donors’) perception of lead organizational donors’ motivations and fit with the project’s objectives will influence the latter’s signaling value, hypothesizing differences across lead donor types (i.e., businesses, governments, nongovernmental organizations [NGOs]). Leveraging data on 303 projects from a leading civic crowdfunding platform, Spacehive, we show that lead donations are significantly more beneficial when supplied by NGOs rather than businesses or government entities. Further, based on perceptual measures reported by human coders about the more than 500 organizational lead donations in our sample, we confirm our theorized mechanisms, finding that lead donor involvement is more likely to be perceived as credible, and to lead to support for a project, when the lead’s involvement is believed to be a result of value motives (rather than strategic motives), and when the lead is a natural fit with the project (i.e., congruence). We discuss the implications of our work for both theory and practice.

Keywords: Civic Crowdfunding, Lead Donor, Public Good, Fundraising, Signaling, Source Credibility, Motivational Attribution, Congruence

## 1 Introduction

Online crowdfunding is a technology-enabled process wherein causes, projects, or ventures are collectively financed by many individuals, i.e., the crowd, typically via the internet. Crowdfunding has disrupted many areas of traditional finance, circumventing the need for conventional financial institutions such as banks, venture capitalists, and business angels (Beaulieu et al., 2015).

While in the earliest literature, crowdfunding was initially treated as something of a monolith, scholars have subsequently begun to distinguish between the different crowdfunding contexts (Block et al., 2018; Davies & Roberts, 2015; Pollack et al., 2019). Although these different crowdfunding contexts share many high-level similarities, they differ markedly in practice, e.g., in terms of the mechanisms they employ and the stakeholders they attract (Pollack et al., 2019).

Civic crowdfunding is a subgenre of crowdfunding targeted at the provision of public goods that benefit a local community, i.e., projects of civic nature (Davies, 2014a). To resolve complex social problems, it is often necessary to facilitate collaboration between stakeholders from different segments of society. Civic crowdfunding platforms (CCPs) serve this role by providing technological infrastructure and governance structures that can enable and accelerate cross-sector interaction (Logue & Grimes, 2022).

Akin to other types of crowdfunding, civic crowdfunding presents unique informational challenges. First, fundraising typically takes place within a short time frame and in a virtual environment, implying that contributors have limited opportunity to assess creators or their projects. Second, most civic crowdfunding projects are at a very early stage of development and lack many implementation details, making it difficult for potential backers to evaluate projects’ potential and value. Finally, most project organizers lack a proven track record of prior success, making it difficult for them to convince potential backers about their ability to execute the project. This constellation of factors translates to a scenario of acute information asymmetry (Agrawal et al., 2014; Bapna, 2019; Courtney et al., 2017; Kumru & Vesterlund, 2010; Vesterlund, 2003).

One approach to alleviating these information asymmetry problems that has been advocated in the public finance literature is to obtain and announce lead donations from prominent entities (Andreoni, 2006; List & Lucking‐Reiley, 2002; Potters et al., 2001, 2007; Vesterlund, 2003). A lead donation is a large donation (typically larger than 10% of the fundraising target) made very early in the fundraising process (Andreoni, 2006). The involvement of a prominent lead donation can help to address information asymmetry by signaling the quality of the project and the credibility of the organizer to less-informed, subsequent donors (Karlan & List, 2020; Potters et al., 2007; Vesterlund, 2003). Cognizant of this potential, traditional charities have long sought to acquire and advertise lead donations (Huck & Rasul, 2011; Karlan & List, 2012; Kumru & Vesterlund, 2010; Vesterlund, 2003). Like traditional charities, many crowdfunding campaigns have sought to leverage a similar strategy, given the inherent information asymmetry problems they also face. Examples of such campaigns include a number of reward- and equitybased crowdfunding campaigns (Bernstein et al., 2017; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Kleinert & Volkmann, 2019; Shen et al., 2020) and civic crowdfunding campaigns.<sup>1</sup> However, whereas the inferences that onlookers might make about lead contributions are likely to be relatively straightforward in reward- or equity-based crowdfunding, in civic crowdfunding the project objectives and stakeholders (and their motivations), are quite different (Logue & Grimes, 2022). Given these differences, it remains unclear whether the previously demonstrated benefits and theorized mechanisms of lead investment will generalize to the civic crowdfunding context (Pollack et al., 2019). Therefore, an understanding of the role of lead donation in civic crowdfunding success is still needed.

A notable distinction in the case of civic crowdfunding, however, is that lead donors can take many forms. Whereas lead contributors in equity- or reward-based crowdfunding typically hail from the private sector, civic crowdfunding campaigns often attract lead donations from entities operating across the private, government, and nonprofit sectors (Logue & Grimes, 2022). This variety of contributor types raises natural questions of how subsequent donors will perceive a lead contributor’s involvement. While the returns of financing an equity or reward-based crowdfunding campaign are clear (equity in a company or a campaign reward), simplifying inferences that onlookers might make about other contributors’ motives, the motivations, and interests at play in civic crowdfunding can vary a great deal. Depending on their perspective, the onlookers of civic crowdfunding campaigns may thus view lead contributors’ participation positively, with suspicion, or as altogether irrelevant (Logue & Grimes, 2022).

Understanding heterogeneity in the influence of lead donors in civic crowdfunding is important for at least three reasons. First, the success of civic crowdfunding projects requires actors from different sectors of society to come together and collaborate on identifying and solving civic problems (Davies, 2014b; Logue & Grimes, 2022). Campaign organizers and civic crowdfunding platform operators would thus benefit from understanding how different types of actors perceive each other’s involvement because it would help inform the approaches they take in managing collaborations. Second, the success of civic crowdfunding projects depends heavily on the organizer gaining early momentum (Etter et al., 2013; Hong et al., 2018) and maintaining it throughout the campaign (Lu et al., 2014). Attracting the “right” lead donor can play a key role in establishing that early momentum. Third, the activities involved in attracting support from the different types of donors can vary substantially. This implies that an understanding of ideal lead donor characteristics can enable campaign organizers, who are typically resource constrained, to allocate their time and effort efficiently toward achieving their fundraising goals.

Given the above points, we address the following research question in this study:

RQ: What are the relative fundraising benefits of involving different types of lead donors in a civic crowdfunding campaign?

To examine this question, we focused on organizational lead donors for several reasons. First, crowdfunding platforms draw large numbers of small contributions from many individuals. As a result, organizational donations are the exception rather than the norm and are thus more likely to be noticed by visitors to the campaign page. Second, organizations typically have greater resources than individuals. Accordingly, organizations can supply larger amounts of seed money. Third, for the lead donation to convey a reasonable indication of project quality and organizer credibility, onlookers need to believe that the lead donor is capable of conducting a meaningful evaluation. Organizations are naturally more capable of conducting such inspections because they have more resources at their disposal. Fourth, and last, for the lead donation to convey a signal of quality, onlookers must believe that the lead donor is credible (i.e., experienced and trustworthy) and that the lead donor’s motives for donation are aligned with their interests (Forehand & Grier, 2003). Because organizations frequently publish their mission statements, values, or organizational objectives, their motives and credibility are more readily assessed than those of semi-anonymous individuals.

We theorize the importance of onlookers’ perceptions about organizational lead donors’ motivations, their “fit” (congruence) with the project, and the resulting credibility of their involvement. Considering the diversity of lead donor types that participate in civic crowdfunding campaigns (namely, businesses, government entities, and nongovernmental organizations [NGOs]) and how those organization types tend to be perceived by the public, we hypothesize about whether and which organizational lead donor types will deliver benefits for civic crowdfunding campaigns. To test our hypotheses, we examined archival data on 303 fundraising campaigns that took place on a prominent civic crowdfunding platform in the United Kingdom, Spacehive, between December 2011 and January 2017. Our findings indicate that dollar-for-dollar, the effect of lead donations varied across the three donor organization types. Specifically, consistent with our theory, we report evidence of an outsized influence from NGO lead donors. To establish mechanisms, we complemented the archival analysis by enlisting human coders to report their perceptions of each lead donation record in order to directly operationalize our theorized constructs. Our analyses of those perceptual encodings support our theory that perceived donorproject congruence and perceived donor motivations influence donor credibility perceptions and, in turn, onlookers’ expectations of broader support that a campaign is likely to receive.

This work makes several important contributions to the academic literature on lead donations, lead investment, third-party endorsement, and the IS literature on online crowdfunding, both in general and in terms of civic crowdfunding in particular. Our work also provides important practical implications for the various stakeholders involved in the civic crowdfunding ecosystem, including project organizers, lead donors, and platform operators, as discussed later.

## 2 Literature Review

## 2.1 Lead Donations & Traditional Fundraising

A large stream of literature in public finance has studied the signaling value for charities of announcing prominent lead donors (Andreoni, 2006; Huck & Rasul, 2011; Jack & Recalde, 2015), arguing that lead donors can lend credibility and signal the fundraiser’s quality to subsequent donors. Credibility and quality are known to play important roles in attracting subsequent donations (Rose-Ackerman, 1986; Vesterlund, 2003). Experimental work in this stream has consistently shown that lead donors are particularly influential when a charity’s quality is not common knowledge and the lead donor is high-profile (Huck & Rasul, 2011; Karlan & List, 2012; Potters et al., 2007). Prior work has thus established that the effects of lead donors on fundraising outcomes are not only driven by the up-front infusion of capital that can help the charity reach its target—though this can also play a role, of course, inducing confidence that the charity will reach its target threshold or provision point (Andreoni, 1989; List & Lucking‐Reiley, 2002). Rather, the lead donor’s participation and willingness to supply early funds has a distinct influence on others’ willingness to follow suit and donate in turn, because the lead donor’s participation also signals private information they might have about the charity’s quality and credibility.

While several researchers have independently examined the influence of lead donors across numerous contexts and donor types, including NGOs (Khovrenkov, 2019), governmental entities (Jack & Recalde, 2015), and businesses (e.g., LeClair and Gordon, 2000), most prior work has ignored potential heterogeneity in their influence. This is notable because the signaling effect of a lead donation likely depends on several factors, including the characteristics of the lead donor, the potential audience, and the nature of the cause or project. Additionally, the context of the fundraising campaign and the overall fundraising ecosystem can play a key role in shaping the interpretation of the signal conveyed by a lead donation. One useful example from the public good literature that demonstrates this point relates to government donations.

While some work has observed that lead donations to charitable fundraisers (by, for example, private foundations) generally have a positive effect on subsequent donations(Andreoni, 2006; Huck & Rasul, 2011; Jack & Recalde, 2015), other studies have observed that the effect can be weakened or even be reversed if a lead donation is provided by a government entity (Andreoni, 1993; Andreoni & Payne, 2003, 2011; Bernheim, 1986; Bernheim & Bagwell, 1988; Kingma, 1989). The reason for this negative influence is that potential subsequent donors may believe that they have already donated indirectly because the government donation is ultimately funded by their tax dollars (Andreoni, 1993; Andreoni & Payne, 2003, 2011; Bernheim, 1986; Bernheim & Bagwell, 1988; Kingma, 1989).

To our knowledge, no prior work has sought to understand the role of lead donations in the unique context of civic crowdfunding, nor has previous research sought to systematically compare various lead donor types or their particular characteristics in a single setting to understand their relative influence, as we do here.

## 2.2 Civic Crowdfunding

Civic crowdfunding is a subgenre of online crowdfunding that focuses on the provision of a specific form of public good, namely goods that benefit a local community (Davies, 2014a). Civic crowdfunding campaigns thus pertain to communityfocused development projects, e.g., public parks, festivals, community centers, and public transportation infrastructure (Davies, 2014a). One of the most important aspects of CCPs is the opportunity they create to link civic project creators with a variety of relevant stakeholders (Logue & Grimes, 2022). These stakeholders can include (for-profit) businesses, governments, (nonprofit) NGOs, and interested citizens. By facilitating collaboration and communication between stakeholders, CCPs provide a novel source of project capital for community advocates at a time of constrained government budgets (Gray, 2013; Stiver et al., 2015).

Although civic crowdfunding first emerged nearly a decade ago (Davies, 2014c), little work has examined the drivers of civic crowdfunding campaign success (Milosavljević et al., 2021). Three exceptions include Lee et al. (2019a), who examined the influence of linguistic style and message substance in project descriptions, van Montfort et al (2020), who explored the impact of campaign characteristics and the demographics of local populations for campaigns conducted in the Netherlands, and Hassna (2022), who studied the impact of online funding communities (OFCs), i.e., online communities that are built around commonly shared goals, ideologies, enthusiasm for, or interests in specific funding activities. Lee et al. found that the use of positive affective and perceptual language can improve civic crowdfunding success, while extensive use of social language has the opposite impact. Moreover, they showed that incorporating issue-relevant information in campaign descriptions, e.g., regarding budget or risk, positively influences funding outcomes. Van Montfort et al. reported that the main factors associated with fundraising success include donation-matching initiatives, fundraising targets, project objectives, the age of local residents, and the degree of local urbanization (van Montfort et al., 2020). Finally, Hassna explained that identifying and targeting the right OFCs can positively improve civic crowdfunding success. Employing textual similarity measures, Hassna provided evidence that the congruence or fit between a civic crowdfunding campaign and the targeted OFC may be associated with a higher success rate. We thus build on this limited work here and extend it by exploring the role of lead donor types on the fundraising success of civic crowdfunding campaigns.

Logue and Grimes (2022) argued that successful participation and collaboration on CCPs to solve civic problems requires stakeholders’ perception that their values and motivations intersect with those of other parties. However, some stakeholder types may view participation by other stakeholder types with suspicion, given their divergent values and potentially conflicting motivations (Logue & Grimes, 2022). The intermingling of different types of stakeholders within a single campaign thus raises important questions about how parties perceive one another’s involvement, the implications of those perceptions for civic crowdfunding success, and the efficacy of various lead donor types (Logue & Grimes, 2022). We thus address this gap in the literature by examining the impact of lead donations on civic crowdfunding success in general and by considering sources of heterogeneity in lead donor characteristics.

## 2.3 Relevant Crowdfunding Literature

Although very little work has focused specifically on civic crowdfunding, a large body of work has studied other forms of online crowdfunding (Beaulieu et al., 2015; McKenny et al., 2016; Pollack et al., 2019; Short et al., 2016). The vast majority of prior work has considered reward-, debt-, and equity-based crowdfunding, where contributors typically supply funds with the expectation of a reward, whether tangible or financial (Beaulieu et al., 2015). Fewer studies have focused on donation-based crowdfunding, where contributors supply monetary or nonmonetary resources with no expectation of receiving any reward for their contributions (Beaulieu et al., 2015; Salido-Andres et al., 2021).

Among these studies, a well-established stream of work has focused on identifying factors that influence crowdfunding success (Ahlers et al., 2015; Du et al., 2015; Hui et al., 2014; McKenny et al., 2016; Mollick & Kuppuswamy, 2014; Moritz & Block, 2016; Pollack et al., 2019; Short et al., 2016). Information asymmetry and signaling theory have commonly been used as theoretical lenses to explain how promoters attract and persuade potential funders by disclosing relevant information or displaying signals of credibility (Ahlers et al., 2015; Anglin et al., 2019; Bapna, 2019; Bernstein et al., 2017; Calic & Mosakowski, 2016; Courtney et al., 2017; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Kleinert et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2013; Shen et al., 2020). Due to the aforementioned information asymmetry between fundraisers and potential donors, previous research has found that the volume of information disclosed about campaign organizers can have a heavy impact on the extent of donor support, particularly in donation-based crowdfunding (Polzin et al., 2018; Salido-Andres et al., 2021, 2022). In parallel, studies have also found that disclosing information about the project itself can have a positive effect on fundraising success, particularly because information disclosure improves the perceived credibility of the campaign (Salido-Andres et al., 2021, 2022).

Within this broader literature, past works examining the effect of third-party endorsements (Anglin et al., 2019; Bapna, 2019; Calic & Mosakowski, 2016; Liu et al., 2022) and prominent early contributors, such as lead expert investors (Bernstein et al., 2017; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Shen et al., 2020), are most relevant to our study. These past studies have demonstrated positive effects of third-party endorsement and lead expert investment in reward-, equity-, debt-, and donation-based crowdfunding, showing that the involvement of these third parties can mitigate information asymmetry by signaling project quality and thereby draw support from other potential backers.

For example, in a reward-based crowdfunding context, Calic and Mosakowski (2016) reported evidence that media endorsements, e.g., by Wired, PC Magazine, and CNET, play an important role in driving fundraising outcomes for certain types of campaigns. Kim and Viswanathan (2019) reported evidence that expert (in terms of software development skills and investing) early investors in crowdfunded software applications positively influence subsequent investment, a result they attribute to signaling effects. In an equity-based crowdfunding context, Bernstein et al. (2017), studying AngelList, found that on average, information about early investors does not influence subsequent investment. <sup>2</sup> Bapna (2019) considered third-party endorsements from different types of organizations, including business customer testimonials and industry association certifications. She reported evidence that endorsements from each organization type are complementary to one another in driving investors interest. Shen et al. (2020) examined the psychological mechanisms by which lead investors influence other members of the crowd to invest. They found evidence that the crowd responds more positively when lead investors provide more funds and have more prior investing experience. In the debt-based crowdfunding context, Anglin et al. (2019) leveraged signaling theory to explain how entrepreneurs’ affiliation with microfinance institutions (MFIs) works as a salient third-party signal that shapes their crowdfunding performance and how the social and financial performance of these MFIs drive their campaign fundraising performance. Finally, in a donation-based crowdfunding context, Liu et al. (2022) analyzed 1,839 scientific crowdfunding campaigns from Experiment.com and reported evidence that explicit endorsements from prominent researchers in the field have a general positive effect on campaign outcomes.

While we acknowledge the significant contribution, that these prior studies make to the crowdfunding literature, gaps that need to be addressed remain. First, while prior studies have established the effects of individual lead expert investors or contributors on crowdfunding success, there are no studies investigating the impact of lead donations from organizations. This is particularly important because, unlike other forms of crowdfunding, organizational lead donations are common in civic crowdfunding. Second, and relatedly, civic crowdfunding is unique because the projects, underlying mechanisms, and stakeholder groups involved are quite diverse and distinct from those observed in other forms of crowdfunding (Logue & Grimes, 2022; Pollack et al., 2019). Contributors in other crowdfunding contexts are typically private individuals motivated by a tangible reward or financial return. The inferences that onlookers might make about individual lead contributors’ motivations and interests are likely to be relatively straightforward in other crowdfunding contexts, given the homogeneous nature of participants. However, in civic crowdfunding, the different groups of stakeholders that participate may view each other’s involvement differently, given the potential for divergent interests and motivations (Logue & Grimes, 2022).

For example, a lead donation from a business entity may face scrutiny or skepticism because businesses are primarily profit-seeking entities, and their involvement in public goods may be perceived as an attempt to improve their public image or reputation. Therefore, other donors or stakeholders may question their motives and scrutinize their involvement more closely. As a second example, while lead donations from government organizations may have a positive signaling effect on subsequent potential donors, their involvement may also induce a crowding out effect that may offset any signaling benefits partially or entirely (Andreoni, 1993; Andreoni & Payne, 2003, 2011; Bernheim, 1986; Bernheim & Bagwell, 1988; Kingma, 1989). When individual donors believe that the government has already provided sufficient support for a cause, they may assume that they have (indirectly) already contributed, given that the government is funded by citizens’ tax payments, leading to a reduction in individual donations. Additionally, individual donors may feel that their donations are less necessary when governmental funding is available, as they may believe that the government should be primarily responsible for providing funding for the kinds of public goods that civic crowdfunding campaigns tend to support. These examples highlight the need to explore the role of lead donor characteristics in civic crowdfunding in order to understand the conditions under which a positive versus negative effect may arise. Our study thus aims to investigate how the type of lead donor (e.g., government organization, private business, or NGO) affects the fundraising performance of civic crowdfunding campaigns.

## 3 Theory & Hypothesis Development

In this section, we formulate our hypotheses. For the sake of clarity, we also present a visual summary of our theoretical framework in Appendix A.

## 3.1 Lead Donation Effect in Civic Crowdfunding

To theorize the effect of lead organizational donations on civic crowdfunding success, we build on signaling theory to explain how these donations signal project legitimacy and organizer credibility.

Legitimacy is a key concept in organizational theory that refers to the degree to which an entity’s actions are perceived as desirable, proper, or appropriate by its stakeholders (Suchman, 1995). Pfeffer and Salancik (2003) make the point that legitimacy is a conferred status that is determined by onlookers. They note that one way to obtain legitimacy is through endorsements from notable external third parties.

The entrepreneurship literature highlights the importance of establishing legitimacy during the early stages of venture formation. Establishing legitimacy has been shown to aid in securing the resources needed to bring a business idea to fruition (Calic & Mosakowski, 2016; Colombo, 2020; Schoonhoven & Romanelli, 2001). This is particularly crucial under extreme degrees of information asymmetry, as this uncertainty can create doubts among potential investors and stakeholders (Colombo, 2020).

In civic crowdfunding, legitimacy amounts to the perception and belief that a project is desirable and will have a positive impact on the local community. This means that the project is seen as aligned with the values and interests of the community and its stakeholders, and, further, that the organizers have a clear vision to serve the public interest. Several factors can thus contribute to the legitimacy of a civic crowdfunding project, including the track record of the project organizer, the level of transparency and accountability in the project’s planning and execution, the perceived value to the community, and the reputation of the project’s partners and endorsers (Tanaka & Voida, 2016). Legitimacy is an important consideration in civic crowdfunding because projects that are seen as legitimate are more likely to attract support and funding from the community. Additionally, legitimacy can affect the long-term success and sustainability of a project, as it may impact the level of community engagement and support over time.

Building legitimacy with potential donors can be challenging in civic crowdfunding, because of various forms of information asymmetry that are at play. The project organizer generally has more information than potential donors about the project, its associated risks, and potential benefits to the community. Further, potential donors may struggle to locate information on the track record of the project organizer in civic crowdfunding, as they may lack a formal online presence. As such, organizers may find it difficult to earn the trust of potential donors, despite any clear or apparent value of their project to the community. Without this information, potential donors may be hesitant to contribute to the campaign, or they may only contribute small amounts.

Signaling theory suggests that information asymmetry can be resolved when the more informed party employs signals of quality and legitimacy, revealing information to the less informed party, e.g., potential backers (Colombo, 2020; Leland & Pyle, 1977; Spence, 1973). For a signal to be effective, it must be credible and should thus either be costly for a lowquality actor to mimic or originate from a third party that bears a cost (Hwang, 2010; Ippolito, 1990). Early involvement by third-party donor organizations should thus help resolve information asymmetry because, in donating, they are risking their financial contribution and their reputation (Andreoni, 2006; Hwang, 2010;

Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Ley & Weaven, 2011). Upon seeing a lead organizational donation, subsequent potential donors may infer project legitimacy and organizer credibility, based on the presumption that the organization would not risk its funds or reputation (Colombo, 2020; Vesterlund, 2003). Further, subsequent potential donors may perceive that because the organization has more resources it has greater expertise or insight, enabling it to make an informed judgment about the project legitimacy and organizer credibility (Colombo, 2020; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019).

Accordingly, we expect that in general, lead donations from third-party organizations positively influence the arrival of subsequent funds in civic crowdfunding by signaling project legitimacy and creator credibility. Further, the larger the donation, the stronger the signal should be, given that the third-party organization has staked greater resources on the project. Despite the presence of some scenarios where lead donations from specific organizational donors (e.g., business) might be perceived skeptically by some onlookers or where lead donations from some organizational donors (e.g., government) might have a crowding-out effect, the success of a civic crowdfunding project will ultimately depend on the net effect of these countervailing considerations and the basic positive influence of the quality signal. We thus begin with the following two hypotheses and posit that on average, the effects of lead contribution will remain positive in civic crowdfunding.

H1: Lead donations from organizations will positively impact the success of a civic crowdfunding project (organizational lead donation as a binary variable: any organizational lead donor).

H2: The size of lead donations from organizations will positively impact the success of a civic crowdfunding project (organizational lead donation as a continuous variable: amount of organizational lead donations in GBP).

Next, we explore lead donor heterogeneity to understand the conditions under which a positive effect arises and when a negative effect may arise.

## 3.2 Heterogeneity in Lead Donor Effects

For the lead donation to serve as an effective signal, onlookers must be convinced that the lead donor is informed, capable, and has motives that align with the community’s interests. It is thus necessary that the lead donor is perceived to have engaged in rigorous due diligence before making their donation (Karlan & List, 2020). Further, it must be perceived that the lead donor is motivated by the public good rather than personal benefit (Forehand & Grier, 2003). Naturally, these perceptions are likely to be influenced by the lead donor’s identity and characteristics (Karlan & List, 2020).

Civic crowdfunding campaigns attract lead donations from different organizations operating across the business, government, and nonprofit sectors. These varied participants exhibit unique characteristics and, at times, conflicting motivations for and interest in participation (Logue & Grimes, 2022). Business organizations are for-profit entities whose primary goals are to generate profit, benefit owners or shareholders, and fund further investments in the business. Nonprofit organizations, also known as nonprofit companies, nonbusiness organizations, or NGOs, are private organizations that are dedicated to furthering a particular social cause or delivering a specific public good (Ilhan, 2013). Nonprofit organizations primarily employ revenue surplus in further pursuit of the public good rather than delivering financial returns to stakeholders. Examples include foundations, charities, clubs, and community organizations. Finally, government agencies are public sector entities that advise policymakers, implement policy, and oversee the provision of public services. Government organizations are typically funded via taxation and are thus not profit driven. Examples of government entities include councils, bureaus, departments, or ministries. Given their unique characteristics and divergent agendas and motivations, lead donations from these various types of entities may be perceived quite differently by other potential donors (Logue & Grimes, 2022). Again, this is important because, as Logue and Grimes (2022) argue, successful participation and collaboration on CCPs to solve civic problems requires the perception of intersecting values and motivations, despite the potential for divergence.

An established stream of research on corporate social responsibility (CSR) examines how stakeholders perceptions of a company are affected by its sponsorship of causes, a form of CSR initiative (Geue & Plewa 2010). Rifon (2004), for example, proposed a causal model to explain the effect of corporate-CSR congruence, the attribution of corporate motivations to engage in CSR, and perceived corporate credibility— i.e., how credible consumers find a company’s CSR involvement to be—on customers’ attitudes toward the organization (Perez & Bosque, 2013). Wang and Lee (2018) also examined the effect of congruence on perceived corporate credibility and reputation. More relevant to our study is the work by Lee et al. (2019b), which examined the impact of CSR credibility on customers’ intention to participate in CSR activities. This theoretical framing aligns naturally with our context, because lead donations from organizations to civic crowdfunding campaigns are investments in social initiatives, and our research objective is to understand how lead donors’ involvement affects an analogous outcome, i.e., whether members of the crowd also engage with the social initiative (Lee et al., 2019b; Rifon et al., 2004). This theoretical perspective enables us to elaborate on standard signaling theory to consider how psychological processes contextualize quality signals, how they are interpreted/perceived by the crowd, and the benefits that the signals produce for fundraisers.

As in traditional consumer contexts, potential donors can be expected to make inferences about the credibility of a lead donor organization’s involvement with a civic crowdfunding campaign. Based on the literature, we expect that potential donors will make similar assessments of organizations’ motivations for contributing to a civic crowdfunding project and their congruence with the project, to arrive at a perception of the organization’s credibility. The perception that an organizational lead donor’s involvement is credible (or not) will then dictate whether the lead donation positively influences subsequent donations. We next draw more formally on theories of source credibility, attribution theory, and congruence to formulate hypotheses about the relative influence of different lead donor types on campaign fundraising success in this context.

## 3.3 Source Credibility

Source credibility theory deals with the relationship between a communication’s persuasiveness and the perceived credibility of its source (Hovland et al., 1953; Hovland & Weiss, 1951, 1952). The theory suggests that the recipient’s initial perception of source credibility may be used, on its own, to infer information quality. Thus, source credibility theory posits that a more credible source will be a more persuasive source. Similar to individual source credibility, studies have found that organizations’ credibility also determines their persuasive power over customers’ attitudes and behavioral intentions (Lafferty et al., 2002).

Organization credibility refers to the extent to which customers believe that the organization, as the source of communication, has the knowledge or ability to fulfill its claims (i.e., expertise) and whether the organization can be trusted to tell the truth or not (i.e., trustworthiness) (Lafferty et al., 2002). In the context of CSR, Lee et al. (2019b) proposed and validated the CSR credibility construct as a distinct aspect of corporate credibility, which refers to whether customers believe that a corporation’s CSR involvement in a public-benefit project is credible. CSR credibility has been defined as “how credible consumers find a company’s CSR involvement to be” (Lee et al., 2019b).

Lee et al.’s (2019) CSR credibility construct is a natural lens for our study because prior literature suggests that potential donors to civic crowdfunding projects are likely to consider the credibility of a lead donor organization’s involvement in a public good initiative. Similar to Lee et al., we theorize about the relationship between CSR credibility and a third-party individual’s intention to engage in the same initiative (in our case, making a donation to the same civic crowdfunding campaign). However, whereas Lee et al. theorized a variety of moderators on that basic relationship, e.g., consumer awareness, media use, and relationship to the firm, we theorize about the antecedents of CSR credibility—arguing that it derives from an organization’s perceived motives for supporting a cause and its congruence with the cause.

## 3.4 Motivational Attribution

Attribution theory can offer a useful theoretical foundation for understanding the crowd’s perceptions of a lead donor’s intentions. Attribution theory suggests that when people learn of a particular action to achieve a specific goal, they make inferences about the actor’s underlying motives (Jones & Davis, 1965; Wang & Lee, 2018). An established stream of research has focused on customers’ perception of the relative extent to which the organization itself (versus those external to the organization) benefits from its CSR initiatives (Geue & Plewa, 2010; Groza et al., 2011; Kim & Choi, 2018; Lange & Washburn, 2012; Wang & Lee, 2018). When presented with evidence of an organization’s CSR involvement, customers elaborate on the message and make motivational inferences, attributing the organization’s involvement to one of two primary types of motives (Becker-Olsen et al., 2006): an intrinsic, altruistic interest in serving society (e.g., assisting community development or helping citizens in need) or an extrinsic, self-serving motive (e.g., increasing profit or enhancing reputation— Becker-Olsen et al., 2006; Geue & Plewa, 2010; Rifon et al., 2004). Various terms have been used to refer to this dichotomy of motivational attributions in the literature, such as altruistic versus egoistic (Handelman & Arnold, 1999), other- vs. self-centered (Webb & Mohr, 1998), public- vs. firm-serving (Forehand & Grier, 2003), and strategic- vs. valuedriven (Geue & Plewa, 2010). We adopt the latter terminology here.

Prior research suggests that attributions of organizations’ CSR efforts to public-serving motives generate more favorable evaluations than attributions to self-serving motives (Becker-Olsen et al., 2006; Chen et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2019b; Pérez & Rodríguez del Bosque, 2013; Rifon et al., 2004; Wang & Lee, 2018). This prior work also suggests that those positive evaluations enhance the organization’s perceived credibility and that profit-seeking motives generate less favorable perceptions, driving negative perceptions of credibility.

In the context of civic crowdfunding, and consistent with the earlier discussion, we expect that organizational lead donations may trigger similar motivational attributions and credibility inferences (Groza et al.,

2011; Wang & Lee, 2018). If onlookers infer selfserving motives (strategic-driven) on the part of a lead donor organization, the lead donation will be discounted, if not ignored entirely (Dean, 1999). In the worst case, undesirable perceptions of strategic-driven donation may cause the lead donation to negatively influence subsequent fundraising activity (Dean, 1999).

## 3.5 Donor-Project Congruence

Onlookers will also expect that there is an intuitive alignment between an organizational donor’s core mission (or line of business) and its CSR activities. This notion is known in the literature as “congruence” or “fit,” and its presence (or absence) can also influence onlookers’ perception of and response to a CSR initiative. According to Sen and Bhattacharya, (2001), congruence can be defined as the extent of overlap that consumers perceive between the firm’s core business and its CSR activity (García-Jiménez et al., 2017). In the cause-related marking literature, congruence is referred to as “fit” (Bainbridge, 2001), while in the sponsorship literature, congruence is referred to as compatibility, relatedness, or relevance (Johar & Pham, 1999; McDonald, 1991; Ruth & Simonin, 2003). We interchangeably adopt the terms congruence and fit here.

Existing literature suggests that the perceived level of congruence between an organization and a CSR activity positively impacts consumers’ perception of the organization’s broader CSR efforts (Hoeffler & Keller, 2002; Pérez & Rodríguez del Bosque, 2013). Research also suggests that congruence positively influences consumers’ behavioral intentions to participate in the same CSR activity (García-Jiménez et al., 2017; Geue & Plewa, 2010; Lee et al., 2019b; Rifon et al., 2004). In the context of cause sponsorship, Rifon et al. (2004) developed and tested a cognitive model of sponsorship effects using attribution theory, finding that perceived sponsor credibility mediates the relationship between sponsor-cause congruence and consumers’ attitudes toward the sponsor. Lee et al. (2019b) demonstrated later how such perceived credibility could influence consumer intentions to participate in the CSR activity.

## 3.6 Onlookers’ Perceptions of the Different Types of Organizational Lead Donors

Based on the above discussion, we now consider the perceptions onlookers may hold of the different types of lead organizational donors in civic crowdfunding.

We consider these perceptions in light of the publicgood nature of civic crowdfunding projects and the perceived congruence, motives, and credibility that have often been documented in the literature on social initiatives undertaken by the different types of organizations.

Whereas civic crowdfunding projects generally pursue public goods, businesses mainly produce private goods and services, with the primary goal of generating profit. Businesses are thus more likely than government or nonprofit entities to be perceived as incongruent with the objectives of a civic crowdfunding project. Government entities, by nature, are not driven by profit and are primarily focused on the delivery of public services. We therefore expect that government entities will be perceived as more congruent with civic crowdfunding projects objectives than businesses.

Finally, like governments, NGOs operate with the primary purpose of delivering public services. However, NGOs typically focus on a narrower, more targeted scope of activities, implying a greater degree of alignment with the causes they support. Most NGOs have also historically emerged in response to failures in the delivery of public services by government and private enterprises (Bebbington et al., 2013). Research has evidenced the impact of NGOs over the last few decades, with a shift away from the conventional wisdom that societal development is the primary responsibility of the government or the free market (Bebbington et al., 2013). Given their pure focus on targeted public good provision and their more targeted focus on specific causes, we expect that NGO lead donors will be perceived by onlookers as more congruent with the objectives of civic crowdfunding projects than businesses or government entities.

Being motivated mainly by profit, businesses may be perceived as unsympathetic and self-interested; thus, their philanthropic efforts may be viewed skeptically by onlookers. Prior work on public-private partnerships, for example, has presented evidence that investors from the private sector are seen in many cases to be securing large profits to the detriment of the longterm social outcomes of the public good (Austin, 2010; Logue & Grimes, 2022). Prior work has also discussed the potential indirect benefits to businesses from pursuing corporate philanthropy (Andreasen, 1996), as well as consumer reactions to CSR initiatives (Groza et al., 2011). That work reports evidence that consumers, in most cases, attribute CSR initiatives to businesses’ strategic motives rather than their values or empathy for stakeholders. <sup>3</sup> Accordingly, we expect that lead donations from business organizations are more likely to be attributed to impure motives (compared to lead donations from other types of organizations), e.g., that businesses might seek to build a positive brand image or establish a public service that has spillover benefits for their operations. At the same time, government motives are most likely to be perceived as other-regarding rather than selfregarding. Research on public administration has documented that government employees are systematically more likely to report prosocial motives in their work. Moreover, government employees are more likely to be perceived as having a positive social impact (Bullock et al., 2015). Thus, we expect a lead donation from a government organization to be perceived by onlookers as being driven by pure motives, i.e., serving others.

Given their nonprofit nature and their pure focus on serving civil society through their efforts to bring more justice, democracy, participation, transparency, and better public goods and services, in addition to their emergence as a direct alternative to the state or free market (Bebbington et al., 2013), we expect that lead donations from NGOs are most likely to be attributed to pure motives.

Given the above, we ultimately expect that NGOs will garner the highest perceived credibility of civic involvement among the three types of lead donor organizations due to their particularly high congruence with the social initiatives they support (exceeding that of government, given NGOs’ more targeted scope of work) and their public focus. Further, between government and business organizations, we expect that government organizations will be perceived as more credible in their civic involvement because of their anticipated greater congruence with civic project objectives and because their donations are more likely to be perceived as having pure motives.

Based on the above points and discussion, we anticipate that lead donations from business organizations will have systematically weaker impacts on civic crowdfunding campaign success compared to other organizational donor types, namely NGOs and governments. Further, given their narrower scope of work, we expect that NGO lead donors will also have greater impacts than government lead donors. Despite the positive perceptions of credibility, a secondary reason why we might expect NGOs to outperform government is the economic mechanism of “government crowd-out” (Andreoni, 1993; Andreoni & Payne, 2003). Government crowd-out refers to the frequent observation that government contributions crowd out (entirely or partially) individual or private contributions to public goods (Andreoni, 1993; Andreoni & Payne, 2003, 2011; Bernheim, 1986; Bernheim & Bagwell, 1988; Kingma, 1989). The primary driver of crowding out here is that potential donors may perceive that a government donation is a donation that they have personally made, albeit indirectly, via their tax dollars (Andreoni & Payne, 2003). Thus, although government organizations are likely to have high credibility with potential donors in the context of civic crowdfunding, the negative effect of government crowd-out may offset the positive influence of lead donor quality signals. More formally, we therefore hypothesize the following:

H3: Lead donations from NGOs will have a larger impact on civic crowdfunding success than those provided by businesses or government organizations.

H4: Lead donations from government organizations will have a larger impact on civic crowdfunding success than those provided by businesses.

## 4 Methods

## 4.1 Study Context

To evaluate our hypotheses, we collected data from the largest civic crowdfunding platform in the United Kingdom, Spacehive. Established in 2011, Spacehive is one of the most popular civic crowdfunding platforms, dedicated to projects focused on community programs, social-oriented events, public space, and infrastructure or facility development. Spacehive implements a fixed fundraising mechanism, such that a project succeeds only if cumulative pledges reach the stated funding goal within the specified fundraising period. Upon reaching the fundraising target, fundraising ceases; funds are not collected beyond the target amount. Project fundraisers must specify pertinent campaign information, including project type, description, target amount, fundraising duration, and budget. Additionally, they can post videos, pictures, or a gallery and links to their website and social media accounts. As pledges arrive, backers and their pledge amounts are recorded as part of the backers list on the campaign page. A donation breakdown information bar is also available on each project’s backers tab to inform prospective backers about who pledged what.

Our dataset combined proprietary fundraising and project details obtained from Spacehive <sup>4</sup> with data from four open data sources provided by the UK government, which we employed to classify organizational donors as businesses, government entities, or NGOs. We identified all projects publicized and conducted on Spacehive between its inception in December 2011 and January 2017. Data on a total of 908 civic crowdfunding projects was collected. Of these 908 projects, 597 were in either the idea development or fundraising phase and were thus excluded from consideration.<sup>5</sup> We thus focused on the 311 projects that had completed their fundraising cycle, either failing or succeeding. Some of these projects lacked important pieces of data, e.g., information on the fundraising target. Thus, our final estimation sample included 303 projects, 158 of which were successful. We also scraped individual donation records for each campaign when collecting campaign data, totaling 11,284 transactions across the 303 projects. Notably, transaction records did not include time stamps; that is, we only obtained details on the sequence of donor arrival.

## 4.2 Measures

Dependent variables: We gathered project characteristics and pledge records, including pledge sequence, the total number of pledges, and pledge amounts, for all projects initiated on Spacehive between 2011 and 2015. Our key dependent variable, Success, is a binary indicator of whether the campaign met its fundraising target. Focusing on this binary measure is appropriate for at least two reasons. First, because of the fixed fundraising mechanism that Spacehive employs, funds are released to a project’s organizer only if it reaches its goal within the stated funding duration. Accordingly, if the project fails to reach its fundraising goal, all pledges are canceled, and the funds are returned to the backers. Second, overfunding is not possible because fundraising ceases as soon as the target is reached. That said, as part of our analyses, we explored alternative outcome measures, including the fraction of the goal the campaigns raised, the total number of backers attracted, and the average donation per backer.

Key independent variables: Our independent variables of interest reflect the presence of lead donations from different types of organizational entities to a given campaign, as well as measures of the size of those donations. Previous studies have used different techniques to identify early contributions. For example, Colombo et al. (2015) based their measure of lead donation on timing, looking at the total amount pledged within the first 20% of the fundraising duration. Given our focus on organizational lead donors, we focused on large donations made by any formal organization at the onset of fundraising. In the public finance literature, lead donations typically arrive first and are announced at the onset of the fundraiser (Andreoni, 2006). Given the open nature of donations on civic crowdfunding platforms, we accounted for the possibility that some individual donations may have arrived prior to a lead donation, particularly given that Spacehive actively advises all campaign organizers to never start a campaign with zero pledges and explicitly encourages organizers to self-pledge, i.e., contribute to their own campaign at the very beginning. <sup>6</sup> This consideration, in tandem with the fact that we lacked time stamp information on donations, led us to employ a sequence-based measure. When coding the lead donation variable, we considered any organizational donors among the first three pledges. <sup>7</sup> By considering the first three donations, we accounted for the possibility that organizers or their friends and family may have donated first, prior to an organizational lead donor.

To decide whether an early donation was large enough to qualify as a lead donation, we referred to a practitioner definition from The Nonprofit Handbook: Fund Raising: “The lead gift should be at least 10% of the overall goal” (Andreoni, 2006; Lawson, 2001, p. 756). This stylized rule is appropriate as it has become the conventional wisdom among all fund-raising professionals and is repeated in many fundraising training and advice manuals (Andreoni, 2006). All subsequent visitors can observe these lead donations at the top of the backer list or in the donations breakdown information bar on the same page.

To distinguish between the impact of any organizational lead donation (i.e., received or not), the impact of the intensity of the lead donation (i.e., the amount of the lead donation), and the type of donating organization (i.e., government, business, nonprofit), we constructed several alternative measures. We began with a binary measure, AnyOrgLead, which equals 1 if any of the first three pledges received by the project arrived from a third-party organization and are larger than 10% of the stated fundraising target. The second variable, AmntOrgLead, is a continuous measure reflecting the total amount of lead donations supplied, in GBP. To construct measures of involvement by different organizational lead donor types, we drew upon three open data sources provided by the UK government to distinguish between NGOs, government entities, and business entities (Appendix B for details). Based on this, we constructed a set of organizational lead donor type-specific measures, analogous to those described above: AnyGovLead reflecting a lead donation from a government organization, and AmntGovLead, a continuous measure capturing the total dollar amount of lead donations in GBP provided by government entities. The same approach was used to construct analogous measures for businesses, i.e., AnyBusLead, AmntBusLead, and NGOs, i.e., AnyNGOLead, AmntNGOLead.

Control variables: We also incorporated a set of control variables related to the project and organizer features to better isolate the effects of organizational lead donations on the success of civic crowdfunding projects.

Project controls: We incorporated a set of control variables related to project features to better isolate the effects of organizational lead donations on the success of civic crowdfunding projects. We included the logarithm of the project goal in GBP, minus funds raised among the first three pledges, referred to as GoalAfter3. Here, we subtracted the total amount of funds raised in the first three pledges to remove any influence associated with the size or amount of the first three pledges derived from a direct reduction in the amount of money left to raise. In doing so, any effect we observed must have operated via a different mechanism, e.g., signaling effects.

Previous studies have also suggested that the inclusion of media, namely images and videos, may send quality signals, reflecting the preparedness of the project creator. This can influence crowdfunding performance positively and lead to more funds and success (Courtney et al., 2017; Hobbs et al., 2016; Mollick, 2014). Thus, we included several variables to control for media use. Specifically, HasVideo equals 1 if the project creator included a video and 0 otherwise, while HasGallery equals 1 if the creator included a gallery of images in the pitch and 0 otherwise.

Other studies have suggested that the quality of the textual description could also influence crowdfunding performance for the same reason (Du et al., 2015; Mitra & Gilbert, 2014). To control for features of the project pitch text, we included the logarithm of BriefLength, the length (in words) of the brief project description provided by the creator on Spacehive, and the logarithm of DescLength, which captured the length of the detailed project description. We also included Readability, based on the Gunning Fog Index, as suggested by Du et al. (2015), to control for the readability of the project text description. The Gunning Fog Index is a famous text readability score that estimates the reading level of a block of text. For example, a Fog Index of 12 indicates that a block of text was appropriately written for the reading level of a US high school senior.

Finally, we accounted for any systematic project category differences in civic crowdfunding by including six dummy variables for each of the six (optional) categories highlighted by Spacehive: Streets & Infrastructure, Building, Park & Gardens, Sports & Play, Food & Farming, Art & Culture. Thus, a project may have associated category tags but this is not required. Moreover, these category tags are not mutually exclusive; a given project can have up to two category associations. Since our data included projects launched at different times over the four years between 2011 and 2015, we also included year fixed effects to control for unobservable temporal trends and shocks that applied across the whole market, which may have influenced civic crowdfunding project success.

Here, we should note that we did not incorporate specific controls for campaign duration. From a feasibility perspective, this was not possible, because campaign durations are not publicly disclosed after the fact. At the same time, this is not a serious concern, because campaign durations on Spacehive are likely to be highly associated with a fundraising goal—the host platform provides clear guidelines and recommendations to campaign organizers about campaign durations based on the amount of money being sought. <sup>8</sup> Therefore, in controlling for fundraising goal, we implicitly accounted for campaign duration at the same time.

Project creator controls: We also collected data related to social media accounts associated with the project creator, including the presence of a Facebook account or Twitter account. HasFacebook equals 1 if the project creator provided a Facebook account on the project page and 0 otherwise. HasTwitter equals 1 if the project creator provided a Twitter account handle on the project page and 0 otherwise. Although data was available on the number of friends and followers for each account, respectively, we refrained from including these variables in our analyses because we collected them post hoc, long after the completion of the campaign. Because fundraisers may attract friends and followers throughout a campaign, each value may be a reflection of campaign success rather than simply a cause.

Project creators can also choose to target several user communities (i.e., known as Hives or, more recently, Movements) with their fundraiser on the Spacehive platform. The variable Community indicated whether the project had been shared with at least one specialized (focused) user community, such as a council program or a city mayor community. Finally, to control for any credibility or legitimacy benefits that may have been afforded by presenting oneself as a representative of an organization, we included CreatorType, which equals 0 if the project creator presented themselves purely as an individual and 1 if the creator presented themselves as working on behalf of an organization or group.

Appendix C provides a summary list of all the variables used in our study along with their definitions. Appendix D provides detailed descriptive statistics for each variable, and Appendix E provides their correlation matrix, incorporating Pearson’s two-tailed tests of statistical significance.

## 4.4 Robustness Checks

We performed several robustness checks, to assess the sensitivity of our results and to address potential endogeneity in the lead donation effect estimates. To assess the sensitivity of our findings to our choice of dependent variables, we first explored an alternative measure of fundraising success. Specifically, as reported in Appendix I1, we repeated our focal estimations replacing the outcome variable with the fraction of the fundraising goal that was ultimately obtained. We observed consistent results employing the fractional outcome.

Next, we replaced our outcome variable with the average donation per backer and the number of unique backers a campaign received. We observed an interesting pattern of results when considering these two outcomes. Specifically, we found that government and business lead donors drive divergent effects—they reduce backer counts while increasing average donation amounts. By contrast, NGO lead donors only induce increases in average donation amounts, with no statistically significant impact on backer counts. These results are suggestive of underlying mechanisms behind the main findings of heterogeneity; however, as we discuss in the Appendix, the results need to be interpreted carefully.<sup>9</sup>

Next, we explored the sensitivity of our findings to our choice of independent variables. In Appendix I2, we report the results of replacing our continuous lead donation amount variables with measures reflecting the fraction of the fundraising goal that those lead donations represented. Further, we explored the possible role of outliers, winsorizing our measures of lead donation and outstanding campaign goal amounts at the 1% and 5% levels. In all cases, we continued to observe consistent results.

Regarding endogeneity, in our first robustness check (Appendix G), we report a set of instrumental variable regressions based on Lewbel’s method (2012). This instrumental variable approach has been recently applied in the IS literature, e.g., Hong et al. (2018). In Appendix H, we report estimations following the application of propensity score matching (PSM) (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). Using PSM, we focused on achieving covariate balance, specifically around the NGO lead donation treatment. The results we observed are consistent with our main set of results. Finally, to confirm our theorized mechanisms, we undertook an additional data collection and analysis exercise. We employed a pair of independent coders, asking them to inspect each campaign and organizational lead donor and report their perceptions, e.g., regarding the donor’s likely motive and fit. We then analyzed the resulting data to understand how those perceptions related to the coder’s stated interest or willingness to donate to a project. We report the result of that coding and analysis exercise in Appendix J. Importantly, the results are consistent with our main theorizing and findings and speak to the explicit mediating role of motivational attributions and project congruence.

## 5 Discussion

## 5.1 Summary of Results

Crowdfunding is a social phenomenon that has exploded in recent years. Civic crowdfunding is a vibrant subgenre of crowdfunding that deals with the financing of public goods that benefit local communities. Lead organizational donations in civic crowdfunding function as important signals of project legitimacy and organizer credibility that can improve fundraising performance. We demonstrate that the benefit of these lead donations depends on how the crowd perceives the lead donor’s involvement. Our study offers a first attempt to quantify such heterogeneity. We build on a long stream of IS research on crowdfunding, particularly work dealing with third-party endorsements. Drawing on the CSR literature dealing with source credibility, motivational attribution, and congruence, we hypothesize and evaluate how and when lead donations can be expected to influence fundraising outcomes.

We show that lead donations from business organizations are viewed less positively, on average, than those provided by government entities and NGOs. We theorize that this occurs because onlookers perceive business donations to be relatively less aligned with the public objectives of civic projects because they are motivated by the business’s interests; therefore they perceive the business to be less credible in its involvement. On the other hand, we also show that lead donations from government entities are viewed more positively, on average, by onlookers because of positive perceptions about the underlying public-serving motives of the government—in addition to the government’s natural fit with the public good objectives of civic crowdfunding projects. Nevertheless, we did not find direct support for H4, which posited that having lead donations from governmental organizations would be of greater benefit to campaigns compared to having lead donations from businesses. A possible explanation for this null result is that the signaling benefits of government donations are uniquely offset by crowding out. Prior literature has notably found that government donations to public good campaigns tend to crowd out individual or private donations. When private donors perceive that the government is already providing support for a cause, they may assume that they have already contributed through their tax dollars, leading to a reduction in private donations (Payne, 1998; Simmons & Emanuele, 2004). Additionally, private donors may feel that their donations are less necessary when government funding is available, as they may believe that the government should be primarily responsible for providing funding for important causes.

In contrast to the other two organizational lead donor types, NGOs tend to align most closely with the (public good) objectives of civic crowdfunding projects, and they are not subject to the issue of crowding out that government donations present. NGOs have emerged historically as a response to the failed efforts of the state (i.e., government organizations) and free market (i.e., business organizations) to respond to the needs of civil society (Bebbington et al., 2013). By nature, NGOs are more specific and focused regarding the scope of public goods that they address. Moreover, they are perceived to be more truthful than business organizations in their aim to serve society. Based on these various points, we theorized (and found empirical evidence) that lead donations from NGOs would be most beneficial.

Robustness checks and an analysis of perceptual measures support our main conclusions, demonstrating the theorized mechanisms, and establishing the stability and causal nature of our results. Our collection and analysis of human-coded perceptual measures of lead donor project congruence, motivational attribution, CI credibility, and expectations of others’ support yield results consistent with our theory. Moreover, the tests conducted assessing systematic perceptual differences across lead donor organization types confirmed that onlookers view them differently.

## 5.2 Theoretical Contributions

Our study makes several unique contributions to the literature. First and foremost, our work extends a lengthy body of work in IS on crowdfunding (Burtch et al., 2013, 2016; Jiang et al., 2020; Kim & Hann, 2019; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Roma et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2020). Our study is one of the very few works to consider the drivers of fundraising success in civic crowdfunding (Hassna, 2022; Lee et al., 2019a; van Montfort et al., 2020) and the only work, to our knowledge, that examines the impact of organizational lead donations. Our study is also the first to examine heterogeneity in lead donors’ influence, demonstrating how lead donors’ characteristics affect the interpretation of the quality signal their involvement conveys and thus the benefits their involvement provides to fundraisers.

While crowdfunding was considered a monolithic phenomenon less than 10 years ago, it is now clear that important theoretical and practical differences exist between different crowdfunding formats (Block et al., 2018; Pollack et al., 2019). While each crowdfunding context bears similarities, different stakeholders, motivations, and mechanisms are often at play (Pollack et al., 2019). Prior work on third-party endorsement and lead investors has focused on reward, equity, and debt-based crowdfunding, where contributors almost always hail from the private sector and are motivated by a tangible reward or financial return. In the case of civic crowdfunding, the concept of project quality takes on a broader meaning compared to other crowdfunding formats, encompassing not only the potential for success but also the perceived value the project holds for its benefactors, i.e., its local community. Our work thus demonstrates that in the unique context of civic crowdfunding, lead donations from organizations serve as powerful signals, not only of a project’s potential for success but also of its likely impact on the community. When an organization makes a lead donation, it signifies to potential donors that the organization firmly believes in the project's value to the community and its ability to bring about positive change. This act of signaling fosters trust and engagement among other potential donors in the local community who are more inclined to support a project that is seen as legitimate and genuinely valuable to the community.

The differences between civic crowdfunding and other, traditional crowdfunding formats emphasize the importance of understanding the distinct mechanisms at play in crowdfunding contributors’ decisionmaking. Notably, the theorizing and analyses we undertake here directly address this point. In doing so, our work also addresses recent calls in the IS literature for work examining the drivers of fundraising success in crowdfunding contexts that do not involve rewards or expectations of financial return (Beaulieu et al., 2015). Further, these analyses also address recent calls by Pollack et al. (2019) for work that explicitly examines theories and mechanisms across different and understudied crowdfunding contexts, such as civic crowdfunding.

Second, our work contributes to the broader literature dealing with the economics of information and public goods. We provide a first demonstration of the moderating influence that lead donor characteristics have on the signaling benefits of lead donations. We extend recent work that has shown the importance of lead donor identity (Karlan & List, 2020) and provide a more nuanced consideration of donor identity, considering how perceptions deriving from different aspects of donor identity may affect onlookers’ quality inferences. We achieve this via a novel integration of signaling theory and multiple theoretical lenses, i.e., source credibility, congruence, motivational attribution, and government crowd-out. While signaling theory tends to treat all third-party endorsements as equivalent, attribution theory, source credibility theory, and the notion of government crowd-out suggest that signaling benefits will vary depending on lead donor characteristics. This novel perspective suggests an opportunity to explore related sources of heterogeneity in the benefits of lead investment and third-party endorsement in other crowdfunding contexts, including equity- and rewardbased crowdfunding as well as traditional charitable fundraising.

Third, most recent work on crowdfunding has relied on archival data to test their hypotheses, with rare exceptions (Greenberg & Mollick, 2017; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). Our study demonstrates the value of alternative data-collection methods, i.e., human-coded perceptual measures, as a means of evaluating more complex nomological networks and relationships among theoretical constructs.

## 5.3 Practical Implications

Our study has several practical implications for civic project creators and platform operators. Our findings provide crowdfunding creators with insights into the ideal features of a lead organizational donor. Our results suggest that creators of civic crowdfunding projects should invest effort in attracting organizational lead donor funds before or as they launch their civic crowdfunding campaigns but emphasize that they should do so with lead donor characteristics in mind. Specifically, effort should be targeted toward organizations that are likely to be perceived as congruent with the project’s goals and organizations that will be perceived as having pure motives, e.g., cause-oriented firms. Campaign organizers should thus be attentive to the unique context and features of a particular project and candidate lead donor.

Our study provides evidence that lead donations from NGOs are beneficial for the success of civic crowdfunding campaigns. Conversely, our findings indicate that lead donations from business organizations are generally viewed less positively compared to those from government entities and NGOs. Consequently, lead donations from business organizations are the least beneficial in this scenario. Furthermore, our robustness analysis reveals that government and business lead donors tend to attract significantly fewer backers following the lead donation. This suggests that relying on these types of lead donors may have drawbacks beyond the initial acquisition cost.

That being said, it is important to note that while our findings suggest that lead donations from NGOs are, on average, more beneficial than those from government or business organizations, there may be exceptions. For instance, an NGO may occasionally receive negative media coverage, or its social focus may not align well with a specific campaign’s objectives, resulting in a noncredible perception of its lead donation. Conversely, exceptional scenarios may arise where a business’s incentives align exceptionally well with those of the community, making its lead donation appear credible. These exceptions highlight the significance of considering the unique circumstances and characteristics of both the project and the lead donor organization.

In practical terms, our findings suggest that civic crowdfunding project creators should prioritize attracting lead donations from NGOs while carefully considering the fit and intentions of potential lead donor organizations. At a broader level, our findings indicate that the benefit of a lead donation depends on the unique context and features of a crowdfunding project, as well as the characteristics of the candidate lead donor organization. The signaling value of a lead donation is contingent upon how the lead donor is perceived by onlookers in terms of congruence, motivation, and credibility.

This insight is important because attracting funding from a lead donor entity can be time-consuming and costly. The philanthropy landscape is highly fractured and by no means standardized, making it difficult to navigate. For example, the organizational structure around philanthropy may vary across organizations, e.g., it may be managed from a dedicated department in some organizations, or as part of a marketing department in others.<sup>10</sup> Successfully attracting a lead donation will often depend on first establishing a direct relationship with the donor organization, particularly if the organization does not already maintain a formal philanthropy program. While government entities and NGOs may be relatively more standardized in their granting procedures than corporations, e.g., maintaining regular application cycles or evaluation procedures, campaigns need to tackle these issues early and quickly to build and maintain as much momentum as possible (Etter et al., 2013; Lu et al., 2014). Accordingly, pursuing the right type of entity upfront is essential from an efficiency standpoint.

Moreover, successful participation and collaboration to solve social problems requires the perception of intersecting values and motivations, despite stakeholder diversity. Although we do not assess the possibility here, it is reasonable to expect that the value of a lead organizational donation might be amplified if campaign organizers can appropriately manage onlookers’ perceptions and interpretations. Platform operators, fundraisers, and lead donors themselves should perhaps be encouraged to articulate the rationale for the lead donor’s involvement and clarify how that donor’s interests align with those of the public and community.

Finally, understanding the dynamics of lead donations can assist platform operators in providing guidance and support to project creators in their search for suitable lead donors. It is perhaps notable that following our study, Spacehive initiated a program to formally pair campaigns with large donor organizations. Our results suggest that as platform operators seek to facilitate lead donations, matching lead donors to civic crowdfunding campaigns, they should consider project-donor alignment and the crowd’s attributions of the lead donor’s motivation for involvement. More generally, our findings are also useful because they can help platform operators streamline participation and grantseeking from, specific types of entities, offering appropriate coaching to project organizers about why, where, and how to seek funds.

## 6 Limitations & Future Research

Although this study provides significant contributions to the IS literature on crowdfunding and offers several practical implications, our work is subject to at least four notable limitations.

First and foremost, we draw on data from a leading crowdfunding platform in the UK. Although the platform is prominent in the UK, having been endorsed by the former Prime Minister, David Cameron for its transformative impact, the generalizability of our findings to the broader civic crowdfunding domain remains unclear. For example, the policies and design of Spacehive may play a role in our results. For example, projects on Spacehive cannot be overfunded; after reaching their stated fundraising target, the platform ceases fundraising. Other civic crowdfunding platforms that do allow overfunding may exhibit different outcomes. Future work could explore this generalizability.

Second, the dataset we employed in our analyses was cross-sectional. As a result, we were unable to assess dynamics in the crowdfunding process that may have resulted from the arrival of different types of lead donors. Future researchers could attempt to collect dynamic data, which could help them understand how lead donors influence the pace at which contributions arrive at the campaign throughout its lifecycle and how lead donations interact with other aspects of the campaign’s conduct, e.g., social media activity.

Third, while our analysis and findings suggest that organizational lead donors positively influence civic crowdfunding performance, our study does not distinctly clarify whether this effect is attributable to subsequent individual donors, organizational donors, or a combination of both. We acknowledge this as a limitation of our study. Future research could delve deeper into this aspect, exploring the dynamic interplay between organizational lead donations and the subsequent contributions from both organizational and individual donors. Such investigations could greatly expand our understanding of donor behaviors and their collective impact on civic crowdfunding initiatives.

Fourth, and finally, our work does not speak to the antecedents of lead donations. Future work could complement our study by identifying the antecedents of lead donor acquisition.

## Appendix B: Coding Donor Types

We inspected donors’ profiles to determine whether a donor was an organization and, if so, what type of organization. To arrive at a determination about the type, we first considered URL links to an organization’s website provided in the profile. If the website included a “.gov” domain extension, we identified it as a government entity. Any profile lacking a .gov URL that nonetheless indicated it was associated with a city council, a borough, or some other forms of regional government was also identified as a government entity after double checking with a dataset provided by UK government and containing 450 departments, agencies and public bodies. <sup>11</sup> We considered employing a similar approach for nonprofits, based on a “.org” domain extension; however, although the original intent of this extension was that it be reserved for nonprofits, the restriction was never formally enforced and has subsequently been eliminated. Accordingly, a .org extension no longer indicates the domain is held by a nonprofit entity. To address this issue, we conducted a search of Metasoft’s Foundation Search Index, a database of more than 22,000 registered nonprofits and charities in the UK. Any matches to organizations’ profile names were identified as NGOs.<sup>12</sup> Lastly, performing a similar search using a public index of registered corporations and companies maintained by the UK federal government, known as “Companies House,” matched organizations were identified as businesses.<sup>13</sup>

## Appendix D: Descriptive Statistics

The final dataset consisted of 303 projects, including 158 successful and 145 failed projects. Projects could belong to up to two project categories. The projects in our sample were distributed over categories as follows: 137 in Streets & Infrastructure, 78 in Buildings, 51 in Parks & Gardens, 109 in Arts & Culture, 47 in Sports & Play, and 23 in Food & Farming. The average (raw) fundraising target was 31,775 GBP. The average project in our sample attracted 35 backers and raised 19,463 GBP. The total number of financial transactions (i.e., pledges) for the 303 projects was 10,558 pledges; the total amount collected by all successful projects in our sample (158) was 4,751,460 GBP. Out of the 303 projects, 136 projects received a lead donation from an organization. More specifically, 67 projects received a lead donation from an NGO, with an average lead donation amount of 2,484 GBP and a max of 232,500 GBP; 51 projects received lead donations from government entities, with an average lead donation amount of 4,736 GBP and a max of 315,000 GBP, and 18 projects received lead donations from business organizations, with an average lead donation amount of 377 GBP and a max of 50,000 GBP. In terms of media use, 86 projects listed a Facebook account on their project pages and 186 projects provided a Twitter account handle. ## Appendix F: Lead Organizational Donation Effect

Recall that our main analyses focused on the first three donations to any campaign. Although the prior literature highlights that lead donations are typically the first donation, we focused on the first three because Spacehive actively encourages campaign organizers to self-pledge, i.e., contribute to their own campaign, or solicit pledges from friends and family, at the outset of fundraising. Accordingly, by considering the first three donations, we allowed for the possibility that organizers or their friends and family might have donated first, in advance of an organizational lead donor. That said, for this section, we assessed the robustness of our findings to relaxing this assumption. We reconstructed our main variables of interest, limiting our consideration to the first one and then the first two donors, and expanding out to consideration to the first five donors—we then repeated our regressions, comparing those results with our findings in the main manuscript, which are based on measures considering the first three donations.

In Table F1, we present logistic regression results employing alternative measures of organizational lead donations, considering a measure based on the first donation alone, the first two donations, and the first three donations (the latter being the measure we focus upon in the main analyses). As expected, we found that the effect of the NGO lead donations is stronger than lead donations from other organization types in all three models. Moreover, the effects were strongest when we focused on the first donation and gradually weakened as we expanded the definition of our measure to consider the first two donations and, finally, the first three donations. These results are broadly in line with what we expected to see, and they indicate that our results were not driven by our choice to consider “lead donors” as those among the first three. For Table F2, we considered a wider set of initial donors, defining lead donor organizations as any who make their donations among the first five donors to a campaign. Using this definition, we again obtained consistent results. Note: the effects again weakened as we relaxed our definition.

## Appendix H: Propensity Score Matching Robustness Check

We implemented propensity score matching (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983), or PSM, using a logistic propensity score function, as follows, employing the R “MatchIt” function. First, we considered the set of matching covariates available in the dataset (excluding variables that are downstream outcomes of the campaign process, e.g., likes, comments and so on). We believe these set of covariates are informative and comprehensive, such that they influence the “treatment” assignment (i.e., receiving lead donation from an NGO) and yet are not affected by the “treatment”, thus satisfying the unconfoundedness or selection-on-observables identification assumption of PSM. We first evaluated balance on these covariates in the original sample and saw that a large number of the variables are unbalanced between campaigns that have a lead donation from an NGO and campaigns that do not. For example, the “streets” indicator, the “arts” indicator, and whether a campaign has a Facebook page. Table H1 presents mean comparisons and statistical tests of imbalance.

Next, we estimated a logistic regression (the propensity function) relating our NGO lead donation variable to all of these other covariates given we have no theoretical basis for excluding any of them (this approach is recommended by Rubin & Thomas, 1996) and subsequently recovered predicted probabilities of NGO lead donation for each campaign, based on the resulting model. Figure H1 depicts density plots of the resulting predicted probabilities (propensity scores), by “treatment” status. Campaigns that received NGO lead donation (red) clearly had systematically higher propensities for receiving NGO lead donations than campaigns that did not (blue).

## Appendix J: Perceptual Coding & Extra Analyses

Our archival analysis based on organizational lead donor types yielded results consistent with our theorization and expectations. However, to confirm our theorization about the perceptions onlookers may hold about the different types of lead organizational donors and the mechanism by which these perceptions influence civic crowdfunding success and to mitigate the possibility that our results were driven by other, confounded differences between lead donor organization types or unobserved factors that confounded the presence of different organizational lead donor types, we undertook an extra perceptual data collection and analysis.<sup>16</sup>

## J1. Data Collection & Coding Procedure:

We hired two human coders to visit the fundraising web pages for the campaigns in our sample, to consider the first three donors, identify those that were organizations, and conduct background research on each. That is, if the lead donor’s profile indicated an organization web page, the coders were asked to navigate there and read about the organization, its purpose, mission, history, location, etc. If this information was not present on the Spacehive profile page, the coders were asked to conduct searches of news outlets in the United Kingdom, and a more general Google search, employing the organization’s profile name, to locate similar information. Subsequently, for each organizational lead donor, the coders were asked to independently respond to 26 distinct survey items that were drawn from past work, to capture the coders’ perceptions about the organization’s civic involvement CI credibility, its fit with the project (i.e., congruence), the organization’s likely motives for participating, and the coder’s belief that they or others would be moved positively to support the campaign in question as a result of the lead donor’s participation. On average, the coders reported that this process required an average of approximately 15 minutes per donor. Given the presence of hundreds of organizational lead donors in our data, the overall process took approximately 60 hours per coder and was conducted over three weeks (each coder spent about four hours per working day).

Because the coders were tasked with performing their coding efforts independently, for the measures to be valid, we felt it would be important to establish that the two coders exhibited some commonality in their perceptions. Therefore, we assessed the level of agreement between our coders. Indeed, the two coders were not made aware of one another. Despite this, when we calculated the average Cronbach’s alpha across all survey items, we observed a value of approximately 0.7. This value is on the lower end of acceptability for intercoder agreement according to Nunnally and Bernstein (1994), but this is also somewhat expected given the highly subjective nature of individual perceptions.

Note, although we did not expect strict agreement (given the perceptual nature of the item coding generally), we did assess agreement on coders’ classification of the organization types. The coders were largely consistent in this regard, with fewer than twenty instances of disagreement. Most disagreements pertained to organizations’ classification as government or nonprofit. In all such cases, the second author manually resolved the disagreements by inspecting the websites, social media profiles, and news articles the coders identified. Although the second author resolved disagreements on entity types, the coders’ perceptual responses were taken at face value.

Please refer to Section J5 of this online appendix for details about the coding instructions, Section J6 for details about the survey items that the coders used when reviewing the projects, and Section J7 for the correlation matrix of all survey items.

## J2. Convergent & Discriminant Validity:

We evaluated the convergent and discriminant validity of our survey items and associated latent constructs, namely perceptual measures of donor congruence (fit), value-driven motivation, strategic-driven (opportunistic) motivation, CSR credibility, and perceived likelihood that the campaign would receive support from the crowd. To assess convergent and discriminant validity, we first fit a structural equation model that relates the various survey items to their latent constructs. Subsequently, we calculated squared correlations among the latent constructs as well as their average variance explained.<sup>17</sup>

As explained by Fornell and Larcker (1981), convergent validity is the extent to which a set of indicators reflecting the same construct are positively correlated with one another. It can be claimed that the convergent validity of a latent construct is demonstrated when the construct is able to explain at least 50% of the variance arising in its indicators, on average. This measure, commonly referred to as average variance extracted (AVE), is calculated as the sum of squared standardized factor loadings, divided by the number of indicators.

Discriminant validity can be established when the AVE for a latent construct is larger than its squared correlation (SC) with any other latent construct in the model. This inequality establishes that each latent construct shares more variance with its own associated indicators than with any other latent variable expressed by other sets of indicators in the model. As can be seen in Table J1, all latent variables exhibit an AVE greater than 0.5, and the AVEs exceed all squared pairwise correlations among latent variables.

## J4. Analysis & Results

Having established the intercoder reliability as well as the convergent and discriminant validity of our perceptual measures, we proceeded to average the survey items within each construct, a standard practice in the survey literature. Leveraging the resulting human-coded measures allowed us to conduct a more direct estimation of the theorized mechanisms. Recall that we theorized that organizational lead donors have a positive influence on donation activity when the lead donor’s congruence with the project is clear and when the lead donor’s perceived motives for participating are positive (e.g., value-driven motives, rather than strategic motives). We theorized that these positive effects are partially mediated by the effect those perceptions have on the perceived CE credibility of an organizational lead donor. Accordingly, we evaluated those mechanisms employing the perceptual responses from our coders. We conducted these analyses via mediated regression models via a causal-mediation analysis (Imai et al., 2010), employing the “mediation” package in R (Tingley et al., 2014).

To conduct the causal mediation, we began by specifying our first- and second-stage regression models. The first-stage model regressed the mediator, CI credibility, on the amount of the donor’s contribution, and the other perceptual measures, namely perceived congruence between the donor and the project, and perceptions of the extent to which the organizational lead donor was driven by value-based motives or strategic interest. The second-stage model regressed the coder’s stated expectations about whether the crowd’s intention to support the campaign would be positively affected by that donor’s participation onto the mediator and other first-stage independent variables. These two models are presented below in Equations (5) and (6).

$$
\begin{array}{l} C I \text {Credibility} = L n (A m o u n t) + \text {Congruence} + \text {ValueMotives} + \text {StrategicMotives} + \varepsilon \\ \text {CrowdSupport} = L n (A m o u n t) + \text {Congruence} + \text {ValueMotives} + \text {StrategicMotives} + C I \text {Credibility} + \varepsilon \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

(6)

With this causal mediation analysis, we could quantify both the direct and mediated effect of each first-stage independent variable on the final outcome. The analyses also isolated the directionality of each effect, as well as the proportion of the total causal effect that operated through the mediator. We thus reported both the direct and mediated effects on expected crowd support for each of our key perceptual measures, namely congruence, value-driven motives, and strategic motives, as well as the proportion of the total effect that was mediated

To interpret the estimates as causal, this analysis depends on an assumption of sequential ignorability—i.e., that the effect of the mediator on the final outcome (expected crowd support) is exogenous. To assess this, we employed the method of sensitivity analysis introduced by Imai et al. (2010), which is conceptually analogous to the notion of Rosenbaum (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983) bounds, for propensity score matching (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). The analysis quantifies how the estimated ACME would change in the presence of an unobserved confounder in the secondstage regression—that is, in the presence of a confounder for the mediator-outcome relationship. The analysis proceeds by assuming the presence of a confounder (which manifests as a correlation between the errors of the first- and secondstage regression models) and recovering the ACME under that assumption. This process was repeated for alternative strengths and directions of confounding (the sensitivity parameter, ρ, which operationalized the direction and magnitude of correlation between the mediator effect and the assumed confounder, is adjusted by incremental correlations of 0.10 with each reestimation) to recover the distribution of changes to the ACME and thus its implied sensitivity to confounding.

We present the results of these sensitivity analyses for all three first-stage independent variables, namely congruence, value-driven motives, and strategic motives, in Figure J1, finding very similar results in each case. We present the results graphically for the sake of comprehension. Changes in the assumed confounder are captured by the x-axis, and the change in the estimated ACME is captured by the y-axis, in each case. For the effect of “Fit” (congruence), we see that the effect would remain positive and statistically significant in the presence of any negative confounder. Further, the effect would remain positive and significant in the presence of a weak positive confounder (up to a correlation of approximately 0.10). The effect would shift and become statistically significant and negative only in the presence of a positive confounder exceeding a correlation of approximately 0.30. Based on this, the resulting estimates appear rather robust.

Next, considering the sensitivity analysis associated with the value-driven motives ACME estimates, we observe a very similar result. Any negative confounder would only amplify our estimates, and a positive confounder would only negate our estimates if its correlation were to exceed 0.15. The direction of the effect would further only invert and become statistically significant, in the presence of a positive confounder with a correlation exceeding 0.3. Third, considering the estimated ACME for perceived strategic motives, again, any negative confounder would only amplify our observed effects, as with the other two first-stage variables. Further, as before, the estimated ACME would only become null with a positive confounder exceeding a correlation of 0.10, and the effect would only invert in direction and become significant in the presence of a confounder with a correlation exceeding 0.30.

He holds a PhD from the Haas School of Business, University of California at Berkeley. He was a chair professor at the City University of Hong Kong and University of Arizona, respectively. Dr. Zhao’s research focuses on blockchain, business intelligence, fintech, and human-AI collaboration. He has published over three hundred research papers in journals and conference proceedings, including such journals as Management Science, Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, INFORMS Journal on Computing, Journal of Management Information Systems, and IEEE and ACM Transactions. He is the co-editor of Financial Innovation, a Springer open access journal, and has co-edited over 20 special issues for academic journal including MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Operations Management, Decision Support Systems. Dr. Zhao has chaired over 30 conferences covering information systems, service sciences, fintech, and blockchain. He has received an IBM Faculty Award and a Chang Jiang Scholar Chair Professorship at Tsinghua University.
