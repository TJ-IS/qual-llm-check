# From panopticon to heautopticon: A new form of surveillance introduced by quantified-self practices

Jean-François De Moya | Jessie Pallud

EM Strasbourg Business School, Université de Strasbourg, Humanis (UR 7308)

Correspondence

Jessie Pallud, EM Strasbourg Business School, Université de Strasbourg, Humanis (UR 7308). Email: jessie.pallud@em-strasbourg.eu

## Abstract

In this research, we investigate whether quantified-self (QS) technologies, based on wearable technologies, enable individuals' empowerment or lead to their disempowerment. To understand better the potential paradoxical effects of QS technologies, we adopt a critical approach by mobilising the panopticon metaphor from Foucault's original writings and more precisely, his four core concepts, namely power, knowledge, body and space. Relying on a qualitative study that consists of the analysis of 12 interviews, 14 142 messages extracted from online forums, and 30 blogs, we explore the discourses associated with the usage of QS technologies. Our findings indicate that self-quantification has ambivalent, conflicting effects—13 empowering and 11 disempowering— spanning the dimensions of power, knowledge, body and space. Our research also shows the emergence of microsurveillance of oneself, which we name the heautopticon. The implications of this critical research also offer possibilities for change by discussing social and individual emancipation.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The European Parliamentary Research Service identifies wearable technology as one of the 10 technologies that wil change people's lives (Van Woensel, Archer, Panades-Estruch, & Vrscaj, 2015). Wearables offer unlimited opportuni ties for improving human life, such as enhancing health (Barrett, Humblet, Hiatt, & Adler, 2013; Majmudar, Colucci, &

Landman, 2015; Stiglbauer, Weber, & Batinic, 2019), boosting self-esteem (Karapanos, Gouveia, Hassenzahl, & Forlizzi, 2016), preventing chronic diseases and empowering patients (Barrett et al., 2013; Nelson, Verhagen, & Noordzij, 2016). In 2018, nearly 117 million wearables were sold worldwide (Lamkin, 2018), with leading brands suc as Fitbit and Nike+. Revenue in the wearables segment is projected to reach \$15,376 million in 2020 and \$17,856 million by 2024 (Statista, 2019). An increasing number of people use wearables to track their physical data (e.g., steps, calorie intake and heart rate) and share their information with friends, a movement called the quantified self (QS). The QS movement relies on different self-tracking tools, such as bracelets, smartwatches, sensors, fitness trackers or any other device that can measure biological attributes (Chan, Estève, Fourniols, Escriba, & Campo, 2012 De Moya, Pallud, & Scornavacca, 2019). A branch of the Internet of Things, QS focuses on the data generated b human bodies (Couturier, Sola, Borioli, & Raiciu, 2012). “The philosophy behind the QS movement is that by using quantifiable data, which can be collected relatively easily through readily available technology, one can significantl improve the understanding of one's health and gain deeper insights into different approaches to improving health” (Kim, 2014, p. 552). As such, prior research has suggested that QS contributes to individuals' empowerment b enabling better self-management, higher autonomy, more control and knowledge about one's health conditio through their access to personalised data (Petrakaki, Hilberg, & Waring, 2018)

While QS technologies are promising in empowering people, “giving them control over the determinants of their quality of life” (Tengland, 2016, p. 34), they also raise new issues related to surveillance (Goodyear, Kerner, & Quennerstedt, 2019). Jarvenpaa and Lang (2005) point out that users' experiences with mobile technologies are paradoxical as they “seem to have contradictory or inconsistent qualities” (p. 7). Because wearables assist individuals in daily life, they are worn continuously and create ubiquitous monitoring of habits and activities (Lupton, 2015), thus contributing to dependence and enslavement (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005). While this monitoring helps in correcting anomalies and solving weight problems (Depper & Howe, 2017), it also facilitates an extensive collection of privat data and general surveillance of humans (Turel et al., 2019). Research points out a digitised individual (Matt, Trenz Cheung, & Turel, 2019), a kind of data double (Haggerty & Ericson, 2000) that leaves traces every time the perso uses the technology (Kunst & Vatrapu, 2019; Poster, 1990). Thus, researchers also view the QS as a threat to privacy (Lanzing, 2016; Lupton, 2016; Ouellet, Ménard, Bonenfant, & Mondoux, 2015). As Jensen and Draffan (2004, p. 56) state, “The transformation of living beings to numbers, or rather our own transformation from people who perceive others as willful beings with unique identities to subjects who perceive others as objects—whether through science economics, or the issuing of identity numbers—is all about control.” Moreover, “the less control one has, the les empowered one is” (Tengland, 2016, p. 35). While this tension between empowerment and disempowerment has been examined in the context of mobile phones (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, Isaac, & Kalika 2014), information system (IS) researchers have not discussed it in the context of QS technologies. Therefore, in thi article, we aim to answer the following research question: to what extent do QS technologies enable or restrict indi viduals' empowerment and how does this manifest in their life?

To answer this research question and understand better the latent paradoxical effects of QS technologies, we adopt a critical approach by mobilising the panopticon metaphor from Foucault's (1977) original writings. This meta phor has been used to examine electronic surveillance introduced by devices, such as cameras and smartphones, and modern technologies (Jensen & Draffan, 2004; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014; Lyon, 1993). The panopticon describes a prison architecture (Bentham (1791); Foucault, 1977), where the cells are distributed in such a way that the supervisor can see all the inmates without being seen. This arrangement creates an economy of surveillance b the ability to automate power, making it visible to anyone, while eliminating the need for an observer. Its application results in the inmates' self-monitoring. Therefore, the panopticon bears ambivalent effects; it enables empowerment by providing new knowledge and more control but also leads to disempowerment by creating subjection and confinement. The metaphor has shown its usefulness in the understanding of new forms of intertwined surveillance (Mann, Nolan, & Wellman, 2002; Mathiesen, 1997; Mitrou, Kandias, Stavrou, & Gritzalis, 2014). It has also been employed in QS research on docile bodies and the biopolitics of bodies (Depper & Howe, 2017; Jones, Marshall, & Denison, 2016).

Following up on this stream research, we implemented a qualitative methodology that combines several types of data collection techniques, including primary data from 12 semi-structured interviews, secondary data from the anal ysis of 14,142 messages from online forums and finally, 30 blogs. Our findings indicate that self-quantification ha ambivalent, conflicting effects spanning the constitutive dimensions of the panopticon metaphor that are power, knowledge, body and space. Our research also shows the emergence of micro-surveillance of oneself, which we name the heautopticon (heautos meaning self, opticon meaning vision). This concept describes self-surveillance that both empowers and disempowers individuals, involving the 24 effects observed in our study

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 | QS technologies

Dating back to the 16th century, the first experiments to quantify the human body were performed by a physician Santorio Santorio, who invented the thermoscope, the ancestor of the thermometer. Several centuries later, thank to miniaturisation and advances in computing, the quantification of the human body has been made available on th mass market with QS technologies, such as bracelets, smartwatches and sensors. Emerging in 2007, the QS move ment is described as a “collaboration of users and toolmakers who share an interest in self-knowledge through self tracking” (Wolf, 2007).

These wearable technologies open up opportunities for people empowerment. In the medical realm, such tech nologies help patients manage their chronic diseases (Riggare, Duncan, Hvitfeldt, & Hagglund, 2019), improve their lives and thus contribute to a reduction in diabetes-related morbidity and to a healthier lifestyle (Jang & Kim, 2019; Martin et al., 2006), and even to the definition of an augmented human (Lupton, 2013; Lupton & Maslen, 2018; Swan, 2013). Wearable technologies also promote communication with and daily monitoring of the public (Chib 2013; Kratzke & Cox, 2012). When used in companies, they can reduce work accidents and support the training of less experienced workers (Lavallière, Burstein, Arezes, & Coughlin, 2016). QS practices are in line with the Europea Commission's recommendations that encourage citizens to take charge of their health (Vesnic-Alujevic, Breitegger, & Guimar\~aes Pereira, 2018, p. 15; Wolbring & Lashewicz, 2014). Pettinico and Milne (2017) suggest that by increasing self-knowledge, QS technologies could contribute to self-empowerment. Using QS technologies by incorporating sensors simplifies data collection and creates detailed records of physical activity (Hoy, 2016), with data visualisatio and dashboards. QS also represents a way to increase self-awareness by providing information on one's life (Schüll 2016; Wolf, 2010).

Several researchers have also been able to reveal the empowering affordances of QS technologies. For instance Zhang and Lowry (2016) rely on a motivational affordance perspective to understand how QS running technologies help users maintain their physical activity. These authors reveal several affordances related to learning, task accom plishment and social comparisons. Rockmann and Gewald (2017) identify different affordances embedded in popula activity trackers, namely self-monitoring, achievement, exercise guidance, social recognition, social comparison and self-presentation. All these affordances tend to empower users by giving them control over the determinants of thei quality of life (Tengland, 2016).

Despite considerable enthusiasm for QS practices, the QS literature also highlights these technologies' limita tions, not only social normalisation of habits and bodies (Ruckenstein & Schüll, 2017), but also safety, reliability and security issues in the healthcare domain (Thierer & Castillo, 2015). Because wearables assist individuals in daily life they are worn continuously and lead to disempowerment through ubiquitous monitoring of habits and activitie (Lupton, 2015; Lyon, 1994). The digitisation of the body or “datafication” (Ruckenstein & Schüll, 2017) represents a type of surveillance of individuals through data collection and analysis (Gandy, 1989; Van Dijck, 2014).

For example, Jones et al. (2016) show how tracking professional football players with GPS techniques—initiall used to vary the training intensity and thus reduce the risk of injury—represents disciplinary technologies that lead to disempowerment, making athletes' bodies docile. Athletes who wear jackets with GPS devices feel constantl monitored even if the data are not systematically collected and used by the coach (Jones et al., 2016). Rather than serving as risk-prevention tools, GPS technologies are used by sport coaches to measure athletes' performance and sanction the laziest (Jones et al., 2016). Combined with several self-tracking technologies, GPS technologies can reduce athletes to their performance data and can be used to rectify behaviours to achieve the required performanc (Jones et al., 2016).

Ouellet et al. (2015) apply the Frankfurt School's administered world theory and Foucault's concept of governmentality to discuss the conflict between the emancipatory potential of QS and governmental institutions desire to control people. The administered world theory offers a general critique of capitalist societies that view governments as coercive instruments of production and consumption behaviour. Conversely, the governmentalit concept captures governmental policies' intrusiveness into social life. Ouellet et al. (2015) argue that QS technologie increase governments' power by allowing surveillance and data collection.

Consequently, several authors argue that the QS movement adds a new stone to the edifice of the panopticon surveillance society (Depper & Howe, 2017; Hermsen, Moons, Kerkhof, Wiekens, & De Groot, 2017). To demon strate the panopticon metaphor's relevance to understanding QS practices, we present the premises of this meta phor in the next section.

## 2.2 | Presentation of the panopticon metaphor

Bentham (1791), a British philosopher (the precursor of liberalism and the father of utilitarianism), created th panopticon concept to describe the architecture of a circular prison where cells are distributed in the circumference making any communication between prisoners impossible.

In 1975, Foucault (a French philosopher, known for his criticism of social institutions) updated the panopticon concept in his analysis of modern disciplinary societies. His works highlight the domination techniques and the rol of the “technology of the self” in modern societies (Foucault, Martin, Gutman, & Hutton, 1988).

These domination techniques are intertwined and can take different forms, including self-domination, domina tion over others and finally, others' domination over someone. This last form of domination, called governmentalit by Foucault (1991), represents a “subtle integration of coercion-technologies and self-technologies” (Foucault & Blasius, 1993, p. 204). Foucault developed the panopticon metaphor as a prelude to the notion of governmentalit and a way to “thematise the relationship between truth, knowledge, values and the social institutions and practice in which they emerge” (Mingers & Willcocks, 2004, p. 247). Foucault (1977) considered the panopticon a way of defining power relations in terms of people's everyday lives. The panopticon is also regarded as a laboratory of experimentation and knowledge building. This architectural structure makes it easier to observe people and collec and analyse data, thus offering a new statistical order and a quantification of individuals

The panopticon's main function is to monitor people. Its strength undoubtedly lies in the subjects' uncertaint regarding whether or not they are being watched (Foucault, 1977). The architectural system is present, but th supervisor is not constantly present. There is power saving while maintaining some effect on the individual who feel permanently under surveillance. It is this effect that is most often mentioned in the academic literature. “He who i subjected to a field of visibility, and who knows it, assumes responsibility for the constraints of power; he makes them play spontaneously upon himself” (Foucault, 1977, p. 202). For Foucault, subjugation in a fictitious relationship is created since the individual does not even see his/her guardian. Nevertheless, the person will adopt the behaviou desired by the supervisor.

Beyond its optical vision, by pointing out behaviours, the panopticon leads to seeing what might have seeme invisible. Due to this newly acquired information, the panopticon produces knowledge. Actually, thanks to its mecha nisms of observation, the panopticon identifies, records, differentiates and compares human behaviours, thus creat ing new knowledge for the observer. This is undoubtedly why Foucault considers the panopticon a metaphor o modern society since it brings together the dimensions of power, knowledge and discipline of the bodies. Thanks to the panopticon, power is in charge of knowledge, it decides whom to observe, when to observe and for what purpose; by this means, it controls and adjusts behaviours. Despite its apparent dictatorial function, this system is viewed as democratic by Foucault. The guard's identity remains anonymous because he/she is never visible, inducing the possibility of having surveillance distributed among the population. Anyone can find oneself in the supervisor's place; individuals in their cells can regularly find themselves having to watch their comrades from the central tower The panopticon is therefore a powerful tool for control, monitoring and knowledge creation.

Individual surveillance through the panopticon is transforming the way that society is governed. The crowd is no longer an indivisible set but a collection of individualities separated, arranged and ordered in contiguous cells. It i then possible to differentiate, count and note each person's abilities and performance, as well as observe the symp toms of sick individuals. Once measured, observations aggregated into a large body of data that feed algorithms wil be used to derive the actions to be taken on individuals; “action is individual but power is diffuse” (Foucault, 1977 p. 203). The aim is therefore not to confine but to spread discipline and make it function in the whole social body This means extending the disciplinary institution so far that its boundaries would no longer be seen, the entire soci ety being either encompassed in or creating its own panopticon; the cell is enlarged, but surveillance is maintained.

## 2.3 | Technological panopticon

Zuboff (1988) introduced the panopticon metaphor in the technological field by revealing how technologies in the workplace contributed to developing an information panopticon. Technologies offer features and capacities that allow real-time and continuous surveillance through automatic data collection and storage (Zuboff, 1988). Her research was one of the first to emphasise the role plaved by IS in emplovee surveillance. Since then. IS researchers have used the panopticon metaphor to “highlight the role played by new technologies in strengthening surveillance in contemporary society” (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, 2010, p. 59). Several studies have been conducted on enterprise systems, such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) or customer relationship management (CRM) systems, which confirm the prevalence of the information panopticon (Alferoff & Knights, 2008; Elmes, Strong, & Volkoff, 2005; Gosain, 2004; Sia, Tang, Soh, & Boh, 2002; Zwick & Dholakia, 2004). These systems offer central databases that make not only information but also individuals more visible, creating an equivocal effect on power relationships. Fo instance, Sia et al. (2002) examine how ERP systems perpetuate management power. They show that ERPs facilitat both control and empowerment of workers through self-discipline and normalisation. They also categorise the panoptic power of ERP systems into three dimensions: comprehensive system tracking capability, enhanced visibility to management, and enhanced visibility to peers (p. 25). The panopticon can have counterproductive effects in th workplace; for example, when the users perceive the control exerted by the ERP, they tend to use the system super ficially because they feel distrusted by their organisation (Sia et al., 2002). Overall, IS research that uses the panopticon metaphor points out the effects of visibility and invisibility on power relationships and the need for th person being controlled to exercise self-discipline or self-control (Alferoff & Knights, 2008; Sia & Neo, 2008)

Research also depicts a positive view of electronic surveillance since information technology contributes to creating new skills (Sewell, 1998). The technological panopticon is rather well received by managers because it allow remote and invisible control (Vieira da Cunha, Carugati, & Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, 2015), thus avoiding the unpleas antness and the tension caused by face-to-face control and physical confrontation with employees (Zuboff, 1988) Computer surveillance tends to guarantee the maintenance of efficiency (Gandy, 1989) and the consolidation of exis ting control techniques in companies. The panopticon also reinforces the power of the manager who can then exer cise nominal vertical surveillance (Sewell, 1998). Other studies show that the technological panopticon contributes to a stronger delegation of responsibilities and employee empowerment (Elmes et al., 2005; Sia et al., 2002).

The technological panopticon has also been addressed by sociologists who have used the metaphor to analys the pervasiveness of surveillance in modern societies (Lyon, 1993, 2001). Several recent studies have examined th emergence of new forms of surveillance with new technologies. According to Manokha (2018), it is important to study new ubiquitous technologies under the prism of the panopticon to understand the new power relationship that individuals maintain with electronic device. Manning and Stern (2018) show how interactive online technologies introduce surveillance in intimate human relationships and how they affect sexual bodies, a phenomenon captured with a novel construct called interpersonal panopticism. Another sociological study using an ethnographic approach in secondary schools reveals a technological panopticon based on closed-circuit TV cameras, mobile phones and e learning platforms (Nemorin, 2017).

While the panopticon still seems to be adequate for understanding modern technologies, some research criti cises this metaphor (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014; Zuboff, 2015). For instance, Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al. (2014) indicate that the panopticon (disciplinary power, visibility and self-control) occurs for mobile technologies, but they also highlight a new form of surveillance called “free control” that involves (a) the shift in the location of author ity, (b) the emergence of a time-related system, (c) trust-based control and (d) unawareness of the control bein exercised. While the panopticon offers centralised power, Zuboff (2015) points out the emergence of surveillanc capitalism, where power is currently distributed. Thus, Zuboff (2015) no longer focuses on the workplace but scruti nises surveillance at the intersection between institutions and private spheres. She discusses the accumulation of data permitted by Big Data (especially the Google search engine), which she calls “hyperscale assemblages,” and shows how it contributes to pervasive surveillance. “Information technology alone has the capacity to automate and to informate” (Zuboff, 2015, p. 76).

Carton, Brezillon, and Feller (2016) also note the emergence of a digitised self. They argue that digital transparency of resources contributes to empowerment because visibility represents a prerequisite for decision-making

Appendix A shows some of the main IS articles, published in leading IS journals, which mention the panopticon. Thi literature review shows that surveillance technology, whether used for self-quantification or in the workplace, are redistributing the boundaries of power. Actually, “disciplinary technologies such as comparative surveillance ISs are not exclusively constraining” (Doolin, 2004, p. 346). Technologies create self-empowerment but can also lead to disempow erment with partial delegation of power to the machine. Counting the occurrences of the term “panopticon” in each arti cle reveals that only five papers really rely on the panopticon metaphor. Thus, our literature review confirms a low exploitation of this concept. Furthermore, we conclude that the panopticon metaphor has been simplified, which is gen erally reduced to the permanent gaze and the visibility (or invisibility) of the mechanism of control

The panopticon “brings together knowledge, power, the control of the body, and the control of space into an integrated technology of discipline” (Dreyfus, Rabinow, & Foucault, 1983, p.189). These four dimensions reflect the panopticon's conceptual complexity, which, to our knowledge, has not been fully addressed in IS research. Most researchers have simply examined one aspect of the panopticon as a form of surveillance that “[…] induces in the prisoner a conscious and permanent state of visibility that ensures the automatic functioning of power” (Foucault 1977, p. 202).

## 3 | METHODOLOGY

In this section, we justify our methodological choices, namely our epistemology and the types of collected data, th coding of the panopticon metaphor and then explain our data analysis

## 3.1 | Epistemological stance

This research has a critical underpinning for three reasons: (a) it relies on the writings of Foucault, who is considere one of the main critical theorists; (b) it deals with the “substantive social issue” of power; and (c) it aims at “challeng ing prevailing assumptions” regarding QS technologies (Myers & Klein, 2011, p. 17). Our literature review on technological surveillance reveals that while people have the power to decide whether or not they will equip them selves with technologies, once they adopt such technologies, they seem entangled in a network of surveillance and domination techniques that involve companies, other users and governments. As stated by Myers and Klein (2011, p. 22), “Foucault suggests that contemporary society is penetrated through and through with various disciplinary mechanisms which regulate the behaviour of individuals, and these operate largely through self-surveillanc (i.e., where people, often unconsciously, monitor their own behaviour).” These disciplinary mechanisms have only been slightly examined by IS researchers, even less in the context of QS technologies. Thus, this research gap ha invited us to investigate user behaviours with QS technologies by examining both downsides and opportunities offered by the technologies, as recommended by Myers and Klein (2011). In Appendix B, we present how ou research fits with the six principles for conducting critical research in IS suggested by Myers and Klein (2011).

## 3.2 | Data collection

Our research relies on triangulation of primary and secondary data to gain a richer understanding of QS prac tices. For the primary data, we conducted semi-structured interviews until theoretical saturation (Saunder et al., 2018) was reached, “when the complete range of constructs that make up the theory is fully represente by the data” (Starks & Brown Trinidad, 2007 p. 1375). The secondary data comprised the analysis of the mes sages extracted from online forums and blogs. The data were collected over a 12-month period from January to December 2017.

We chose the participants who voluntarily already used a QS technology. They desired to control their own bod ies and improve their health (Lupton, 2015) by using different types of devices, ranging from a simple step counte to more advanced devices, such as the Apple Watch.

## 3.2.1 | Interviews

The interview guide comprised 20 questions, divided into four principal themes, as follows:

1. the decision to adopt a QS technology;

2. the usages of the QS tool and their consequences;

3. privacy and quantification issues;

4. the relationship between lifestyle and quantification.

Our interview guide was tested in a pilot interview phase with five users of QS technologies. Based on the eval uation of this pilot test, revisions were made to refine the questions. We added questions on the adoption of QS to contextualise users' practices (motivations, factors of adoption and roles played by relatives). The questions on usage were supposed to clarify users' practices. For instance, adoption for fun versus adoption for performance achieve ment or health monitoring could generate different practices. The questions on privacy aimed at understanding if users were taking QS technologies at face value or if they also had concerns regarding these devices. The last set of questions was intended to reveal the effects of quantification on people's lifestyles and the potential changes o transformations induced by the technologies.

The subjects were chosen by recruiting people in our locality who wore fitness trackers. We combined thi approach with a snowball strategy, which entailed identifying new cases through our respondents' acquaintance (Biernacki & Waldorf, 1981). The 12 semi-directed interviews, each lasting between 30 and 80 min, were al audiotaped and transcribed. The respondents (six women and six men) were between 25 and 65 years old (se

Table 1 for details). All the participants were French. Therefore, all the interview excerpts, presented verbatims in this article, were translated into English.

## 3.2.2 | Forums and blogs

We examined the discussion forums of Fitbit (Appendix C), the only manufacturer offering a discussion platform on QS products. The use of web messages as secondary data sources has been popularised by the netnographic approach in the marketing field, as described by Hine (2000) and Kozinets (2002). It has the advantages of bein faster, simpler and less costly than traditional research methods (Kozinets, 2002), and these secondary data have proven effective in understanding consumer experiences (Finch, 1999). The Fitbit forums are structured around th company's different brands and products. There are also some forums for customers willing to share their experi ences. Using a forum extraction tool, we retrieved all the discussions from Fitbit's Francophone forums since thei creation in 2013 until August 2017. This dataset represented a total of 14,142 messages (Table 2)

Our goal was not to study online communities and their functioning but to focus on the online messages in order to reveal QS practices, usages and perceptions. We opted for an approach similar to that of Finch (1999) or of Romano, Donovan, Chen, and Nunamaker (2003), who all proposed an analytical treatment of the messages b reduction and classification. The reduction stage involves selecting the pertinent messages and disregarding the threads related to technical questions asked by users who encounter problems with their Fitbit tools. Next, w sorted the messages according to their relevance to our research question. The remaining messages were then code with the four themes of the panopticon metaphor. This second dataset allowed us to confirm our coding of the inter views and to identify some codes that had not appeared in the interview transcripts.

To select the blogs, we conducted a Google search using well-known tracker brands associated with the key word “experience” (“Fitbit OR Withings OR Jawbon OR Xiaomi OR Tom-Tom OR Polar” and “experience OR test”) In total, 30 blogs were retained for examination, as presented in Appendix C. Some of these blogs presented Q products and new releases; others displayed technology demonstrations and tests.

## 3.3 | Coding the panopticon metaphor from Foucault's writing

A metaphor allows thinking about a problem with an analogy, understanding a situation through another (Lakoff & Johnson, 2008) or understanding an element of one's experience from that of another (Morgan, Gregory, & Roach, 1997). A metaphor is a way to explain a phenomenon by shaping reality to structure people's thoughts (Kendall & Kendall, 1993). It is an analogy mechanism that makes someone compare something unknown (the destination) with a well-known system (the source) to understand the former more quickly (Oates & Fitzgerald, 2007). Although analogy is often used in IS to describe technologies (e.g., the metaphors of cloud computing and Big Data), analogy reasoning leading to a theory remains rare (Hassan, 2014; Hekkala, Stein, & Rossi, 2018).

We chose to code Foucault's panopticon metaphor (1977, pp. 201-211), as did Burton-Jones (2014) with Zuboff's (1988). Dreyfus et al. (1983) provided the four categories a priori. However, we had to break down examine, compare and conceptualise the description of the panopticon metaphor presented in Foucault's Disci pline and Punish (1977, pp. 201-211). We chose the sentence as our unit of analysis to perform the coding; each sentence that was related to the panopticon was added to one of the existing categories as an occurrence. W used open coding to label the different phenomena under study. The open-coding process produced the 20 labels that we reviewed and then attributed to a more abstract category (power, knowledge, space and body).

Following Miles and Huberman (1994), we performed filling in, which involved adding codes and reconstructing a coherent scheme as new insights and new ways of examining the data set emerged (p. 62). A such, we also used coding to open up the text. Consequently, we found new codes (presented further in Section 4.5) that were not described by Foucault and are probably more specific to the QS context, namely falli bility of the machine, reassurance, assemblage and feeling of guilt. However, some sentences from the excerpts could not be classified under the four categories. These codes refer to the architecture of the panoptic prison, general information describing the panopticon's mechanisms without specifying a dimension, and other orphan codes that we are unable to categorise. In the following subsections, we explain each of the 20 codes more precisely (see Appendix D for a detailed description).

The panopticon relies on the prisoners' subjection to the guards (DPOW1). However, the subjection is “bor mechanically from a fictitious relation” (Foucault, 1977, p. 202) because the guard is not necessarily present, whic makes the power exerted on prisoners both discontinuous (DPOW2) and automatic (DPOW3). This “induces in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power” (Foucault, 1977, p. 201). Based on a central tower's visibility (DPOW4), the panopticon's architectural system reminds the prisoner that they are constantly monitored. Since it is impossible for the prisoners to verify the potential supervisor's pres ence, they learn to discipline and control themselves (EPOW1). As such, the panopticon acts as a powerful motivational organ for the mind (EPOW2). This mechanism disembodies power and turns it completely anonymous (DPOW5). It also creates dissymmetry between the guards and the prisoners (DPOW6). Indeed, the guards are the only ones knowing the reality of the surveillance; by this means, they can control behaviours.

## 3.3.2 | Theme #2: Body

Body is the second most recurring theme in Foucault's (1977) writings, with 22% of the occurrences. Five codes (EBOD1-EBOD4, DBOD1) are classified under this category. The aim of the panopticon is “to alter behaviour, to train or correct individuals” (p. 203) and as such, to create docile bodies that act according to the rules (EBOD1). Another objectiv is to observe the changes and thus act on the bodies before the “offences, mistakes or crimes have been committed” (Foucault, 1977, p. 206) (EBOD2). Foucault (1977, p. 208) acknowledges that bodies are “irregular bodies, with their details their multiple movements, their heterogeneous forces, their spatial relations”, thus requiring specific attention and treatment (EBOD3). Consequently, experimentation is required to collect data on bodies, assess the effects of the surveillance and adjust the treatment (EBOD4). In fact, the panopticon favours the objectification of observation, meaning that individuals are no longer considered thinking subjects but statistical objects (DBOD1) to facilitate experimentation.

## 3.3.3 | Theme #3: Knowledge

According to Foucault, the panopticon also represents a source of knowledge and a way to transform how society is governed. Five codes (EKNOW1-EKNOW4, DKNOW1) belong to this category. The crowd is no longer an indivisi ble whole but a collection of separate individualities, arranged and ordered in contiguous cells. It is then possible to record, differentiate and compare each individual's skills and performance and observe the symptoms of sick individ uals (EKNOW1). The ordered individuals are immediately recognisable (DKNOW1). Actually, the panopticon leads to seeing what might have seemed invisible and in that way, grants the guards increased knowledge on their prisoners (EKNOW2). The architecture allows light to pass through each cell, chasing away the protecting darkness and offer ing visibility. This new knowledge encompasses new objects on which power can be exercised (EKNOW3). The panopticon can be compared to a school or a social laboratory, where human behaviour is observed almost in vitro, leading to the production of knowledge and the teaching of new techniques (EKNOW4).

## 3.3.4 | Theme #4: Space

Space represents a minority theme in the book chapters dealing with the panopticon (14% of the occurrences). Two codes (ESPAC1 and DSPAC1) belong to this category. The panopticon confines individuals in their cells, conferring a safe space that protects an individual from others (DSPAC1). Despite this isolation, the idea is not to lock up individ uals but to spread the feeling of discipline and make them work in the whole social body (ESPAC1). Specifically, th concept involves extending the disciplinary institution so far that its boundaries cannot be seen, the entire societ being either encompassed or is itself the panoptic; the cell is expanded, while surveillance is maintained.

In the next section, we introduce how these four themes help in understanding user practices with QS technolo gies. We also debate on the extent to which QS technologies lead to empowerment or disempowerment.

## 4.1 | Theme #1: QS, a technology of power

As the most recurrent theme in our data, power is materialised by the virtual coach (the software installed in the QS tool). The virtual coach's power tends to reduce people's control over the determinants of their quality of life b using several means of control (DPOW1-6; see Table 4).

As soon as users adopt a QS technology, their voluntary and chosen subjugation to the tool occurs (DPOW1) Some users actually refer to the terms “force” and “demand” when describing the relationship established with their virtual coach. Interestingly, with the QS, this real subjugation arises from a fictitious relationship between the tech nology and the human. In a conversation between two users who compare what their QS tool imposes on them, th following reply illustrates how the tool is perceived as a person with real power:

“Your Surge is severe! 42 km in less than seven hours. Fortunately, my Alta is more indulgent; lately, he's been demanding just 250 steps an hour. I'm waiting to see if he gets meaner after” (Fitbit forum)

In one of the examined blogs, the word “cop” is even used to describe the power of the QS technology. Subjuga tion also occurs because QS users agree to achieve the tool's recommended objectives and listen to their virtual coach. For example, one user says, “For now, I've let the tool impose things on me, but I think I can define my goals, too” (User #4).

Even if the virtual coach does not need to intervene constantly, it maintains the subjugation relationship b punctually warning users of the actions to be undertaken (DPOW2). When receiving those notifications, users gener ally execute the recommended actions.

This power is made automatic (DPOW3) due to the machine's constant control over users and the latter's lack of knowledge about how the control is practised (DPOW4). Indeed, users neither know how and when their data ar collected nor which algorithms are used for data analysis.

Besides, users do not know who is really behind the coach. The power therefore remains unverifiable and anon ymous (DPOW5). Actually, users rarely know exactly how their data will eventually be managed because privacy pol icies can be too long to read and complex to understand. Norway's Consumer Council (2016) examined four fitnes wristband companies, including Fitbit, and filed a formal complaint about their privacy policies that all have long terms and conditions. This issue also raises dissymmetry (DPOW6), with an imbalance between the virtual coach an the users (DPOW6). In fact, generally, not all the data are at the users' disposal (the data are often transmitted in an aggregate form with simple graphs), while the electronic coach or the company stores the whole information in its database. Users have been satisfied with the analysis offered by the machine and lack access to raw data for thei own exploitation.

While QS technologies tend to lead to user disempowerment, in some instances, they contribute to user empowerment. Despite the relationship of consented subjection, users remain in control of their actions (EPOW1 by deciding whether or not to move. ## 4.2 | Theme #2: QS, its influence on body

The influence of QS technologies on bodies can be empowering when these devices personalise recommendations (EBOD3), safeguard users from dangers (EBOD2), encourage healthy behaviours (EBOD1) or favour experimentation (EBOD4) (Table 5). Changing user behaviour by encouraging movement is one of the primary goals of a selfquantification tool (EBOD1). Some users spontaneously modify their behaviours by getting off a bus one stop before their final destination or lengthening their daily walk with the dog.

“To reach it, all means are good: at work, I use the toilets farthest away from my office; I get some water from the farthest fountain” (Fitbit forum).

The QS sounds like an injunction to take control of one's health, a kind of re-appropriation of one's body. This means relearning to move, run, eat and sleep in healthy ways and re-establishing good habits. In this sense, it empowers individuals.

QS technologies are also versatile enough to be in charge of a large number of different profiles, ranging from athletes to patients (EBOD3). QS technology providers multiply the indicators to offer users a large selection of tracking options and personalisation features.

These indicators also serve as preventive mechanisms (EBOD2), such as when they warn a runner who is about to exceed his/her maximum heart rate. The technology prevents risks by constantly monitoring and identifyin abnormal measures. Notifications can then be sent to users to help them return to their normal condition

Behaviour modification can sometimes involve an “experimental” phase (EBOD4). For instance, some users experiment with different sleeping hours and check if these changes will improve their health. Many user testimo nials on the Fitbit forums reveal that users will not go to bed until they have reached their walking goal. We recorded a user who had been counselled to sleep more in order to limit snacking; thus, she experimented on the correlatio between sleep and her eating patterns.

However, QS technologies can also have a disempowering effect on bodies. Indeed, one consequence of the permanent measurement is that users describe themselves only in terms of numbers. They are no longer the subjects of the conversation but become “objects” (Foucault, 1977, p. 202). Caught up in a spiral of quantification, user adopt an objective vision of their bodies (DBOD1):

“For my first week of ‘diet’, I burned 2300 calories per day on average over 7 days, and I consumed on average over six days, 1917 cal/day, so about 400 calories less per day instead of 500” (Fitbit forum)

## 4.4 | Theme #4: QS technologies and space

QS technologies empower users by allowing them to perform their activities freely wherever and with whoever they want (ESPAC1), but they can also lead to disempowerment when they isolate users from others (DSPAC1) (se Table 7).

On the one hand, QS technologies provide users with the mobility to practise freely wherever they want indoors and outdoors (ESPAC1). For example, medical monitoring to measure sleep quality that traditionally occurs in hospitals is now possible from home, with a tracking tool. Some users even bring the reports of their tracking data to their physicians, as illustrated by a Fitbit user: “My doctor loves these clear and concise report printouts” (Fitbit forum). As such, QS technologies can reduce the need to consult a physician to obtain classic physiological informa tion because the tool constantly collects these data. Alternatively, the tracker can suggest planning a medical visit, a explained by a user: “The cardiometric functionality is the one I use the most as it is impressively accurate and very usefu to monitor my vitals. It indicates to me if I need to make an appointment with the doctor” (Fitbit forum). QS brands hav also opened up space by providing online communities to their customers, as illustrated by the thread “Looking fo Fitbit friends” on Fitbit forums. These online communities can provide emotional support and motivation to lonel users. Exchanging ideas and experiences with people worldwide also broadens individuals' horizons and offers new perspectives on meeting people.

On the other hand, since the QS practice is individual, the risk of isolation also exists (DSPAC1). Some users think that QS tools isolate them from others. This phenomenon is illustrated by a forum dedicated to Fitbit Chal lenge, where many users look for online members who could join or partner with them in the challenge because no one around them owns a Fitbit watch to participate in the same challenge. An interviewee also notices the impossi bility of comparing his performance with those of his friends who do not own the same technological device. Th diversity of QS devices and brands, with their distinctive features and closed platforms, does not favour openness.

## 4.5 | Theme #5: QS as a new technological surveillance (the new codes)

The four Foucauldian concepts elucidate a great part of the user experience with QS and confirm their adequacy to provide an understanding of the human relationship with modern technologies. The 20 codes applied to the QS phe nomena reveal the power relationship between individuals and the technology, how QS can be a tool of knowledg and illustrate user behavioural reactions (concerning body and space). These findings also highlight empowerment and disempowerment tensions under each of the four themes

Nonetheless, the 20 codes do not explain all user experiences with QS technologies. QS technologies rely on a virtual coach and voluntary adoption by users, while the panopticon metaphor describes a guard-prisoner relationship with a strong subjugation. Furthermore, QS technologies are worn on people's bodies, creating persona panopticons not described by Foucault (1977). “In a nutshell, just as snails carry their homes, so the employees of the brave new liquid modern world must grow and carry their personal panopticons on their own bodies” (Bauman & Lyon, 2013, p. 59). A few studies have already amended the application of the panopticon metaphor to understand technological surveillance (Carton et al., 2016; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014; Zuboff, 2015). Therefore, w expected to observe some new manifestations of technological surveillance emerging from our findings. In fact, ou analysis of our qualitative data points out other themes that do not belong to one of the 20 codes identified earlier and that complement the traditional panoptic vision. First, QS tools offer permanent surveillance that makes users feel protected and in good hands (ENEW1). Som users tell about how QS technologies reassure them. For instance, in one of the Fitbit forums, a woman thought that she was not drinking enough and was worrying about its potential consequences. After her intake had been measured for several weeks, she was reassured about her hydration level. A blogger also explains that after 9 months of using his Fitbit, the measurements confirmed that he was sleeping poorly. This outcome relieved him as he was finally able to understand why he was feeling so tired during the day. As such, QS technologies bring reassurance b providing new information relevant to user conditions and in real time. The users explain that before adopting QS technologies, they had no precise idea how poorly or how well they were maintaining their physical conditions and performing sports activities. Everything was based on their intuition, and this uncertainty was related to higher anxi ety. Currently, by delegating to QS technologies, the power to set personalised objectives, check achievements an determine what is beneficial for them, users can reduce their cognitive load, anxiety or stress regarding their health condition. Compared with the panopticon metaphor, characterised by the dissymmetry of power (knowledge is solel held by the guard), the surveillance operated by QS promotes a mutual relationship between the “guard” and th “prisoner.” Actually, the knowledge transfer from virtual coaches to users contributes to reducing the asymmetry of information.

We also observe a new form of surveillance, especially when users assemble different technologies to monitor their data (ENEW2). The assemblage of several QS technologies can be explained by the fact that these tools, whil exerting power, are incapable of holistic control. Trackers can have more or less advanced technologies, ranging from wearables, such as smartwatches, to medical health devices (Barrett et al., 2013; Ma, Zhang, Dung, Li, & Zhang 2015) or implantable sensors (Peppet, 2014; Trickler, 2013). Therefore the use of multiple wearable technologies creates detailed records of different physical activity or body parameters. The whole system is a deep source of motivation and empowerment for users because it brings advanced knowledge. Some interviewees mention coupling their fitness tracker with a weight scale to build a solid system of permanent monitoring and improve their health management.

The codes that emerge from our findings also include disempowering dimensions. While QS technologies hold knowledge, they are also fallible and occasionally make mistakes or errors (DNEW1). QS technologies sometimes provide unreliable data since the context of use is often neglected or insufficiently exploited (Karapanos et al., 2016). In most cases, the coach's advice is accompanied by scientific evidence and recommendations from reputable organi sations. For instance, the World Health Organization recommends walking 10,000 steps a day, which is now a standard in most trackers/wearables (Tudor-Locke et al., 2011). but it can be inappropriate for some individuals based on their health conditions. For instance, wearables make no difference between a pregnant woman and an overweight person (Bietz, Hayes, Morris, Patterson, & Stark, 2016). Therefore, users try to find palliative solutions online; one user seeks a way to adapt the tool to her pregnancy need, and another person in a wheelchair wants to convert steps into arm movements. One participant also tells us that his watch encouraged him to move while he was in the the atre. Thus, the digital coach can also make mistakes in its recommendations by not considering contextual data and user environments. QS technologies do not always succeed in taking into account the heterogeneity of bodies. Furthermore, many users complain in online forums about different problems they encounter with their Fitbit tracker, including data inaccuracy, bugs, lack of synchronisation, connection or configuration issues and other technica glitches proliferating online. One section of the Help Fitbit website is dedicated to maintaining data accuracy. These problems tend to reduce users' perceived behaviour control over the technologies.

Feelings of guilt can also be reinforced when users belong to an online community or decide to share their dat with other users. While QS technologies broaden horizons, they also contribute to online visibility, which creates accountability. Users therefore feel obliged to justify their poor results or failure to achieve their objectives. After missing a training session, one informant was worried that other users could comment on that; thus, he tried to mak up for it. Therefore, we observe that users can sometimes submit their individual QS practices to be evaluated by other users. Then, low achievements conflict with the technology recommendations or the groups' demands and generate feelings of guilt

## 5 | DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

To what extent do QS technologies enable individuals' empowerment? Our findings indicate that self-quantification has ambivalent, conflicting effects—13 empowering and 11 disempowering—spanning the dimensions of power, knowledge, body and space.

On the one hand, using QS technologies represents a means of continuous improvement, allowing the achievement of an optimal state of health and self-empowerment by “increasing the potential utility of individuals” (Foucault, 1977 p. 211). Such technologies produce positive effects, such as motivation (power of the mind), experimentation, disease prevention, acquisition of new knowledge and techniques to take care of one's health and body, openness and reassur ance. The use of wearable technologies by incorporating sensors simplifies data collection and creates detailed record of physical activity (Hoy, 2016), with data visualisation and dashboards that enlighten user practices. These tools seem effective in arousing and developing user motivation. Discovering new information about their bodies and being able t experiment with new techniques encourage users to reach or even surpass their goals. QS technologies also open user to online communities, allowing them to share and compare their performances with those of others. Barta and Nef (2016) observe that sharing in a QS context increases social capital and generates bridging and bonding capital. Hence

QS technologies contribute to a beneficial and desired panopticon (Lucherini, 2016). These findings can be related to the fact that the users we interviewed or observed online were part of the free and voluntary use context. Their objec tive was to take care of themselves, what Foucault would call the context of “souci de soi” or “Epimeleia heautou” (1988 p.19). They also had the opportunity to make their technological choices regarding the trackers they adopted corresponding to a form of IT empowerment (Junglas, Goel, Ives, & Harris, 2014, 2019)

On the other hand, QS technologies can be sources of disempowerment. Our findings show that some individ uals do not make self-determined decisions but delegate the power to make decisions and to determine what is ben eficial for them to the technologies. As users do not necessarily have the knowledge or the motivation to manage themselves, they use QS tools to achieve these objectives. They are disempowered by the fact that everything is done automatically (without anyone knowing how the data are collected), the information remains unclear, and the lack control over their data. They are turned into spectators instead of actual actors of their datafication. Furthermore, such objectification and datafication of bodies can lead to the normalisation of bodies (Depper & Howe 2017). Actually, the objectives given by trackers are standard statistics that people apply to reach the norm (Ruckenstein & Schüll, 2017). Our emerging codes confirm this disempowerment by revealing that individuals' self imposed surveillance can lead to guilt feelings. Guilt can be defined as “an interpersonal phenomenon that is func tionally and causally linked to communal relationships between people” (Baumeister, Stillwell, & Heatherton, 1994, p. 243). Therefore, the emergence of guilt in a human-tracker interaction is unexpected. As guilt represents a sign of commitment in a relationship, it indicates that users feel committed to their self-tracking practice and are no completely free from the technological pressure. The technologies exert power through persuasion mechanisms (Danaher, Brendryen, Seeley, Tyler, & Woolley, 2015). Since it is self-surveillance, failing to achieve objectives is first and foremost self-deceiving. In our data, this feeling of guilt is also expressed in relation to other users (i.e., onlin communities and friends) because it is elicited when there are “self-presentational concerns with how one is viewed by others” (Baumeister et al., 1994, p. 250). Therefore, joining a group can disempower users in their QS practice.

Nonetheless, there are also differences between the surveillance system described by Foucault (1977) and the sur veillance induced by QS technologies. Besides, IS research has already revisited Foucault's panopticon and uncovered dif ferent forms of surveillance, such as Mathiesen's (1997) discussion of the emergence of synopticon surveillance, in which the population sees and admires the few, especially through mass media. Ganascia (2010) developed the catopticon, type of sub-surveillance based on decentralised and democratic power exercised not from above but from below. Finally, Mitrou et al., 2014 discuss the participatory surveillance called omniopticon, in which the many watch the many, thank to social media. These studies outline the need to investigate other facets of surveillance with the emergence of nove technologies. Since QS technologies have only been slightly examined in the surveillance prism of IS research, we describ hereafter four key differences between QS surveillance and alternative forms of surveillance.

First, QS surveillance does not solely establish power relationships but also serves positive purposes, such as health management and entertainment (Haggerty, 2006), due to the acquisition of new knowledge, comparison or prevention, for instance. According to Lyon (2003), the new surveillance facilitated by technologies “appears much less sinister” than the panopticon because it allows “new levels of efficiency, productivity, convenience, and comfort that many in the technologically advanced societies take for granted” (p. 18). Alternative views on the panopticon also acknowledge the entertaining side of surveillance that occurs, for instance, when people watch others on televi sion (i.e., synopticon, Mathiesen, 1997) or contribute to participatory surveillance with social medi (i.e., omniopticon, Mitrou et al. (2014)). However, QS surveillance does not only convey entertainment but also contributes to greater hedonic purposes, such as recovery of damaged bodies, monitoring of health and well-being

Second, QS surveillance is deliberate even if the QS adoption can be the result of social pressure from the society that focuses on self-care (Foucault et al., 1988). This surveillance is not imposed as in the panoptic prison; users remain in control of their self-surveillance, called private self-tracking (Lupton, 2016). Similarly, the canopticon, the omniopticon and the synopticon describe the technological surveillance that is conducted willingly by individuals. In the QS context, users' free will can be influenced by the notifications of the QS technology. However, our inter viewees explain that notifications and recommendations to move are not always followed; the motivation to act greatly depends on a user's state of mind when receiving the notification. Therefore, it is a complex relationship of subjugation consented to by the user, who has the power to disregard the tool's recommendations

Third, while the panopticon instils subjugation and coercion of bodies, QS surveillance tends to instil mental punishment by inducing a feeling of guilt when individuals do not achieve their goals of the day. This feature is unique to QS surveillance. As Lupton (2016, p. 107) states, “In this context, the personal data generated from self-tracking ar represented as pedagogical and motivational, a means of encouraging self-reflection or emotional responses such a fear, guilt or shame that will then lead to the advocated behaviour changes.” Similarly, our research highlights that QS technologies elicit emotional reactions, oscillating between guilt and reassurance. As such, they affect the three distinct, classic psychological faculties: cognition (thought or intellect), conation (volition or the will) and feeling (sentiment or emotion) (Hilgard, 1980). Interestingly, QS users are both inspired and motivated by modern surveillanc practices, on the one hand, and anxious about this atmosphere of surveillance and risk that characterises moder society, on the other hand (En & Pöll, 2016). Foucault (1977, p. 202) explains this situation as follows: “The mor numerous those anonymous and temporary observers are, the greater the risk for the inmate of being surprised and the greater his anxious awareness of being observed.”

Fourth, QS surveillance not only relies on panoptic mechanisms but also extends them. We observe the emer gence of a surveillance assemblage that some individuals build by accumulating different measurement technologies to quantify weight, blood sugar, blood pressure, mood, back posture and so on. This technology-based assemblag can recreate a person's complete profile through several sources of information. Furthermore, we observe the emergence of lateral surveillance, “not the top-down monitoring of employees by employers, citizens by the state, but rather the peer-to-peer surveillance of spouses, friends, and relatives” (Andrejevic, 2005, p. 481), which is also mani fest with the omniopticon (Mitrou et al. (2014)). Prior research has pointed out the online communities' role in the practice of QS that provides moral support (Barta & Neff, 2016; Stragier, Evens, & Mechant, 2015). Similarly, ou results indicate that friends and online communities can motivate and encourage users, as well as support lateral sur veillance, leading some users to change their trackers' parameters to the private mode in order to prevent othe users from seeing their performances.

Overall, the four aforementioned points could be captured with a new concept that more accurately describe the power relationship between QS technologies and individuals, namely the “heautopticon” (heautos meaning self, opticon meaning vision). More precisely, the heautopticon can be defined as a deliberate system of surveillance worn on the self, which can be extended due to the assemblage of several technological devices, which affect cognition conation and feeling. Through the four dimensions of power, body, knowledge and space, this study details the mani festations of the heautopticon for QS users. The heautopticon empowers users by providing power of the mind reassurance, freedom and knowledge, but it can also have detrimental consequences, such as subjection, identifica tion and feeling of guilt. In conclusion, the body-punishment surveillance described in the panopticon has morphe into an accentuated psychological and emotional surveillance with the heautopticon.

As such, the heautopticon is in line with this IS research stream that reveals different facets of technological surveil lance. However, this concept departs from these other conceptualisations of surveillance as it focuses on consented self surveillance that both empowers and disempowers individuals, involving the 24 effects observed in our study.

## 5.1 | Theoretical contributions

First, we rely on the panopticon metaphor to understand QS practices and new forms of surveillance related to Q technologies. Similar to all symbolic languages, the use of this metaphor has the advantage of clarifying and enriching the understanding of a system by bringing meaning to subjects that are difficult to define from the outset (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2001). Using this metaphor helps researchers comprehend how individuals engage in Q practices and the mechanisms that result from changing their behaviour. Moreover, the metaphor is a conceptua artefact that can be used for theorisations and can lead to knowledge creation (Alter, 2017). While the literature provides some models to explain QS and personal informatics systems (Li, Dey, & Forlizzi, 2010), the studies focus on user actions and do not examine human interactions with QS tools (Almalki, Gray, & Martin-Sanchez, 2016 Almalki, Sanchez, & Gray, 2015; Epstein, Ping, Fogarty, & Munson, 2015). Therefore, our qualitative research that mobilises three sources of data provides a deeper understanding of QS practices

For our second contribution, we offer an in-depth re-analysis of Foucault's writings because the panopticon has often been reduced to a form of surveillance that “[…] induces in the prisoner a conscious and permanent state of visibility that ensures the automatic functioning of power” (1977, p. 202). Indeed, prior IS research has focused solely on the concepts of power and space when addressing the panopticon (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, 2017b). Through our examination of body and knowledge as complementary to power and space, we show that these four core concepts act together to provide an understanding of QS technologies' effects on user empowerment. QS technologies rely on a virtual coach and voluntary adoption by users, while the panopticon metaphor describes a guard-prisoner relationship with a strong subju gation. This latter relationship can be found in the workplace, where the panopticon was first applied to analyse how technologies are used by employers to monitor employees (Sewell, 1998; Zuboff, 1988), but it does not fit with the indi vidual practice of QS. Over the last decade, IS researchers have encouraged broadening the domain's main field of investi gation beyond the organisational context (Vodanovich, Sundaram, & Myers, 2010). For example, Yoo (2010) calls on the IS community to study “the digital experience in daily activities using common artefacts that have built-in computer capa bilities” (p. 213). Self-quantification of everyday practices falls totally within this field of study.

Third, we thus introduce a new concept, the heautopticon, which describes more accurately the surveillance introduced by QS technologies, namely consented self-surveillance, which affects power, body, knowledge and space dimensions and produces both beneficial and detrimental outcomes. Past studies have already highlighted the ambiv alence of technological effects (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, 2017a). As expressed by Dooli (2004), “Disciplinary technologies such as comparative surveillance ISs are not exclusively constraining. Indeed, such systems are “double-edged,” in that they also empower by providing a legitimate space for action” (p. 346). We confirm that self-surveillance also bears ambivalent effects, and our findings offer a unique addition to this debate by highlighting the 24 effects observed in our empirical study. Such a detailed description of technological effects has not been explored in past works.

Finally, Myers and Klein (2011) set as an objective of critical research to improve the application of a social the ory, which we achieved in our critical study. By coding Foucault's (1977) work, we enrich the understanding of th panopticon metaphor, and by developing a new concept, we also show that theory's limitations in explaining moder surveillance with QS technologies. Indeed, QS technologies are worn on people's bodies, creating persona panopticons not described by Foucault (1977). Furthermore, Bauman and Lyon (2013) encourage researchers to study the effects of self-imposed mini-panopticons. Therefore, our study responds to Bauman and Lyon's call and offers a critical understanding of the functioning of those mini-panopticons.

## 5.2 | Recommendations for practitioners and for user emancipation

Myers and Klein (2011) particularly encourage critical researchers to identify possibilities for change by discussing at least one element of transformation (individual emancipation, improvements in society or improvements in social theories). Since improvements in social theories have already been discussed in the prior section, we have developed several recommendations to counteract the disempowering effects of QS technologies and as such, contribute to individual emancipation. Consequently, this research offers levers on which organisations and practitioners can act to either reduce the negative aspects of QS technologies or make their potential more effective in improving socia emancipation.

The anonymous, invisible and dissymmetric power raises mainly security and privacy issues. Actually, QS data tend to be hosted on the cloud (Lupton, 2016), making them vulnerable and reusable by third parties. According to a survey conducted by Evidon on behalf of the Financial Times, health management applications share or trade their data with third parties, and several large insurance companies also partner with health application developers (Dredge, 2013). Therefore, focusing on consumer rights and needs in terms of privacy should be a priority reflected in both the design of QS technologies and the digital services provided to users. The General Data Protection Regu lation (GDPR) that prevails in Europe and the California Consumer Privacy Act of 2018 have both developed regula tions on data ownership, control and security, which guide our practical recommendations.

While our interviewees know the brands of their wearable technologies, they do not know who is behind thos and do not always know how and when their data are measured. Therefore, QS providers should adhere to the prin ciples of privacy by design, which include a set of data-oriented strategies (minimise, separate, abstract and hide per sonal data) and process-oriented strategies (inform, control, enforce and demonstrate personal data processing (Colesky, Hoepman & Hillen, 2016). Besides, the objectives of privacy by design are explained in Article 25 of th GDPR. For instance, providing informed consent that stipulates the intended usage of private data should be enforced as part of the privacy by design framework. In terms of design, providers of QS solutions should develop mechanisms for either allowing or denying data collection to offer users the opportunity to decide whether and when to accept data collection (Perez & Zeadally, 2018). Providers of QS technologies should also set up notifications to inform users in case of data leakage. The two aforementioned privacy frameworks also stipulate consumers right to data portability, so QS manufacturers should develop data formats that can be exported to and imported from different QS devices.

Moreover, we advise companies to clearly indicate their brands' levels of measurement reliability in different contexts of usage. Indeed, no quality standards are required to sell QS technologies, in contrast to the case of medical devices. In view of this problem, the measurements' reliability can vary by up to 20% between brands (Fokkema, Kooiman, Krijnen van der Schans, & De Groot, 2017; Kooiman et al., 2015). As demonstrated in this study, the digital coach can make mis takes when offering recommendations to QS users or provide them with inaccurate data. Schiller, Brogt, Schuler, and Strobel (2018) point out the necessity to develop a holistic quality model to assess smart connected products. This research should encourage regulators to develop international standards and quality labels that will be applied to the eval uation of QS technologies. These standards will help users choose devices of higher quality.

Finally, QS technologies tend to increase security and privacy concerns. This study highlights several key challenge regarding personal privacy that can be violated, considering the quantity of information collected by QS providers an hosted online. Our interviewees reported how they tried to change their privacy settings to make parameters, such as weight or number of steps, invisible to friends or others. Belanger and Crossler (2019) show that users' mobile privacy concerns negatively affect their attitude towards information sharing, which stresses the necessity to take action to improve security. To address security and privacy concerns, Esposito, De Santis, Tortora, Chang, and Choo (2018) encour age the use of the blockchain technology to protect healthcare data hosted on the cloud. The blockchain is “a technolog able to build an open and distributed online database, which consists of a list of data structures (also known as blocks) that are linked with each other (i.e. a block points to the following one, hence the name blockchain)” (Esposito et al., 2018, p. 34). Since changes to the blockchain are visible to all members of the network, users could have higher control over their data. Overall, the blockchain-based approach ensures transparency, consistency and security against cyberattacks (Esposito et al., 2018). The encryption of identifiable user details, such as name, email, age, city of residence and health information, is also crucial in the blockchain. Consequently, providers of QS technologies could embrace distributed led gers' ecosystems to ensure stronger privacy and integrity of the data.

## 5.3 | Limitations and future research directions

This research has some limitations. While we collected a huge amount of data, we only conducted 12 semi structured interviews. We reached semantic saturation when coding the interviews, but we acknowledge that thes participants may not be representative of all QS users. The qualitative data retrieved from the forums tend to sup port the analysis of our interviewees. Nonetheless, we intend to conduct further research with users and non-user of wearable technologies in different countries to gain a more thorough understanding of QS technologies' effect and implications. Furthermore, it could be very interesting to examine other profiles of QS users, such as the elderl and people with medical conditions, who use trackers for managing their health, not only for their well-being (Fox & Connolly, 2018). Health trackers offer them the opportunity to stay at home instead of going to the hospital or mov ing to a nursing home, thus improving their quality of life. Nonetheless, disempowering facets may also exist. Thus we could investigate if the tensions between empowerment and disempowerment in a health context are more or less increased. Additionally, examining the responses of different groups of people would help acknowledge individ ual agency and potential variations, which was neglected by Foucault (Pylypa, 1998)

Another limitation is related to our choice of the panopticon metaphor to understand QS practices. While w provide a systematic coding of Foucault's (1977) work and try to enrich our understanding of this metaphor, other metaphors or theories that focus on power relationships could be applied. For example, Weber's (as cited in Mitchel (1990)) metaphor of persuading/coercing examines power relationships that can either persuade a person to change one's behaviour or be an object of pressure to follow some advice. This theory focuses more on power relationships than on surveillance technologies. However, these concepts of power and technological surveillance are closely related and deserve further attention. Nudging could offer an interesting research avenue for QS systems. Recently Ho and Lim (2018) have examined how mobile personalisation could match recommendations to a consumer's physi ological needs and current location. Their findings indicate that suggestions based on location, context awareness and mood can represent good nudges for unplanned purchases. Researchers can examine if QS technologies shoul also provide personalised recommendations as nudges to change user behaviours. Future studies could therefor implement a quantitative methodology to test Ho and Lim's model in a quantified context.

Future IS research could certainly refine the concept of the heautopticon by examining the antecedents or the con sequences of this self-surveillance with a quantitative approach, testing the 24 (dis)empowering effects as constructs Other dimensions could also be tested. For instance, the feelings of reassurance and guilt could be complemented b other emotions, such as frustration and anxiety that both play a role in power asymmetries on online platforms (Curchod Patriotta, Cohen, & Neysen, 2019). Since power can shift over time (Jasperson et al., 2002), a longitudinal study of QS practices could offer a richer description of the empowerment/disempowerment tensions. Additionally, according to Jasperson et al. (2002), four different lenses could be used to study power: rational, pluralist, interpretive and radical These perspectives represent other possibilities to examine the power relationship between QS technologies and users.

Another avenue for future research is the investigation of the link between control and surveillance (Jensen & Draffan, 2004). Some authors argue that the shift from a Foucauldian society of panoptic surveillance to a Deulezia control society has already occurred (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte et al., 2014; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Isaac, 2013) This discussion goes beyond our study's scope but deserves further investigation under the guidance of the QS

“Knowledge is not always power. There are other ways to be and perceive in the world. Knowledge can be love It can be relationship. It can be connection” (Jensen & Draffan, 2004, p. 19). Our study has focused on the power and the knowledge created by QS technologies but has not examined the diversity of the relationships that may exist between the machine and the individual. Therefore, a more in-depth study of other types of human-computer rela tionships, such as virtual intimacy or friendship with a digital coach, could also represent an original development of our research. A sociomaterial perspective could also reveal the structuring properties of QS technologies, especially in the presence of several devices (assemblage), in order to compare their respective features and roles. Since th heautopticon addresses self-surveillance with technologies, a sociomaterial lens could also offer a novel perspective on the relationship between human and non-human agency.

## 6 | CONCLUSION

Our findings indicate that self-quantification has ambivalent, conflicting effects—13 empowering and 11 dis empowering—spanning the dimensions of power, knowledge, body and space. Our research also extends prior studies that have relied on the panopticon metaphor by revealing a new power relationship between the machine and the individual, which we call the heautopticon, a consented micro-surveillance of oneself that both empower and disempowers individuals, involving the 24 effects observed in our study. The implications of this critical researc cover possibilities for change by discussing individual emancipation, improvements in society and improvements in social theories. The growth of micro-surveillance technologies will probably intensify personal mini-panopticons and the digitised self, opening many avenues for future IS research
