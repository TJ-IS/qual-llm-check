# Distilling a body of knowledge for information systems development

Nik Rushdi Hassan\* & Lars Mathiassen

Finance and MIS, University of Minnesota Duluth, Duluth, USA, email: nhassan@d.umn.edu

Abstract. As a contribution towards consolidating the information systems (IS) <sup>fi</sup>eld, we offer a systematic method for distilling a canonical body of knowledge (BOK) for information systems development (ISD), an area that historically accounts for as much as half of all IS research. Based on an integrative synthesis of the literature, we present a map of the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ISD research, uncover gaps in its canons and suggest fruitful lines of inquiry for new research. Our review combines citation analysis, which identi<sup>fi</sup>es the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s evidence of cumulative tradition, with computer-aided textual analysis, a hermeneutically guided method that organizes the fragmented corpus of ISD literature into coherent knowledge areas. From a pool of over 6500 articles published in the IS Senior Scholars Basket of Journals, we <sup>fi</sup>nd 940 IS citation classics, and from that list, 466 ISD articles that offer canonical ISD knowledge distinctive to IS and complementary to other disciplines such as software engineering and project management. From this study, we offer two contributions: (1) a justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for an ISDBOK grounded in the theory of practice and professionalism, and (2) a canonical map of disciplinary ISD knowledge with areas that have demonstrated cumulative tradition and others that require the attention of IS scholars.

Keywords: information systems development (ISD), IS professionalism, disciplin ary body of knowledge, citation analysis, content analysis, cumulative tradition

## INTRODUCTION

Over the past few decades, the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has been concerned about its relevance (Keen, 1991; Applegate, 1999; Benbasat & Zmud, 1999; Grover et al., 2008; Desouza et al., 2006; Straub & Ang, 2011; Rosemann & Vessey, 2008), identity (Gray, 2003; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; E Sawy, 2003; Alter, 2003; Robey, 2003; Agarwal & Lucas, 2005), diversity (Benbasat & Weber, 1996; Robey, 1996; Banville & Landry, 1989; Taylor et al., 2010) and theoretical development (Keen, 1991; Keen, 1980; Weber, 2012; Weber, 2006; Lyytinen & King, 2006; Weber, 1987; Lyytinen & King, 2004; King & Lyytinen, 2004). This state of affairs, often referred to as a ‘fragmented adhocracy’ (Banville & Landry, 1989), re<sup>fl</sup>ects what internally is perceived as communication de<sup>fi</sup>cits among its numerous research subareas, and externally as disconnects from practitioners (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003).

To address these issues, some have argued for the need to consolidate the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s largely fragmented body of knowledge (BOK) (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Alter, 2012; Iivari et al., $2 0 0 4 ^ { 1 } )$ . We respond to these calls by offering a systematic method for distilling a BOK of information systems development (ISD). Our contribution complements other proposals by IS scholars to consolidate the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, such as developing a community of practice (Klein & Hirschheim, 2008), focusing on conceptual development (Markus & Saunders, 2007) and building native theories (Gregor and Jones, 2007). Together, all these efforts engender the development of a vibrant, socially relevant and in<sup>fl</sup>uential IS discipline.

We demonstrate our method using ISD because it is the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s oldest subarea (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003) with a long and venerable tradition (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Hirschheim et al., 1995; Iivari et al., 1998). Since the inception of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, ISD was conceived as the de<sup>fi</sup>ning core of the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Blumenthal, 1969; Langefors, 1973), and with historically as much as half of all IS research related to ISD (Morrison & George, 1995), it continues to be a core concern (Gregor and Jones, 2007; Nunamaker et al., 1991; Hevner et al., 2004). These characteristics of ISD provide a stable and credible starting point for considering the relationship between development of IS knowledge – the raison d’etre for IS as academic discipline – and professionalism as the engine that will produce IS professionals capable of addressing the most pressing needs of business and technology.

## NEED TO ADDRESS FRAGMENTED ISD KNOWLEDGE

An indication that a <sup>fi</sup>eld has consolidated is the existence of canonical texts in its history, such as Newton’s Principia and Opticks, Lavoisier’s Chemistry, Adam Smith’s The Wealth of Nations and William James’ Introduction to Psychology, that ‘expound the body of accepted theory, [and] illustrate many or all of its successful applications’ (Kuhn, 1970, p. 10). Today, such canonical texts can be found in established disciplines in the form of Gray’s Anatomy, Pauling’s General Chemistry, Samuelson’s Economics and Koontz’s Management. During the early history of IS, a number of authors wrote the discipline’s <sup>fi</sup>rst textbooks for ISD (Langefors, 1973; Blumenthal, 1969). As technologies advanced and methods for ISD proliferated, these textbooks lost their appeal, and the emphasis on ISD methods created a ‘methodology jungle’ (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1988, p. 262) with scores of different methodologies all claiming to be the solution to effective systems development. Despite extensive studies undertaken to address this methodology jungle (Iivari et al., 2001; Iivari et al., 2000; Iivari & Hirschheim, 1996; Iivari et al., 1998), the ISD knowledge landscape remained fragmented with standard texts largely missing and little evidence-based guidance for manoeuvring the diverse and constantly changing set of methodologies.

Various attempts were made to de<sup>fi</sup>ne this ISD knowledge. Besides the studies by Iivari et al. (2001; 2004) on the need for an ISDBOK, Glass and Vessey (1995; 1992) highlighted the importance of taxonomies for application domains, a major concern for ISD (Curtis et al.,

1988). Barki and Rivard (1988) formulated an ‘agreed-upon list of keywords for describing IS content’ (p. 299) in IS research; both versions of this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Barki et al., 1993; Barki & Rivard, 1988) contain a section on ISD titled ‘IS Development and Operations’ with keywords for development strategies, life cycle activities, development methods and tools, implementation and operation. In the area of IS education, the IS’97 curriculum (Davis et al., 1996) represented an early attempt to circumscribe the BOK of IS. However, because this early curriculum was developed using surveys from practitioners and academics, and incorporated curricula from computer science, it included numerous computer-related topics (e.g. algorithmic design and CPU architectures) that would not be considered part of the BOK of IS. The struggle to de <sup>fi</sup>ne IS knowledge is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the IS 2010 Curriculum Guidelines (Topi et al., 2010), which admits that many of its major categories of knowledge are ‘largely determined outside computing. Therefore, the IS discipline will [only] have to develop and maintain a body of knowledge structure for the knowledge areas that are truly IS speci<sup>fi</sup>c’ (emphasis added, p. 383).

To develop an ISDBOK, IS academics and practitioners could arguably refer to other closely aligned institutions that promote their bodies of knowledge such as the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) and its Software Engineering Body of Knowledge (SWEBOK), and to the Project Management Institute (PMI) and its Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBOK). However, IS scholars (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Iivari et al., 2004) claim that ISD is distinct from Software Engineering (SE), and an extensive paradigmatic study of ISD suggests that the 10 knowledge areas in the SWEBOK are ‘insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient for IS experts’ (Iivari et al., 2004 p. 316). Similarly, the general principles of the PMBOK may not be specialized enough for ISD professionals. For example, we can expect the ISD content for topics such as requirements, design. testing and software maintenance to differ considerably from that of SWEBOK. and the content for risk management and project evaluation to differ from that of PMBOK. A more recent effort in this direction is being undertaken by the International Institute of Business Analysis (2015), which publishes A Guide to the Business Analysis Body of Knowledge (BABOK), currently in its third edition. The BABOK focuses on the front end of ISD speci<sup>fi</sup>cally business analysis, systems analysis, requirements analysis, project management, consulting, and process improvement. It does not cover the gamut and extensive coverage required of ISD but represents a valuable contribution towards linking IS research with practitioner practice.

Outside the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, the need for consolidating computing and SE knowledge is also felt. The computer science discipline, even after the publication of the SWEBOK, continues to cal for ‘an intellectually rigorous, formalized, and teachable body of knowledge about the principles underlying software-intensive systems and the processes used to create them’ (Freeman & Hart, 2004, p. 20). Ivar Jacobson, co-developer of the UML standard, established the Software Engineering Method and Theory initiative in 2009 (Jacobson & Spence, 2009), in an effort to transform the foundations of SE by enhancing its language, methods and theories (http://semat.org/). According to key scholars, empirical research in SE is still in its infancy (Jackson, 2001: Basili, 2013), and there is a need for better theory development (Endres 8 Rombach, 2003; Johnson et al., 2012; Sjøberg et al., 2008; Stol & Fitzgerald, 2013). Following similar endeavours, and given the fragmentation of ISD, we need a system of knowledge that can pull together the academic and practitioner community into a zone of mutual discursive interaction (Ramiller et al., 2008).

## DEVELOPING A CANONICAL BODY OF ISD KNOWLEDGE

Against the backdrop of these efforts within and outside the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, our ultimate goal is to develop a canonical body of ISD knowledge that is distinguishable from, yet complementary with SE and project management, and that can foster closer integration between practitioner concerns, empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings and theoretical analyses. SWEBOK de<sup>fi</sup>nes SE as ‘the application of a systematic, disciplined, quanti<sup>fi</sup>able approach to the development, operation, and maintenance of software; that is, the application of engineering to software’ (Bourque, 2014, p. xxxi), focusing therefore on applying engineering principles. Very little work has been carried out on developing the larger picture of what constitutes ISD. Systems developers come from a variety of disciplines and training, bearing many titles such as systems analyst, systems architect, project manager, consultant and business analyst to name a few. Like their counterparts in SE, they deal with the technical aspects of programming; however, system developers also deal with the political and sociological aspects of systems development, the practicalities and <sup>fi</sup>ner aesthetics of good design and the complex task of synthesizing innovative sociotechnical structures. Hence, systems developers have to understand the working situation of others, become savvy politicians who can handle project uncertainty and con<sup>fl</sup>ict, be familiar with different domains of industry and work with people having different educational backgrounds and experiences (Andersen, 1990). The computer science <sup>fi</sup>eld itself is beginning to acknowledge the importance of these social and practice-related aspects of SE (Päivärinta & Smolander, 2015; Dittrich, 2016).

To circumscribe this larger picture of ISD, we need to describe the object of study in a way that allows for a precise and theoretically fruitful discourse of its constituting elements (Hempel, 1952). Thus, Andersen et al. (1990) de<sup>fi</sup>ne ISD as a combination of product-oriented performance to develop computer-based systems and process-oriented management of technical and organizational change within a unique organizational context. This view of ISD is distinct from that of SE, which surrounds the application of engineering principles to software. Similarly, Hirschheim et al. (1995, p. 15) use Welke’s (1983) de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ISD as ‘a change process taken with respect to object systems in a set of environments by a development group to achieve or maintain some objectives (original emphases)’. This notion of the ‘object system’ allows for multiple representations and possibly con<sup>fl</sup>icting views, extending ISD beyond a rational process towards a more open-ended, situation-dependent and iterative process in which the mental models of developers and users change as a result of learning and other in<sup>fl</sup>uences. While some treat ISD as synonymous to development methods, we propose that any de<sup>fi</sup>nition should incorporate its human and organizational aspects that make ISD a unique area of study (Doherty & King, 2005). Both de<sup>fi</sup>nitions given re<sup>fl</sup>ect the practice-based (Bourdieu, 1977; Schatzki, 1996) nature of ISD (in the terms ‘performance’ and ‘process’). Hence, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne ISD as follows:

The integrated social and technical practices of conceptualizing and realizing information technology-based systems, and managing the associated changes and implications to accomplish speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals in organizational contexts.

This de<sup>fi</sup>nition includes all kinds of social and technical concerns surrounding efforts to develop systems including the subsequent implications (use, adoption and success-failure of systems), but is speci<sup>fi</sup>c enough to not include the more general IS research topics such as everyday management of IS, knowledge management, strategic analysis of e-commerce, media richness or business process outsourcing.

A unique de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ISD leads to the possibility of a unique BOK capable of supporting and enhancing such practice. Typically, any BOK is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ‘the complete set of concepts, terms and activities that make up a professional domain, as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the relevant learned society or professional association’ (Body of Knowledge, n.d.). But for a <sup>fi</sup>eld of study that is stil emerging or may not yet have established a professional domain, this de<sup>fi</sup>nition becomes limiting as it connotes a static body of concepts, terms and activities acceptable only by that profession. We instead view a BOK as a dynamic system of disciplinary knowledge that is gathered by its scholars and applied by the discipline’s practitioners. As a requisite to successful practice (Rossides, 1998), disciplinary knowledge forms a bridge between the abstract principles of the academic and the conventional practices of the craftsman (Hiltner, 1954). Unlike vocational training, which is ‘content in producing repetitive knacks and automatic skills’ (see Beck (1952, p. 2) cited in Hiltner (1954)), disciplinary knowledge allows for a wide range of applications and can only be gotten from professional education. As such, it impacts the associated discipline’s functional authority, demarcates its own jurisdiction, links the discipline’s core concepts within a coherent body of theory and has a level of abstraction capable of bridging between academia and practice.

The disciplinary connotations for this kind of knowledge implies, as Kuhn (1970) describes, ‘a set of canons and problems' (p. 108) that the maiority of scholars accept to be their discipline's core concerns and standards. The notion of the ‘canons’ of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c thought is derived from Medieval Latin canonicalis, and from Late Latin canonicus meaning ‘according to rule’. In the ecclesiastical sense, it means ‘decree of the Church’ and was used as early as circa 1600 to mean ‘standard of judging’ (Harper, 2001). Unlike its ecclesiastical cousins, scienti<sup>fi</sup>c canons evolve and change often with each new discovery and scholarly contribution. While they hold sway, these canons form the foundation for on-going intellectual efforts that result in valid questions raised, controversies examined and theories tested in the continuing search for answer to the discipline’s legitimate problems.

## PROFESSIONALISM, ISD AND THE BOK

In the context of consolidation, Iivari et al. (2004) argue that successful efforts to develop a BOK will (1) revitalize the discussion on the lack of a cumulative tradition in IS, (2) ful<sup>fi</sup>l the requirements for a future legitimization of IS as a recognized profession and (3) form a valuable resource for teaching and bridging with practitioners. In addition to these benefits. a BOk represents the canons of the <sup>fi</sup>eld that most members consider to be the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s core concerns. For IS education, a truly IS speci<sup>fi</sup>c BOK is critical for teaching and learning and curriculum development. Knowledge of ISD can be found in management, computer science, economics and social psychology, and unless a recognizable ISDBOK is available for students to study, there is little justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for ISD courses to be taught from a distinct IS curriculum. ISD differs from pure craft work because it is a form of knowledge work in which its experts require intellective skills (Zuboff, 1988) in order to accomplish complex tasks. Without an ISDBOK, these experts can still function by referring to other disciplines, but will be missing the deeper theoretical understanding of their work, and cannot take advantage of a system of knowledge that offers ‘facts, rules, techniques, case histories (cases), stories, theories, hypotheses, philosophies, metaphors’ and other useful resources (Iivari et al., 2001, p. 1027) that are unique to the associated discipline.

The link between this ‘system of knowledge‘ and practice is articulated by practice theorists in different ways. Bourdieu (1977) describes the link between knowledge and practice as a dialectical relationship between the ‘habitus’ and the ‘objective event’. The habitus is a system of ‘lasting, transposable dispositions which, integrating past experiences, functions at every moment as a matrix of perceptions, appreciations and actions and makes possible the achievement of in<sup>fi</sup>nite tasks’ (Original emphases, pp. 82-83). The ISD professional demonstrates this link between knowledge and practice by consolidating the relevant habitus, rules, principles and instructions to accomplish the diverse tasks and projects in ISD, especially in messy, poorly de<sup>fi</sup>ned situations. Schatzki (1996) describes the link between knowledge and practice using the notion of ‘integrative practice’, which establishes ‘understandings, of what to say and do … explicit rules, principles, precepts, and instructions … [and] teleoaffective structures embracing ends, projects, tasks, purposes, beliefs, emotions, and moods’ (p. 89). What is common in these descriptions is how these elements of practice are consolidated into an integrative body, which we can call the BOK.

Abbot (1988) describes this form of consolidation and close relationship between the ‘system of knowledge’ as the diagnosis, treatment and inference aspects of practice in his theory of professionalism. He argues that early studies on professionalization focused on the profession’s formal appearances (Carr-Saunders & Wilson, 1933; Wilensky, 1964) – its mode of association, licensure and ethics – ignoring the substantive elements of professional life that includes the critical role of abstract, formal professional knowledge. Notwithstanding the criticisms on the merits of professionalization (Buckley, 1993; Orlikowsk & Baroudi, 1989; Davis, 2009), we focus on its more substantive characterization, which carries signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for the associated occupation, including the profession’s ‘intellectual jurisdiction’ (Abbott, 1988, p. 75). As Abbot (1988) explains, unlike practical knowledge typically found in the diagnostic and treatment classi<sup>fi</sup>cations, professional knowledge is organized along ‘logically consistent, rationally conceptualized dimensions’ (p. 53) like a BOK. Unlike efforts in professionalization, the theory of professionalism emphasizes the role of the disciplinary knowledge system that not only serves social needs but also affords different levels of jurisdiction, and explains how professionals demonstrate dominance and autonomy (Abbott, 1988; Freidson, 1986; Johnson, 1972; Larson, 1977). The degree of abstraction of the knowledge determines whether or not a profession can compete in a particular historical and social context. As Abbott (1988) emphasizes, ‘The ability of a profession to sustain its jurisdiction lies partly in the power and prestige of its academic knowledge' (pp, 53–54). For example, by redefining the ab. straction of the knowledge system in medicine, the medical profession was able to claim jurisdiction created by vacancies in a particular area of practice (e.g. obesity) and seize practices that were originally within the jurisdiction of psychology, social work and the clergy such as alcoholism, hyperactivity in children and mental illness (Abbott, 1988).

The early studies following the formal trait-based de<sup>fi</sup>nition of professionalization ignored distinguishable expertise, technical autonomy and control over curricula development, education and training, exhibited by occupations that do not necessarily qualify as full professions. Etzioni (1969) highlights this class of expert occupations as ‘semi-professions’, which includes occupations such as teachers, nurses, librarians, social workers and knowledgework related occupations that demonstrate qualities of professionalism at par with ful professions (Larkin, 1978; Forsyth & Danisiewicz, 1985; Malhotra & Morris, 2009; Von Norden<sup>fl</sup>ycht, 2010). What is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant about these semi-professions is the extent of intellectual jurisdiction they can claim. For the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, it is safe to say that there is unanimous agreement concerning the value in educating and training IS professionals capable of addressing the needs of the new digital age. Despite this urgent need for more IS professionals within an increasingly competitive system of professions, we know little about the jurisdictional claim of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld and its knowledge.

Drawing on Goode’s (1969, p. 277-278) distinction between service and knowledge within an occupation, professionalism among its practitioners is captured by the ideal of service, while the development of its knowledge involves the ideal of cumulative tradition among its scholars. An occupation then becomes more or less professional depending on the abstraction of knowledge and where on the continuum of service and cumulative tradition they fall. We have adopted this basic logic to understand the on-going process of consolidation of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Figure 1). At any stage of development, our <sup>fi</sup>eld’s disciplinary knowledge may contribute to both professionalization efforts among practitioners and towards building cumulative tradition among researchers. Both of these activities and interactions between them may contribute to the on-going development of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld by modifying and enhancing its disciplinary knowledge.

Development and codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the abstract disciplinary knowledge offer the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld a sym bol of agreement regarding its core concepts and theories, thereby freeing its members from the distracting tasks of constantly re-examining them and responding to questions from the laity. It also frees members from deep and debilitating debates over which methods are legitimate and which methods are not, what constitutes an object of study and which belong to other disciplines, which problems are considered signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and which ones less so – questions and debates that have characterized the history of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. By affording opportunity to address a smaller range of problems that scholars agree are relevant to core concerns, the <sup>fi</sup>eld will be able to investigate phenomena in a degree of detail and depth that would not be possible otherwise as illustrated by the processes of uni<sup>fi</sup>cation and specialization in the medical <sup>fi</sup>eld (Schwartz, 2014).

Thus, we contend that the difference between IS professionals and non-professionals lies in the distinctive abstract knowledge and jurisdiction they exercise, and the skills of IS professionals <sup>fl</sup>ow from an evolving fund of disciplinary knowledge organized into an internally consistent body of concepts and theories. Non-professionals may be able to perform well in their tasks; in Bourdieu’s (1990) terms, they have practical knowledge – but it is a competence that is oblivious to itself. Computing professionals have already embarked with some success in the development of requisite skills (e.g. the Information Systems Audit and Control Association and its Certi<sup>fi</sup>ed Information Systems Auditor, the International Professional Practice Partnership (Johnson, 2010), the eSkills UK (http://www.e-skills.com), the Association for Information Tech nology Professionals and its Institute for Certi<sup>fi</sup>cation of Computing Professionals and the International Institute of Business Analysis (2015)). Although these efforts are focused on developing skills and legitimacy, they are, as illustrated in Figure 1, intertwined with efforts to develop the system of disciplinary knowledge. Herein lies an opportunity for the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld to en hance its intellectual jurisdiction and contribute to its practitioners’ professionalization efforts, bridge the gap between theory and practice and reduce the communication de<sup>fi</sup>cit between researchers and practitioners.

## IDENTIFYING THE ISD CANONS

Theory is when you know everything but nothing works. Practice is when everything works but no one knows why. In this room, theory and practice come together: Nothing works and no one knows why.\~Herb Kelman cited in Mohrman and Lawler (2011, p. 103).

Despite this rather cynical view of the state of management and organizational sciences, we argue that in the case of IS, the <sup>fi</sup>eld has developed in the past 40 or so years since its inception, its own set of useful research or canons that can be found in its discipline-speci<sup>fi</sup>c journals (Association for Information Systems, 2011). These journals are generally accepted as repositories of IS scholarship, regardless of the quali<sup>fi</sup>cations of the author – computer scientist, manager, psychologist or economist. Even if scholarly IS work may be published in non-IS journals, the professional standards set by the canons distinguish such IS scholarship from those of other allied disciplines. The more developed and speci<sup>fi</sup>ed the disciplinary knowledge, the more likely the associated occupation can be professionalized. Lowry et al. (2013) provide scientometric evidence showing that the Senior Scholars Basket of Journal represents the highest quality of research in the <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Identifying the canons of a <sup>fi</sup>eld requires selecting the research that its scholars agree to be most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. As Kuhn (1970) and other philosophers and sociologists of knowledge (Popper, 1969; Mulkay, 1979; Lakatos & Musgrave, 1970) emphasize, the growth of knowledge in any discipline is predicated on the agreement of its scholars notwithstanding any ideological strife and controversies that it faces. Moreover, as Kuhn (1970) argues, this agreement becomes the sanction for any scholarly activity to claim legitimacy. The canonica bodies of knowledge for PM and SE were also derived from the authority of their scholars. In 1983, the PMI initiated development of the PMBOK based on evidence from various PMI handbooks, PMI symposia papers, Project Management Journal articles and textbooks authored by PMI members (Allen, 1995). In the mid-1990s, the IEEE and Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) consulted software engineering textbooks and research articles to build their <sup>fi</sup>rst set of draft standards for the SWEBOK (Bourque et al., 1998; Bourque et al., 1999). In both cases, centralized committees provided the necessary guidance and direction to develop the standards. Given the long history of ISD since the inception of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, a similar distilling process based on the authority of its scholars offers a viable option. However, the fragmentation of the <sup>fi</sup>eld requires a more inclusive and nondiscriminatory approach. Recent advances in extracting key information from large corpus of texts (Baeza-Yates & Ribeiro-Neto, 1999; Titscher et al., 2000) provide such an inclusive approach. For example, the biomedical sciences apply information retrieval methods to discover knowledge from its large and often fragmented corpus of biomedical literature for hypothesis building, diagnosis, prevention and treatment (Cohen & Hersh, 2005; Hersh, 2009).

Scientometrics offers an indirect method of quantifying the level of agreement and, at the same time, a way to identify what is considered signi<sup>fi</sup>cant form the large corpus of text from any discipline. Information scientists (Cronin, 1981; Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1955; Leydesdorff, 1987; Ormerod, 2000; Price, 1963) suggest using the number of citations received as an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and useful proxy for the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of any study. This notion of publication signi<sup>fi</sup>cance is based on the qualitative assessment performed by the total number of citing authors that confer the cited work a certain level of authority. The higher the number of positive citations, the higher the level of agreement and the more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant the study is in the eyes of the discipline’s scholars. Evidence from historiographical studies has shown that the number of honours conferred on authors correlate well with the number of citations they receive (Cole & Cole, 1967). Data from the Science Citation Index show that Nobel Prize winners are highly cited with total citation counts 50 times more than the average scientist. Using this evidence, Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld and Malin (1968) used citation counts to successfully predict Nobel Prize winners in 1969, and, since 1989, Thomson Reuters continues to successfully predict who’s likely to receive the Nobel Prize.<sup>2</sup>

Citation analysis offers more than just a count of citations. It remains a key method in mapping and analysing ideas as part of comprehensive literature reviews (Hart, 1998). Citation analysis has developed to become a sophisticated and increasingly common research-onresearch evaluation method (Bornmann & Daniel, 2008). Historically, scholars have shown it is effective in verifying scienti<sup>fi</sup>c data (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1955), measuring the growth of science (Gilbert, 1978), studying the quality and impact of research (Leydesdorff & Amsterdamska, 1990), analysing the emergence of new scienti<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>elds and subspecialties (Mulkay, 1974), describing the intellectual structures of the sciences (Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al., 1974), guiding national economic development policies (Moravcsik, 1985) and, more recently, providing re<sup>fi</sup>ned measures for researcher impact and productivity (Ball, 2005). Although it has been applied in IS numerous times since the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s inception (Hamilton & Ives, 1982; Hamilton & Ives, 1980; Hamilton & Ives, 1983; Culnan & Swanson, 1986; Culnan, 1986; Culnan, 1987; Cheon et al., 1992), the potential for a more sophisticated application within the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld is not immediately forthcoming (Straub, 2006; Hassan & Loebbecke, 2016).

An early example of such scientometric analysis is Price’s (1965) proposed measure of when research quali<sup>fi</sup>es to become sources for the canons of a discipline. In studying the half-lives of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c works, Price (1965) estimated the number of journal articles and the number of scientists double every 15–20 years. At this exponential rate, on average, 35% of articles published will never be cited at all, another 49% cited only once, leaving the remaining 16% cited only 3.2 times on average. Out of this 16%, only 1% of papers are cited six times or more. Although it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to say exactly how many articles from this 16% represent the canons of the discipline, it is safe to say that this 16% draw the most attention of the scholars in a discipline. Price (1965) and Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld (1984) call the works that are cited at least four times a year (i.e. cited 40 or more times within 10 years) the ‘citation classics’ of the discipline (as opposed to a ‘classic’ in the traditional sense). Those that survive at that rate for more than a decade become ‘super classics’. These citation classics <sup>fi</sup>nd their way into canons of a discipline, and they represent evidence of cumulative tradition by demonstrating how members of the discipline jointly contribute to a subject matter they consider signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

While the number of citations received alludes to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the study, its conceptual signi<sup>fi</sup>cance is embedded in the complex and changing network of varied elements surrounding the researchers’ social, political, cognitive and technical environment (Callon et al., 1983) as represented by the words or key terms applied in the research documents. Thus, each term or set of terms (n-grams) becomes a code that symbolically assigns a summative, salient and essence-capturing attribute to the portion of the text (Saldana, 2009). Using content analysis (Titscher et al., 2000; Krippendorff, 2004), we can organize large volumes of textual and other materials into manageable content categories, which can then be used for discovering and describing the focus of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld on ISD. Content analysis allows inferences to be made from data, which would otherwise be too costly, tedious or too obtrusive if carried out using other methods (Holsti, 1969; Krippendorff, 2004; Weber, 1990). Content analysis has been successfully used in IS research (Todd et al., 1995; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006; Ghose, 2009). However, despite the explosion of content in digital form, IS qualitative research and content analysis studies largely rely on manual coding without taking advantage of the more automated means of textual analysis. Notable exceptions include Sidorova et al. (2008) using latent semantic analysis, an evolution of co-word analysis, and Larsen et al. (2008) using latent categorization. Computer-aided textual analysis (CATA) (Kabanoff, 1997; Matthes & Kohring, 2008) affords higher reliability as compared with human coding with lower cost and greater speed (Conway, 2006; Neuendorf, 2002), allowing analysts to focus on other aspects of the inquiry such as validity, interpretation and explanation (Weber, 1990; Morris, 1994). Indeed, several studies in organizational research have shown the effectiveness and validity of combin ing the processing power of the computer and the interpretive skills of the human analyst (Shor et al., 2010: Gephart. 1997), and CATA is therefore used increasingly for interpretative research and theory-building (Duriau et al., 2007).

Although numerous options are available for fully automating content analysis of large amounts of data, these automated methods follow a deterministic path once the search terms, dictionaries or category systems are selected. An interactive-hermeneutic process of content analysis provides for a more judicious use of rapid processing of text and human interpretive skills and allows for the results to grow and change with the growth and development of the subject in question (Krippendorff, 2004). The process is interactive because the categories of analysis and search terms applied are not <sup>fi</sup>xed but become apparent to the analyst by interrogating the text. It is hermeneutic because the process is directed by the analysts’ growing understanding of the body of texts. It allows for any form of coding, grounded or a priori and provides for course corrections in midstream, as more interesting categories and insights appear. For this study, we used n-grams (sequence of up to n words) as search terms to classify, categorize and retrieve information and to increase the precision of classifying text (Bekkerman & Allan, 2004; Tan et al., 2002; Crossley & Louwerse, 2007). To increase the precision, the analyst iteratively re<sup>fi</sup>nes the selection of words to become as relevant as possible to the class of concepts being classi<sup>fi</sup>ed (Stryker et al., 2006).

Although the detailed development of knowledge in a given area cannot be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed without examining the patterns discerned from its complex network of terms within observations, arguments and claims (Amsterdamska & Leydesdorff, 1989), we can reasonably say that the collection of such patterns that alludes to cumulative tradition can be found within these citation classics. In other words, we capture both the qualitative and quantitative signi<sup>fi</sup>cance that is demonstrated by canons of the discipline by combining the qualitative inquiry using content analysis afforded by these terms, with citation analysis, thereby identifying the knowledge that IS scholars agree is most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant – its canons.

The two methods combined do not exhaust the ways to identify canons. Because the results represent a systematic synthesis of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant body of relevant empirical evidence (Rousseau, 2006; Rousseau et al., 2008) related to ISD, it becomes more than a simple literature review. The synthesis becomes a <sup>fi</sup>rst step in establishing conclusions concerning ISD, identifying where <sup>fi</sup>ndings are clear (and where they are not) and suggesting productive lines for future research. Because of the practical nature of ISD, the synthesis should include practitioner input, and as this study argues, such efforts within practice are already underway. What is missing is linking these efforts within practice with academic research to produce research that is useful and that will advance both theory and practice (Mohrman & Lawler, 2011). We introduce this useful research as ‘professional knowledge’ and take a small step towards establishing this knowledge in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld by proposing a speci<sup>fi</sup>c approach for developing the ISDBOK. Much of that knowledge is captured by the citation classics by virtue of the incorporation of practitioners’ works into academic research. However, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to determine how well that practice is integrated into academic research because no bibliographic evidence is available to validate the extent of such integration. Therefore, part of the research method for identifying this professional knowledge should include strong evidence outside the ci tation classics.

## RESEARCH METHOD

Zuckerman and Merton (1973) call the process of distilling the canons within a discipline the ‘codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge’ (p. 506) and de<sup>fi</sup>ne codi<sup>fi</sup>cation as ‘the consolidation of empirical knowledge into succinct and interdependent theoretical formulation’ (p. 507). To distil disciplinary ISD knowledge as a contribution towards consolidating the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, we extend previous studies on the ISDBOK (Iivari et al., 2004; Iivari et al., 2001) by performing a synthesis (Cooper, 1988; Hart, 1998; Khoo et al., 2011) of the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ISD articles in the IS literature. Iivari et al. (2004) reviewed articles published between 1996 and 2000 in MIS Quarterly and Information Systems Journal to provide evidence of a considerable body of distinct ISD process knowledge. Our study broadens this pool of articles to the Senior Scholars Basket of Journals (Association for Information Systems, 2011), which most IS scholars agree are the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The distillation process progressed iteratively between search and acquisition of material in the literature and several steps of analysis and interpretation of content (Cooper, 1998; Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014).

Based on citation counts, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 726 articles as citation classics from a total of 6,693 articles published up to August 2012 in the basket of eight journals. Another 214 articles received at least 20 citations because they were catalogued and therefore were considered borderline or ‘would be citation classics’. We added these articles to the pool of sources resulting in a total list of 940 citation classics and would be citation classics. Although several high-impact journals such as Management Science, Communications of the ACM and IEEE Transactions of Software Engineering also publish high-quality articles on ISD, the number of ISD articles in these journals is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly smaller compared with articles in IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c journals. More importantly, when an IS author publishes in, say, IEEE Transactions of Software Engineering, the contents re<sup>fl</sup>ect the traditions, concepts and style of argumentation characteristic of SE research. As our primary goal is to focus on knowledge that is distinctive to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, it made sense not to include articles in high-impact journals outside the <sup>fi</sup>eld in the initial iteration. However, as we describe in the earlier section, these sources can corroborate and enhance the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c journals.

Sociologists of knowledge describe how knowledge from earlier sources is often incorporated into later publications and travels across different disciplines, especially if they are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant to the development of the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Merton, 1973; Merton, 1968; Zuckerman, 1987). Because the sources of knowledge outside the basket of eight journals could become inspiration for the ISD citation classics, they should be included in subsequent distillations. Examples of such studies include signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ISD research published during the early stages of the development of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, in journals such as Management Science and Communications of the ACM. As such, these studies may help assess the results of our research and suppor critical self-correction and future advancement. For example, highly cited works from the computer science <sup>fi</sup>eld may help validate or qualify IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c research. As the results will show, certain well-known topics (e.g. Testing) can be found within the ISD citation classics but are not extensively researched; however, other classics outside the ISD citation classics (e.g. Myers et al., 2012) support the argument that such knowledge areas should be a canonical ISD knowledge area.

To analyse the sampled articles, we followed a hermeneutic process (Webster & Watson, 2002; Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014; Hart, 1998), beginning with an initial reading of several articles on disciplinary ISD knowledge (Iivari et al., 1996; 1998; 2000; 2001; Barki et al., 1988; 1993). These articles helped us develop the set of a priori search terms and n-grams for content analysis of the 940 citation classics. For example, we combined the ‘requirements construction’ category of Iivari et al. (2004) in the ISD performance process with the related categories of Barki et al. (1988; 1993) such as ‘requirements determination’, ‘systems analysis’, ‘user needs assessment’, ‘information analysis’, ‘requirements speci<sup>fi</sup>cation’, ‘requirements de<sup>fi</sup>nition’ and ‘information requirements’. We imported data from the Institute for Scienti<sup>fi</sup>c Information Web of Science database of cited references up to August 2012, the list of authors, year published, number of citations received, titles and abstracts from these articles into NVivo (QSR International Pty Ltd, Doncaster, Victoria, Australia) (Bazeley, 2007; Bazeley, 2013) to assist in coding and categorizing all articles. We used the titles, abstracts and keywords as basis for content analysis instead of the full article because these elements encapsulate the core concepts from the study. The use of abstracts to analyse text has a venerable history not only in the natural and life sciences (Ding et al. 2002) but also in the social sciences (Zimbalist, 1978). Using a priori coding terms from the initial reading. and an interactive-hermeneutic CATA method. we identified ISD-specific studies from the list of 940 IS citation classics. This hybrid automatic-interactive process supported our exploratory-discovery-development-based approach and at the same time ensured an acceptable level of rigor and validity.

The high-level structure that organizes the various ISD knowledge areas is drawn from Andersen et al. (1990), and extensive studies undertaken by Iivari et al. (1996; 1998; 2000; 2001). These studies identify a tiered framework representing the deep structure of ISD, which we refer to as ‘dimensions’. We call them dimensions because they represent theoretical struc tures that do not yet qualify as theories (Mohr, 1982) but help to explain the nature of ISD. Following standard content analysis techniques (Holsti, 1969), the citation classics are coded into categories that are: (1) exhaustive, (2) mutually exclusive, (3) independent and (4) conceptually distinct. Categorization needs to be exhaustive so that all aspects of ISD are covered. They need to be mutually exclusive so that a speci<sup>fi</sup>c code or utterance in an article cannot be categorized in more than one category. However, doing so does not preclude the possibility that a speci<sup>fi</sup>c article may be categorized simultaneously into more than one category.

Each category within the dimension is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a ‘knowledge area’. For example, in the keyword classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of Barki et al. (1988; 1993), the term ‘Strategic Planning’ occurs both under ‘A: Reference Disciplines’ and ‘E: IS Management’. For the proposed distillation process, ‘Strategic Planning’ can only be categorized under one knowledge area. The knowledge areas need to be independent such that the assignment of any article into a knowledge area does not affect its classi<sup>fi</sup>cation into another knowledge area. Finally, they need to be conceptually distinct so that each knowledge area belongs to a single principle allowing conceptually differ ent levels of analysis to be kept separate. Although this coding process will create a hierarchical structure, the fact that an article may be categorized into more than one category coupled with the dynamic nature of knowledge growth will result in a network structure in which elements within each knowledge area are interrelated with each other as well as with elements within other knowledge areas. Throughout the process, memos were created to document how decisions were made concerning certain search terms and how articles should be logically coded. The existing ISD knowledge areas evolved during each iteration, and new knowledge areas emerged to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings from coding all 940 citation classics.

In addition to using citation classics to identify the knowledge areas, we also accepted evi dence from outside the citation classics that corroborate or strengthen the argument for inclusion of certain knowledge areas. Examples of such evidence include cognate concepts that are found within SE or PM, highly cited articles from practitioner or textual sources. More examples of how this principle is complied with are provided in detail in Appendix A for selected knowledge areas such as Organizational Alignment, Infrastructure, Architecture, Implementation, Acceptance and Use. The complete list of knowledge areas and the <sup>fi</sup>nal set of their associated n-grams and codes interpreted from the pool of 940 IS citation classics are provided in Appendix B (Tables B1, B2, and B3).

## RESEARCH RESULTS

From the considered basket of eight journals. MIS Quarterly. Information Systems Research and the Journal of Management Information Systems published the vast majority (78%) of the ISD citation classics (Table 1). Out of the 940 articles in the pool, 466 or 50% were catego rized as ISD citation classics. This proportion is consistent with Morrison and George (1995) that found 45% of IS articles related to SE from MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM and Management Science during 1986–1991. Because SE restricts itself primarily to the life cycle phases of software development, a slightly larger proportion can be expected as ISD research also includes subjects such as systems evaluation and measures of adoption and acceptance (Iivari et al., 2000; Iivari et al., 2004). Out of the extensive paradigmatic analysis of ISD, Iivari et al. (1996; 1998; 2000; 2001) developed a descriptive theory that organized the numerous ISD approaches, methodologies, techniques and tools into a coherent classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme (see Appendix A for details). Following concepts from Andersen et al. (1990), this process knowledge is categorized into performance processes, processes focused on the direct tasks of completing the product (e.g. requirements construction and software design), and management processes, focused on the management of the former (e.g. project organizing and supplier management). A third dimension was added to include the guiding practices and principles, along with methods and tools applied in ISD.

We apply this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme as a starting point for building the canonical map of disciplinary ISD knowledge. The list of 466 ISD citation classics from over four decades of ISD research were coded and categorized into one or more knowledge areas based on the n-grams that represented those knowledge areas.

## Canonical map of disciplinary ISD knowledge

The <sup>fi</sup>nal iteration of the distilling process concludes with a canonical map of disciplinary ISD knowledge (Figure 2) anchored in three knowledge dimensions: ISD Framework, ISD Manage ment and ISD Performance. The ISD Framework can be viewed as principles of generating and structuring practices together with representations operating at different levels of abstraction. At the most abstract level is the Paradigm knowledge area of ISD that describes the most funda mental set of assumptions about the physical and social world surrounding ISD, while the mos concrete knowledge area within the ISD Framework concerns hardware and software tools (e.g. Integrated development environments) applied in ISD practice.

The ISD Management dimension describes the processes of enabling the coordination, organization and practice of ISD among participating actors. The distilling process found eigh knowledge areas in this dimension, representing process-oriented practices. The third dimension, ISD Performance, de<sup>fi</sup>nes processes that directly contribute to the production of IS artefacts, containing 13 product-oriented knowledge areas. The following subsections describe each dimension and associated knowledge in more detail.

It is important to realize that this map is not yet an ISDBOK. Much work needs to be performed before the set of knowledge areas, concepts, theories and accompanying material can be called a <sup>fi</sup>rst BOK, including discussions that need to be undertaken with practitioners. Nevertheless, this knowledge map conceptualizes the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt at circumscribing the content and boundaries of that ISDBOK. The map answers several questions related to ISD. (1) What topics are of interest to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld in systems development? (2) What are the core concerns of ISD? (3) What relations does ISD have with its context and its environment? (4) What are the boundaries of ISD? (5) What are the inputs, processes and products of ISD? Thus, the disciplinary ISD knowledge area map offers more than just a list of research topics. Together with the list of coding keywords (Appendix B), it determines tasks and processes that need to be completed, problems that must be solved, possible solutions to problems and ways of contributing to knowledge in ISD. A clear example of this recursive relationship is Methoo Management, a knowledge area indicating that the ISD Framework support different methodologies and techniques for performing the tasks of building systems, and the methodologies and techniques themselves are impacted and shaped by ISD Management. For instance, Fitzgerald (1997) found that formal methods are not simply applied; they are often tailored (managed) as a result of needs arising from practice in the ISD Management dimension.

The number of citation classics categorized into each dimension provides an indication of the level of research activity that IS scholars agree is the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (Table 2). Out of the 466 ISD articles, 393 articles (84%), the vast majority, are about ISD Performance, 90 articles (19%) are on ISD Management and 62 articles (13%) are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as ISD Framework articles. According to Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld (1973), the number of citations garnered by citation classics requires careful interpretation. He discovered that although methodological papers were highly cited, they made up a smaller proportion (less than 25%) compared with other kinds of papers (e.g. theoretical or experimental, review and reference articles).

Based on Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld’s insight, we averaged the number of citations the top 10 articles received in each category to obtain a sense of the amount of research activity taking place. The averages in the last column in Table 2 suggest that not only are the proportions of citation classics in ISD Management and Framework signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower than those in ISD Performance, their average citation numbers are also signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests more efforts are expended on ISD Performance compared with the other dimensions of ISD disciplinary knowledge.

Because the ISD Framework dimension drives the interpretation and actions of both the ISD Management process and the ISD Performance process, together, these three dimensions shape the practice of ISD, and the practice of ISD also shapes the three dimensions. In other words, this disciplinary knowledge map for ISD describes a dynamic living system for a deeper study of ISD. The map itself does not prescribe any particular approach or methodology, but it highlights the major differences between say Approach and Methodology and that it is possible fo an organization to apply one approach but practice different methodologies. Beginning with the ISD Framework, researchers can investigate the paradigms that shape various management processes in ISD, which in turn leads to an investigation of speci<sup>fi</sup>c performance processes, all of which together shape ISD practice. As a start, the canonical map of ISD knowledge helps scholars and professionals navigate the tenuous and often confusing methodology jungle in a way that pro tects the legacy of useful methodologies and exposes the richness of diverse approaches and methodologies capable of addressing the complex landscape of ISD problems.

## Review of ISD framework knowledge

The ISD Framework dimension is inspired by the approach dimension (not to be confused with the Approach knowledge area) of Iivari et al. (1996; 1998; 2000; 2001), which they de<sup>fi</sup>ne as alternative and complementary knowledge of how to conduct the Performance and Management Processes. The ISD Framework is akin to Bourdieu’s (1977, 1990) notions of habitus and knowledgeability. The knowledge areas in the ISD framework are various kinds of habitus because they are principles of generation and structuring of practices and representations operating at different levels of abstraction. An ISD professional is said to be knowledgeable in Bourdieu’s terms because that knowledge is re<sup>fl</sup>exive on practice, not just repetitive skills that are practiced without a deep understand of its underlying foundations. We refer to this ‘knowledgeability’ as having the body of theory that informs practice and is shaped by practice.

At the most abstract level of the ISD Framework is the contentious concept of the Paradigm made famous by Kuhn (1970) to mean the exemplary problem-solutions held by a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research community. The paradigm is broadly de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the IS community as ‘the most fundamental set of assumptions … about knowledge … and about the physical and social world’ (Hirschheim et al., 1995, p. 46-47). Previous studies (Hirschheim et al., 1995; Iivari et al., 1998) identify ISD Paradigms (e.g. functionalist paradigm) based on the ontological, epistemological or ethical assumptions of that practice. Paradigms are concretized into different Approaches based on the similarity of their methodologies. Approaches (e.g. object-oriented approach) are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘a set of goals, guiding principles, fundamental concepts and principles for the ISD practice that drive interpretations and actions in ISD’ (Iivari et al., 1998, p. 166). The notions of Paradigms and Approaches in ISD are distinctive and unique to the <sup>fi</sup>eld with no equivalent concepts in SWEBOK. They provide important structuring of the methodology jungle by grouping similar Methodologies into a single Approach.

As a term, Methodology is often confused with methods and techniques. Although the suf<sup>fi</sup>x – logy suggests Methodology is the ‘study of methods’, the term is often used as a more grandiose term for methods (Fitzgerald 1996). Following Hirschheim et al. (1995), Iivari et al. (1998, p. 165) de<sup>fi</sup>ne Methodology as ‘an organized collection of concepts, methods, beliefs, values and normative principles supported by material resources’. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition views Methodology as more elaborate than methods by incorporating beliefs, values and norms. Hence, the traditional SDLC methodology may apply the same interview methods as the object-oriented methodology, but they differ in concepts, beliefs and expectations (Sircar et al., 2001). Methods are therefore closer to Technique, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘a well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned sequence of elementary operations, which permits the achievement of certain outcomes if executed correctly’ (Iivari et al., 1998, p. 165). Most IS authors, as well as those in SE, use Methods and Methodology interchangeably (as in Object-Oriented Methods), which obscures the other aspects of development (e.g. beliefs and values) contained in a methodology. Certain Tools (e.g. the Information Engineering Workshop) are designed with speci<sup>fi</sup>c methodologies in mind, expecting their user to comply with them, while others (e.g. generic CASE tools) are more agnostic and admit any methodological principles.

The higher number of citation classics in Approach and Methodology compared with other ISD Framework knowledge areas (Table 3) suggests a more established cumulative tradition. Within the Approach knowledge area, ISD demonstrates cumulative tradition related to IS planning (Earl, 1993), open source development (e.g. Fitzgerald, 2006; Chau and Tam, 1997) and process and change management (Davenport and Stoddard, 1994; Robey et al., 2002). There are even signs of cumulative tradition in agile development (Fitzgerald 2006) and object-orientation (Agarwal et al., 1996; Burton-Jones and Meso 2006), which are also topics within the Methodology knowledge area. Advanced methodological issues such as the move away from formal and traditional methodologies (Naumann and Jenkins 1982; Fitzgerald 1996) and how methodologies are actually applied in practice (Fitzgerald 1997; Hardgrave et al.. 2003) have also been extensively studied and demonstrate cumulative tradition

The organization of the ISD Framework into paradigms, approaches, methodologies, tech niques and tools may therefore help resolve inconsistencies in methodologies and use of terms.

Hence, the ISD Framework provides a coherent view of industry practice, not necessarily in their pure pristine forms, but in messy pragmatic and emerging forms that constantly adapt to address changing application, technology and artefact domains (Conboy, 2009; Fitzgerald, 1997; Fitzgerald et al., 2006b).

The body of theory behind the ISD Framework accommodates seemingly different concepts and the tenuous and often confusing methodology jungle into a coherent structure, describing how paradigms, approaches, methodologies, techniques and tools relate to each other as part of ISD dis ciplinary knowledge. The organization of studies into the knowledge areas of Paradigm, Approach and Methodology supports comparisons of different methodologies, placing focus less on the ‘methodology of the day’ and more on the theories and principles shared across methodologies. A survey of the body of theory shows that the dominant philosophy (called the paradigm in ISD articles) behind ISD still is functionalism – the notion that the subject (the developer) can rationally and objectively control and manipulate certain representations of reality in the development project (Goles & Hirschheim, 2000). What follows from this philosophy are various theories aligned to Anthony’s (1965) and Simon’s (1981) empiricist, mathematical, behavioural-functional and problem-solving approach that aims at changing existing situations into preferred ones.

For instance, as Iivari et al. (1998) explain, the common theory underlying the DeMarco and Yourdon structured methodologies is to build maintainable systems that have components that are highly cohesive, but weakly coupled, and both essentially apply a data<sup>fl</sup>ow concept. In contrast, object-oriented approaches underpin various methodologies such as the Rationa Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Process, XP, Scrum and other agile methodologies with the goal of building reusable components for rapid deployment that apply information hiding and inheritance concepts. These two groups of methodologies apply different techniques; the structured methodologies apply data <sup>fl</sup>ow diagrams and entity-relationship diagrams, while the object-oriented methodol ogies apply use cases and object modelling techniques. Nevertheless, both structured and object-oriented approaches are driven by the functionalist philosophy.

On the other hand, less dominant approaches and theories such as the soft-systems methodology and critical theory underlying the European systems development tradition move towards achieving emancipatory goals and making sense of the environment in order to align with subjective needs. The strength of functionalist philosophy lies in its ability to rationally organize complex systems and has been the approach of choice for large systems. The alternative philosophies are more open to the ongoing social construction and emerging changes in de<sup>fi</sup>ning system requirements (Hirschheim et al., 1995). Thus, viewing the jungle of methodologies through the lens of the ISD Framework enables both researcher and practitioner to better understand and manage complex ISD Performance and ISD Management.

## Review of ISD management knowledge

The second dimension reflects the process-oriented work of ISD Management, which enables the coordination, organization and practice of ISD amongst participating actors. This dimension consists of eight knowledge areas ranging from the management of ISD methods to the management of ISD suppliers. The ISD Management citation classics include concepts that are similar to the concepts in the PMBOK (Project Management Institute, 2013) but with more focus on IT-related projects such as controlling systems development projects (Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003; Kirsch 1997) and adapting IT project management strategies to a technolog ical environment (Markus 2004a). Many of these concepts are generic to project management (e.g. see Appendix B for keywords ‘project management’, ‘project scope’, ‘project costs’, ‘project communication’ and ‘project schedule’), and we folded citation classics covering these concepts into the general knowledge area of Project Organizing. Other project-management related concepts that are more speci<sup>fi</sup>c to ISD identi<sup>fi</sup>ed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more citation classics that justi<sup>fi</sup>ed their own knowledge areas (Table 4).

For example, People Management features prominently in ISD, and although it is similar to Human Resource Management in the PMBOK, the focus of People Management in ISD is less on staf<sup>fi</sup>ng and distinctively more on the generally higher level of interpersonal con<sup>fl</sup>ict inherent in ISD (Barki and Hartwick 2001a), the closer involvement of users in the development process (Barki and Hartwick 1994; Tait and Vessey 1988) and challenges in managing relationships between users and analysts (Newman and Robey 1992). Also, Risk Management is similar to Project Risk Management in the PMBOK, but focuses speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on software project risks (Barki et al., 1993; Barki et al. 2001b; Schmidt et al. 2001) and portfolio-based management of software risks (Lyytinen et al. 1998). The higher risks inherent in managing ISD projects are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the areas of Risk Management and Performance Management (Nidumolu 1995). Although there is some overlap between the two areas, Risk Management concerns managing project portfolio risks, whereas Performance Management concerns the measurement, evaluation and handling of project progress, prominently featuring issues of project escalation (Keil 1995; Keil et al. 2000), de-escalation (Keil and Robey 1999) and measuring project performance (Akkermans and van Helden 2002: Guinan et al. 1998).

Supplier Management is especially unique in ISD because of outsourcing arrangements that have received considerable media coverage. However, most citation classics that address outsourcing are about outsourcing of IS functions or business process outsourcing (Ang and Straub 1998; Grover et al. 1996; Lacity and Willcocks 1998; Loh 1993), which are not strictly ISD outsourcing, although ISD may very well be part of the package being outsourced. Therefore, such classics are not categorized as ISD citation classics. Hence, speci<sup>fi</sup>c coding terms (e.g. ‘outsourced development’ and ‘manage vendor’) were used to target only ISD related outsourcing arrangements. Such ISD citation classics include controlling outsourced projects (Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003) and the management of external consultants and vendors who act as suppliers (Ko et al., 2005; Thong et al., 1996).

Out of the 27 articles categorized as Stakeholder Management, we found <sup>fi</sup>ve articles about partnering with vendors and suppliers in developing trading networks (e.g. Hart and Saunders 1998; Kern and Wilcocks 2002; Klepper 1995), six articles on the impact of top management involvement on ISD success (e.g. Beath 1991; Lucas Jr. 1978; Thong et al. 1996), six articles on managing stakeholder trust, control and relations in ISD projects (e.g. Guinan et al. 1998; Kirsch 1997), three articles on managing con<sup>fl</sup>ict among inter-organizational stakeholders (e.g. Kumar and van Dissel 1996; Webster 1995) and two on stakeholder analysis (Kambil and van Heck 1998; Pouloudi and Whitley 1997). The body of theory drawn to research ISD Management is eclectic, ranging from various con<sup>fl</sup>ict, management and resolution theories in People Management (Barki and Hartwick 2001a) to social network theory in Stakeholder Management (Kambil and van Heck 1998). The body of theory in each area is directly relevant to that area, but they are all connected by a concern for the management of ISD. Other than con<sup>fl</sup>ict theories to manage people in ISD projects, psychology and social psychology theories feature prominently in measuring user participation, attitudes and involvement (Barki and Hartwick 1994; Tait and Vessey 1988), as do group process theories (Guinan et al. 1998) that explain software project team performance from behavioural as opposed to technical factors.

Software Con<sup>fi</sup>guration Management, a major knowledge area in SWEBOK and suggested by Iivari et al. (2004) as an ISD construct, did not <sup>fi</sup>nd support from the ISD citation classics. In the ISD citation classics, software-based con<sup>fi</sup>guration such as the early expert system XCON (Sviokla 1990) is more about task con<sup>fi</sup>guration than software con<sup>fi</sup>guration. The other under-represented category in ISD Management is Quality Assurance. This category is related to assuring the quality of the product of ISD rather than ensuring project quality, which is already addressed in Performance Management. The SE tradition considers Software Quality as an important topic that deserves its own knowledge area. Quality culture, veri<sup>fi</sup>cation and validation, testing, reviews and audits and software quality processes such as the Capability Maturity Model (Herbsleb et al. 1997; Humphrey 1989) feature prominently. Although the evaluation of system quality after development has a long tradition in IS (Delone and McLean 1992; Delone and McLean 2003), less attention is spent on managing, controlling and improving systems quality during development. Also, a search for highly cited IS articles outside the basket of eight journals did not <sup>fi</sup>nd majo Quality Assurance studies.

## Review of ISD performance knowledge

The third dimension captures the product-oriented work of ISD Performance, directly related to the production of IS artefacts. This dimension consists of 13 knowledge areas (Table 5), mostly associated with the development life cycle from establishing requirements for applica tions, aligning new systems to organizational needs, designing business processes and providing application support, all the way to getting applications evaluated and accepted. ISD Performance receives by far the most attention with a total of 393 citation classics. The citation classics in Acceptance and Evaluation make up more than half of all citation classics (56%) demonstrating that research in these two knowledge areas have accumulated a long tradition. The popularity of Acceptance and Evaluation could be attributed to the nature of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld that straddles both the social and the technical dimensions of technology. From the remaining eleven knowledge areas, four knowledge areas – Implementation, Organizational Alignment, Software and Systems Design and Process Design – demonstrate evidence of cumulative tradition, together taking up nearly the other half of the research in ISD Performance. The status of the remaining seven knowledge areas is not as de<sup>fi</sup>nitive. The following sections offer a detailed integrative review of the ISD Performance knowledge areas.

## Acceptance, evaluation, quality assurance and testing

The ten most cited ISD Performance articles include <sup>fi</sup>ve TAM-related studies investigating the acceptance and adoption of information systems. This is consistent with <sup>fi</sup>ndings from Fitzgerald (2008) that two TAM articles accounted for 30% of the impact factor score for MIS Quarterly in 2005, and the overcrowding of research space in IS by TAM-related arti cles (Benbasat & Barki, 2007). Moreover, the 10 most cited ISD Performance articles include three from the Evaluation area. Not only does Acceptance re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the quality o the ISD, the degree by which the artefact is accepted (or rejected) will have serious consequences for ISD. It re<sup>fl</sup>ects the concern of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld for whether or not users <sup>fi</sup>nd the system useful, a concern that is not necessarily shared by other computer-related <sup>fi</sup>elds. This concern makes the knowledge areas of Acceptance and Use unique among the allied computing <sup>fi</sup>elds (Table 5).

The uniqueness of Acceptance and Evaluation can be seen by comparing with the concept of acceptance in the SE tradition that takes the form of acceptance tests as part of the Requirements Validation and Software Testing knowledge areas in SWEBOK. Although both IS and SE evaluate user perceptions of system quality, Acceptance and Evaluation in IS focus on quality after the system is delivered. The concept of evaluation in the SE tradition is more generically applied in two major knowledge areas in SWEBOK, Software Engineering Management and Software Quality Management, concerned with quality before delivery. For example, in Software Engineering Management, evaluation focuses on determining the satisfaction of requirements for project milestones, while in Software Quality Management, evaluation is an intricate part of assuring the quality of the development process as well as its product. To these ends, various management processes in the SE tradition such as veri<sup>fi</sup>cation and validation, reviews and audits, inspections and walkthroughs all evaluate both development process and product.

The apparently disparate concepts Acceptance, Evaluation, Quality Assurance and Testing are really cognate concepts that can be found within the SE tradition. For example, usability and reliability are both part of the Software Quality knowledge area in SWEBOK (Bourque & Fairley, 2014) and DeLone and McLean’s (2003) Model of IS Success. Testing is the Performance process undertaken to ensure both usability and reliability. Therefore, even though ISD does not refer to testing using SE concepts such as ‘software testing’, ‘inspections’, ‘audits’ and ‘walkthroughs’, strong evidence from SE strengthens the argument that Testing is also a canonical knowledge area in IS. albeit conceptualized differently within IS. Outside the ISD citation classics, experts in SE (Cohen et al., 2004; Weinberg, 2008) attest to the psychological and economic issues related to testing, and classic texts on software testing describe many human-intensive testing techniques (e.g. inspection and walkthroughs), which makes these techniques as much of art as they are sciences (Myers et al., 2012; Marick, 1995). Recent articles published in top IS journals focus on business concerns surrounding testing, such as the length of the testing period and the timing o software releases, providing evidence for the potential of this new body of theory (Jiang et al., 2012; Onita & Dhaliwal, 2011). Hence, despite the low number of citation classics in both Testing in ISD Performance and Quality Assurance in ISD Management, these studies and the concerns surrounding the complex managerial and social challenges of the testing and quality environment suggest that Testing and Quality Assurance deserve their own canonical knowledge areas.

## Organizational Alignment, Requirements Construction and Architecture

livari et al. (2004) suggested four ISD Performance areas would contain the most distinctive content: (1) Organizational Alignment, (2) Requirements Construction, (3) IS Implementation and Acceptance and (4) IS Evaluation. According to Barki et al. (1988; 1993), Organizational Alignment is a subset of IS planning. However, the coding suggests Organizational Alignment contains elements of design, not just planning as suggested by previous IS research. Following

Iivari et al. (2004), Organizational Alignment is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as re<sup>fl</sup>ecting ‘the “<sup>fi</sup>t” of an IT artefact, an information system or a software product, with the organizational and social context of its use rather than the “<sup>fi</sup>t” between IS plans and organizational objectives’ (p. 323). Therefore, for the ISDBOK, Organizational Alignment is conceptualized into two knowledge areas: (1) planning of any and all types of resources, commonly called ‘infrastructure’ and also (2) design, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, architectural issues of the IT artefact. Concerns for infrastructure in ISD is distinct from concerns for software infrastructure in SE. ISD infrastructure (e.g. hard infrastructures in the form of centralization and decentralization of hardware and software and soft infrastructures in the form of data, information and human skills) is about how value or <sup>fl</sup>exibility afforded to the organization (Byrd and Turner 2000; Zhu 2004) whereas SWEBOK focuses on software process infrastructures. More distinctively, in ISD, the concept of information infrastructure (Gosain et al., 2004; Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010) offers a unique view on managing and designing inter-organizational systems. Outside of the basket of eight journals, the highly cited works of Ciborra and colleagues on the global information infrastructure (Ciborra, 2000) and local information infrastructures (Hanseth & Monteiro, 1997) offer new design theories on how technology evolves and how standards are institutionalized.

In SWEBOK, architecture refers to computer (hardware and software) architecture and architectural design (architectural styles, object-oriented organization and design patterns), whereas architecture in ISD refers to information systems that combine structural <sup>fi</sup>rmness (form), utility (function) and representation (aesthetics) following the metaphor suggested by Lee (1991). Architecture is in this sense largely ignored in SWEBOK (Alexander, 1999) and provides a distinctive area for potential IS contributions. This proposed breakdown of knowledge areas opens up IS research to new possibilities. Architecture has not received as much attention in ISD and, if interpreted as ‘design’ in the traditional sense of ‘architectural design’, promises to be a fecund area of future research. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with Lee’s (2010; 1991) assertion that the architectural discipline holds much promise as a foundation of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Requirements Construction is an example of a critical ISD knowledge area that is underresearched. King (2013) also claims the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has distinctive content when it comes to Requirements Construction, but the coding process <sup>fi</sup>nds Requirements Construction makes up a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly smaller proportion (4%) of ISD Performance. Although there are numerous studies outside the citation classics, very few distinguish Requirements Construction from the perspective of IS from that of requirements engineering in SE. Unlike its counterpart in SE, Requirements Construction assumes a close relationship between the developed system and its speci<sup>fi</sup>c social context (Davidson 2002), as well as a need to emphasize application domain knowledge (Schenk et al.1998; Vessey and Conger 1993) and business rules (Wand and Weber 1995). ISD is especially cognizant of the many communication gaps and obstacles between users and developers (Montazemi and Conrath 1986; Byrd et al. 1992) and of the different ways stakeholders interpret and frame existing and new technologies (Davidson 2002; Watson and Florick 1993). Highly cited works outside Senior Scholars’ Basket of Journals (Curtis et al., 1988; Davis, 1982; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994) legitimize Requirements Construction as a critical research area in ISD and speak to the need for more research.

## Modelling and Design

The topic of design experienced a resurgence in IS as a result of efforts in design theoriz ing (Gregor and Jones 2007; Hevner et al. 2004). However, the paucity of articles in this area (March & Storey, 2008) and critiques concerning its legitimacy as a separate research paradigm (Lee et al., 2012) raise questions about the canonical status of design in IS. The results from this study offer insights into how these recent developments relate to existing ISD knowledge. What areas of IS design or design science have reached canonical status or are at least emerging as such? Iivari et al. (2004) found only three software design articles in MIS Quarterly and Information Systems Journal published between 1996 and 2000 and equally few articles that are relevant to software design-related topics such as user interface design (eight articles), design of user support systems (<sup>fi</sup>ve articles) and desian of system controls and monitors (three articles). We found 19 citatior classics on software design using broader search terms such as ‘design science’, ‘software design’, ‘design theory’, ‘design methods’ and ‘systems design’. The results capture the different perspectives of design ranging from the design of group decision support (Huber 1984), the organizational processes related to designing systems (Robey and Markus 1984), design of advanced knowledge-based systems (Byrd et al. 1992; Markus et al. 2002) to several emerging citation classics in design science (Arnott 2006; Peffers et al. 2007). The body of theory for this knowledge area is also eclectic and can be divided into three major groups: (1) the design science sub-knowledge area that is inspired by Simon’s (1981) design science paradigm, (2) cognitive psychology theories that support user interface design and usability and (3) various organizational theories ranging from organizational politics, con<sup>fl</sup>ict and resistance, power and control, media richness and task technology <sup>fi</sup>t. Four citation classics in this category received more than 100 citations each, whereas nine of the remaining citation classics received 50 or less citations, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the topic’s relatively modest progress. This level of conceptual development and the number of citation classics in the design sciences re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the level of research activity justify a category for Software and Systems Design. However, the modest progress shown in this knowledge area suggests it needs the close attention of IS scholars.

## Implementation, Training and Use

Our coding of the ISD citation classics highlighted Implementation as a life-cycle phase intricately connected to the social domain of users and customers. Because of this important relationship, Implementation is one of the most researched areas in ISD with 41 cita tion classics and with numerous forms of theory related to the dif<sup>fi</sup>cult task of implementing a technological artefact within a social environment. Similar to Acceptance and Evaluation, the Implementation knowledge area is also focused on concepts that straddle the social and the technical, and this explains why it is a well-researched area. Not surprisingly, the theories applied in Implementation re<sup>fl</sup>ect critical sociotechnical concerns including communication, change management, innovation, participation, institutionalization, motivation, marketing, organizational culture, politics, diffusion of technology and assimilation.

The coding for Implementation identi<sup>fi</sup>ed Training as an emerging knowledge area that is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly addressed in SWEBOK or other development-related works. In the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme of Barki et al. (1988; 1993), it is included as part of Implementation. However, our coding of Implementation and Training identi<sup>fi</sup>ed only one would - be citation classic (Sharma and Yetton 2007) that overlapped the two areas suggesting that these are two separate knowledge areas. Social cognitive theory and various forms of learning theories, not found in Implementation research, inform the body of theory of Training. Altogether, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings justify Training as a separate knowledge area.

Another knowledge area closely related to Implementation and Training is Use, a well-known topic in ISD either as a dependent variable in IS evaluation (Delone and McLean 1992), a measure of acceptance (Davis 1989) or as the integral part of any number of other core IS concerns. Despite the long history of research in Use, it has received little theoretical scrutiny and has been operationalized by varied means (Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006). A systematic understanding of Use is critical for ISD because a system that is not used suggests a weakness in its design, development, implementation or training. The coding process for Use found 22 ISD citation classics, suggesting that this topic is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant with articles on re-conceptualizing usage (Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006), changes of behaviour and attitudes during use (Bhattacherjee and Premkumar 2004), adaptation in use (Majchrzak et al. 2000) and appropriation of technology (Dennis et al. 2001). In the SE tradition, Use does not appear to play any role, and Training takes a minor support role under Software Engineering Management. Therefore, in our ISD disciplinary knowledge map, both Training and Use contribute to the distinctiveness of ISD.

## DISCUSSION AND WAY FORWARD

Much like the experience of the SE community, the ISD community has only just begun to address its foundations, generally accepted core theories and maior principles that can be saic to be truly IS speci<sup>fi</sup>c. The realization that SWEBOK and other allied bodies of knowledge are insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient for IS experts needs a de<sup>fi</sup>nitive response from the ISD community. Practitioner efforts towards professionalization are not being supported by academic circles. Only a system of disciplinary ISD knowledge that is coherent and credible and that can bridge the academic and practitioner community will be able to satisfactorily address these concerns. To build this bridge, the ISD community needs to be able to de<sup>fi</sup>ne in its own terms what ISD means and how it will contribute to other related efforts including SE and Project Management.

Beginning with our de<sup>fi</sup>nition, we characterize ISD not just as a technical activity, but as an integrated social and technical system of knowledge with multiple dimensions consisting of product-related (Performance dimension), process-related (Management dimension) and goal-related (Framework dimension) knowledge areas. By ‘conceptualizing and realizing technology-based systems’, ISD addresses product-related goals. By ‘managing associated changes and implications’, ISD emphasizes the need for process-related management practices. By accomplishing ‘speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals in organizational contexts’, ISD incorporates belief systems, goals, approaches and the methods and tools embedded in the organization. These knowledge areas are part of the integrative body of professional knowledge that has a continuously reinforcing relationship with practice.

This effort in distilling ISD knowledge is the <sup>fi</sup>rst step in a long journey towards consolidation. Are we satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with our current state of ISD knowledge such that we can convene a productive meeting with practitioners and clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ne and state ISD’s core concepts? For instance, on the subject of system Testing, what will be our response to practitioners’ questions concerning what we mean by Testing? Will we have to rely on SWEBOK’s de<sup>fi</sup>nitions on software testing for systems testing? Do we as a community agree on what is meant by Testing? What do we agree about the Capability Maturity Model? What forms of testing are considered canonical in ISD? Al of these questions will be answered by a BOK that receives the agreement of the ISD community.

Because ISD itself de<sup>fi</sup>nes half of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, focusing on this effort is not an option the <sup>fi</sup>eld can take lightly. The disciplinary system of ISD knowledge <sup>fi</sup>rst needs to be organized, either of<sup>fi</sup>cially as a formal BOK or unof<sup>fi</sup>cially as a knowledge resource that is accessible to the whole IS community. The community then needs to agree on what is considered canonical and what is not. Fruitful conversations can then take place between academic circles and practitioner efforts in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. These conversations will further shape and enhance the BOK, and the virtuous cycle continues as the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld builds bridges that mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>t both communities.

## CONCLUSION

Our study demonstrates the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a simple, effective and systematic way of distilling disciplinary ISD knowledge from its complex and fragmented corpus as a means towards consolidating the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Given the fragmented nature of the IS literature, this method allows researchers to navigate effectively amongst numerous journals and different academic disciplines to locate relevant articles, key topics, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant concepts and theories. As a result, the study provides evidence for cumulative tradition and uncovers our <sup>fi</sup>eld’s distinctive theoretical contributions as a bridge to practical knowledge. The study uncovers the core concepts and theories that de<sup>fi</sup>ne ISD disciplinary knowledge, but have remained largely hidden since the inception of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Similarly, practitioners will be able to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from available knowledge from classical studies and also critique contemporary trends in knowledge accumulation that leave gaps open in important areas of industrial practice. Such efforts will go a long way in consolidating the fragmented intellectual structures in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, enhancing the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s legitimacy and providing a solid foundation for advancing both research and practice.
