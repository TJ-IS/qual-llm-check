## A B S T R A C T

IT outsourcing is one of the most prevalent trends in business today. Yet traditional outsourcing models force tradeoffs by both the enterprise and its outsourcer. The former often compromise operational flexibility and innovation while the latter price deals to win, resulting in razor-thin margins and aggressive cost reduction. Strategic Out-Tasking can be seen as a ‘‘win–win’’ model that breaks the traditional, vicious cycle of negative compromise. Many visionary enterprises and leading outsourcers are embracing the guiding principles of a Strategic Out-Tasking model. From an operational perspective, this helps drive greater innovation within enterprises, yields higher outsourcer margins, and, more importantly, reduces partnership risk. We believe that Strategic Out-Tasking can, over the next few years, deliver \$16 to \$23 billion in industry bottom-line savings that enterprises and outsourcers can share.

## 1. Introduction

IT outsourcing is estimated as a \$233 billion global industry with an impressive 8% annual growth rate [13]. In a recent Gartner survey of financial services companies, roughly 60% of respondents reported high or moderate levels of IT outsourcing [14]. According to this survey, the next 2 years will see these IT outsourcing levels increase to nearly 80%.

## 1.1. Compelling motivations to outsource

Enterprises have several strong business drivers for outsourcing IT. Nearly 75% of respondents to a recent survey of IT executives (from companies with annual revenues greater than \$1 billion) identified cost reduction as their primary reason [8]. As shown in Fig. 1, the Gartner survey also confirmed cost takeout as the top driver for outsourcing. Through economies of scale and incremental efficiencies in services delivery, outsourcers can gain cost reductions of 15% or more [23]. Not surprisingly, average contract terms have increased to nearly 6 years in duration, allowing outsourcers sufficient time to recover up-front personnel and system investments needed to realize promised cost reductions [15].

## 1.2. The current model has flaws

With the current outsourcing model, enterprises are forced to compromise operational flexibility and innovation. In today’s marketplace, enterprises continually need to adapt to shifts in customer preference and market realities. Yet the common trap is for them to award outsourcing contracts primarily on cost. Later, a few years into their outsourcing relationship, the enterprise realizes that the singular focus on cost savings often penalizes innovation and business flexibility. Fig. 2 shows the results of a Morgan Chambers survey that found that, while innovation ranked first in enterprise needs, it ranked last in what outsourcers delivered [12]. This loss of innovation can significantly compromise the ability of an enterprise to respond rapidly to changing business conditions.

To illustrate the problem, let us imagine an enterprise that, in the late nineties, was locked into a long-term IT outsourcing contract. Such a traditional, long-term contract would probably have restricted the deployment of emerging, Web-based technologies for operational transformation. Delays in adopting such a disruptive innovation would clearly result in a severe competitive disadvantage and perhaps even risk the survival of the firm.

Yet even today we see examples of restrictive contracts. A Forrester Research survey confirmed that nearly 70% of enterprise respondents claimed IT outsourcers often failed to meet expectations on innovation (see Fig. 3) [17]. One of Cisco’s leading manufacturing customers, for example, faced a significant price premium when it wished to migrate to a next-generation converged IP telephony infrastructure. The expensive change request forced lengthy contract renegotiations, delaying the much needed technology deployment.

## 1.3. The need for a new model

Strategic Out-Tasking (also termed out-tasking here) is a promising win–win model that breaks the cycle of negative compromises associated with traditional outsourcing. In describing this new model, we draw extensively from Cisco’s experience. In addition, the concepts have been validated by more than 50 of our top enterprise customers, many of whom are among the largest global companies. These validations took different forms, ranging from structured CXO conversations to 3 to 6 month intense engagements that helped some of the leading practitioners develop their own out-tasking strategies. And while the details of almost all of our out-tasking engagements are confidential, we were successful in capturing a few of the more innovative outcomes as high level case studies [3–5].

We worked closely with many of the top global outsourcers to help develop their own managed services portfolios and strategies [6]. In addition, we partnered with outsourcers to help implement processes, such as acquisition integration and governance, which are critical for the successful execution of their services [7].

## 2. Survey of existing research

IT outsourcing has attracted significant interest. Gonzalez et al. performed a detailed analysis of the current body of research publications [16]. This revealed some interesting trends:

\- More recent research has gravitated towards empirical work. Since 2001 there were twice as many publications using empirical rather than theoretical research. As a baseline, this ratio of empirical to theoretical publications was 1:1 before 1995. Consistent with this empirical focus, about one third of the recent studies were field based.

\- The research focus had also transformed from being enterpriseoriented (more than 70% of pre-1995 publications) to a balance between enterprise and outsourcer interests. Since 2001, the percentage of primarily enterprise-oriented publications has dropped to 30%. Importantly, a significant number of recent publications focused on the overall relationship.

Our work on Strategic Out-Tasking parallels these trends. The origins of our work were primarily experiential. We drew on Cisco’s experience with Strategic Out-Tasking as well as our personal experience in helping many leading enterprises and outsourcers succeed with their own out-tasking. And like many recent publications, our work primarily focused on the success of the overall partnership. The Strategic Out-Tasking model fundamentally ensured a winning partnership with success for both the enterprise and its outsourcer.

We used the framework for outsourcing provided by Dibbern et al. [10] to highlight our contributions to overall research on outsourcing. They viewed outsourcing as an organizational decision process and adapted Simon’s stage model of decision making. This decision making model focused on five key questions.

## 2.1. Question 1: why outsource?

Our direct customer work (and third party industry data) indicated that most leading companies had a good understanding of their expectations from IT outsourcing as well as its inherent risks. Many leaders view IT outsourcing as strategic to their business and recognize that its benefits extend beyond cost. DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani [11], for example, provided a comprehensive framework of three categories in which to understand a company’s strategic intent to outsource:

1. IS improvements that range from productivity improvements to transformation of the IT organization,

2. Business impact that varies from better business IT alignment to the execution of IT-intensive business processes, and

3. Commercial exploitation that ranges from targeting the external sale of existing IT assets to establishing new IT-based businesses.

They argue that companies may pursue one or more of these strategic intents. In their view, traditional outsourcing partnerships fail because companies do not properly align the structure of their outsourcing relationships (performance measures, pricing, incentives, communications and change management) with the strategic intents being pursued.

Barthelemy [2] further refined this hypothesis by an in-depth analysis of 91 outsourcing cases, nearly two-thirds of which related to the outsourcing of information and communications technologies. He found that 37% of the cases resulted in dissatisfied managers in the outsourcing enterprise. They rated their satisfaction with the outsourcing relationship as 3 or below on a 5 point Likert scale, with 5 being ‘‘totally satisfied’’ and 1 being ‘‘not satisfied at all’’. The root cause analyses yielded seven ‘‘deadly sins of outsourcing,’’ with ‘‘writing a poor contract,’’ ‘‘outsourcing activities that should not be outsourced,’’ and ‘‘failing to plan an exit strategy’’ as the top three.

## 2.2. Question 2: what should be outsourced?

This focuses on understanding the outsourcing alternatives. A number of papers have addressed different models. Linder [19], for example, proposed ‘‘Transformational Outsourcing’’ as a model to help companies design the right strategy. Leveraging this model, companies like TiVo could use outsourcing to rapidly scale up their new businesses. And underperforming agencies, such as National Savings & Investments (NS&I), have successfully used outsourcing to redirect their performance trajectories.

We extended the partnership design question in three important ways. First, we proposed a holistic approach that considered the perspective of the outsourcer when defining the success of the out-tasking partnership. We firmly believed that any partnership model that did not result in a win–win outcome would not be sustainable over the long term. Second, we broke away from a function-oriented view of the IT organization to a model that was more services-oriented. With this view, enterprises could focus their out-tasking decisions on services that were mature in their internal implementation and less differentiated in the marketplace. Third, we recommended that outsourcers also focused on delivering services in which they were ‘‘best-of-breed.’’ We believed that such an approach would help outsourcers realize better margins and reduce some of their operational risk.

## 2.3. Question 3: which to outsource?

We believed that our mode of identifying specific processes to out-task was our major contribution. We recommended that enterprises retain in-house key service creation and delivery processes that are critical in realizing their desired business returns. We adapted the core/context framework proposed by Moore [20] to help companies select specific processes along a service lifecycle that they should retain versus those that may be candidates to out-task. We expect to present details of our methodology in a future paper.

Best-of-breed strategies will probably result in narrower service contracts. But, as enterprises move towards out-tasking models, best-of-breed outsourcers are more likely to win large global deals for a targeted set of services. And as tightly scoped contracts often result in higher operating margins, we believe that outsourcers can expect to secure better financial returns from pursuing such an approach.

## 2.4. Question 4: how to outsource?

A number of papers have focused on this stage of outsourcing. Dibbern et al. organized much of these into three activities: (i) selecting one or more vendors, (ii) building and structuring outsourcing relationships, and (iii) managing the resulting partnership.

Our contribution in this area results from our experience—in implementing Strategic Out-Tasking concepts within Cisco and in testing and validating these concepts with many of our top customers. Our win–win mindset required us to consider new outsourcing models (such as ‘‘time-to-value’’) that are more aligned with the innovation focus that most enterprises expect.

## 2.5. Question 5: what are the outcomes?

Previous efforts have focused on estimating the success of outsourcing relationships [21]. We however believe that there is a gap in current methods: they directly quantify outcomes from proposed outsourcing strategies.

We proposed a simplistic estimate of the financial impact. We arrived at this impact of \$16 billion to \$23 billion in two ways:

From an enterprise perspective, we built our industry estimates by extrapolating a few customer-specific analyses for which we were able to quantify the reduction in operational risks and the incremental investments needed when using the out-tasking approach. We then validated this estimate from an outsourcer perspective. An examination of company financials indicated that best-of-breed outsourcers were able to realize significantly higher operating margins than traditional ones.

## 3. Strategic Out-Tasking overview

At its heart, the Strategic Out-Tasking model requires enterprises to retain ownership of all operations. This operational accountability forces enterprises to invest appropriately in accelerating innovations and improving business flexibility in their out-tasking partnerships. The model requires outsourcers to partner aggressively with enterprises to translate their business requirements into industry-accepted service-level agreements (SLAs) and then deliver them through a set of discrete managed services. By offering innovative services through standards-based delivery platforms, outsourcers can achieve better economies of scale and minimize their operational risk. A greater focus on innovation also helps them differentiate themselves from competitors while building a foundation for sustainable, long-term partnerships with customers.

Strategic Out-Tasking offers a common framework and vocabulary for creating and deploying IT services. By adhering to industry standards, it enables the rapid integration of new services, many of which may be unplanned or unforeseen. On the other hand, traditional outsourcing approaches are optimized to meet a present day need, challenging enterprises to modify their operating models and systems so they can realize the promised functionality. Interoperability and future extensibility of traditional outsourcing can thus be difficult to execute.

## 3.1. Investing in discrete managed services

The Strategic Out-Tasking model views IT as a collection of discrete but interoperable services in which an enterprise can vary service levels to respond rapidly to changes in customer and market conditions. The enterprise remains accountable for business returns from services even after they have been outtasked. However, this accountability has implications:

\- Enterprises must retain processes that are critical in defining business requirements and creating new services, even though the service delivery capabilities are fulfilled by outsourcers. Further, the out-tasking model assumes that enterprises will continue to maintain their operational visibility and influence over out-tasked operations.

\- Enterprises are more able to select best-of-breed providers for individual services. Such a best-of-breed approach does however put considerable emphasis on process and data integration among out-tasked and in-house services. While the outsourcer or other third-party systems integrators may perform the actual integration, the enterprise IT team must retain overall responsibility for the integration.

Outsourcers also need to make strategic choices to succeed. They must build a portfolio of service offerings in which they are best-of-breed. A best-of-breed portfolio involves less execution risk and can yield significant financial benefits including:

\- Higher margins from differentiated expertise and delivery competencies needed for these focused service offerings.

\- New revenue streams from global contracts, especially with large multinationals. Such global deployments should offset any revenue loss from the contracts now being more tightly scoped.

\- Reduced up-front deployment costs and ongoing operational expenses by adhering to industry standards.

Outsourcers can, in turn, share some of these financial benefits with their enterprise customers, further cementing a win–win partnership.

## 3.2. Investing in superior partnerships

With the out-tasking model, enterprises are likely to have several outsourcer partnerships, each of which requires greater process coordination and increased access to enterprise data. Enterprises should take an architectural approach when describing the vision and roadmap for relevant business processes and deploying the necessary technology infrastructure. Such architectures should be viewed as ‘‘living blueprints’’ that reflect changing business needs and the emergence of new, innovative solutions. Importantly, this architectural approach minimizes risks to intellectual property, customer privacy, and other sensitive information.

While enterprises own their architectural blueprints, outsourcers can contribute. Leading outsourcers are investing in standards-based service delivery platforms that help them scale existing services efficiently as well as provision new services rapidly. Such global delivery platforms also facilitate easier integration with other standards-based enterprise services, including those delivered by third-party providers.

Executive sponsorship and governance are critical for the success of a partnership. Many companies have consolidated their partner management skills into shared centers of excellence to identify and resolve any outstanding issues.

## 3.3. Investing in innovation

By out-tasking operational transactions, companies can free resources and focus on higher-value interactions, such as improving customer experience and accelerating the adoption of new innovations. For example, since out-tasking many routine operations, the CIO of a major U.S. university hospital and her IT team were able to partner with hospital functions in using imaging and wireless applications in transforming patient care and clinical processes. Such business collaboration has been critical in repositioning IT as strategic to the hospital’s goal of enhancing its industry position.

A rewarding out-tasking partnership requires outsourcers to introduce new technology and business solutions proactively. For example, a retailer of consumer packaged goods is likely to value an outsourcer who can help integrate RFID solutions in its supply chain, thereby accelerating time to replenishment and reducing sales-cycle times.

## 3.4. Returns are worth the investment

From an enterprise perspective, our customer work revealed that companies anticipated incremental benefits of 7–10% from pursuing our approach. A North American public sector customer, for example, estimated

1. A \$10 to \$15 million reduction in operational risks from using the out-tasking approach for its \$100 million network partnership contract. This reduction was primarily made in two ways:

(a) The agency estimated a \$3 to \$5 million reduction in the risks due to not realizing projected operational cost reductions.

Outsourcing contracts on average targeted operational cost reductions of 15% or more. But many industry analyses revealed that over a third of the outsourcing contracts failed to realize some or all of the projected cost reductions. Strategic Out-Tasking could help mitigate some or all of the risks and achieve the projected cost reductions.

(b) The agency also conservatively estimated a further \$7 to \$10 million reduction in the risk of not achieving associated architectural synergies and driving new innovations. Strategic Out-Tasking recommends adhering to architectural standards; they can reduce the total cost of ownership by as much as 25% [22]. We know that nearly 70% of IT outsourcing deals fail to meet at least some of their target, with nearly 20% of them completely missing their goals. The out-tasking model can mitigate some of these risks as it requires enterprises to make the financial and people investment needed to drive innovation over the lifetime of the contract.

2. An additional investment of \$3 to \$5 million was, however, needed to switch from traditional outsourcing to our approach. This incremental investment included:

(a) Additional full time equivalent resources that the enterprise now needed to retain in-house in order to remain accountable for business outcomes.

(b) Costs associated with 10–15% of service delivery operations that the agency continued to operate in order to retain its operational competencies.

(c) Incremental cost of management overhead and IT systems that were needed to support the enhanced governance and to change management processes.

Extrapolating this 7–10 percentage point increase in benefits across the \$233 billion global IT outsourcing industry, we estimated \$16 billion to \$23 billion in additional industry benefits from adopting our approach.

Viewed from the perspective of outsourcers, we validated this 7–10 percentage point increase in industry benefits. Traditional IT outsourcers currently operated on net margins of 5–10% [1]. Specialized business process providers, however, routinely realized much higher net margins (in the 12–20% range [9]). Strategic Out-Tasking can help outsourcers rise to these higher operating margins, resulting in an estimated of \$16 billion to \$23 billion in additional benefits.

In summary, we believe that Strategic Out-Tasking can result in tangible benefits that can be shared by enterprises and outsourcers. Our estimate of incremental returns was only meant to be illustrative. Increased industry experience would allow us to determine a better estimate.

Enterprises should own the processes, data, and management capabilities to identify and deploy new, innovative services; actively manage the delivery of services for operational accountability; and accelerate service lifecycles.

## 4.2. Manage the delivery of services

Internal IT organizations should actively monitor and influence operations even after they are out-tasked. Best practices in partner management include:

\- Mechanisms to track business returns and proactively optimize operations: Such visibility gives enterprises the ability to locate and address any negative performance issues or unmet customer demands. A real-time view of customer satisfaction scores can, for example, prompt an enterprise to work with its partners to identify and resolve any performance issues. As a best practice example, Cisco maintains an integrated call queue for all its frontline customer support centers, both in-house and outtasked. Such an integrated call queue allows Cisco to shift calls away from underperforming centers to ones that provide higher satisfaction scores.

\- Partner incentives that accelerate desired returns: Contractual penalties often fail to compensate for the negative business impact from poor partner performance. Enterprises are better served by investing in their partner success. For example, incentives, such as bonus payouts, should reward partners for exceeding performance targets. Leading practitioners often provide tools and training for partner agents so they can better answer customer requests. Interestingly, such targeted investments directly impact partner performance and consequently allow them to realize their bonus payouts, creating a positively reinforcing relationship.

\- Retention of 10–15% of operations in-house: By retaining a fraction of out-tasked operations, enterprises maintain operational experience to collaborate more effectively with outsourcers in improving performance. In addition, enterprises are better positioned to structure future contracts. In extreme cases, the retained operational experience allows the enterprise to bring operations back in-house if it becomes necessary.

Management of out-tasked services should be a priority. Ideally, the CIO would be the corporate sponsor and provide executive guidance that complements day-to-day supervision provided by the in-house team. Successful out-tasking requires next-generation relationship management competencies. Enterprises need to nurture a talent pool with effective partner management skills and a deep understanding of the IT services industry economics. Enterprises often choose to consolidate their partner management resources into shared competency centers that help drive best-in-class practices throughout their organi zation. These practices could include programs to pre-qualify partners, negotiate win–win contracts, maintain executive relationships, and evolve partnerships to support changing needs.

## 4.3. Accelerate the services lifecycle

Enterprises should view out-tasking as a scaling strategy for most IT services. In the Strategic Out-Tasking model, enterprises accelerate new services through their maturity lifecycle with outtasking as a possible end state. To do so, enterprises should:

\- Engage outsourcers early in the service lifecycle to ensure scaling readiness: This is similar to the design-to-production practice perfected by Japanese automakers in the late eighties.

\- Provide outsourcers with standards-based interfaces for their architectural blueprints: By so doing, they can better orchestrate linkages among different internal and out-tasked services, facilitate the integration of new services, and mitigate potential security threats attempting to pass through the various partner interconnections. The use of offshore providers and remote delivery centers further accentuates the need for holistic enterprise architectures.

\- Own an innovation agenda and require partners to contribute: While enterprises own the creation of new services, outsourcers should offer access to their own research investments and relevant innovation insights. Innovation should be a critical factor in selecting partners, and out-tasking contracts should offer incentives for partners to introduce innovations and drive their adoption.

Strategic Out-Tasking as an eventual scaling strategy does result in challenges. Enterprises sometimes struggle to scale successful in-house pilots through out-tasked operations. This is especially true for disruptive innovations that potentially undermine existing out-tasking arrangements. One of our enterprise customers was, for example, looking to engage specialized thirdparty systems integrators to help deploy converged VoIP infrastructure technologies within its out-tasked operations. Several open issues remained, however. For instance, it was unclear what role the primary outsourcer would play in operating the new infrastructure after initial deployment had been completed.

## 5. Outsourcer priorities to support Strategic Out-Tasking

Outsourcers need to adapt their business and operating models to succeed with our model. Specifically, an outsourcer needs to create a portfolio of discrete managed services, deploy time-tovalue sales models, and invest in superior integration with other enterprise and third-party services.

## 5.1. Create a portfolio of managed services

Outsourcers must consider their offerings as a set of discrete but modular services that can be offered either standalone or bundled into a more comprehensive solution. The performance of these services can be described in terms of industry-accepted service levels. For seamless integration with other enterprise or thirdparty services, these modular service offerings must have welldefined interfaces for process and data interconnection, documented operational dependencies, and computational engines to execute business rules.

In order to create and deploy such modular services rapidly, outsourcers need to manage their offerings as a portfolio. Fig. 7 shows a three-phase services lifecycle framework to help outsourcers create their own service portfolios, including those that are ready for market deployment. This framework suggests that outsourcers should evolve their focus over the lifecycle of the service.

\- Early in the service development, outsourcers should focus on creating innovative services, often in partnership with other technology providers.

\- As a service matures, outsourcers can focus on standardizing the service definition and creating an ecosystem with other complementary technology providers and systems integrators to provide a complete solution.

\- For well adopted and mature services, outsourcers can leverage scalable delivery models to achieve operational efficiencies. During this phase, outsourcers are best served by building tightly coupled alliances with other industry leaders to create and deliver standards-based solutions.

We recommend that outsourcers maintain a portfolio of services with specific offerings at each of the different phases of the service lifecycle. Fig. 8 shows such a portfolio of network services that is being used by one of our outsourcer customers.

## 5.2. Deploy time-to-value sales models

For outsourcers to realize the expected returns from a Strategic Out-Tasking partnership, they need to move from traditional costplus pricing models to others focused on time-to-value. Our experience with leading IT outsourcers has provided three key goto-market initiatives that help outsourcers in executing a time-tovalue model.

1. Focus on business processes: At its heart, this focus is about helping enterprise customers succeed in their marketplace. Many outsourcers have business process competencies within their management consultancy practice and business process outsourcing operations (BPO). But often they struggle when linking traditional IT services with business consulting teams. Traditional selling models may need to be modified. Typically, distinct IT services and BPO teams can pursue a comprehensive approach in understanding the customer context and identifying different ways to expand existing contracts profitably. For example, instead of a singular focus on call-center operations, an outsourcer’s BPO team could require its IT services team to transform the overall customer experience.

2. Build innovation into the partnership from the start: Out-tasking contracts should, as a rule, include incentives for introducing new innovations and accelerating their adoption. Technology lab visits and performance benchmarking workshops can provide ways for outsourcers to introduce new technologies and practices into existing contracts. A joint innovation agenda could help outsourcers track and report their contributions in exploiting innovations for their customers.

3. Expand the wallet share of existing customers: Given the prevalence of IT outsourcing, outsourcers will need to increasingly focus on expanding existing contracts to grow revenue. For the calendar year 2007, for instance, we estimated that about 25% of the \$927 billion in worldwide IT spend would be offered

## 5.3. Invest in superior integration with enterprise and third-party services

By selecting best-of-breed services, enterprises typically expand the number of their out-tasking partnerships. Easy and flexible integration is then likely to be a competitive differentiator for outsourcers. This is especially true for disruptive services for which the required application functionality and integration requirements may be hard to specify at the start. For example, ‘‘storage-on-demand’’ services that currently integrate with e-mail hosting and archiving applications may, in future, be required to store and archive Internet chat and Web-conferencing sessions. And these new requirements will probably come with different security and retention policies.

Winning outsourcers must integrate both within their own services portfolio and also with other enterprise and third-party services. Some approaches for easier integration include:

\- Deploying sophisticated infrastructure and application management tools which can help outsourcers better orchestrate enterprise processes and integrate information among other ecosystem partners.

\- Pre-integrating services with other industry-leading offerings which can help mitigate common implementation risks and increase the outsourcer’s chance of winning partnership contracts.

\- Building leveraged service delivery platforms which allow outsourcers to deploy and integrate their service offerings rapidly. Ideally, the delivery architecture is the same for both external customer services and internal development and collaboration. These integration capabilities often benefit from a strong internal IT organization. Ideally, the IT function can aspire to become an innovation engine, helping showcase go-tomarket services from the perspective of being the first and best customer.

## 6. Summary: a win–win opportunity

Strategic Out-Tasking is a model that breaks the cycle of negative compromises associated with traditional outsourcing approaches. By out-tasking routine transactions, enterprises can free in-house expertise and management attention to focus on disruptive innovations and higher-value interactions to enhance customer experience. Enterprises gain business flexibility from being able to modify service levels to respond effectively to changing market conditions. Outsourcers can also enjoy higher margins from deploying more targeted services. While out-tasking contracts may be narrower in scope, global deployment opportunities, especially in large multinationals, may create opportunities for significant revenue growth. Successful Strategic Out-Tasking partnerships create opportunities for outsourcers to upsell higher-margin consulting and managed services, enabling profitable expansion of their account relationships.

For Strategic Out-Tasking to succeed, both enterprises and outsourcers need to have a win–win mind-set. Our model requires enterprises to remain accountable for business outcomes and align what they expect outsourcers to deliver with their overall business goals. They must select highly integrated and secure services from best-of-breed providers and retain the processes and resources necessary for achieving the desired returns. Outsourcers, in turn, need to invest in the long-term success of their customers. They must invest in global delivery platforms and scalable business models to develop and provision differentiated service offerings rapidly. Investments in disruptive innovations and integrated solutions with other outsourcers can help differentiate services offerings, lower delivery costs, and, ultimately, win more business.

In short, the benefits can be dramatic and the time to act is now.
