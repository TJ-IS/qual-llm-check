# What kind of world do we want to help make with our theories?

Southern Methodist University, Dallas, TX, USA

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 17 January 2017 Accepted 17 January 2017 Available online xxxx

## a b s t r a c t

In response to Demetis and Lee's proposal that Information Systems needs to adopt system science in order to live up to the identity implied by its name and in order to develop better theory, this commentary seeks to answer two questions: What kind of reality does systems science produce? And, given that technological advances have moved the IS research agenda beyond the organizational realm, how likely is it that systems science will help generate better IS theory? Drawing on a relational ontology, reflections on these two questions are offered.

“If methods help to make the realities they describe, then we are faced with the question: which realities might we try to enact? Neoclassical ones? Amerliorist agendas? Revolutionary realities? Anti-patriarchal or post-colonial worlds? Realities composed of poststructuralist partialities and shifting identities? Cyborg-like and materially heterogeneous worlds?”

[Law & Urry (2004: 396)]

I&O has a tradition of engaging scholars in debates on philosophical and methodological issues in Information Systems. I find this a valuable endeavor that can help our field sharpen its theoretical and methodological tools. I am therefore pleased to be able to contribute to this particular debate on the value of systems theory in IS, which has been prompted by Demetis and Lee's paper. In my reading, the argument that Demetis and Lee make in their contribution goes something like this:

1. The IS discipline has been remiss in taking the concept of system – which is part of its name and therefore identity – sufficiently seriously in its research. In order to develop better theories, systems science should be adopted by the IS community.

2. To ensure that systems theory is applied correctly in IS research, methodological requirements to guide this research are needed.

3. Based on General Systems Theory and Luhman's theorizing on social systems, six requirements that need to be satisfied for an IS study to qualify as systems science, are developed and empirically demonstrated.

Even though I was disappointed that the authors did not use this article to demonstrate how systems science helps us develop better IS theory by means of the Drosia Bank case they present, I nevertheless found the paper deeply thought-provoking and highly generative. In particular, it raised the following key questions for me:

• What kind of reality does systems science produce?

• Given that technological advances have moved the IS research agenda beyond the organizational realm, how likely is it that sys tems science will help generate better IS theory?

In my commentary, I draw on a diffractive reading (Barad, 2007) of Demetis and Lee's piece through a relational ontology (Emirbayer, 1997; Ingold, 2000; Scott & Orlikowski, 2014). In order to contextualize my reflections on these two questions, I will begin by outlining my theoretical position.

## 1. Relational theorizing

A relational ontology regards reality in “dynamic, continuous and processual terms” rather than in substantialist (or essentialist) ones (Emirbayer, 1997: 281). A substantialist ontology advances a view of the world as a collection of substances, i.e., essential, static things, that are more or less able to act under their own power. Specifically, humans are accorded rationality and free will, thus giving them an agency that is independent of other things. Even though the substances are seen as interdependent and relationships among them are examined, such inter-entity engagements are assumed to leave interacting objects largely xed and unchanged

A relational ontology breaks with the separation of entities (e.g., people, technology) from relationships by making the primary unit of analysis the transaction rather than its constituent elements. With entities secondary, their identities, meaning and significance are derived from their role in the dynamically unfolding process of practice. People and things are thus brought into being through everyday activity. They do not pre-exist action, but are enacted

The enactment nature of phenomena implies that they are entangled and emergent. Importantly, entanglement is not about adding relationships to connect ontologically separate entities; rather it is about a “lack [of] independent, selfcontained existence” (Barad, 2007: ix) or ontological indeterminacy. The concept of entanglement signifies that the ‘things that make up reality were never separate before they were brought into interaction; instead, they were always constitutively intertwined.

However, the ontological indeterminacy that fluid entanglements create, is resolved – albeit temporarily and in-situ – by means of agential cuts (Barad, 2003). Nyberg (2009) offers the following example of agential cutting: call center customer experience the components that constitute service delivery (i.e., the telephone system, the computer systems, the customer service representative, etc.) as an entangled whole until customer service representatives distance themselves from the (failing) technology with such utterances as ‘the computer has a mind of its own’. Enacting such cuts between technological and human actors, the elements in the sociomaterial assemblage are given identity, properties and agency.

Barad highlights that agential “cuts are enacted not by willful individuals but by the larger material arrangements of which ‘we’ are a ‘part’” (Barad, 2007: 178). In other words, agential cutting is not merely a discursive act performed by an agential individual, but the performance of everyday material-discursive practices that draw on the sociomaterial infrastructure in which an individual is entangled. Furthermore, “cuts cut ‘things’ together and apart. Cuts are not enacted from the outside, nor are they ever enacted once and for all” (Barad, 2007: 179). Agential cuts thus provide a situated and relational way of understanding the cat egorization and thingification that occurs in everyday practice while avoiding such dualisms as human/non-human, nature/culture and male/female, which are generally taken to be ontologically real.

By replacing taken-for-granted, fixed boundaries between entities with a notion of people and things being enacted in practice, a relational view also challenges the possibility of representations that reflect a given reality (Barad, 2003). Instead it proposes that representations (e.g., concepts, frameworks, models) are performatitve, meaning that they do not only mirror reality but enact it (Austin, 1962: Butler, 1997). For example, an analytical model (e.g, of the US housing market) reflects the phenomenon by representing its essence. However, such virtual representations are used to make decisions that affect – in real life – the phenomena (e.g., US housing market) they model (MacKenzie, Muniesa, & Siu, 2007).

This assertion that representation are performative is particularly significant in scientific endeavors, which are frequently held up as pure reflections of an external reality. Even though there are some acknowledgements of these representations being partial or even subjective – e.g., “What we observe is not nature itself, but nature exposed to our methods of questioning” (Heisenberg, 1958) – performativity makes an even more forceful claim, namely that our instrument-dependent practices that present reality in a certain way, actually produce the world.

The conclusion that Law and Urry (2004) draw from the performative nature of (social science) research, is that we as researchers can influence the reality we study through our choice of theories and methods. Our work is not just epistemological (i.e., the way we know things), but also ontological (i.e., what things are). This creates tremendous ethical responsibilities for researchers (Barad, 2007) in that the theoretical-methodological approaches we adopt, create worlds. This makes the decision to adopt a given theoretical lens – such as systems theory – a high-stakes game.

The two questions that I ponder in this commentary emerged out of my reading of Demetis and Lee's advocacy for systems theorizing through concepts such as enactment, practice, entanglement, performativity and agential cuts. Since these concepts are increasingly being applied IS research – under the conceptual umbrella of sociomaterial theorizing (e.g., Introna & Hayes, 2011; Jones, 2014; Mazmanian, Cohn, & Dourish, 2014; Scott & Orlikowski, 2014) – my commentary can be seen as somewhat of a continuation of recent debates on sociomateriality in IS (e.g., Bratteteig & Verne, 2012; Cecez-Kecmanovic, Galliers, Henfridsson, Newell, & Vidgen, 2014; Hassan, 2016; Kautz & Jensen, 2012). Indeed, one of these debates took place in this journal (Kautz & Jensen, 2013; Mutch, 2013; Scott & Orlikowski, 2013).

## 2. Reflections on the two questions

## 2.1. What kind of a reality does systems science produce?

Demetis and Lee do us a great service by distilling systems theory into six requirements that capture key concepts of an oth erwise vast theoretical landscape. By way of summary, the key theory elements they highlight are:

• Whole is more than the sum of its parts: When components interact with one another, this synthesis creates a new substance. This also implies that the same component interacting with another environment or component generates a new substance. For example, different outcomes emerge when the same ERP system is implemented in two different organizations

• Goal seeking: The interaction among components (or sub-systems) must result in the achievement of some final goal (e.g., equilibrium, survival).

• Transformation: The key mechanism by which systems achieve their goals is the processing of inputs into outputs. The complexity of this operation leads to a hierarchical decomposition of a system into sub-systems (e.g., a computer program split into modules and sub-routines).

• Autopoiesis & self-reference: Autopoiesis is the system's ability to reproduce itself. This regeneration relies on self-referential information (i.e., information about the system) to self-correct and reproduce itself.

• System/environment distinction: While the environment is external to a system, it is also constitutive of it.

• Communication: This process includes not only the utterances that send information to another system, but also the understanding that is achieved by the utterance's targeted person or entity.

In addition to these six requirements, Demetis and Lee highlight that the goal of systems theory is to tackle complex phenomena though increasing levels of abstraction, which are built through the synthesis (i.e., combining) of more and more extant components. Additionally, they point to systems theory's contingent construction of observations, noting that by observing the observer differences in meaning can be understood in terms of observer-relative distinctions.

Despite Demetis and Lee's assurance that systems are contingent on observer, environment and interacting components, the overall impression one gets from their exposition is that systems are regarded as ontologically real and that their boundaries are unproblematic and fixed. For example, the boundaries of the three information technologies (e.g., a case and transaction management system) that were used in Drosia Bank's money laundering detection example offered by Demetis and Lee, are treated as self-evident and static. Similarly, the anti-money laundering (AML) department and the organization (i.e., Drosia) are treated as hierarchically related systems with little consideration for the contingent nature of their boundaries vis-a-vis their respective environments. Thus, while systems theorizing might support a more fluid and conditional understanding of how the interdependent components that make up the Anti-Money Laundering system are configured, such a conceptualization is not evident in Dementis and Lee's illustrations.

To problematize such boundary-drawing, it is useful to recall Bateson's (1972: 466) deliberation about the limits of the individual:

“Suppose I am a blind man, and I use a stick. I go tap, tap, tap. Where do I start? Is my mental system bounded at the handle of the stick? Is it bounded by my skin? Does it start halfway up the stick? Does it start at the tip of the stick? But these are nonsense questions. The stick is a pathway along which transforms of difference are being transmitted. The way to delineate the system is to draw the limiting line in such a way that you do not cut any of these pathways in ways which leave things inexplicable. If what you are trying to explain is a given piece of behavior, such as the locomotion of the blind man, then, for this purpose, you will need the street, the stick, the man; the street, the stick, and so on, round and round. But when the blind man sits down to eat his lunch, his stick and its messages will no longer be relevant—if it is his eating that you want to understand.”

This example of the blind man and his cane not only challenges taken-for-granted cuts that IS researchers typically make between people and artifacts (i.e., information technologies), but also between individuals and their environment. Bateson highlights that boundaries are contingent on the practices that we seek to understand. In other words, the blind man's self as a particular mind-body-stick configuration is brought into being in locomotion and eating. It does not exist as an organism onto which material, psychic and social components are layered.

Furthermore, Bateson argues that distinctions between ‘inside’ (i.e., within the skin-delimited body) and ‘outside’ (i.e., the environment) that are an inevitable consequence of the thingification that systems thinking promotes, are not meaningful to the blind man who relies on the cane as a pathway – or extension of the self – rather than as a separate, inanimate object. Instead of locating mind in the head, Bateson (1972) envisages cognition – and thus an individual's sense of self – as extending outward into the environment along multiple pathways.

Gibson's (1979) theory of affordance similarly challenges humans' separability from their environments. Instead of treating the environment as the world ‘out there’ that provides input to an individual's mind – that then creates representations on which the individual's actions are based – the environment is perceived directly in terms of action possibilities. Gibson's notion of affordances thus stands in conflict with system thinking's principle of transformation (i.e., input-process-output) and its system/environment distinction – unless this boundary is operationalized as dynamic and contingent.

The fixity of system boundaries and the hierarchical relationships among components is further evident in Luhmann's (1990) ontology of systems (Fig. 1 in Demetis and Lee), which reflects well-worn distinctions between people as biological, psychic and social beings. Indeed, these categories have formed the basis of disciplinary divisions in the social sciences. This institutionalization highlights not only that the Luhmann's (1990) classification scheme has been materialized and become ‘real,’ but also that it is exceedingly difficult to regard the systems in this hierarchy as contingent. Instead, it seems that this categorization is intended to capture essential differences among the systems that make up human existence.

Ingold (2000), an anthropologist, recounts his struggles to develop a more integrated and holistic understanding of human beings as biological organisms and social persons, in the face of this categorization scheme. While his research objectives seem highly consistent with systems theory, Ingold's experience suggests that science's tendency to abstract, categorize and thingify in order to then bring components back together again in orderly relationships, makes it difficult to achieve this goal within a system-theoretic infrastructure. In order to conceptualize human beings as the integration of two separate but complementary component – i.e., “as organisms within systems of ecological relations and as persons within systems of social relations” (p.3)—a reciprocal interplay between dualisms such as nature-culture and mind-body needed to be developed

The futility of this exercise led Ingold (2000) to pursue a relational conceptualization of human life “that eliminates the need to slice it up into these different layers” (p. 3). Referring to individuals as “organism-person[s]” (p. 50) to help overcome the Cartesian dualism, Ingold (2000) focused on the practices by which people dwell in the landscape and taskscape that they inhabit. He highlighted the entwinement of human bodies with their environment and illustrated that it is through embodied experiences, which are shared with others in particular places, that cultural meaning and social relations emerge. The history of these embodied and emplaced actions are held as much in “muscular consciousness” (Ingold, 2000: 204), as in the traces they leave in the landscape. Ingold (2000) thus eschews the notion of cultural knowledge residing in people's minds and being imported into a specific environmental setting and further challenges the system-environment distinction.

By adopting an ontology of dynamic, unfolding relations, the process of becoming organism-persons represents Ingold's primary unit of analysis. Holding together what a substantialist view separates, Ingold's (2000) relational view foregrounds joint complex activity. By giving primacy to process, the relations between components in a given action are also not treated as static ties, but as ongoing, dynamically unfolding processes. It therefore makes no sense to envision constituent elements apart from the ows in which they are involved.

Systems theory's requirements of goal seeking and autopoeisis further solidifies the apparent stability of systems/entities and their boundaries. They do so by imbuing systems with intentionality, agency and self-identity. In this way both systems and their components/sub-systems are not only essentialized, but the possibility that they (e.g., individuals, technology, social structures) act under their own power is created. This creates an image of systems as networks of input-output and hierarchical relationships within which self-motivated, self-regenerating and rational components pursue their own agendas.

This can lead to quite paradoxical situations, especially with respect to technology. On the one hand, the materiality of technology places it in a dualistic position with agential humans; on the other hand, information systems – as autopoeitic systems – are accorded agency. While Leonardi (2011) distinguishes between human and material agency, it is not clear how the differences between these two agencies can be reconciled with systems theory's universalist definitions of agency.

In summary, the world that systems science seems to be producing is one of things whose identities and boundaries are quite stable. These static entities are anterior to relationships with other entities. When they are then brought together – through synthesis – their interactions are dominated by vertical (i.e., hierarchical sub-ordination) and horizontal (i.e., input-output) interdependencies. However, interactions among system components are secondary to the identification of objects, meaning that relationships are seen as separate and in addition to the system elements, thereby generating a whole that is greater than the sum of its parts. Importantly, these interactions add supplementary and external modifications to the entities involved, thereby leaving their 'true' nature untouched, like billiard balls (Emirbaver. 1997).

This essentialization and entification of phenomena (re)create ontological dualisms such as mind/body, culture/nature, human/ non-human, self/environment, and social/material. As we see from Ingold's (2000) account of his journey as an anthropologist, these dualisms are performative rather than representative of actual reality. As they become institutionalized in language, academic disciplines, and technologies, for example, these dualisms and the boundaries they create are increasingly difficult to reconceptualize, unpick and reconfigure. This seems evident in the Drosia bank example that Demetis and Lee rely on to illustrate the requirements of system theorizing. Information systems are defined in purely technological terms (i.e., case and transaction management systems), rather than socio-technical or sociomaterial terms.

Having outlined what kind of reality is produced by systems science, Law and Urry's (2004) question about whether this is the kind of reality we as IS researchers want becomes pertinent. This question however raises further issues about what criteria should determine our preferences. Relevance to practice? Theoretical and methodological rigor? The phenomena that we seek to explain? In my reflections about the kind of reality that our discipline might want to produce with our theories, I have chosen the nature of contemporary and emergent phenomena that we are seeking to explain as the primary consideration.

## 2.2. In light of technological advances, is systems science likely to generate better IS theory?

Demetis and Lee motivate their advocacy for systems science by highlighting the prominent place the term “systems” has in the IS field's identity. Additionally, they propose that systems theorizing can help generate better IS theory. In my reflections on these two arguments, I consider the kinds of phenomena to which advances in technology have led the field. These include mobile (e.g., Mazmanian, Orlikowski, & Yates, 2013) and social technologies (e.g., Kane, Alavi, Labianca, & Borgatti, 2014), as well as grid (e.g., Ribes, Jackson, Geiger, Burton, & Finholt, 2013; Venters, Oborn, & Barrett, 2014) and experiential (Yoo, 2010) computing

Thus a key consideration in determining whether the kind of world that systems science produces is desirable, is the extent to which the systems science world can harmoniously accommodate these contemporary and emergent phenomena.

Technological advancement is continuously changing the issues and questions IS researchers need and want to explore. For example, technological developments are fundamentally changing the nature of work and institutions as the workforce is becoming more mobile, virtual and global. This implies that work life is more fleeting, distributed and multiple than in the past (e.g., Orlikowski, 2007). Taken for granted categories are being challenged, including the distinctions between organizations and their customers in an era of open innovation (Thorén, Ågerfalk, & Edenius, 2014).

Furthermore, technology is becoming so entangled in individuals' everyday lives, that traditional dualisms of work-vs-play, actual-vs-virtual and human-vs-machine are rendered less and less meaningful. Highly personal uses of technology (e.g., wearables and ubiquitous computing), where issues of multiple embodiments and the integration of technology with the user as an embodied, sensory being, form part of the research agenda Yoo (2010) outlines for experiential computing. Experiential computing is contrasted with traditional computing that occurs as a separate activity in organizations and that is driven by the goal of improving organizational performance. Defined as “digitally mediated embodied experiences in everyday activities through everyday artifacts that have embedded computing capabilities” (Yoo, 2010: 213), experiential computing is moving the field of IS beyond the organization.

Illustrative of studies in experiential computing, research on social media (e.g., Schultze, 2014) and mobile technologies (e.g., Symon & Prichard, 2015) have demonstrated what it means to be a cyborg – that is, the dialectic synthesis between humans and technology (Haraway, 1991) and between physical and digital bodies (Borer, 2002) – in contemporary society. In particular, these studies highlight that user identities are produced as they enact everyday practices; in other words, they are performative rather than essential. Furthermore, the increasing intertwining of people with devices such as mobile phones and wearable, as well as digital bodie such as avatars and social media pages, renders users' experiences with technology more embodied, sensory and emotional.

In contrast to the personal and embodied experiences that cyborgian users have with technology, the increasingly distributed, global and digital nature of large-scale infrastructures (e.g., Østerlie, Almklov, & Hepsø, 2012) represents the other end of the spectrum of contemporary IS phenomena. Importantly, these large-scale infrastructures (e.g., Mazmanian et al., 2014; Ribes et al., 2013), which includes the Internet of Things, integrate digital (e.g., algorithms) and physical material (e.g., oil, sand in deep sea drilling). They also tend to generate vast quantities of digital traces that serve as resources for (big) data analytics and computational approaches (e.g., Gaskin, Berente, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2014; Howison, Wiggins, & Crowston, 2011), which seek to uncover enacted patterns of relationships. Rather than relying on preconstituted categories and dualisms as their starting point, such bot tom-up data mining techniques treat events and entities as emergent.

Contemplating the kinds of sensibilities these and other emergent phenomena demand from our theories and methods, Law and Urry (2004: 403–404) argue that our contemporary approaches to research are inadequate:

“Current methods do not resonate well with important reality enactments. They deal, for instance, poorly with the fleeting – that which is here today and gone tomorrow, only to reappear the day after tomorrow. They deal poorly with the distributed – that is to be found here and there but not in between – or that which slips and slides between one place and another. They deal poorly with the multiple – that which takes different shapes in different places. They deal poorly with the non-causal, the chaotic, the complex. And such methods have dif culty dealing with the sensory – that which is subject to vision, sound, taste, smell; with the emotional – time-space compressed outbursts of anger, pain, rage, pleasure, desire, or the spiritual; and the kinaesthetic – the pleasures and pains that follow the movement and displacement of people, objects, information, and ideas.”

In light of the phenomena that I believe the IS discipline should – and most probably wants to – explore, my sense is that systems theory is unlikely to adequately equip the field for the types of realities, questions and conceptualizations that these phenomena demand. The thingified, dualistic and categorical reality that systems theory produces is more likely to stifle than promote contemporary theory development in IS. In my mind, relational theories that are currently being explored under the umbrella of sociomaterial theorizing, hold more potential for advances in IS theory development.

## 3. Concluding thoughts

A central concern motivating my commentary is that the stakes of advocating and adopting a theoretical lens are high. This is because the representations that we generate through our research practices are performative rather than reflective of reality. In other words, they produce the very world that we seek to describe and explain

A key consequence of this perspective is that we can shape reality through the theories and methods we adopt. It therefore behooves us to envisage the world a given theory creates. To develop an image of what a system science world could look like, I found it helpful to read Demetis and Lee's distillation of systems theory through the literature on relational ontology (e.g., Emirbayer, 1997) and sociomaterial theorizing in IS (e.g., Introna & Hayes, 2011; Jones, 2014).

Through this diffractive reading (Barad, 2007), the reality that systems science seems to produce emerged as one dominated by static entities that are brought into orderly vertical (i.e., hierarchical decomposition) and horizontal (i.e., input-output) relationships with one another. Even though system science stresses interdependence and abstractions of complex wholes, these re lationships are layered on top of the independent, static and agential components that make up a system.

While such a world might be consistent with phenomena in traditional computing environments (e.g., organizational decision making with a Decision Support System), I have argued that it is at odds with contemporary IS phenomena such as experiential computing and large-scale infrastructures. For this reason, I have argued that the substantialist ontology that systems science (re)creates is unlikely to advance IS theory. I maintain that a relational ontology, which is currently being explored under the umbrella term of sociomaterial in IS, holds more promise for theorizing the new phenomena confronting the field.

Another implication of a performative view of the scientific endeavor, is that as researchers we carry an ethical responsibility for our world-making. Barad (2003: 384) expresses this as follows:

“Ethics is not simply about the subsequent consequences of our ways of interacting with the world, as if effect followed cause in a linear chain of events. Ethics is about mattering, about taking account of the entangled materializations of which we are a part, including new configurations, new subjectivities, new possibilities.”

Mattering, which is a process of signification that combines with materialization (i.e., actualization) to configure reality over time. This process stabilizes boundaries, agencies and identities of phenomena, thereby creating an impression of fixity and stability associated with “matter.” However, it is important to remember that the matter thus produced is not ontologically real and independent of its becoming. Instead, matter is “a doing” (Barad, 2007: 151), implying that phenomena might have developed differently and become something different.

This implies that as IS researchers we need to examine our own practices – including the questions we ask, the methods we deploy and the theories we adopt – to understand and critically review our world-making. Given that we make agential cuts to enact temporary resolutions of the ontological indeterminacy of entangled phenomena, a set of questions that might guide this analysis could include: When do we cut (i.e., create entities though distinctions, categories and dualisms)? How do we cut (e.g., through language, instruments)? And, why do we cut (i.e., what motivates this particular forms of mattering)?

Moving from a substantialist to a relational agenda requires significant shifts in our view of scientific practice, reality and the purpose of the scientific enterprise. To shift from a notion of the world being composed of stable things that interact to one that is fluid, constantly becoming and constituted through practices is disorienting. Letting go of the moorings that essences, things and dualistic categories have provided, is not easy. However, there is considerable evidence that this shift if being embraced by some in IS.

Despite the challenges of relational theorizing in our field – as evidenced by recent debates on sociomateriality (e.g., Kautz & Jensen, 2013; Mutch, 2013; Scott & Orlikowski, 2013) – I believe that the world it creates is more open, generative and capable of dealing with contemporary and emergent IS phenomena, than the world produced by systems theory
