# CULTURAL DIFFERENCES AND GEOGRAPHY AS DETERMINANTS OF ONLINE PROSOCIAL LENDING

Gordon Burtch

Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455 U.S.A. {gburtch@umn.edu}

Anindya Ghose

Stern School of Business, New York University, New York, NY 10012 U.S.A. {aghose@stern.nyu.edu} Korea University Business School, Korea University, Seoul 136-701, SOUTH KOREA

Sunil Wattal

Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA 19122 U.S.A. {swattal@temple.edu}

In this paper, we analyze patterns of transaction between individuals using data drawn from Kiva.org, a global online crowdfunding platform that facilitates prosocial, peer-to-peer lending. Our analysis, which employs an aggregate dataset of country-to-country lending volumes based on more than three million individual lending transactions that took place between 2005 and 2010, considers the dual roles of geographic distance and cultural differences on lenders’ decisions about which borrowers to support. While cultural differences have seen extensive study in the Information Systems literature as sources of friction in extended interactions, here, we argue and demonstrate their role in individuals’ selection of a transaction partner. We present evid ence that lenders do prefer culturally similar and geographically proximate borrowers. An analysis of the marginal effects indicates that an increase of one standard deviation in the cultural differences between lender and borrower countries is associated with 30 fewer lending actions, while an increase of one standard deviation in physical distance is associated with 0.23 fewer lending actions. We also identify a substitution effect between cultural differences and physical distance, such that a 50 percent increase in physical distance is associated with an approximate 30 percent decline in the effect of cultural differences. Considering approaches to overcoming the observed cultural effect, we offer some empirical evidence of the potential of IT-based trust mechanisms, focusing on Kiva’s reputation rating system for microfinance intermediaries. We discuss the implications of our findings for prosocial lending, online crowdfunding, and electronic markets more broadly.

Keywords: Prosocial lending, microfinance, cultural differences, geography, crowdfunding

## Introduction

In recent decades, the information systems literature has seen a number of studies contending that physical distance is of lessening importance, attributing this to a reduction in search costs (Bakos 1991) and the ability of information technology to facilitate transactions over greater distances. These trends have driven an (incomplete) shift toward a so-called “flat world” (Gefen and Carmel 2008). At the same time, an increasing number of studies have reported on the growing importance of cultural frictions in organizational activities, arising from the increasing prevalence of offshoring and global sourcing (Krishna et al. 2004; Rai et al. 2009), distributed software development (Levina and Kane 2009;

Levina and Vaast 2008) and distributed virtual teams (Wakefield et al. 2008). With the advent and prevalence of global sourcing, some have even argued that cultural intelligence (CQ) is key to firm competition and differentiation because the ability to navigate across a variety of cultures on an ongoing basis is not easy to develop or maintain (Ang and Inkpen 2008; Earley and Ang 2003).

These cross-cultural differences are not limited to transactions involving firms; they also arise in electronic markets that facilitate transactions between individuals. Many such markets now enlist millions of participants each day. Individuals partake in a multitude of transaction types, beyond the simple sale of goods; they contract workers in online labor markets, they invest in entrepreneurial ventures on crowdfunding platforms, or they seek out personal relationships at online dating websites, to name a few. Kiva.org, the context of our study, provides a prime example, because it enables prosocial, peer-to-peer lending between individuals numbering in the hundreds of thousands, who collectively represent more than 190 countries around the world.

Considering the above, it is interesting to note that there is a dearth of research on the role of cultural distance and, to a lesser degree, physical distance, in affecting individual exchange online. Although recent work has considered distance-based frictions within markets for online peer-to-peer transactions, including the sale of goods in online auctions (Hortacsu et al. 2009), loan-based crowdfunding (Lin and Viswanathan 2013), and reward-based crowdfunding (Agarwal et al. 2011), no such efforts have been undertaken with respect to cultural frictions. This observation is particularly poignant when one considers that cultural differences have previously been shown to impact a variety of individual selection decisions, such as the choice of travel destination (Ng et al. 2007) or a spouse (Kalmijn 1994). It is this gap in the literature that we seek to address here; that is, we seek to understand the dual roles of physical and cultural distance in online peer-to-peer transactions.

As noted above, we situate our study at Kiva.org. Kiva is the world’s largest online platform for prosocial lending, having facilitated loans to more than one million borrowers since its inception.<sup>2</sup> Kiva is the ideal setting for our analysis, given that in many online markets frictions stemming from interpersonal differences will be confounded with the costs of transacting over physical distance. For example, on eBay or Amazon, physical distance implies increases in shipping costs. In contrast, on Kiva, the content of the transaction requires no physical handling, and the transaction itself is fully mediated by the marketplace purveyor because both loan monitoring and customer service are under the purview of Kiva and its lending partners. Further, although prosocial lending is conceptually distinct from the sale of goods or services, in that the economic risk to lenders is decidedly lower (because loans on Kiva, by construction, offer lenders no monetary return), prior work has argued that lenders will still be motivated to select a high quality borrower (Vesterlund 2003) who will make the biggest impact with the funds provided. Thus, in other contexts where individuals face greater economic risk, it is likely that any effects we identify here will be more pronounced.

We draw on a large-scale aggregate dataset that is based on more than three million lending actions that were initiated between 2005 and 2010. We combine this sample with recent data on national cultural attributes from the World Values Survey, as well as data on GDP differentials and shared language. We identify a number of important findings. We find that both cultural differences and physical distance have a significantly negative association with lending, consistent with expectations. An analysis of their marginal effects indicates that a one standard deviation increase in cultural differences between any given pair of countries is associated with approximately 30 fewer lending actions, while a one standard deviation increase in physical distance is associated with 0.23 fewer lending actions. We also find evidence of a substitution effect between physical and cultural differences. A 50 percent increase in physical distance above the average is associated with an approximate 30 percent decrease in the effect of cultural differences. Although this result may be explained by a number of different phenomena, it suggests that lenders approach different types of distance in a similar manner. We demonstrate the robustness of each of these results to a variety of model specifications, controls and data splits.

These results provide the first empirical evidence of the role of cultural frictions in interpersonal, brief transactions, suggesting that culture impacts individuals’ selection of transaction partners to a significant degree. Further, they suggest that the impact of cultural differences can interact in interesting ways with other sources of friction. Bearing this in mind, we also consider IT-based institutional trust mechanisms as a possible route to mitigating the culture effect, noting the prior literature’s suggestion that the mechanism by which cultural differences impact transaction likelihood is primarily through a reduction in trust. We demonstrate this potential empirically with respect to Kiva’s built-in reputation mechanism, the published Moody’s reputation rating of the microfinance institutions associated with each loan.

Our work contributes to the literature on online crowdfunding (Agarwal et al. 2011;Burtch et al. 2014a; Burtch et al. 2013), demonstrating that cultural differences play a particularly important role in crowdfunders’ decision making. Our study suggests a number of design implications for crowdfunding platforms, such as the incorporation of IT-based institutional trust mechanisms, or the development of adaptive web interfaces that account for user characteristics and culture (Reinecke and Bernstein 2013).

We also contribute directly to the literatures on prosocial lending and charitable contribution (Baron and Szymanska 2011; Galak et al. 2011) by demonstrating the importance of cultural differences in borrower and charity selection. We extend this literature by showing that cultural differences can preclude lending, but that these issues have the potential to be overcome through the use of IT-based mechanisms. Past research on prosocial behavior and charity has demonstrated that individuals are more likely to support charities and borrowers in need when they can empathize (Piff et al. 2010), or when they view themselves as part of the same “in-group” (Baron and Szymanska 2011). Here, it seems that cultural similarity may allow for such empathy, or that it may form the basis of a perceived in-group.

Finally, our work contributes more broadly to the literature in economics dealing with cultural biases in bilateral trade between nations (Guiso et al. 2006, 2009), showing the import of each factor in an individual transaction context, which is notably characterized by brief exchange. In some sense, our work bridges the literatures on cultural differences in IS and economics. In the IS literature, cultural frictions have been argued to impact the outcomes of collaborations, once they have been undertaken. This is somewhat different from the economics literature, which has argued that the influence of cultural differences operates via parties’ decreased willingness to transact in the first place. We take the latter view here, which has not, to our knowledge, seen consideration by IS scholars.

In the following sections, we begin with a review of the relevant IS literature pertaining to cultural and geographic distance, as well as the role of these factors in economic exchange more broadly. We then discuss the relevant literature on charitable contribution, prosocial lending, and crowdfunding, before describing the study context and formulating a set of theoretically motivated testable hypotheses. In order to evaluate these hypotheses, we present a macroeconometric model based upon the aggregation of the individual choice process faced by a Kiva lender, which we ultimately take to the data. We then present our results and robustness checks. We conclude by offering a discussion of the limitations of our work, as well as its implications for both practitioners and scholars dealing with globalized, online economic exchange between individuals, prosocial lending, and crowdfunding platforms.

## Literature Review

## Cultural Differences and Information Systems

There is a large body of work in IS examining the role and impacts of cultural differences across an array of contexts (Leidner and Kayworth 2006). The most relevant work from this literary stream pertains to offshoring and global sourcing in IS development, distributed software development, and distributed virtual teams.

In studies of outsourcing arrangements and offshore development, physical and cultural distance have been argued to impose a variety of costs associated with communication, control, coordination, and the formation of social bonds (Carmel and Abbott 2007). Krishna et al. (2004) suggest that in the presence of greater cultural differences, some outsourcing projects are likely to be more feasible than others. Ang and Inkpen (2008) offer a more abstract discussion of the subject, drawing on the cultural intelligence (CQ) construct, which is proposed as a strategic resource for firms engaged in global sourcing projects. The authors decompose CQ into three elements: managerial CQ, competitive CQ, and structural CQ. Notable in their framework is the suggestion that one key facet of managerial CQ is managers’ cognizance or awareness of the potential for cultural differences when interacting with other team members (i.e., cultural awareness).

Rai et al. (2009) provide an empirical study of cultural differences in an offshoring context. These authors find evidence that cultural similarity results in fewer overruns and greater client satisfaction, attributing the results to increased trust, communication, and joint problem solving among team members. Levina and Vaast (2008) study distributed software development involving offshore teams in India and Russia, and report that some managers are able to overcome cultural differences by expending accrued capital (e.g., social capital), or by using IT-based mechanisms to highlight dependencies between sub-teams.

More recently, Levina and Kane (2009) discuss the use of immigrant onshore managers to oversee offshore projects in their country of origin as a way of bridging differences. Wakefield et al. (2008) examine sources of conflict in virtual teams. These authors note that IT-based mechanisms, namely communication technologies, are frequently used to reduce task conflict.

In sum, the extant IS literature on cultural differences notes a variety of frictions arising from cultural differences between team members in distributed work. Perhaps most notable among these is the report that cultural differences lead to reduced trust between team members (Rai et al. 2009), or an inability to accept a culturally distant team member as part of one’s own group (Levina and Kane 2009). Further, the majority of these studies demonstrate the successful application of IT-based mechanisms in overcoming these frictions.

It is worth noting that the above findings parallel a number of relevant studies in economics and finance, which have considered the importance of cultural difference for bilateral trade (Guiso et al. 2006, 2009) and interfirm interactions in the context of foreign investment (Kogut and Singh 1988), stock investments (Grinblatt and Keloharju 2001) and, more recently, syndicated bank loans (Giannetti and Yafeh 2012). A notable difference between these literary streams, however, is that the IS literature contends that cultural differences drive poorer project outcomes, stemming from difficulties encountered over the course of extended interaction, while the literature in economics and finance has typically framed cultural differences as primarily impeding trust, thereby lessening the probability of a transaction taking place at all.

While all of the above studies have found that cultural distance produces frictions, the reasons for this in studies of economics and finance are quite different from those noted in the IS literature. What is common to both streams of work, however, is the frequent reference to the notion of trust, and its negative correlation with cultural differences. Giannetti and Yafeh’s (2012) results provide particularly clear support this interpretation, as they find that culturally distant lead banks are more likely to require third-party guarantees on syndicated bank loans (i.e., institutional trust mechanisms).

## Charitable Contribution and Prosocial Lending

The literature on charitable contributions is extremely lengthy. Work in this area that is most relevant to the present study consists predominantly of experimental efforts that highlight the role of biases in donors’ selection of recipients (e.g., charities). Baron and Szymanska (2011) summarize a number of these biases, which include, among others, the notions of evaluability and parochialism. Evaluability refers to the fact that individuals tend to focus on factors that are easy for them to evaluate (Hsee 1996). Notably, recent field work undertaken in an offline setting by Fisman et al. (2012) provides empirical evidence that evaluability plays a similarly important role in individuals’ ability to obtain consideration from lending officers at Indian banks.

Parochialism refers to individuals’ preference for alternatives that benefit their own group (Schwartz-Shea and Simmons 1991). Galak et al. (2011) provide some empirical evidence of this phenomenon in their study of prosocial lending. Those authors show that lenders prefer to support others who are of the same gender or who hold a similar occupation. In a similar vein, Freeman et al. (2009) report on an experiment in which they attempt to overcome donors’ biased preference for recipients of the same race. Finally, Piff et al. (2010) provide another useful example. Counter to intuition, they find evidence that individuals of lesser means tend to donate in greater amounts, arguing that this happens because such donors are better able to identify (empathize) with the recipients. Taken together, these studies demonstrate the importance of empathy and in-group preferences for prosocial behavior.

Other recent work that is highly relevant to the present study is that which has looked at loan-based (Lin et al. 2013; Lin and Viswanathan 2013), donation-based (Burtch et al. 2013), and reward-based (Agarwal et al. 2011; Burtch et al. 2014b, 2014c) crowdfunding. Here, the studies by Lin and Viswanathan (2013) and Agarwal et al. (2011) are particularly notable. Lin and Viswanathan examine geographic distance among lenders and borrowers on Prosper.com, and find evidence of an apparent home bias, while Agarwal and his colleagues explore the role of physical proximity between contributors and fundraisers in a reward-based crowdfunding market for musical artists, finding that contribution is more likely when the fundraiser lives nearby. Each of these studies provides evidence of contributors’ aversion to geographic distance in crowdfunding contributions. Importantly, however, neither touches on a potential preference for cultural similarity. Here, because we have the benefit of truly global data, lenders and borrowers in our setting exhibit strong cultural variation. This makes it relatively easy for us to examine the role of cultural differences.

## Methods

## Study Context

Kiva is an online peer-to-peer lending platform that supports lending to entrepreneurs who, by and large, are located in the developing world. Kiva’s stated mission is to alleviate poverty by ensuring access to credit. Loans are supplied to individuals or groups of borrowers, and the lenders earn no interest on any funds they supply. All lending through Kiva takes place in U.S. dollars, although lenders repay the loan in their local currency. A local microfinance institution (MFI) initially screens potential borrowers and disburses a loan, drawing on its own financial resources. The MFI then assists the borrower in creating their “borrower profile,” which is submitted to Kiva. The profile typically includes the borrower’s biography, loan amount, repayment schedule, loan purpose, and so on.

The borrower profile may initially be submitted in a non-English language; however, such profiles are then translated into English by Kiva volunteers, prior to publication on the Kiva website. Potential lenders from around the world browse available loan requests, which number in the hundreds or thousands at any given point in time. They then select the entrepreneurs they wish to support and provide funds in \$25 increments. Upon arriving at the Kiva website, active loans are presented to potential lenders in random order.<sup>4</sup> Posted loans have 30 days to reach full funding. If a loan reaches its target, Kiva transfers money from the lenders to the MFI to ‘backfill’ the loan, and the borrower then repays in accordance with their repayment schedule. The MFIs are responsible for loan collection, after which the funds are passed to Kiva for return to the lenders.

## Hypothesis Development

Drawing on our literature review, we next formulate a series of hypotheses pertaining to the impacts of physical distance and cultural differences on lending frequency. Beginning with cultural differences, we again note that IS research has found them to negatively impact outcomes in distributed software development and outsourcing, at least in part because of reduced trust (Rai et al. 2009) and a possible unwillingness to accept culturally distant others into the ingroup (Levina and Kane 2009). Further, the literature in economics and finance has presented extensive evidence that bilateral trade between countries, syndicated bank loans, and investment in stocks are all subject to cultural biases (Giannetti and Yafeh 2012; Grinblatt and Keloharju 2001; Guiso et al. 2009). The prosocial lending literature also suggests that donors will prefer to support others who belong to the in-group, who are easier to evaluate (Baron and Szymanska 2011) and with whom they can easily empathize (Liu et al. 2012; Piff et al. 2010). Taken together, these various findings support our first hypothesis around the role of cultural differences.

## H1 (Culture Effect): Greater cultural differences between a lender and borrower country will result in fewer lending transactions.

For reasons similar to the above, we would also expect that greater physical distance would negatively impact lending. As noted previously, studies in the crowdfunding literature by Agarwal et al. (2011) and by Lin and Viswanathan (2013) have found that physical proximity is positively correlated with crowdfunder contributions. This may be attributable to the fact that distance is often a proxy for familiarity or awareness, as has been suggested by a number of scholars (Grinblatt and Keloharju 2001; Grossman 1998; Hattari and Rajan 2011). This is an important point, because a number of researchers have shown that individuals prefer to invest their funds in the familiar (Huberman 2001; Leblang 2010). Recent work by Hortacsu et al. (2009) has also demonstrated the impacts of distance on eBay and MercadoLibre transaction volumes between geographic areas. Bearing these various studies in mind, we formalize the anticipated impact of physical distance on lending volumes as follows.

## H2 (Geography Effect): Greater physical distance between a lender and borrower country will result in fewer lending transactions.

Finally, over and above these main effects, we also consider that different types of distance (i.e., cultural and geographic) may in fact be substitutable from a lender’s perspective. First, as greater distance may imply lesser exposure to a foreign culture and thus less familiarity, it may result in a lack of cultural awareness (or cultural blindness). In the absence of cultural awareness, a number of scholars have argued that individuals tend to assume cultural similarity as a result of ethnocentrism: the presumption that one’s own culture is the norm (Adler 1991; Burger and Bass 1979).

Further, Grinblatt and Keloharju (2001) find that the marginal effect of distance on investment declines in a nonlinear manner once a certain threshold is exceeded (in their case, 100 km). This would suggest that, beyond a threshold, increases in distance cease to matter, perhaps because a certain degree of proximity (or cultural similarity) is necessary but not sufficient to result in investment. Baron and Szymanska (2011) also point to the bias of prominence, where donors focus on one aspect to the exclusion of others, whether because it is the most prominent or accessible, or because they believe it to be the most important. A substitution effect between distance and cultural differences might, therefore, arise if geographic distance were to grow overly extreme in the lender’s eyes, drawing their attention from cultural differences (or vice versa). We formalize the anticipated substitution effect as follows.

H3 (Substitution Effect): Geographic distance and cultural differences will be substitutable, resulting in a moderating (attenuating) effect, such that, as physical distance grows larger, the effect of culture on lending volumes will decline (and vice versa).

Before progressing to our formulation of the econometric model, we should note that the arguments above regarding the role of in-group preferences are supported by anecdotal evidence. In particular, a recent study by Liu et al. (2012) examined Kiva lenders’ motivations. Conducting a textual content analysis of user profiles, these authors identified eight primary motivators for lending, of which three directly reflect a preference for similar others: religion, empathy, and groupspecific altruism. It is also notable that a variety of third party applications, and the design of the Kiva website itself, support prefiltering of loans based on borrower characteristics and location.

## Model Formulation

In evaluating the hypotheses proposed above, we are faced with two options relating to the level of analysis. On the one hand, we can approach these questions employing an individual-level analysis. This approach would entail an examination of individual lender activity in terms of how they distribute loans to borrowers and how lender and borrower cultural disparity or geographic proximity relates to those decisions. This approach would have the benefit of allowing us to directly model the decisions of individual lenders, and perhaps study how those decisions change with lending experience. Unfortunately, this approach would present issues in terms of measurement and estimation. This is because we have little information about the lenders’ themselves. Most of the independent variables in our data are operationalized at the national level. Indeed, we only have lender-specific information around the actual occurrence of lending transactions and lenders’ country of residence.

On the other hand, we might instead pursue an aggregate estimation, examining volumes of lending activity between pairs of countries, and how these volumes vary with differences in culture or distance between nations. This approach has the benefit of aligning well with the prior literature in economics on bilateral trade. Further, this approach would allow us to maintain agreement between our variable operationalizations and our level of analysis. Of course, this approach also has a number of drawbacks. In particular, it becomes more challenging to infer individual lender decisions and behavior. In the end, we opt to employ this second, aggregate approach, because, as noted, this is the approach that the bulk of the prior literature dealing with globalized interpersonal trade has employed, both in offline (Anderson and Wincoop 2004; Guiso et al. 2009; Helpman et al. 2008; Santos Silva and Tenreyro 2006) and online (Blum and Goldfarb 2006; Hortacsu et al. 2009) contexts.

The outcome of interest in our empirical estimations is the volume of lending actions between pairs of countries. In our initial modeling, we take an approach similar to that of Hortacsu et al. (2009), who estimate the impact of physical distance on eBay and MercadoLibre transaction volumes between buyers and sellers across pairs of geographic locations. Those authors estimate their model employing a form of “gravity equation” (Anderson and Wincoop 2004), relating the volumes of buyers and sellers in each geography, as well as the distance between them, to the volume of resulting sales. Here, we estimate the impacts of cultural and physical distance on transaction volumes involving lenders and borrowers across pairs of geographies.

Similar to the individual choice process articulated by Hortacsu and his colleagues, we begin here by assuming that a lender, upon landing on Kiva’s website, faces a multinomial choice setup, as in Equation 1. The array of available borrowers on Kiva’s website represent potential alternatives that the lender can select from. Here, δ captures the observable quality of the borrower, assumed to be common to all lenders, and $\mu$ represents shifters on the utility of lenders in location (or of culture) l, donating to borrowers in location (or of culture) b (e.g., physical distance, cultural differences). Finally, the N terms capture the population weighting of lenders across geographies (or cultures). As the effect of distance or cultural differences (i.e., the shifter μ) grows more negative, the probability of a given lender in geography (or of culture) l having a sufficient preference for a borrower in location (or culture) b to issue a lending action will decline. At the same time, the higher the number of lenders in $l , N _ { l } ,$ the more likely there is to be a lender with sufficient preference for the borrower in b to execute a loan.

\operatorname * {P r} (l \text {   lends   to   borrower   } j \text {   in   location   } b) = \frac {N _ {l} \exp (\delta + \mu_ {l , b})}{\sum_ {l ^ {\prime} = 1} ^ {L} N _ {l ^ {\prime}} \exp (\delta + \mu_ {l ^ {\prime} , b})}\tag{1}
$$

Finally, k is a constant term capturing the effect of the observable quality of the borrower.

$$

It is this relationship that forms the basis of our estimation approach, although we further adapt it to include a series of control measures, which we detail subsequently. The shift in utility from physical and cultural distance, captured by $\mu ,$ might arise from a number of different sources, all of which are noted in our literature review and hypothesis development sections, including the biases of parochialism (i.e., an ingroup preference) and evaluability (i.e., lower evaluation costs), among others.

However, rather than simply estimate this log–log specification employing an OLS estimator, it is also important to note recent developments in the economics literature regarding estimation techniques for gravity equations. Santos Silva and Tenreyro (2006), in particular, have shown that the estimation of gravity equations via OLS with a logged dependent variable is likely to be inappropriate, because this approach can result in severely biased estimates. Instead, the authors argue (and demonstrate, using simulation data) the increases in estimation efficiency and consistency that can be achieved using an untransformed outcome variable and a Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood (PPML) estimator. Accordingly, we take this approach.

Further, as advocated in the literature dealing with the estimation of gravity equations using cross-sectional data (Anderson and Wincoop 2004; Helpman et al. 2008; Santos Silva and Tenreyro 2006), we account for unobservable heterogeneity between countries, incorporating lender country and borrower country fixed effects. In addition, we control for unobservable heterogeneity across lending partners (i.e., MFIs). To achieve this, we incorporate vectors of dummy fixed effects for each unit. As a result, country-specific variables exhibiting no variation, such as available lenders or available borrowers, wash out of the estimation because they are collinear with our fixed effects.

In addition to the above, we also incorporate a number of variables based on our review of the prior literature, which are likely to impact lending activity. Guiso et al. (2009) considered shared language in their analysis of trust formation and bilateral trade, finding that this factor had significant positive effects. Accordingly, we allow that lenders on Kiva may similarly prefer to transact with others who speak the same language, even though all content on Kiva is translated into English. We surmise that this may be the case because translation introduces an added level of effort, and may result in typos, grammatical errors, or miscommunications. Accordingly, we control for linguistic differences between lender and borrower countries.

We must also consider that the goals of the Kiva lending platform are to alleviate poverty and foster entrepreneurship in developing countries. Lenders should, therefore, have a preference for borrowers that face greater difficulty locating capital and raising funds. To operationalize this potential, we incorporate the difference in the gross domestic product (GDP) per capita between the lender and borrower countries, adjusted for purchase power parity.

## Data Set

Tables 1, 2, and 3 provide the definitions, descriptive statistics and correlations for each of the variables in our sample.<sup>6</sup> Our dataset of lending activity, retrieved via Kiva’s public API, covers all transactions that took place between 2005 and 2010, and is thus based on 3,350,393 distinct lending actions, initiated by 373,701 unique lenders, which together supported 165,452 borrowers. Although we have data pertaining to individual lender activities, we aggregate to the country level because (1) our explanatory variables of interest do not vary at the individual level, and (2) a discrete choice analysis of lending activity associated with hundreds of thousands of lenders, 52 borrower countries, and 134 associated MFIs would be computationally intractable. Further, any simplifications would be difficult to justify or interpret. Notably, these challenges are not unique to our study. Blum and Goldfarb (2006) note similar data issues in their study, where they similarly apply a gravity equation setup based on the aggregation of an individual discrete choice process to study American households’ preferences for geographically proximate providers of taste-based (digital) goods online.

Since our outcome of interest is the number of lending actions between lenders and borrowers in pairs of countries, facilitated by a particular MFI, we aggregate the individual lending actions to the level of country–country–MFI tuples, obtaining 25,530 observations. To clarify, an example is useful: Consider the hypothetical case of American lenders supporting Peruvian borrowers. While there may have been hundreds or thousands of lending actions from the United States to Peru in our dataset, facilitated by a particluar MFI, those transactions would be aggregated into a single observation in our sample, with their total count representing the dependent variable.<sup>7</sup>

We combined this Kiva dataset with a number of external sources. First, we drew on a dataset constructed by de Sousa et al. (2005) for their study of international trade. This dataset includes measures of physical distance, as well as an indicator of shared official languages (a binary indicator reflecting the existence of at least one shared official language).<sup>8</sup> Additional data on official languages was supplemented from the CIA World Factbook to replace any missing values, and to ensure complete coverage in our sample. Similarly, any missing values for distance were populated based on data obtained from the Google Maps API.

To construct our measure of cultural distance, we drew on data from the World Values Survey (WVS). The WVS is an attempt by social scientists to measure cultural values around the world; it includes responses from almost 100 countries. The survey consists of a detailed questionnaire on various life aspects, administered via interviews. The average number of respondents in any given country is 1,400. Importantly, the WVS appears superior to alternative measures of culture that have appeared in the IS literature (Hofstede 1991, 2001). We would argue this because the WVS is much more recent and it is not subject to the same selection biases, given that respondents are drawn from the general population.<sup>9</sup>

Inglehart and Weltzel (2010), in their analysis of the WVS results, note that diverse orientations tend to cluster together in coherent patterns. Employing factor analysis, these authors showed that 70 percent of the variation in the features of different cultures could be explained by just two dimensions (values): (1) the extent to which a society emphasizes traditional rather than secular values; (2) the extent to which a society emphasizes values related to survival rather than selfexpression. Societies with traditional values emphasize religion and family ties, while societies with survival values exhibit low interpersonal trust, do not typically support gender equality or environmental protection, and often favor authoritarian governments. Cultural distance between any pair of countries can be measured as the distance between country coordinate pairs, which capture a country’s position along each cultural dimension (Giannetti and Yafeh 2012).

The cross-country cultural differences that emerge are summarized in the cultural map of the world, depicted in Figure 1, based on the 2005 results of the World Values Survey. Because temporal variation in cultural features is generally quite limited, our analysis employs the most recently available results of the WVS for a given country. Figures 2 and 3 respectively depict heat maps of the geographic distribution of borrowers and lenders in our sample for those countries with at least 100 of either user type. In terms of the distribution of characteristics in our dataset, we find that borrowers reside in 52 different countries. The two countries with the largest share of borrowers are Peru and Cambodia, having 20,264 and 15,386 loan postings, respectively. Lenders in our dataset are distributed across 208 countries. The United States is home to the majority of lenders (254,935), with Canada (32,991) and Australia (12,062) hosting the second and third largest numbers, respectively.

As a final note, it is worth clarifying some important features of our data. First, our data includes details of all lending actions, including those made toward loans that ultimately failed to reach their funding targets (i.e., expired loans). Further, our data allows us to explicitly identify the MFI associated with each particular loan. That is, we do not infer the MFI that supported a loan based on the countries involved; rather, we observe the MFI directly. In terms of limitations, our data on country and MFI characteristics is cross-sectional, and thus non-varying. Accordingly, this limits our ability to simultaneously incorporate fixed effects and to identify coefficients associated with within-country or within-MFI characteristics. Of course, this is a limitation faced by any study employing cross sectional data.

## Estimation Approach

As noted above, we assess our hypotheses employing the Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood (PPML) estimator advocated by Santos Silva and Tenreyro (2006). Equation 4 outlines our model, where our outcome of interest is the count of lending actions from lenders in country i, to borrowers in country j, serviced by MFI (partner) q. This model incorporates our dyadic measures of physical distance, cultural differences,<sup>10</sup> wealth disparity, and shared language, as well as a series of lender country fixed effects, φ, borrower country fixed effects, δ, and MFI fixed effects λ. Thus, a given observation refers to a triple of lender, borrower, and MFI. Country pairs have the potential to appear twice in our dataset, if both lenders and borrowers were active in each country during our period of observation.<sup>11</sup>

Our measure of national culture, based on WVS indicators, is not available for all of the countries in our sample. We draw on the dataset of cultural coordinates constructed by Inglehart and Welzel, which includes cultural coordinates for 96 countries. Notably, our sample includes 196 distinct lender countries and 52 borrower countries. We employ list-wise deletion based on missing values, noting that roughly 75% of our observations involve a lender or borrower country for which we have no cultural data. However, we reasonably assume these missing values are in fact missing at random (MAR) because we have no reason to expect that countries selected for the WVS are chosen in a systematic manner that would correlate with our variables of interest. That is, we feel confident that the “missingness mechanism” does not depend on the unobserved data. Further, this WVS data has been applied in a number of studies published within leading journals over the last few years, each conducted with the intent of drawing generalizable conclusions (see Algan and Cahuc 2010; Chen 2013; Esteban et al. 2012).

## Results

Table 4 presents the results of our main model estimation, where we introduce our key variables and interaction terms in a hierarchical manner. We also considered here that cultural differences along each of the different cultural dimensions might have different impacts on lending decisions, thus we examined differences along each dimension independently. Those results are presented in columns 3 and 4. Notably, both sets of results are very similar, thus we focus on column 2 in discussing our findings. First, our fixed effects for lender countries, borrower countries, and MFIs are jointly significant. This suggests that unobservable heterogeneity across countries explains a good degree of lending activity, as we would expect. Further, this indicates that some MFIs draw systematically more lending than others, perhaps because they appear more legitimate or because their policy of borrower evaluation is more rigorous.

Next, looking at our key independent variables, we find that cultural distance has a significantly negative effect on lending counts, providing support for our first hypothesis.<sup>12</sup> As noted above, the type or dimension of cultural difference does not appear to matter, as the effects along each cultural dimension are quite consistent with the effect of our composite measure, in terms of sign, significance, and magnitude. A subsequent analysis of the predicted marginal effects from our model in column 2 indicates that a one standard deviation increase in cultural differences would be associated with approximately 30 fewer lending actions. This effect is quite large when one considers it in tandem with the sheer volume of lending on Kiva, which involves more than 9,000 country pairs. Importantly, this finding demonstrates that lenders prefer to support others who are culturally similar, perhaps because they have a greater level of initial trust in such individuals, because they are perceived as members of the lender’s in-group, or it may simply be that such borrowers are easier to evaluate (Fisman et al. 2012).

Supporting our second hypothesis, we also find that distance has a significant negative effect on lending. This finding confirms our second hypothesis, and suggests that individuals also prefer to transact with borrowers who are in closer proximity. Again considering the marginal effects, we see that this relationship is more moderate: a one standard deviation increase in physical distance is associated with 0.2 fewer lending actions, on average. However, again, this effect is fairly prominent when we consider the scale of Kiva.

Finally, and perhaps most interestingly, we find evidence in support of our third hypothesis. That is, we find that cultural differences and geographic distance are substitutable from the lender’s perspective. This is reflected by the positive, significant interaction between culture and distance. Once again, we examine the marginal effects, considering the impact of cultural differences at different points in the distribution of physical distance. Doing so, we find that an approximate 50 percent increase in physical distance over the average is associated with a 30 percent decline in the negative effect of cultural differences. In order to better convey the implications of this interaction term, in Figure 4 we plot the marginal effect of cultural differences on the expected count of loans over the entire distribution of distance in our sample, along with the associated confidence intervals.<sup>13</sup>

As can be seen in the figure, the marginal impact of cultural differences on the count of loans decreases at greater physical distance. Based on this finding, one might expect, for example, that a Canadian lender will be more likely to lend to a borrower in Peru than a borrower in Mexico, despite that the cultural differences are greater (2.5 versus 1.4), perhaps because the cultural differences are much less salient to lenders in the former case due to physical separation.

Beyond our main results, it is also worth noting the effects associated with our control measures. First, the difference in GDP between a lender and borrower country has a significant positive effect. This is in line with our expectations, because it suggests that Kiva lenders are more inclined to provide funds to those borrowers who are in countries that are relatively less wealthy (Kiva’s mission statement is “Loans that Change Lives”). In contrast, our control for linguistic overlap is surprisingly insignificant. This may be attributable to the fact that translations are conducted by professionals, who in many cases may translate non-English content into English of a quality that is actually higher than that exhibited by the average native English speaker on Kiva.

## Robustness Checks

We conducted a variety of robustness checks as well, in order to assess the stability of our main results. We began by reestimating the main model using alternative estimators. We explored a PPML estimation excluding zero observations (i.e., country partner triples where no lending actions took place, even though lenders, borrowers,and MFIs were active and had the potential to undertake lending), as well as OLS estimation using the log of lending actions as the outcome. In both estimations, we see once again that cultural differences continue to have a negative, significant effect on lending propensity (see Table 5).

We next considered our identification, and attempted to delve into cross-country heterogeneity that might be contributing to lending outcomes.<sup>14</sup> In assessing this, we performed several additional estimations. First, we noted the possibility that lending on Kiva may be severely influenced by the occurrence of natural disasters in a borrower country. This notion seems reasonable, given that Kiva employees often advertise the occurrence of natural disasters in certain regions on the company blog with the aim of soliciting loans for borrowers in that area.<sup>15</sup>

We also repeated our estimation of our model, this time including controls for the diversity of the population and the volume of foreign-born residents in a given lender country. Our measure of diversity is calculated as the empirical probability that two randomly drawn individuals from the same country will share the same ethnicity (i.e., one minus the summed product of the largest and second largest ethnic groups in each country). The diversity variable was calculated using data drawn from a study by Ellingsen (2000) on cultural conflict, while the immigration statistics were drawn from the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs’ publication on countries’ immigrant stock.<sup>17</sup>

## Overcoming Cultural Differences

Having established that culture’s effects are persistent across our various model estimations, we next considered approaches to overcoming these cultural biases. It is notable that the prior IS literature has suggested reduced trust as a key consequence of cultural differences (Rai et al. 2009), and similar suggestions have been made in the economics and finance literature (Giannetti and Yafeh 2012; Guiso et al. 2009). In addition, a number of studies in IS have reported that IT-based mechanisms can act as a (partial) solution, helping to establish trust and overcome these issues (Levina and Vaast 2008; Wakefield et al. 2008). We therefore consider the same possibility here, fcusing, in particular, on institutional trust mechanisms (Pavlou and Gefen 2004).

Before evaluating any particular approach to building trust, we first explored the notion that trust (or rather, distrust) was indeed in play at Kiva. To do so, we drew on a published index of a national trust, which is based on specific survey items in the WVS that are intended to assess respondents general level of trust in others.<sup>18</sup> The key survey item, which has seen recent use in the economics literature (Aghion et al. 2010), poses the following question to respondents:

Generally speaking, would you say that most people can be trusted or that you need to be very careful in dealing with people?

Because this trust data is lending-country specific, it is collinear with fixed effects. Accordingly, in our estimation we focus simply on the moderating relationship between this trust index and our cultural distance variable.

We explored the relationship of this trust indicator with cultural differences and lending propensity, anticipating that increased levels of trust should attenuate the effect of cultural differences. The results of this estimation are reported in Table 8. Here, we see as expected that greater trust among lenders is associated with increased lending in the face of cultural differences. These results support our initial supposition that trust is a primary mechanism underlying lending decisions on Kiva.

In their work, Pavlou and Gefen (2004) consider three mechanisms that may help overcome mistrust at eBay, namely escrow services, reputation (feedback) mechanisms, and credit card guarantees. We focus here on the second mechanism, noting that Kiva assigns each microfinance intermediary (MFI) a publicly observable reputation rating. This reputation rating is updated yearly, based on the repayment rate of borrowers with whom the MFI works. We anticipate that this reputation mechanism, enabled by a third party (Moody’s), may attenuate the effects of culture. The MFIs are assigned a reputation rating based on their cumulative history supporting borrowers on Kiva. This history includes repayment rates of the borrowers they support, as well as an assessment of the MFI’s transparency, among other factors. These reputation ratings are assigned on a scale between 1 and 5, with 5 being the most desirable (least risky).

To analyze the impact of these ratings, we incorporated a control for the reputation rating of the MFI associated with each tuple.<sup>19</sup> In the estimation that follows, we operationalize the anticipated mitigating effect of MFI risk rating by interacting it with our cultural distance variable. Here, we expect a significant, positive effect. The results are presented in Table 9 and, as anticipated, we see that the reputation rating does appear to effectively reduce the impact of cultural differences on lending volumes, suggesting that third party trust mechanisms can indeed help to assuage lender concerns about culturally distant borrowers.

## Discussion

Our finding of a negative effect from distance on individual lending parallels many prior findings in the literature, most notably those of Hortacsu et al. (2009) on eBay and MercadoLibre. While it appears that the importance of distance has certainly waned with the introduction of the internet, our findings still demonstrate that other factors remain that could impact individuals’ tendency to seek economic opportunities locally. In this particular case, there are a number of such factors that might explain our findings. For example, prior work has shown that individuals prefer to give to domestic charities, rather than foreign ones, out of a sense of nationalism (Baron and Szymanska 2011).

In contrast, our finding that cultural differences negatively impact brief transactions online represents a novel contribution. Past work in IS has spoken to the impeding effects of cultural differences on project outcomes, attributable to reduced trust, relational conflict, and communication or coordination difficulties. Here, we show that these factors extend to individual transactions in online markets as well.

Further, our finding of a substitution effect between culture and distance is also particularly interesting. We have argued that this substitution essentially arises within an individual lender’s decision-making process, perhaps because they prioritize one factor over another, seeking a minimum threshold of proximity and cultural similarity, or because they focus on cultural differences only when aware of those differences. For this reason, the observed substitution is less likely to manifest in a sustained interaction context, where the effects of culture and distance are based on difficulties and issues arising in actual interpersonal interactions. This finding, therefore, emphasizes a unique difference between electronic markets supporting individuals, and the prior IS literature dealing with interactions between firms and organizations.

What is more, it is also possible that this interaction effect, which has not been identified elsewhere in more traditional settings, such as bilateral trade, stems from the fact that larger, professional institutions will often have greater resources at their disposal than an individual decision maker. As such, it may be easier for larger institutions to expend the effort to understand their trading partner (e.g., to conduct due diligence, acquire private information). In this sense, issues of evaluability and other related heuristics may be particular to individual-level decision making.

Our finding that third-party institutional trust mechanisms appear to mitigate the impacts of cultural differences is also somewhat novel. Our findings in this regard parallel the conclusions of Guiso et al. (2009), who suggest that the reason cultural biases impede bilateral trade is that they get in the way of trust formation, as well as the results of Giannetti and Yafeh (2012), who find that culturally different lead banks are more likely to require a third-party guarantor on their loans. Third-party risk ratings likely have a beneficial effect because lenders are willing to trust a recognized, legitimate third party with expertise in risk analysis to take on the job of evaluation for them. When such third-party systems are not present, the job of borrower evaluation falls more heavily on the lender. In such scenarios, as we have noted in our literature review, it is likely that lenders will prefer to lend to borrowers that are easier for them to evaluate (Hsee 1996), which may also explain the preference for culturally similar, local others. This also suggests that not all third-party trust mechanisms will be equally effective in overcoming cultural biases.

It should be kept in mind that a preference for culturally similar borrowers is not necessarily evidence of inefficiency in the market. Recent work in an offline setting, conducted by Fisman et al. (2012), suggests that bank lenders in India show a preference for borrowers of the same culture. However, those authors also show that this bias is driven, at least in part, by the fact that lenders are better able to evaluate such borrowers. In turn, a cultural bias in that setting results in better loan outcomes. Indeed, those authors report that, overall, the benefits of easier evaluation actually outweigh the costs imposed on the “outside group,” in terms of reduced access to credit. Given this, future research can explore whether the impact of cultural similarity and evaluability translates into an online context in a similar manner.

Our findings have a number of implications for marketplace purveyors as well. Our results can inform the design of international online crowdfunded markets, particularly with regard to decisions about the types and content of information displayed to users. Recent work has proposed a design science approach to the implementation of adaptive web interfaces that account for users’ cultural differences (Reinecke and Bernstein 2013). Our results could serve as useful inputs to this sort of effort in the context of crowdfunding. As a crude example, purveyors might wish to order the presentation of crowdfunding campaigns to a given website visitor based on the cultural differences and physical distance between the visitor and the campaign organizer. Assuming a primacy effect is at play (Haugtvedt and Wegener 1994), those loans that a potential contributor views first should receive greater focus. Thus, by facilitating a focus on the campaigns that contributors are most likely to support, the purveyor can increase the frequency and amount of transactions.

Our findings point at some possible avenues for future research, both theoretically and empirically. Although we have found that cultural differences are associated with lower lending volumes, our aggregate data does not allow us to nail down the precise mechanisms that drive these outcomes. While we have surmised that these differences in culture are impeding trust formation, it would be prudent for future work to explore a variety of alternative mechanisms that may also be at play (e.g., transaction costs from misunderstandings).

## Limitations

On the surface, it may appear that our results would be limited in their generalizability, given that we study prosocial lending. However, we would argue that the unique context of our study is in fact an asset. This is because, as noted earlier, lenders will still have incentive to seek out high quality borrowers. That being said, given that the economic risk facing lenders is quite limited, we would anticipate that any effects identified herein would play out more strongly in other electronic markets that bear greater risk (e.g., on eBay, where the buyer is actually acquiring goods from the seller).

Our analyses are also based upon aggregate data, thus the effects we identify represent average behaviors. We do nonetheless have reason to believe that this analysis can provide meaningful insights. In particular, work has shown that individuals in online markets often ascribe their perceptions of groups to individual group members (Pavlou and Gefen 2005). While a given lender may not be aware of the cultural characteristics of a particular borrower, they may ascribe to that individual the cultural characteristics of the general population in the borrower’s location. In this regard, we make assumptions similar to those implicit in Hortacsu et al.’s work, which inspires our estimation approach. Those authors similarly draw inferences about the impact of physical distance on the utility of eBay buyers based on aggregate associations.

Our data is also cross-sectional in nature. However, crosssectional analysis of this sort is quite common in the economics literature dealing with gravity equations and bilateral trade (Anderson and Wincoop 2004; Helpman et al. 2008; Santos Silva and Tenreyro 2006). Further, to our knowledge, no major changes were implemented at Kiva around operating policy over the course of the period of observation. Still, future work can build on the present study by examining the dynamics of lending behavior in response to cultural differences. For example, subsequent work can examine whether cultural biases are moderated by lender experience, perhaps declining as the lender spends more time on the platform. Alternatively, future work might consider whether cultural biases are exacerbated (attenuated) when culturally distant borrowers default on (repay) their loans, and whether these effects are in turn moderated by the degree of cultural distance.

Another concern with our methodology is whether the pool of respondents to the WVS might be systematically different from the body of Kiva lenders. If these groups are not comparable, then one might be concerned about whether WVS measures for a given country truly reflect the culture of Kiva lenders. While this is certainly a limitation of our study, it is perhaps important to note that a number of past studies have made similar assumptions by attributing Hofstede’s (1991) national cultural indices to a group of particular subjects, based solely on their country of residence (e.g., Muthitacharoen and Palvia 2002; Png et al. 2001; Straub 1994; Straub et al. 1997).

While we cannot make a direct determination about whether Kiva users are representative of the general population of a given country, we can at least establish whether they represent the average internet user. This approach addresses concerns similar to those that have been raised in other contexts, namely whether Amazon Mechanical Turkers, who are often used for large-scale data coding or survey responses, are representative of the general internet population (Behrend et al. 2011; Ghose et al. 2012). In our case, leveraging estimates of website visitor demographics from Quantcast,<sup>20</sup> we see that visitors to kiva.org exhibit an ethnic distribution that is very similar to the general internet population.

## Conclusion

This paper demonstrates that prosocial lenders prefer to contribute their funds locally and to culturally similar others. Importantly, however, while distance is generally always important, it appears that cultural differences and geographic distance are substitutable for lenders. Further, we have provided evidence that the negative effects of cultural differences can be mitigated somewhat by the application of third-party institutional trust mechanisms. These findings are robust to a variety of alternative model specifications, variable operationalizations, and data splits. Further, our results have particular implications for the purveyors and participants in online prosocial lending, crowdfunding, and electronic markets generally.

Our findings suggest that participants would do well to market themselves, their projects, and their wares to others who are physically nearby or to whom they have strong cultural or community ties. Purveyors would similarly do well to help facilitate interaction between physically and culturally proximate participants (e.g., perhaps by offering locational search mechanisms or by “matching” users based on available profile information). In addition, based on our finding that institutional trust mechanisms can attenuate the effects of cultural differences, and bearing in mind the recent work of Guiso et al. (2006, 2009), it seems likely that cultural differences operate chiefly by reducing trust.

Future work can build on the present study by exploring the types of information that are most effective at mitigating cultural difference effects. Although Kiva is limited in that MFI reputation ratings are based primarily on financial performance, other types of information may prove more or less useful in a trust building capacity. For example, information on lending strategy, corporate values, or corporate social responsibility might have their own distinct moderating effects. What is more, a comprehensive analysis of different third-party mechanisms, to understand which are most effective in mitigating cultural issues, might prove useful (e.g., escrow services, reputation mechanisms). It is our hope that this work will present a first step toward the exploration of the consequences of culture in electronic markets. This will become increasingly important as interpersonal trade and the marketplaces in which it is conducted continue to grow more prevalent.
