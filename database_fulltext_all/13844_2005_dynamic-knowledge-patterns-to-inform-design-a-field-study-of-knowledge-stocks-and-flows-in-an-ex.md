# Dynamic Knowledge Patterns to Inform Design: A Field Study of Knowledge Stocks and Flows in an Extreme Organization

MARK E. NISSEN

MARK E. NISSEN is an Associate Professor of Information Systems and Management at the Naval Postgraduate School. His research focuses on knowledge dynamics. He views work, technology, and organizations as an integrated design problem and has focused recently on the phenomenology of knowledge flows. Professor Nissen’s publications span information systems, project management, organization studies, knowledge management, and related fields. In 2000, he received the Menneken Faculty Award for Excellence in Scientific Research, the top research award available to faculty at the Naval Postgraduate School. In 2001, he received a prestigious Young Investigator Grant Award from the Office of Naval Research for work on knowledge flow theory. In 2002, he spent his sabbatical year at Stanford integrating knowledge flow theory into agent-based tools for computational modeling. Before his information systems doctoral work at the University of Southern California, he acquired over a dozen years’ management experience in the aerospace and electronics industries.

ABSTRACT: Knowledge represents a critical resource in the modern enterprise. But it is dynamic and distributed unevenly. Capitalizing on this dynamic resource for enterprise performance depends upon its rapid and reliable flows across people, organizations, locations, and times of application. From a technological perspective, this points immediately to the design of information systems to enhance knowledge flows. The problem is, the design of information systems to enhance knowledge flows requires new understanding. The research described in this paper concentrates on understanding the dynamics of knowledge phenomenologically and on developing and applying techniques for modeling and visualizing dynamic knowledge flows and stocks. We draw key, theoretical concepts from multiple literatures, and we build upon integrative modeling work that composes a parsimonious, multidimensional, analytical framework for representing and visualizing dynamic knowledge. We then conduct field research to learn how this theoretical framework may be used to model knowledge flows in practice. By focusing this empirical work on an extreme organization and processes that involve and rely upon tacit knowledge, we illustrate how dynamic knowledge patterns can inform design in new ways. New chunks of kernel theory deriving from this fieldwork are articulated in terms of a propositional model, which provides a basis for the development of testable design theory hypotheses.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: case study, dynamics, information systems, knowledge design, knowledge flow, knowledge management, knowledge transfer, military, organizational learning.

KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTS A CRITICAL RESOURCE in the modern enterprise—so critical that strategists conceptualize it as central to competitive advantage in a knowledge-based view of the firm [15, 34, 81]. Indeed, some scholars (e.g., [22]) argue that knowledge represents the only sustainable source of comparative advantage. But knowledge is dynamic and distributed unevenly throughout the enterprise. It moves, clumps, and accumulates noticeably within specific people (e.g., experts), organizations (e.g., R&D units), locations (e.g., headquarters), and times of application (e.g., shift changes). Capitalizing on this dynamic resource for enterprise performance depends upon its rapid and reliable flows across such people, organizations, locations, and times.

By using the term flows, we refer to dynamic knowledge, and we subsume similar concepts such as knowledge conversion, transfer, sharing, integration, reuse, and others that depict changes, movements, and applications of knowledge over time (see, e.g., [2, 15, 27, 51, 53, 58, 65, 68, 82, 87, 95, 96]). By using this term, we also highlight the tight interrelationship between dynamic flows (e.g., movements and uses) and stocks (e.g., inventories and changes) of knowledge [20]. This mirrors the duality between knowledge as action and potential [37]. The term dynamic knowledge is more general, incorporating action and potential, flows and stocks, uses and inventories, and like dynamic concepts found elsewhere in the literature.

From a technological perspective, the importance of rapid and reliable flows of knowledge point immediately to the design of information systems (IS). At this stage in the advance of research and practice, many textbooks have been written to articulate, codify, and teach IS design methods, with most centering on techniques for modeling, visualizing, and analyzing design alternatives (e.g., [18, 30]) for information technology (IT) artifacts. For instance, the use of entity-relationship diagrams (ERDs), data flow diagrams (DFDs), object models, event-sequence charts, the Unified Modeling Language, architecture diagrams, and the like, represents standard practice in IS design today.

However, dynamic knowledge involves considerably more than the use of IT artifacts (e.g., knowledge transfer and elaboration; organizational socialization and acculturation; people’s peripheral participation, mentoring, and learning; see [65, 96, 100]). Indeed, many scholars and practitioners alike view only a limited role for IT in knowledge management (KM) [63], in large part because knowledge is distinct from information [17, 88]. Although knowledge and information interact dynamically [82, 91, 96], they differ fundamentally: knowledge enables direct action, whereas information provides meaning or context for action [65, 77]. The distinction between knowledge and information becomes particularly critical when seeking sustainable bases for competitive advantage: where action enabled by sticky, situated, experience-based, tacit knowledge is involved [2, 20, 41, 69, 95]. Unlike information, tacit knowledge is regarded widely as offering great competitive value to the organization because of its advantages in terms of appropriability [74] and inimitability [89].

This suggests that design of information systems to enhance knowledge flows requires new understanding. IS design needs to reach beyond the IT artifact. In addition, broader, nontechnological, tacit knowledge considerations—such as flows and stocks associated with people, organizations, and work processes—require integration into IS design theory and practice [63]. Such considerations are noted well as important [49] but are neglected broadly in IS design, particularly as it pertains to KM [1, pp. 126–129].

From the perspective of Information Systems Design Theory (ISDT; see [97]), it appears that extant research and practice do not address well the class of problems associated with flows and stocks of knowledge. Using ISDT terminology, the IS field stands to benefit from research addressing meta-requirements focused on promoting knowledge flows through the organization; meta-design including organizational structures, work processes, and personnel systems, in addition to information technologies; and kernel theories that govern design requirements specific to supporting dynamic knowledge flows and stocks. This gives rise to the research question driving our study: How can kernel theory about dynamic knowledge be used to inform IS design?

The research described in this paper concentrates on understanding the dynamics of knowledge phenomenologically and on developing techniques for modeling and visualizing dynamic knowledge flows and stocks. Such concentration is consistent with long-standing, effective, IS design practice that centers on modeling and visualizing information flows and stocks. But this present research goes further by leveraging unique characteristics of knowledge and its distinctions with information. And it addresses promotion of organizational knowledge flows as a unique class of goals that is largely missing from meta-requirements associated with extant ISDT.

In the balance of this paper, we draw from both theory and practice reported in multiple literatures, invoking approaches and techniques from several disciplines that address the dynamics of knowledge, including tacit knowledge. We draw key, theoretical concepts from these multiple literatures, and we build upon integrative modeling work that composes a parsimonious, multidimensional, analytical framework for representing and visualizing dynamic knowledge. We then conduct field research to learn how this theoretical framework may be used to model the phenomena of knowledge flows in practice. By focusing this empirical work on an extreme organization and processes that involve and rely upon tacit knowledge, we illustrate how dynamic knowledge patterns can inform design in new ways. New chunks of kernel theory deriving from this fieldwork are articulated in terms of a propositional model, which provides a basis for development of testable design theory hypotheses.

## Characterizing Dynamic Knowledge

IN THIS SECTION, WE SUMMARIZE a focused survey of theory and practice reported in multiple literatures to characterize dynamic knowledge in a manner that helps to identify design considerations. We draw key, theoretical concepts from these multiple literatures, and we build upon integrative modeling work to compose a parsimonious, multidimensional, analytical framework for representing and visualizing dynamic knowledge. The resulting theoretical framework provides the means to integrate different perspectives from disparate literatures and helps to guide our field research.

## Knowledge Perspectives, Considerations, and Design Implications

Table 1 summarizes the knowledge perspectives, considerations, and design implications identified through our focused survey of multiple literatures. Several considerations can be identified from each of these diverse knowledge perspectives, and each consideration has one or more implications in terms of design.

Many different social science models and perspectives for understanding knowledge have been developed, over several thousand years of inquiry, across multiple, diverse domains, and fields. As such, even contemporary scholars go “back to the Greeks” for insight [44] and draw from enduring philosophies of epistemology, ontology, pragmatism, and others [65, 81]. Rooted in such insight and philosophy, modern scholars of strategic management conceptualize knowledge as the basis for a theory of the firm [34, 81]. This strategic perspective indicates how knowledge can be used to gain and sustain competitive advantage. This perspective also suggests how different types of knowledge (e.g., firm-specific, idiosyncratic) underlie in part the relative appropriateness of markets versus hierarchies as governance structures [101].

For knowledge to serve as a source of competitive advantage, appropriability and imitability become key to firms striving to preserve any such advantage and to make it sustainable [89]. Tacit knowledge is considered broadly to be more appropriable and less imitable than explicit knowledge is. Hence, tacit knowledge is viewed as promoting sustainable competitive advantage better than explicit knowledge does. But knowledge is dynamic, and factors such as appropriability and imitability can change through time. Even in situations where key organizational knowledge is explicit (e.g., when appropriability is low, imitability is high), a firm may still compete dynamically on the basis of knowledge—for example, by pushing the knowledge frontier faster than do competitors [74].

Economic considerations come to the forefront in many studies involving dynamic knowledge. Knowledge production functions are developed to compare the relative marginal productivities of different types of knowledge (e.g., specialist, generalist) [70]. Such marginal productivities are important in terms of design, for the economics of knowledge vary with degree of specialization [34]. Also, the economics of knowledge stocks and flows can relate factors such as asset substitutability and imitability to effects of knowledge flows such as time compression diseconomies and mass efficiencies [20].

Moreover, knowledge does not represent a single, monolithic concept. Machlup [51] alone identifies over a dozen unique kinds of knowing (e.g., acquaintance, familiarity, awareness). Indeed, many different, usually binary dimensions for knowledge have been proposed and discussed across a diversity of literatures (e.g., economics, IS, organization studies, psychology, strategy). Among these, the distinction between explicit and tacit knowledge [69]—along with related binary distinctions such as knowwhat and know-how [73], declarative and procedural [64], practical and theoretical [81], and others—is the most enduring and prominent in a dynamic context (see [65]). And the economics of explicit versus tacit knowledge differ [34].

Further, explicit knowledge behaves dynamically much like information does. As such, both are characterized often as public goods [4], which foster imitability and impede appropriability. The imitability of actions based on explicit knowledge tends to render ephemeral any competitive advantage such knowledge-based actions may enable. Knowledge—explicit and tacit alike—is also characterized in terms of imperfect factor markets [5]. This implies that knowledge needs to be applied—not just created or possessed—in order for a firm to extract value and to offset opportunity costs [20]. Hence, in addition to pushing the knowledge frontier faster than do competitors [74], firms also compete dynamically on the basis of converting knowledge into output products and services. This reflects a tension between emphasis on knowledge creation versus application. And it articulates concisely a common “knowing– doing gap” [68]. Hence, knowledge alone—even tacit knowledge—appears to be insufficient for competitive advantage.

Communities of practice [10] involve both stocks and flows of knowledge. Members of such communities are generally not part of a single organization. Yet they coordinate through common practices (e.g., architecture, engineering, technical services) to determine the knowledge stocks that are relevant to such practices. People’s legitimate peripheral participation [47] through common practices also enables tacit knowledge to flow across organizations, without explicit arrangements between the organizations themselves. Communities of practice appear to rely upon neither markets nor hierarchies for coordination, and, as such, are posited as an alternate scheme for organization [100]. This complements long-established schemes such as organizing around functions [99], transaction costs [13, 101], task interdependencies [90], coordination mechanisms [55], information processing [28], and information location [85]. Hence, new organizational forms may obtain and even dominate through a focus on dynamic knowledge.

Organization studies scholars conceptualize organizational learning [40, 50] and organizational memory [84, 98] in a manner emphasizing the importance of how knowledge changes over time [41]. Some tension persists in this field between the two concepts. Organizational learning tends to emphasize the kind of tacit knowledge noted above as critical to competitive advantage, whereas organizational memory tends to emphasize explicit knowledge instead; organizational learning also pertains mostly to knowledge flows, whereas organizational memory pertains more to knowledge stocks [1]. Organizational learning corresponds more closely with the concept exploration [52], whereas organizational memory corresponds better with exploitation [60].

Organization studies research reflects continuing tension also between two perspectives on the level of social aggregation associated with learning. One reduces organizational learning to the individual participants in organizations who learn new skills [78], the other characterizes learning by the organizations themselves in terms of new routines [57]. But recent studies are establishing bridges across such levels of social aggregation in learning [16, 41]. For instance, some build upon conceptual links between individual learning (e.g., from the perspective of cognitive psychology) and organizational learning (e.g., from the perspective of absorptive capacity)

[14]. This reveals a contingency perspective [48]: no one design to enhance knowledge flows is likely to be best in all contexts.

Further, within the theoretical framework of this contingency perspective, organizational design reflects a rational view of organizations [76] that emphasizes fit (e.g., with the organization’s environment, strategy, goals) [11]. As a design science [93], organizational design is prescriptive and represents the same kind of purposeful, goaldriven, problem-solving activity associated broadly with IS design [39]: it “is concerned with how things ought to be, with devising structure to attain goals” [79, p. 133]. But the scope of design is broader when the focus is on organizations than when it is limited to IT artifacts [1]. Indeed, many scholars view organizational design as subsuming IS design, with technologies, organizational structures, personnel systems, and work processes all the focus of purposeful design [49]. This suggests that nontechnological characteristics should be analyzable through a design perspective similar to that familiar in IS research and practice.

KM has been studied recently from an IS perspective (see, e.g., [1, 7, 23, 33, 35, 42, 53, 57, 86]), a perspective that cleaves knowledge into areas of particular relevance in terms of dynamics. Many scholars view knowledge as an object, whereas others conceptualize it more as a process. The object view implies managers should seek to build, organize, and maintain knowledge stocks, whereas the process view implies knowledge flows are key [1, p. 110]. As in the discussion above, some tension persists here too between scholars emphasizing knowledge creation [65] versus application [34]. But here we also find emphases on knowledge reuse [53] and even forgetting [3].

Several life cycle models [63] include other activities such as knowledge organization and sharing as well. Such life cycle models help align the different knowledge activities in terms of a process or progression (e.g., knowledge is created first, then applied, and lost ultimately perhaps) and reflect a long-standing perspective in IS design. A “fundamental asymmetry in the economics of knowledge” [34, p. 112] suggests different degrees of specialization may be required along the life cycle (e.g., greater for knowledge creation than for its application). This reveals a contingency perspective also: knowledge activity along the life cycle represents an important design consideration in terms of enhancing knowledge flows.

A growing number of KM researchers are modeling knowledge flow phenomena directly [6, 24, 26, 27, 36, 75, 103] and developing theoretical models with design implications. Research along these lines builds upon a variety of perspectives and reference disciplines to develop integrated models and to examine the dynamics of knowledge through multiple dimensions [16, 66, 89]. In particular, interactions between types of knowledge (e.g., explicit, tacit) and levels of social aggregation (e.g., individual, organization) are revealing insightful patterns (e.g., “knowledge spirals” [65]) and suggesting contingency relationships (e.g., “ease of transferability” [41]) with potential to inform design. Analysis and reuse of patterns in IS design has proven to be very effective in the domain of object-oriented systems [29]. Composing models from multiple dimensions may offer similar potential for design in the domain of knowledge flows.

Researchers (e.g., [41]) are also investigating the organizational processes responsible for knowledge flows. This process orientation helps to explain different ways in which knowledge can flow through the organization (e.g., documents, personnel transfers) and suggests the organization may be designed better to enhance such flows. These studies are demonstrating the power of visualization for understanding diverse knowledge flows and for diagnosing associated work flow designs [45]. Such studies reinforce the importance of visualization in design. They also highlight an important linkage between flows of knowledge and corresponding flows of work in the organization. Linking information flows with work flows (e.g., through use cases) is standard in IS design today. The further linking of knowledge flows suggests an approach to informing design and extending its well-understood, extant techniques.

## Analytical Framework

AS FOLLOWS THE DISCUSSION ABOVE, many theoretical concepts, from a diversity of perspectives in disparate literatures, represent candidates for use in developing an analytical framework for modeling and visualizing dynamic knowledge. Indeed, the plethora of diverse, often-divergent concepts and perspectives makes it difficult to compose an integrated, parsimonious framework. To address such difficulty, we build upon prior theoretical research that focuses specifically on modeling and visualizing dynamic knowledge [59], and we use this framework as the point of departure for our present empirical study.

In particular, this prior research condenses the diverse, dynamic knowledge perspectives summarized above into an integrative framework with four dimensions: explicitness, reach, life cycle, and flow time. Together, the four dimensions compose an integrated, yet parsimonious, set of considerations noted in various literatures as having important design implications. And use of this framework in prior research has illustrated considerable versatility in terms of modeling and visualizing a diversity of dynamic knowledge patterns. The four dimensions comprising this framework also provide a concise, theory-based scheme to guide later coding of qualitative data from our field research (see [59, 61, 62] for detailed rationale and discussion).

The first dimension, explicitness, addresses the type of knowledge. We operationalize this construct as the ability to articulate knowledge: from theory, we expect that explicit knowledge can be articulated readily, whereas tacit knowledge cannot [69]. At present, we retain from the literature a binary contrast between explicit and tacit knowledge [65]. The second dimension, reach, addresses the level of social aggregation associated with knowledge. We operationalize this construct as the breadth of knowledge flows and stocks through an enterprise: from theory, we expect that different knowledge flows and stocks will occur at varying levels of social aggregation [16]. At present, we retain from the literature a set of discrete aggregation levels (e.g., individual, group, organization) [65].

The third dimension, life cycle, addresses the process view of dynamic organizational knowledge. We operationalize this construct as the activity associated with a knowledge flow: from theory, we expect that different knowledge flows will involve unique activities of a life cycle process [34]. At present, we retain from the literature a set of discrete life cycle activities (e.g., knowledge creation, sharing, application) [59]. The fourth dimension, flow time, addresses explicitly the dynamic nature of knowledge. We operationalize this construct as the length of time required for knowledge to flow: from theory, we expect that different kinds of knowledge, at various levels of social aggregation, and associated with particular knowledge activities, will flow at different rates [61]. At present, we use only relative orders of magnitude (e.g., days, months, years) to distinguish between various knowledge flows. Unlike the three categorical dimensions above, the flow time dimension is inherently continuous.

## Research Method

IN THIS SECTION, WE OUTLINE THE EMPIRICAL RESEARCH method used to investigate knowledge dynamics in an operational, field context. Following Yin [102], we conduct a case study and employ the analytical framework developed above to guide the investigation. The case study method provides a focused vehicle to investigate empirically how our theoretical framework can be used to model and visualize dynamic knowledge in practice. It also enables us to study the rich phenomena associated with dynamic knowledge while immersed as researchers within the operational context of the focal organization, work, people, technology, and environment. This interpretive method enables us further to study qualitatively our “how” research question [8] (i.e., “how can kernel theory about dynamic knowledge be used to inform design?”), which does not lend itself toward more positivist or quantitative methods [46].

Moreover, it provides a well-accepted and systematic process for field research, one that both guides and encourages repeated iteration of qualitative data collection and analysis [25]. Such repeated iteration is noted widely as key to grounding theory in the data of a qualitative study [32] and enables us to focus persistently on developing kernel theory with design insights—even if not apparent immediately and if slow to emerge. Of course, as with any research method, there is no guarantee that such insightful kernel theory will emerge or become apparent, regardless of our persistence. Finally, the case study method is particularly appropriate for an investigation such as this [102], which is more exploratory than confirmatory and oriented more toward theory building than theory testing.

The site selected for this study provides a rich environment for investigating knowledge dynamics. It involves work processes that depend critically upon organizationspecific stocks and reliable flows of knowledge. Tacit knowledge employed in such work processes is valued in particular as critical to competitive advantage in the associated organization. Organizational leaders strive to both gain and sustain competitive advantage on the basis of superior knowledge as well as superior technology. This site also represents an extreme case in terms of knowledge dynamics. Factors contributing to the extreme nature of the case include large organizational size, global geographical distribution, time-critical work processes, and mortal penalties for failure. Studying an extreme case such as this represents theoretical sampling [32] and makes it suitable for analytical generalization [102]. As demonstrated recently in the context of strategic learning, this calls, in part, for case selection of “a unique exemplar of a particular phenomenon to bring key dimensions to light” [89, p. 332]. Through the study of this extreme case, we seek to bring the dimensions of our analytical framework to light and to illuminate dynamic knowledge patterns with potential to inform design.

The case selected for study is what the U.S. military calls a Joint Task Force (JTF). As noted, the organization in question is large (e.g., tens to hundreds of thousands of employees) and distributed globally (e.g., operations in 12 time zones). Its work processes are time-critical (e.g., performance often depends upon coordinated execution in minutes and sometimes seconds), hazardous (e.g., death, injury, and political ramifications obtain), and performed by skilled people (e.g., most jobs require years to master) representing a diversity of specialties (e.g., many dozens of specialized jobs require integration). As also noted, many JTF organizations and work processes are technologically intensive. Some technologies are very specific to the military (e.g., specialized equipment for sea-based navigation and warfare), whereas others are common across many different types of organizations (e.g., networked computing and communications). The enterprise as a whole sustains considerable personnel rotation and turnover (e.g., roughly one-third of military personnel change jobs every year). Large, often virtual, project organizations are ephemeral. Groups of people are reorganized routinely and work together for only six to nine months before disbanding.

We employ four techniques for data collection in the field: (1) document review, (2) interview, (3) participant observation, and (4) informant. Briefly, document review provides important background information about the task force organization, roles, jobs, people, technologies, and processes. It also helps the Investigator to ask informed interview questions. The Investigator has ready and prolonged access to a wealth of documents during the study, and his security clearance enables the review of any document deemed to be pertinent. Further, through a unique relationship between the researcher’s university and the U.S. Navy, the Investigator is able to attain an official position in the focal organization, working and living for an extended period onboard a JTF warship. Roughly 350 task force participants are collocated on the ship 24 hours a day. The Investigator is able to interact with practically any person at any time during their waking hours or to simply blend into the background and to observe the people, work processes, and technologies in context. Semistructured interviews [72] are used at the beginning of the study, conducted with probing [56] and snowballing [71] techniques, according to the frame summarized in Table 2. This purposeful sample spans four organizational levels, and it includes participants spanning a wide range of different jobs, competencies, and knowledge areas.

However, most interviews are informal, with more than 200 conducted over a threemonth period. The number, time, and scope of interviews continue until theoretical saturation [32] is reached. Because many aspects of the JTF organization, work processes, and technologies are classified, the Investigator is unable to use a tape recorder for interviews or a camera for observations. But extensive notes are taken and summarized daily. And extended collocation onboard ship enables the Investigator to follow up with interviewees where deemed necessary to clarify issues; to delve more deeply into topics of interest; or simply to verify facts, notes, and comments recorded by the Investigator. Although the Investigator enters the field site with theoretical background in KM as well as design, and with an advance understanding of the analytical framework summarized above, initial coding of data is conducted in a grounded theory manner, letting the data speak for themselves. This helps to ensure that initial interpretations are both grounded firmly in the data and meaningful to organizational participants.

The Researcher’s membership in the focal organization enables ethnographic study through participant observation [83] and includes an open invitation to attend all meetings; eat, exercise, and socialize with the crew; carry on e-mail and telephone/ radio conversations at sea; and arrange trips by helicopter to meet and interact with people onboard other ships in the task force. Following Gioia et al. [31], in part, we employ a multistage analytical approach to data collection, analysis, and interpretation. In the primary stage, data collected and analyzed through the course of our fieldwork lead to first-order coding [94], which reflects terms used by organizational participants. Investigator reactions and analyses generate corresponding first-order interpretations, which are meaningful to organizational participants also. Examples of such interpretations include the organization and context of study, areas and sources of knowledge deemed critical by organizational participants, and a preliminary case description. Where warranted by theoretical sampling, many first-order interpretations lead us to additional data collection and analysis at the same level, reflecting terms used directly by organizational participants. This first-order analysis grounds case interpretations in field data.

In the secondary stage, we treat first-order interpretations as “data” for secondorder analysis. This second-order analysis augments its first-order counterpart with theoretical insight and comparison, bringing in the Investigator’s perspective that is informed by the literature. Gioia et al. [31] explain the benefits of using such a multistage approach. They include exposing and integrating different aspects of the phenomena of study that are revealed separately through first-order versus second-order analysis and interpretation.

Although informant views can reveal the rich means or methods by which members can construct reality, they usually do not address the deep structure of experience. Similarly, although the Researcher views tend to gloss the richness of lived experience, they place in bas-relief the dimensions or structure of phenomena. Because the knower and known are interdependent in this process of understanding, however, the most desirable approach is to triangulate insider and outsider views. [31, p. 367]

Examples of such triangulation through second-order analysis include multidimensional classification and visualization of dynamic knowledge using our analytical framework, identification of patterns in the field data, and an augmented case description. As with the first interpretation stage, these second-order interpretations may lead us, in turn, to collect and analyze additional data, to refine our first-order interpretations, to augment our second-order analysis, and so forth. This second-order analysis bridges grounded data and interpretations with theory.

In the tertiary stage, we treat second-order interpretations as “data” for third-order analysis. This third-order analysis extends its second-order counterpart through pattern analysis to identify themes with potential theoretical relevance and to look for implications in terms of design. Such third-order analysis extends beyond the twostage process outlined by Gioia et al. [31] and helps us to generalize beyond the specifics of our case. Examples of third-order interpretations include proposed pattern generalizations, theoretical design insights, and an extended case description. This third-order analysis builds upon both grounded interpretations and theoretical analyses to address our research question focused on design kernel theory.

In addition to the methods and techniques outlined above (e.g., [32, 72, 83, 102]), the study also employs many of the proven tactics for qualitative research outlined by Miles and Huberman [54, pp. 262–276] (e.g., spending a long time on site, taking a low profile, using informants, sampling people with different views, triangulating across multiple data collection techniques), and others (e.g., cultural immersion, multiple verification efforts, emic perspective; see [9]). Such tactics further serve to mitigate potential bias (e.g., stemming from a single investigator). Moreover, repeated member checking [19] is accomplished through daily interaction with the Strategic Contact and periodic follow-up with the many interviewees onboard ship. Comments pertaining to the three stages of case descriptions are also received from the Strategic Contact and other participants in the study. A preliminary summary of study findings and implications is presented to an open audience of participants in the field.

## JTF Organization and Environment

Synthesizing data obtained through our four collection techniques, here we summarize several aspects of the JTF organization and context. In this study, we sailed on the 569-foot warship USS Alpha,<sup>1</sup> which played the central role in command and control of Task Force Operation DELTA WATCH. WATCH was classified. But it involved “projecting military power” overseas and was manifested through a “campaign of combat operations via air, sea, and land.”

The JTF was organized into “functional departments” for division of labor. It relied upon “standard operating procedures” for coordination. Organization leaders employed “large [technical and support] staffs,” “centralized decision making,” and a “unified chain of command.” In these respects, the JTF represented a classic machine bureaucracy [55]. But this military organization also depended heavily upon “professionalism” for coordination, quality, and reliability of its work processes. It was “steeped in tradition,” with “very strong culture” and concomitant norming forces at every level. Attention to detail by senior-level managers—many referred to this as “micromanagement”—abounded. “Because lives are at stake,” one senior officer told us, “commanders and officers at every level bury themselves in the details” of plans and operations. “Being told what to do” via direct supervision, through “many hierarchical levels” of management, also abounded as a mechanism for coordination.

The JTF workforce was diverse. Various units of specialists, “from all [U.S.] services and [from] other countries” as well, learned to coordinate plans, defenses, and attacks quickly and reliably. They interoperated over “wide geographical areas.” In contrast with the centralized organization structure and unified chain of command noted above, however, time constraints “prohibited” centralized decision making and control over many tactical actions. Nonetheless, “potential strategic and political ramifications” of local tactical actions attracted the attention and involvement of leaders and managers at all levels of the organization. “Even the President” receives regular updates on the progress of, and sometimes provides input directly to, certain JTF operations [92].

The JTF organization was seen as “sophisticated” technologically. This was evidenced, in part, by its ability to “plan, coordinate, and execute continuous, sustained, geographically distributed work activities around the clock.” JTF technologies enabled it to transfer information rapidly across great distances. But the JTF was also risk averse. It relied upon “considerable redundancy” and “could fall back [albeit with considerably less speed and efficiency] upon manual operations” for nearly every work task.

JTFs require extensive organization-specific knowledge of employees. People “spent years” learning how to operate specific technological artifacts and “decades” learning how to lead and manage in this military organization. All managers and executives were developed “from within” the organization. Today the military still has negligible avenues for mid-career entry. The JTF Commander had “25 or more years” in the military. A three-star admiral (i.e., the penultimate rank in the U.S. military), this military executive was “probably the most experienced and senior officer” to observe firsthand and to play a direct role in task force operations from beginning to end. The JTF Commander interviewed in this study expressed the importance of having an experienced, empowered, high-level decision maker close to the source of battle action and information: “there’s no substitute for being there and seeing it for yourself.”

As an extension of context, data pertaining to JTF IT artifacts and uses were included to help focus our analysis on technological support to organizational work processes. A quick look at the first four categories of the list (i.e., computer networks, communication networks, Web portals, large computer displays) revealed the same kinds of technologies and uses found in office environments of most organizations (e.g., use Office applications, communicate with others, disseminate information, display computer images for collaboration). The fifth category represents a suite of classified technologies that served specialized warfare purposes and were unlikely to be found in nonmilitary organizations.

In support of JTF work flows, most of these technologies helped to maintain information flows between people on geographically dispersed ships and other JTF units. Selection of the various communication technologies for use by organization participants was based, in large part, on system latency and time-criticality of the task at hand. The Battle Group Staff maintained a set of “stoplight” status charts via intranet Web site on the carrier, which were reviewed regularly by Battle Watch team members using browsers. The JTF Commander and myriad other participants used this same information source “to keep up with the war.” Televised news programs were similarly monitored. Many JTF officers used one of the large projection screens to monitor news headlines. “Sometimes we learn things through CNN before hearing it from Intel [the Intelligence organization],” said one such officer. He added, “of course the enemy monitors the same news programs, so we gain no advantage here; but we need to keep up in order to avoid losing some advantage.”

## Second-Order Analysis

Treating the first-order analysis above as “data” for second-order analysis provided a bridge between grounded and theoretical interpretations. We organize this second results section into two parts: (1) multidimensional classification and (2) multidimensional visualization.

## Multidimensional Classification

Using the data collected, coded, and summarized in Table 5, we sought to employ our analytical framework to represent each knowledge flow in terms of its four dimensions (i.e., explicitness, reach, life cycle, flow time). Through an iterative process, we would depict each knowledge flow in terms of the four dimensions, review such depiction with our Strategic Contact, discuss the corresponding JTF warfare work and knowledge with other organizational participants, and refine our interpretation of the knowledge flows. Where additional data collection seemed warranted, we would return to grounded data and first-order analysis, iterating as many times as necessary to reach theoretical saturation (e.g., no new insights or compelling data collection leads). What resulted was the summary presented in Table 6, which classified each of the ten knowledge flows from above according to the four dimensions of our analytical framework.

We admit to the relative crudeness of this classification table. For instance, each knowledge flow was inherently dynamic, yet the table representation itself is static. The four, dimensional constructs used for classification are quite coarse, with discrete categories employed to represent continuously changing dynamic phenomena. And only the ten knowledge flows identified in the field are included in Table 6, excluding many other flows (e.g., education, communities of practice, use of simulated environments) reported in the literature. Nonetheless, Table 6 provides the means to consider first-order interpretations at a higher analytical level than could be supported directly by grounded data. Yet this second-order analysis remains grounded solidly in the field data. Repeated construction, analysis, member checking, and refinement ensured that the classifications in Table 6 were understandable by JTF participants. But using second-order analysis enabled us to push beyond the practitioner-level views maintained by military participants in the field.

We employ Table 6 here to classify and compare a diversity of knowledge flows in the field. As such, it operationalizes and instantiates the multifaceted nature of dynamic knowledge. For instance, notice the diverse table entries span the ranges (i.e., include extreme as well as intermediate values) of all four dimensions from our analytical framework. We can observe dynamic processes involving knowledge that is tacit (e.g., OJT) in addition to others entailing explicit knowledge (e.g., Intelligence operations), with flows reaching from the individual (e.g., information synthesis) to multiple organizations (e.g., command), and involving a variety of activities along the life cycle (e.g., create, share, apply). It also ascribes explicitly the temporal dimension flow time to field data, indicating huge variation in the relative speeds of different knowledge flows. For instance, some knowledge flows take place on the order of hours (e.g., IS use) and days (e.g., evaluation), whereas others require months (e.g., mentoring) and years (e.g., OJT).

Working from the classification of Table 6, we also sought to visualize knowledge flows. As with Table 6, this visualization is also admittedly crude. It depicts statically knowledge flows that were inherently dynamic. And it fails to capture dynamic interactions between the different knowledge flows and stocks identified in our discussion above. Nonetheless, Figure 1 provides a visual representation of the tabular classification data. As such, it enables multidimensional visualization of ten diverse knowledge flows identified in the field. The figure also helps to identify patterns in the knowledge flow data. For instance, one can observe some correspondence between flow time and the other dimensions. Specifically, the knowledge flows plot roughly along a diagonal from the bottom-left-front corner (i.e., tacit knowledge, narrow organizational reach, long flow time) to the top-right-rear corner (i.e., explicit knowledge, broad organizational reach, short flow time). We include an oval shape in the figure to highlight the associated knowledge flows that align as such along the diagonal. Notice only the knowledge flow Assignment (i.e., moving knowledge with people from job to job) is outside of the oval and hence diverges a bit from this diagonal.

Visualization through Figure 1 helped us also to delineate another pattern: this one in terms of absence. In contrast with the diagonal pattern noted above, which extends roughly from one corner of the graph to another, notice also that several, relatively large regions of the three-dimensional space are empty (i.e., have no corresponding knowledge flows plotted there). For instance, the region on the “floor” of the figure (i.e., in the tacit plane, low on the explicitness axis) is empty where reach is broad (e.g., organization, interorganization levels). Other “empty” regions of this figure (e.g., where tacit knowledge would involve short flow time) become apparent as well through visualization.

When coupled with multidimensional classification, visualization of dynamic knowledge provided insight into modal variations of, and dynamic interactions between, knowledge flows. Take the OJT process, for instance. Through grounded data collection and analysis, we had captured the learning part of OJT well (i.e., corresponding to the activity knowledge creation along the life cycle dimension). But the direct experience comprising OJT clearly involved a doing part (i.e., corresponding to the activity knowledge application) also. And analysis of Figure 1 suggested that flow time for applying knowledge through doing should be notably shorter than that required for creating knowledge through learning. JTF participants in the field confirmed that even new knowledge could be applied generally within a matter of “days.” Hence the OJT knowledge flow had two, distinct, dynamic modes—one for learning, another for doing. Each mode had qualitatively different flow times (e.g., years versus days), and each represented a unique position along the life cycle dimension (i.e., knowledge creation versus knowledge application). ## Third-Order Analysis

Treating the second-order interpretations above as “data” for third-order analysis provided the means to identify themes and to analyze knowledge flow patterns. At this tertiary stage of analysis, we build upon our bridging of grounded and theoretical interpretations discussed above. We use results of such bridging here to generalize beyond the specifics of the case and to articulate chunks of design kernel theory. We organize the discussion below according to the three high-level themes that emerged through third-order analysis: (1) empirical uses of the analytical framework, (2) dynamic knowledge patterns, and (3) flow linkages. Table 8 summarizes representative second-order “data” and third-order implications associated with each theme.

## Theme 1: Empirical Uses of the Analytical Framework

Recall that the multidimensional, analytical framework used to guide our field research was theoretical: its use in the field represented an empirical consideration to be addressed through the study. Building upon our second-order analysis, several results are noteworthy in this regard. For instance, we note above how all ten of the knowledge flows identified empirically in the case could be both classified and plotted multidimensionally, and how knowledge flow data summarized in Tables 6 and 7 span the ranges of all four analytical dimensions. This illustrates how the analytical framework developed from theory can be used to represent, visualize, and analyze dynamic knowledge empirically. Representing, visualizing, and analyzing stocks and flows of information and data (e.g., via ERDs, DFDs, object models) comprise core techniques of IS design today. By extending such representation, visualization, and analysis to stocks and flows of knowledge (i.e., using our multidimensional framework), we can extend ISDT to address meta-requirements focused on promoting knowledge flows.

## Theme 2: Dynamic Knowledge Patterns

We noted above how the knowledge flows identified empirically in the case plotted in a rough “diagonal” pattern along the three dimensions in Figure 1. We also noted how knowledge flows that cluster in the opposing “corners” of this diagonal represent distinct classes (e.g., sticky versus fluid) in terms of dynamic knowledge. Visualization and analysis enables us further to interrelate these various dimensions of dynamic knowledge. For instance, if a designer were to view the dimensions reach and flow time in terms of performance variables of interest, he or she could extract a valuable relationship from the pattern. Specifically, say that broad organizational reach and short flow time represent goals to be supported through design. Data plotted in Figure 1 suggest that knowledge must take explicit form in order to flow broadly and rapidly, and, hence, that the designer should pursue the tactic of codifying tacit knowledge to enable broad and rapid flows.

Of course, research in artificial intelligence (AI) has been pursuing similar goals through knowledge codification for decades [21, 80]. The diagonal knowledge pattern suggests more than simply a call to integrate AI technologies into KM. Despite the power and progress of AI, its success has been mixed, myriad attempts to represent and codify tacit knowledge have failed, and codification of tacit knowledge remains a challenging problem (see, e.g., [38, 43, 67]). The diagonal knowledge pattern also suggests that other approaches to design—involving nontechnological artifacts, and hence not requiring knowledge to be codified—may be more appropriate for enhancing dynamic knowledge in tacit form. Examples of such nontechnological approaches described in the literature include organizational design (i.e., focused on organizational structure and behavior), business process reengineering (i.e., focused on organizational processes), and human resources (i.e., focused on personnel development and training).

Further, we note above how the knowledge flows identified empirically in the case plotted in a manner that revealed several “empty” spaces. Tacit knowledge to be shared either broadly or quickly represented clear examples. Theoretically, this suggests that organizational processes and technologies to enable such knowledge flows may not yet exist. Indeed, the analytical framework differentiates knowledge into (at least) 240 theoretically distinct segments across its four-dimensional space (i.e., 2 categories of explicitness × 4 categories of reach × 6 categories of life cycle × 5 orders of magnitude in terms of flow time). But most knowledge segments (especially outside the “diagonal”) are empty. As such, each empty region of multidimensional knowledge space highlights an opportunity to develop new IT capable of extending knowledge flows to the region. In a design sense, this multidimensional framework can be employed as a generator of new technological ideas.

Finally, we noted above multiple dynamic modes associated with two knowledge flows in the field: OJT and training. Through pattern visualization, we diagrammed how dynamic knowledge trajectories corresponding to OJT and training flows differed markedly in terms of the dimension explicitness. In particular, the multimodal OJT flows cycle iteratively within the tacit plane, whereas the multimodal training trajectories rise up from this plane. Yet, despite such qualitative differences in terms of behavior, these two knowledge flows were both noted in the case as critical sources of knowledge about how to perform workplace jobs. In an economic sense, dynamic knowledge flows associated with OJT and training represent (perhaps imperfect) substitutes; that is, one flow can be substituted (to some extent) for another in terms of preparing employees to perform particular jobs. In a design sense, OJT and training represent two process alternatives for promoting knowledge flows. Notice these process alternatives may or may not involve IT. Yet process alternatives clearly involve design (e.g., of the process environment within which—conceivably very different— IT would be required to function) as much as do technological alternatives (e.g., different software architectures). Incorporating process alternatives into design can extend ISDT to address meta-requirements focused on promoting knowledge flows.

## Theme 3: Flow Linkages

Findings from the case data and analysis above suggest a number of linkages between different kinds of flows—including dynamic knowledge, information, data, and work— and technologies. For instance, knowledge that was created (i.e., through learning)

and added to stock could be applied very quickly (i.e., through doing) to accomplish work. And work accomplished in the enterprise contributed toward organizational performance. In this respect, knowledge flows were prerequisite to work flows. And work flows were responsible for performance in the enterprise. But performing work also led to the creation of new knowledge. In this respect, work flows were essential for continued knowledge flows. And work flows mediated the effect of knowledge flows on organizational performance.

These patterns suggest that the dynamics of knowledge involve cycles between knowledge creation and application (i.e., between learning and doing). Hence they link knowledge flows with work flows and, in turn, with organizational performance. This reflects the tension described above between exploration (i.e., learning to acquire new knowledge) and exploitation (i.e., working with existing knowledge). But instead of exploration and exploitation (i.e., learning and doing) representing competing pursuits, as suggested in some parts of the literature (see, e.g., [52]), the dynamic knowledge patterns observed and interpreted here reveal that exploration and exploitation are complementary processes, as suggested in other parts of the literature (see, e.g., [65]). Here, our empirical interpretation provides some evidence to help resolve an ongoing theoretical debate.

Moreover, data from the case indicate most IT was employed to support information flows in the organization. But flows of information were essential to many flows of knowledge. Situational awareness knowledge, for instance, was noted above as depending critically upon the knowledge flow information synthesis as a source. The information synthesis knowledge flow was supported by multiple information flows (e.g., via intelligence reports, satellite communications, Web portals, e-mail, and chat), which were supported, in turn, by different IT artifacts (e.g., computers, networks, search engines). Hence, we identify an extended, dynamic linkage between flows of knowledge, flows of information, and IT artifacts. Such linkage is consistent with several theoretical views of information and knowledge [82, 91, 96]. But most such views are static in their depiction of information and knowledge concepts, and they fail to describe how information flows mediate between knowledge flows and IT artifacts. Here, we address dynamic flows and reveal this mediation pattern.

Further, IS have been designed for decades to exploit linkages between flows of data/information and flows of work in the organization. Use cases, for instance, indicate explicitly how IT artifacts—along with the data and information they handle— are used in organizational processes to perform work. Hence, extant ISDT includes linkages already among data, information, and work. Now, by also linking knowledge to information and, in turn, to the IT artifact, we can extend concepts and techniques from design theory to address meta-requirements focused on promoting knowledge flows. For instance, by drawing from our analytical framework, use cases could be extended to address explicitly: what kinds of knowledge (e.g., tacit, explicit) are associated with work flows, what levels of social aggregation (e.g., individual, organization) are associated with such knowledge, what knowledge activities (e.g., creation, sharing) are involved, and what flow times (e.g., rapid, leisurely) are required. Moreover, by linking, in turn, information flows to knowledge flows and by linking further knowledge flows to work flows, we establish an expanded domain of discourse through which IS designers can communicate with knowledge managers. To date, such discourse has been limited principally to technological considerations.

## Propositional Model

We tie together these linkages and relationships with ISDT through the propositional model diagrammed in Figure 4. Following Walls et al. [97], this propositional model articulates new chunks of kernel theory that govern design requirements, and hence extend ISDT, to address meta-requirements focused on promoting knowledge flows. Moreover, such propositional model is rooted firmly in empirical data and analysis through our case study. Hence, its concepts and linkages offer potential for operationalization into the kinds of testable research hypotheses noted as important for developing or extending ISDT. Such hypotheses can address the model as a whole as well as its individual relationships.

The box in the lower-left of Figure 4 represents the traditional domain of IS design (e.g., as covered in most textbooks). This domain includes the IT artifact and the direct influences it has on flows of both data and information. Phenomena and relationships within this box are well understood within extant ISDT and not addressed further here. Beyond this box, we include labels to represent knowledge flows, work flows, and organizational performance. With the exception of work flows—which, as noted above, are addressed, in part, through design techniques such as use cases— these phenomena are generally beyond the traditional IS design domain. Yet the diagram follows our findings from the case to indicate that IS design has an indirect influence over such phenomena through four linkages.

First, the linkage between information flows and knowledge flows is labeled “mediate” (see Figure 4) to represent our finding that flows of information mediate the effect of IT artifacts on knowledge flows. To avoid complicating the model unduly, we do not differentiate here between different types of knowledge (e.g., tacit, explicit, individual, organizational). But findings from the case clearly suggest that IT supports flows of explicit knowledge differently than it does tacit knowledge flows. This provides a basis for developing new, testable research hypotheses regarding direct linkages between knowledge flows and information flows, in addition to a mediating linkage with IT artifacts. A possible extension to this propositional model could incorporate tacit and explicit knowledge flows as two separate phenomena in Figure 4.

Second, the linkage between knowledge flows and work flows is labeled “enable” (see Figure 4) to represent our reflexive finding: flows of knowledge enable work to be performed (i.e., through knowledge application), and flows of work enable knowledge to be stocked (i.e., through knowledge creation). Here, dynamic knowledge represents a prerequisite to the accomplishment of work (e.g., employees must know how to perform their job activities before being able to do so). But the accomplishment of work also represents a source of new knowledge. This provides a basis for developing new, testable research hypotheses regarding bidirectional linkages between knowledge flows and work flows, in addition to indirect linkages with IT artifacts. A possible extension to this propositional model could incorporate knowledge stocks and flows as distinct, tightly intertwined, dynamic phenomena (e.g., as delineated in Figure 2).

Third, the linkage between work flows and organizational performance is labeled “drive” (see Figure 4) to represent our finding that work affects performance directly and mediates the effect of knowledge flows on organizational performance. Knowledge stocks and flows affect work directly, as outlined above. But we find no evidence from the case to suggest that knowledge flows have a direct linkage to organizational performance. Yet they link back—through mediating information flows—to the IT artifact. This provides a basis for developing new, testable research hypotheses regarding direct linkages between organizational work flows and performance, in addition to separate, indirect linkages with both knowledge flows and IT artifacts. A possible extension to this propositional model could address how different kinds of knowledge interrelate with different kinds of work to affect organizational performance differently.

Finally, the linkage between work flows and information flows is labeled “reinforce” (see Figure 4) to represent our conclusion that work and information are reinforcing mutually. Flows of information are essential to most knowledge work. And most knowledge work produces flows of information. We include this linkage to suggest that the IT artifact, through its direct influence on information flows, can also affect work flows—and hence organizational performance—indirectly without being mediated by flows of knowledge. Instead, this would suggest the relationship between information flows and organizational performance is mediated by work flows but is moderated by knowledge flows. This provides a basis for developing new, testable research hypotheses regarding bidirectional linkages between work flows and information flows, in addition to respective mediating and moderating linkages between work flows, knowledge flows, and organizational performance. A possible extension to this propositional model could differentiate between how different kinds of knowledge stocks and flows moderate various work flows differently.

## Conclusion

KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTS A CRITICAL RESOURCE in the modern enterprise. But it is dynamic and distributed unevenly. Capitalizing on this dynamic resource for enterprise performance depends upon its rapid and reliable flows across people, organizations, locations, and times of application. From a technological perspective, this points immediately to the design of IS to enhance knowledge flows. The problem is, the design of information systems to enhance knowledge flows requires new understanding.

The research described in this paper concentrates on understanding the dynamics of knowledge phenomenologically and on developing techniques for modeling and visualizing dynamic knowledge flows and stocks. We draw key, theoretical concepts from multiple literatures and we build upon integrative modeling work to compose a parsimonious, multidimensional, analytical framework for representing and visualizing dynamic knowledge. We then conduct field research to learn how this theoretical framework may be used to model knowledge flows in practice. By focusing this empirical work on an extreme organization and processes that involve and rely upon tacit knowledge, we illustrate how dynamic knowledge patterns can inform design in new ways. New chunks of kernel theory deriving from this fieldwork are articulated in terms of a propositional model, which provides a basis for development of testable design theory hypotheses.

Of course, every study has limitations. The present research investigates only a single case, and the extreme nature of the focal organization poses questions in terms of generalizing our case results. Also, the case study does not examine organizations or work processes that engage directly in IS design or development, which poses questions in terms of linking our results to the design of IT artifacts. Further, the study employs qualitative research methods that involve considerable interpretation and potential for bias. Other, well-understood limitations of fieldwork (e.g., lack of control, threats to internal validity; see [12]) apply here as well.

Alternatively, we employ rigorous, well-accepted, case study research methods to investigate a challenging “how” question, which is not amenable to investigation through most alternate methods. And we utilize multiple, triangulated, data collection and analysis techniques to overcome many of the limitations inherent in a study such as this. We also step, as transparently as possible, through three analytical levels—interpretations that are meaningful to organizational participants, the researcher’s theoretical lens, and pattern analysis—moving systematically from raw field data to a propositional model and associated ISDT extension. Further, our third-order analysis abstracts from the details of the case study. And the propositional model remains general, expanding the purview of IS design well beyond the IT artifact.

The next logical research steps would involve operationalizing testable research hypotheses from our propositional model and subjecting them to rigorous, empirical scrutiny. Fieldwork to study and compare additional cases—perhaps utilizing alternate theoretical frameworks and investigating less extreme, IS design organizations directly—represents a parallel path. Other, multidisciplinary paths to investigate dynamic knowledge flows and stocks—such as computational modeling, vector measurement, inventory optimization, and organizational design—are probably less obvious to the IS researcher. But, together, these various research paths offer potential to unshackle designers from the limitations of IT artifacts: allowing them to benefit from ISDT extended to address meta-requirements focused on promoting knowledge flows.

## Appendix. Interview Guide

THE QUESTIONS BELOW ARE DESIGNED to promote candid and open discussions with participants. The questions derive in part from the protocol developed by Nissen et al. [63] for understanding knowledge systems and processes, and they are specifically crafted to address this case study. The goal is to ask each participant to answer every question, but the interview format leaves ample room for probing, and sufficient time is scheduled in each interview for respondents to mention issues not covered by the specific questions. Through communication by the CJTF, all interviewees know who we are, why we are here, that we are part of the task force staff, and that their participation is expected.

## Introduction

The purpose of this study is to identify the knowledge that is most important for JTF success in [DELTA WATCH] and to better understand how such knowledge is obtained when and where it is needed. We would like to ask you a few questions pertaining to your JTF organization, role, job, and knowledge. Although we are taking notes, you will not be identified by name in any publication, so we encourage you to be candid and open. Also, we have a whole hour scheduled for this interview, so there is ample time for you to elaborate on important points or introduce ideas beyond the set of questions that we ask. You should also know that I have a [level] security clearance, and I am authorized to participate in JTF meetings and discussions. Do you have any questions before we begin?
