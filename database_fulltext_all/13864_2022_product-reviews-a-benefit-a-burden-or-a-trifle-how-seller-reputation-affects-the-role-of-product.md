# PRODUCT REVIEWS: A BENEFIT, A BURDEN, OR A TRIFLE? HOW SELLER REPUTATION AFFECTS THE ROLE OF PRODUCT REVIEWS <sup>1</sup>

Hongpeng Wang School of Management, Lanzhou University, Lanzhou, Gansu P. R., CHINA and School of Economics and Management, Xidian University, Xi’an, Shaanxi P.R., CHINA {whp@lzu.edu.cn}

Rong Du School of Economics and Management, Xidian University, Xi’an, Shaanxi P. R., CHINA {durong@mail.xidian.edu.cn}

Wenqi Shen Department of Business Information Technology, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, VA, U.S.A. {shenw@vt.edu}

Liangfei Qiu Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, Warrington College of Business, University of Florida, Gainesville, FL, U.S.A. {liangfei.qiu@warrington.ufl.edu}

Weiguo Fan Department of Business Analytics, Tippie College of Business, University of Iowa, Iowa City, IA, U.S.A. {weiguo-fan@uiowa.edu}

The sales effect of product reviews has been a contentious issue with competing perspectives about when product reviews serve as a benefit, a burden, or a trifle. Unlike previous research that separately investigates the impact of each eWOM system, our study empirically examines the interaction effects of dual eWOM systems, i.e., product reviews and seller reputation. Drawing on reference point theory, we find that seller reputation systems play a reference-point role and determine the efficacy of product reviews. Specifically, negative reviews cause a significant loss in sales for high-reputation sellers but are less detrimental for low-reputation sellers. In contrast, positive reviews can boost sales for low-reputation sellers but are less helpful for high-reputation sellers. These results highlight that seller reputation is a double-edged sword. While a high seller reputation can reduce seller uncertainty and attract more consumers, it may also raise consumers’ expectations and lead to potential negative expectancy violations. Moreover, we explore what strategies may help mitigate the potentially detrimental effect of reference points for high-reputation sellers. Through the lens of restructuring reference points, the reputation reference effect can be adjusted in a more dynamic reputation system (e.g., a reputation badge). Compared to sellers that have never lost their top-rated badge, sellers that have lost their toprated badge may face an attenuated detrimental impact on sales from the negative expectancy violation due to negative reviews and enjoy a positive impact from positive reviews. We discuss the implications of our findings for both theory and practice.

Keywords: Reference point, electronic word-of-mouth, seller reputation, product reviews

## Introduction

Online retailing has grown tremendously over the past decade. While there are many reasons for this, electronic word-ofmouth (eWOM) has been hailed as the holy grail of ecommerce success (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006). Because eWOM, such as product reviews and seller reputation, can significantly minimize product and seller uncertainty perceived by consumers, e-retailers have expended considerable efforts in developing eWOM systems (Hui et al., 2016; Jiang & Guo, 2015; Mudambi & Schuff, 2010). Collectively, eWOM investment accounts for over 25% of a firm’s entire marketing budget (Social Distillery, 2014). Despite the eagerness of most e-retailers to embrace eWOM, the return on eWOM investment usually fails to match their expectations. For example, eMarketer (2017) reports that more than 80% of marketers regret spending so much money on eWOM content. Several studies have attempted to explain the underlying mechanisms behind eWOM inefficacy (Babić et al., 2016; Cabral & Hortacsu, 2010). However, they have not considered the coexistence of multiple eWOM systems that may have a joint effect on consumers’ purchase decisions.

To bridge this research gap, we systematically investigate the interaction between product reviews and seller reputation behind eWOM by adopting multiple identification strategies. We begin by asking our first research question: (1) What is the impact of positive/negative reviews on the sales of a highreputation seller? Specifically, given that seller reputation is formed based on the seller’s performance, how it has behaved over time? A high-reputation (i.e., well-reputed) seller generally creates high expectations in the minds of consumers, which becomes a reference point for consumers. The prior literature in economics and psychology has demonstrated that reference points play a critical role in decision-making (e.g., Hart et al., 2008; Kamins et al., 2004; Petkova et al., 2014). Both individual and organizational choices, to a large extent, depend on whether people see one option as being above (better than) or below (worse than) a specific reference point (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974).

In e-marketplaces, consumers may also use contextual information to interpret product review ratings. That is, they typically do not focus solely on the absolute value of the product review ratings but put the review ratings in a context to interpret them. As each product belongs to a specific seller, consumers face uncertainty in terms of both product and seller quality. Product uncertainty can be shaped by seller uncertainty (Dimoka et al., 2012). If consumers believe in a well-reputed seller, they tend to think that the seller’s products are likely to be of high quality (Mavlanova et al., 2016). Sellers whom consumers deem to be unreliable, even without opportunistic behaviors, are less likely to convince consumers to accept their products. The carryover effect from a seller to a product implies that online consumers may consider seller reputation (i.e., one key indicator of seller quality) as a reference point in assessing product quality through product reviews. Similar to the various settings examined in previous studies on contextdependent decisions (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Kamins et al., 2004), consumers may compare product review ratings with seller reputation (as a benchmark).

Therefore, if a product sold by a seller receives a negative review rating, the violation of the high (i.e., good) seller reputation comes as a shock to consumers. Such a negative expectancy violation may convey a strong signal to consumers that the product is of poor quality and, in turn, decrease sales. In contrast, when a product sold by a highreputation seller receives a positive review rating, consumers may take such consistent information to be a simple confirmation of their expectations. To some extent, decisionmaking processes hinge on the degree of the discrepancy between the incoming cues and the initial baseline. If there is no clear distinction between a high reputation and positive reviews, consumers may not be overly impressed by the new information presented in positive product reviews. As a result, positive reviews may not significantly improve product sales for high-reputation sellers. In other words, for high-reputation sellers, negative reviews are treated as a burden, and positive reviews are treated as a trifle.

Along similar lines, we ask our second research question: (2) What is the impact of positive/negative reviews on the sales of a low-reputation seller? Specifically, a consumer may form low expectations of a low-reputation (i.e., poorly reputed) seller, which may serve as a benchmark for the consumer when processing product review information. When a product sold by a low-reputation seller gains positive review ratings, the contrast against the context information (low benchmark) conveys a strong psychological cue to consumers that the product is of high quality. Because of the positive expectancy violation, consumers are more likely to make purchase decisions.

On the other hand, negative reviews for sellers with low (i.e., poor) reputations may not further decrease their product sales since these negative reviews simply confirm consumers’ low expectations of low-reputation sellers. For example, McGuire (1961) shows that presenting weaknesses or negative cues is an effective strategy to prevent detrimental outcomes. When negative information is disclosed by an organization itself, a crisis may not harm it substantially, as consumers have already established low expectations (Chandler et al., 2020; Gwebu et al., 2018; Wagner et al., 2009). Therefore, for lowreputation sellers, positive reviews are treated as a benefit, and negative reviews are treated as a trifle.

Our study provides a pioneering effort to investigate the introduction of a new eBay product review system, which offers the unique opportunity to explore the interactions between product reviews and seller reputation systems (e.g., short-term and long-term reputation). Most online platforms have either one or both simultaneously available. It is often difficult for researchers to estimate a baseline level of product sales without the product review system and tease out the interaction effects in those cases. Through this unique setting, we are able to show that seller reputation represents a critical moderating condition on which the effectiveness of product reviews depends. Negative reviews cause a significant loss in sales for highreputation sellers but are less detrimental for low-reputation sellers. In contrast, positive reviews can boost sales for lowreputation sellers but cannot do the same for high-reputation sellers. In other words, a high reputation is a double-edged sword. While a high seller reputation can reduce seller uncertainty (Pavlou et al., 2007; Dimoka et al., 2012) and attract more consumers, it may also be a type of “winner’s curse,” which may raise consumers’ expectations (reference point) and lead to potential negative expectancy violations. These results offer important practical implications for sellers and online platforms and remind them to pay close attention to the interaction effect between seller reputation and product reviews.

We explore ways of mitigating the potential “winner’s curse” of high-reputation sellers (the detrimental effect of negative reviews). In particular, we examine whether a more dynamic reputation system may play a role in alleviating the contextual effect of the reference point for high-reputation sellers. In our context, a reputation badge (such as “top-rated seller”) is a dynamic reputation system because it can be obtained or taken away over short time periods. We find that compared to sellers that have never lost their top-rated badge, sellers that have lost their top-rated badge may face an attenuated detrimental impact from the negative expectancy violation due to negative reviews and enjoy positive impacts from positive reviews. This result further validates the reference-point effect of seller reputation in our context.

## Literature Review

## Seller Reputation and Product Reviews

Compared to traditional markets, consumers in emarketplaces where it is not possible to directly observe sellers and physically examine products generally experience a high degree of both seller and product uncertainty (Pavlou et al., 2007; Dimoka et al., 2012). eWOM systems, including seller reputation and product reviews, are widely used on prominent e-commerce websites, such as Amazon, eBay, and

Taobao, to prevent potential problems arising from information asymmetry. The role of different eWOM systems is of great interest to marketing and information systems researchers. One research stream has found that seller reputation can serve as a proxy for consumer trust in an online store (Ba & Pavlou, 2002). Sellers with a good reputation can reduce consumers’ risk perceptions, charge higher prices, and gain more profit (Cabral & Hortacsu, 2010; Dellarocas, 2003; Hui et al., 2016). Another research stream has revealed that product reviews are a marketing communication tool (Chen & Xie, 2008). Not only can consumers utilize such information to learn about product quality or fitness (Hong & Pavlou, 2014; Kim & Krishnan, 2015), but sellers and manufacturers can also use them to make pricing adjustments or develop products (Archak et al., 2011; Kwark et al., 2014).

While seller reputation and product reviews have received significant academic attention, scrutiny of the eWOM literature indicates that previous studies have mainly investigated the performance impact of different eWOM systems separately and failed to provide consistent evidence (Carter et al., 2016; Ou & Chan, 2014). For example, Chevalier and Mayzlin (2006) found that the stimulating effect of positive reviews on sales is less potent than the detrimental effect of negative reviews. However, negative reviews can be perceived as much more credible and even improve sales (Ho-Dac et al., 2013). Cabral and Hortacsu (2010) and Resnick et al. (2006) also illustrated that positive vs. negative feedback scores have little or no impact on consumers’ willingness to pay. Although prior research has attempted to distinguish the characteristics of products, consumers, and platforms (Babić et al., 2016; Floyd et al., 2014; Zhu & Zhang, 2010), the interaction effects of seller reputation and product reviews have rarely been discussed. When consumers make online purchases, they inevitably need to process both seller- and product-related information (Akdeniz et al., 2013). The examination of how these two types of information cues interact in influencing consumer purchase decisions is imperative for developing a complete picture of eWOM effectiveness.

## Interaction of Multiple Cues

Cue utilization theory (CUT) suggests that consumers may rely on various information cues in their judgments and choices (Olson, 1978). Much of the corresponding work has focused its efforts on understanding how the multiple-cue effects, namely, cannibalization and synergy, work jointly in the formation of an overall evaluation (Akdeniz et al., 2013; Elfenbein et al., 2015; Hu et al., 2010). Through the lens of CUT, the cue utilization process can be involved in obtaining information from the decision-making environment, assigning importance weights to different cues, and utilizing the relevant information to produce purchase behaviors (Purohi & Srivastava, 2001). As such, consumer assessments consist of an array of information cues that can be classified as intrinsic or extrinsic (Richardson et al., 1994). Intrinsic cues are considered part of seller qualification or product quality itself. However, extrinsic cues are seller- or product-related attributes that are not inherent to the seller/product being evaluated. While intrinsic and extrinsic cues are useful, consumers may be more inclined to rely on extrinsic cues when intrinsic cues are not readily available or not easily recognized (Dawar & Parker, 1994; Zeithaml, 1988). These results elucidate that not all cues will be equally used in an overall assessment and some cues will be more important than others when they are integrated.

This line of argumentation is consistent with the notion that humans rarely consider things in absolute terms: they tend to evaluate changes and differences (Tversky & Kahneman, 1991). Comparing the information cue (C<sub>1</sub>) to another cue (C<sub>2</sub>) may result in either a positive deviation (i.e., $C _ { 1 } < C _ { 2 } ,$ a perceived gain for individuals) or a negative deviation (i.e., C<sub>1</sub> $> C _ { 2 } ,$ a perceived loss). In the frame of “gain” vs. “loss,” decision makers seem to behave entirely differently (Hardie et al., 1993). While some studies have explored the relative roles of different information cues on online marketplaces, they have mainly compared multiple intrinsic cues (e.g., product reviews or seller reputation) to other extrinsic cues (e.g., sales volume or prices) and ignored how consumers simultaneously process intrinsic cues (Chen et al., 2011; Ho-Dac et al., 2013; Melnik & Alm, 2005; Miyazaki et al., 2005). When there is a discrepancy between product reviews and seller reputation, the joint effects may not be complementary or substitutive. The aim of our research is to better understand the interaction effect between multiple eWOM systems.

## Research Methodology

## Empirical Setting and Data

We obtained data from eBay.com to empirically investigate our research questions. eBay is one of the world’s largest online marketplaces, with more than 1.3 billion listing products and over 180 million active users (Statista, 2020). Since its launch on September 3, 1995, eBay has been working on the regulation of trading behaviors and the protection of consumer interests. Many platform-based functions, such as reputation systems and money-back guarantees were initiated on eBay, producing farreaching effects on platform design of the whole industry. Other leading e-marketplaces (e.g., Amazon.com and Taobao.com) and nascent online platforms (i.e., Wayfair.com and Airbnb.com) provide similar features as eBay to prevent a market full of lemons. Whereas a growing body of studies is paying attention to the development of eWOM systems (Jiang & Guo, 2015; Masclet & Pénard, 2012), eBay’s eWOM systems are arguably the most established and the most scrutinized by academics and practitioners (Hui et al., 2016; Ye et al., 2014). A recent eWOM improvement on eBay—namely, a voluntary product review system (eBay, 2016)—offers a unique opportunity for us to find answers to our research questions. We subsequently explain how the new product review system works, especially as it relates to our research.

Before May 6, 2016, eBay provided reputation systems only to help consumers make purchase decisions. The implementation of the product review system adds new information to the prior information context (from reputation systems only to dual eWOM systems), which constructs an ideal setting to disentangle the seller reputation’s reference effects. We further examine how consumers change their purchase behaviors as they gain new information cues (i.e., product reviews) in addition to the original information cue (i.e., seller reputation). Interestingly, unlike the mandatory product review system supplied by other platforms, sellers on eBay can decide whether and when to adopt the product review system. This unique function enables us to not only more effectively estimate a baseline of sales for nonadopters but also to assess how sales differ across adopters with different reputation levels.

## Data Acquisition

Our data span sixteen weeks from April 8, 2016, to July 30, 2016, which consists of a 4-week pre-adoption period (i.e., before the introduction of the product review system) and a 12-week post-adoption period (i.e., after the introduction of the product review system). We selected five product categories with a wide variety of items and enough sales to represent the market population on Terapeak, an exclusive eBay insights tool. In addition, these product categories covered both search goods (e.g., digital cameras, smartphones, and laptops) and experience goods (e.g., perfumes and cosmetics) based on Nelson’s categorization (Nelson, 1970). We designed a Python-based cloud agent for each product to crawl the top 1500 items on the daily search page and record the corresponding information on the product page. Since sellers on eBay can easily list and remove items, the dynamic listings constructed an unbalanced panel data set. To examine more similar items, we considered only the items in the buy-it-now format and new-item condition. Our final unbalanced panel data included 642,459 observations for 16,310 items. The following subsections elaborate on the variables of interest.

eWOM: When sellers on eBay complete a transaction, their feedback scores increase by 1 if the buyer posts positive feedback and decrease by 1 if the buyer leaves negative feedback. We mapped 12 reputation grades based on the cumulative feedback scores, which are key metrics indicating sellers’ long-term reputation ratings. A “yellow shooting star” seller (i.e., with more than 10,000 feedback scores) is considered a very high-reputation seller. eBay also reports the number of positive, neutral, and negative feedback scores in the last month for each seller. The most recent feedback is a measure of a seller’s short-term reputation ratings. Sellers that meet specific requirements, such as at least 98% positive feedback, low dispute rates, and selling more than 100 items, are awarded a top-rated seller (TRS) badge during the middle ten days of each month. If sellers fail to meet these requirements, the TRS badge is revoked. The official certification—especially in terms of whether sellers abruptly gained or lost the badge—allowed us to identify a dynamic effect of seller reputation. In addition, we tracked the indicator “Be the first to write a review” to determine when a seller adopted the product review system and recorded the product rating scores from consumers.

Sales: Unlike prior studies using sales rank as a proxy for product sales (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006), our dependent variable, $S a l e s _ { i j t } ,$ represents the actual sales volume on day t. We calculated daily sales via sales history data and log-transformed the variable to reduce skewness and facilitate interpretation (Chen et al., 2019; Fan et al., 2016).

Control variable: To better examine our conjectures, we set several theoretically salient control variables at the individual product and seller levels. The relevant variables include product price, number of inquiries, product inventory, product return, product payment, product description, and the length of membership. ## Econometric Specification

Given the structure of our data set, we operationalized all relevant variables at the seller-product daily level. Let subscript i index each seller. Subscript j denotes the corresponding product that seller i sold, and subscript t is the index of each day in the 16 weeks. Inspired by eBay’s introduction of the product review system, we applied a difference-in-differences (DID) approach to investigate the interaction effects between seller reputation and product reviews on product sales. The benchmark regression is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \log \left(S a l e s _ {i j t}\right) = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} A f t e r _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {2} A d o p t i o n _ {i j} \times A f t e r _ {i j t} + \Theta X _ {i j t} + \\ v _ {i j} + \mu_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t}, \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

where Adoption<sub>ij</sub> is a dummy variable that equals 1 for sellers that adopt the product review system and 0 otherwise; After<sub>ijt</sub> takes the values 1 and 0 for the post-adoption period and preadoption period, respectively. $X _ { i j t }$ represents a vector of covariates that is used to alleviate the possible concern of timevarying confounding factors. The two-way fixed-effects model, including both time-fixed effects $( \mu _ { t } )$ and seller-product fixed effects (ν<sub>ij</sub>), was also employed to control for time shocks and unobserved time-invariant heterogeneity. Moreover, we ran several tests to determine whether the common trends assumption is satisfied. The detailed results shown in Appendix A suggest that the pre-treatment trend is indeed parallel, which provides evidence supporting a valid DID modeling framework. We can thus further specify additional investigations.

## Model Calibration

One could argue that the proposed empirical model may suffer from several endogeneity issues. First, a seller’s decision to adopt the product review system is likely to be endogenous. Because eBay initiated a voluntary product review system, it is possible that seller-specific heterogeneity, for example in terms of marketing strategies, jointly impacts sellers’ adoption behavior and their sales performance. There could also be an endogeneity issue arising from self-selection bias. Second, whether a product receives positive or negative reviews does not occur randomly. The valence of product reviews is contingent on the inherent characteristics of the product or may even be indirectly influenced by other consumers’ preferences. If sample grouping is not assigned randomly, the comparison of different groups could lead to sample selection bias and destroy statistical equivalence and comparability. Third, seller reputation can be an endogenous variable. For example, unobserved operational capacity might simultaneously affect both product sales and seller reputation. Short-term and long-term feedback scores, as well as top-rated seller badges, often correlate with omitted factors, which may result in simultaneity or reverse causality. To alleviate the above endogeneity concerns, we outline our strategies in Figure 1.

Heckman-type Model (HTM): To address the concern of self-selection bias, we adopted a Heckman-type procedure with our DID models (Brown & Mergoupis, 2011). Before running the regression equations (1a-1d), we leveraged a probit model to account for the possible selection process. That is, some types of sellers are willing to adopt the product review system because they have established good electronic word-of-mouth. The seller selection behavior can be predicted as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} w _ {i j t} ^ {*} = \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} \text {Product\_Description} _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {2} \text {Reputation\_Grade} _ {i j t} \\ & + \gamma_ {3} \text {Top\_Rated\_Seller} _ {i j t} \\ & + \gamma_ {4} \text {Recent\_Feedback} _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {5} \text {New\_Seller} _ {i j t} + \tau_ {i j t} (2) \end{array}
$$

Figure 1. Research Design in Addressing Endogeneity

Interactive Fixed-Effect Model (IFEct): Although traditional PSM can account for the observable differences between sellers in the treatment group and those in the control group, it is possible that unobservable differences in these two groups may distinctly drive adoption behaviors (Manchanda et al., 2015). To overcome this limitation, we introduced a latent-factor approach that has become somewhat popular in the economics literature (Gobillon et al., 2016; Xu, 2017). The basic idea is that time-varying covariates can be decomposed into time-varying factors and unit-specific factor loadings. Incorporating unit-specific intercepts $( \lambda _ { i j } )$ interacting with time-varying coefficients (F<sub>t</sub>) can potentially capture a broad set of unobservable timevarying confounders. λ<sub>ij</sub> is a (r × 1) vector of factor loadings, and $F _ { t }$ is a $( r \times 1 )$ vector of common factors, meaning that $\lambda _ { i j } F _ { t } = \lambda _ { i j 1 } F _ { 1 t } + \cdot \cdot \cdot + \lambda _ { i j r } F _ { r t }$ . The multiple interactive effects can be reduced to two-way fixed-effect models. When $\lambda _ { i j } =$ (ν<sub>ij</sub>, 1)<sup>’</sup> and $F _ { t } = ( 1 , \mu _ { t } ) ^ { \prime } , \lambda _ { i j } F _ { t } = \nu _ { i j } + \mu _ { t }$ are identical to Equations 1a-1d. For $r \geq 3$ , there exist nontrivial interactive fixed effects (Moon & Weidner, 2015). Additionally, we report the results of the Rosenbaum bounds test that we used to determine the effect of unobserved selection on the estimates (Becker & Caliendo, 2007) in Appendix D. The results also suggest that unobserved selection did not likely drive our findings.

Look-Ahead PSM (LA-PSM): While it is true that eBay’s eWOM change led to the introduction of reviews via “random” timing, whether certain products received positive or negative reviews did not occur randomly. We therefore applied a look-ahead PSM approach to resolve the possible sample selection bias (Bapna et al., 2018; Jung et al., 2019). Specifically, we extended the observation window by four weeks to identify whether some products would receive product reviews in the near future. The two treatment groups were composed of products that received positive or negative product reviews in the prior period between May 7, 2016, and July 30, 2016. Then, we matched each treated product to a control unit that did not have any product reviews in the prior period but did have them in the extended period between July 31, 2016, and August 27, 2016. Because both the treatment and matched control groups ultimately received the same valence of product reviews, we accounted not only for the observed characteristics via the closest propensity score but also for the unobserved characteristics related to the intrinsic quality of products or the preferences of previous consumers. Using these sample data sets, we estimated Equations 1a-1d. As a robustness check, we relaxed the dependence on the logistic functional form by using a look-ahead coarsened exact matching method. Appendix E shows that the results of DID with LA-CEM are qualitatively similar to those of the approach using LA-PSM.

Instrumental Variables (IVs): We developed instrumental variables for different types of seller reputation to control for simultaneity or reverse causality. Since sellers’ short-term and long-term reputations are the sum of cumulative feedback scores from all the products sold, we extracted two IVs by calculating each seller’s peripheral products’ feedback scores for different time periods (i.e., the product life cycle or the most recent one month) (Fan et al., 2016). For example, Seller A on eBay sells four products (e.g., A1, A2, A3, and A4). For the focal product A1, A2-A4 are the peripheral products. Because the feedback scores from the peripheral products represent a considerable portion of the overall feedback and indirectly impact the focal product’s sales, the peripheral products feedback scores could serve as reasonable IVs for short-term and long-term reputation. We also use the lagged top-rated seller badge status as an IV for dynamic reputation (i.e., the toprated seller badge). In particular, the IV measures whether the seller gained the badge in the last qualification period. Based on the top-rated seller badge mechanism, if a seller’s performance at the beginning of a calendar month $( S a l e s _ { i j t } )$ pushes their performance metrics above the TRS threshold, that seller will receive a badge when eBay makes its next monthly assessment $( T R S _ { i j t + I } )$ . It indicates that the prior status of the badge $( T R S _ { i j t - I } )$ would have a high correlation with the current status of the badge $( T R S _ { i j t } )$ but would be indirectly related to current product sales $( S a l e s _ { i j t } )$ . The F-statistics in the first-stage regressions are all far above ten. Thus, we can reject the null hypothesis that the instruments are irrelevant in the first-stage regressions and rule out the possibility of weak instruments (Stock et al., 2002).

## Results

Table 3 presents the results of incorporating the HTM into a DID model. Remarkably, we found that the joint effect of the two eWOM systems does not reflect that the two are simple complements or substitutes. The sellers’ reputation level as a reference point for consumers plays an important role in determining the additional effect that online product reviews may generate. For high-reputation sellers, receiving positive reviews does not help boost their sales. The $A d o p t i o n _ { i j } \times A f t e r _ { i j t }$ term is insignificant for high-reputation sellers with positive reviews (Column 1 in Table 3). This indicates that positive reviews may merely confirm a consumer’s high expectation of a reputable seller and thus do not contribute to additional product sales. However, for high-reputation sellers, receiving negative product reviews may introduce a negative expectancy violation among potential consumers, which in turn harms product sales. The interaction term $( A d o p t i o n _ { i j } \times A f t e r _ { i j t } )$ coefficient is negative and significant for high-reputation sellers with negative reviews (Column 2 in Table 3).

Interestingly, this negative effect of negative reviews on sales does not exist for low-reputation sellers. The interaction term $( A d o p t i o n _ { i j } \times A f t e r _ { i j t } )$ coefficient is insignificant for lowreputation sellers with negative reviews (Column 4 in Table 3). For low-reputation sellers, receiving negative reviews does not necessarily lead to low product sales. Since consumers may have already formed a low expectation of these poorquality sellers, negative reviews may simply confirm such low expectations among potential consumers and therefore do not cause additional damage to sales. Unlike high-reputation sellers, for low-reputation sellers, receiving positive reviews does bring additional benefits in terms of product sales. The coefficient of $( A d o p t i o n _ { i j } \times A f t e r _ { i j t } )$ is positive and significant for low-reputation sellers with positive reviews (Column 3 in Table 3). This result indicates that the positive expectancy violation that consumers may experience when facing inconsistent information in terms of low seller reputation and positive product reviews, helps amplify the positive effects of product reviews and improves sales.

To better interpret our findings, we conducted stringent Wald tests to compare the equality of the core coefficients in the different subsamples. The Wald tests show that the differences in coefficients between positive and negative reviews are sufficiently large in the high- and low-reputation seller groups $( p = 0 . 0 0 5 , \chi ^ { 2 } = 7 . 7 8 ; p = 0 . 0 0 2 , \chi ^ { 2 } = 9 . 7 2 )$ . When combining this finding with the above discussions, we show strong evidence of the seller’s reputation reference effect on the impact of product reviews on product sales. Specifically, for high-reputation sellers, negative reviews may serve as a burden but positive reviews may serve as a trifle, whereas for low-reputation sellers, positive reviews are beneficial but negative reviews are a trifle. We report additional results by applying the PSM approach, the interactive fixed-effect model, the LA-PSM approach, and instrumental variables in Appendix F. These additional analyses show that our main findings are robust to the consideration of various endogeneity concerns. In addition, we conduct a difference-in-differencein-differences (DDD) model in combination with the IV method (see Appendix G), and our results hold.

## Seller’s Adoption of Product Review System

Our primary analysis finds that low-reputation sellers benefit from receiving positive reviews and that high-reputation sellers suffer from negative reviews. The effects are insignificant for the other two scenarios of the reputation and product review pairs. It seems that high-reputation sellers may not benefit substantially from adopting the product review system, while low-reputation sellers should embrace this opportunity. However, it is not clear whether sellers account for this reference effect when making adoption decisions and whether the adopting sellers are rewarded as expected. There might exist intriguing patterns in the relationship between adoption behaviors and seller characteristics.

Specifically, we explore whether adopting sellers may indeed benefit from their decisions and whether outcomes vary among sellers. To measure the effect of adoption, we conducted a subgroup analysis of the high- and low-reputation systems in terms of Equation (1) with multiple seller reputation systems. Table 4 suggests that adopting the product review system can indeed be beneficial for low-reputation sellers but detrimental for high-reputation sellers. The Wald tests also support the prominent differences between low- and high-reputation groups. These results indicate that sellers may not have been aware of the reputation reference effect when the platform introduced the product review system. If sellers learn about the potential reference effect, high-reputation sellers may not be as passionate as low-reputation sellers about adopting the review system. Moreover, we perform robustness checks via a DDD model with IVs. The negative three-way interactions in Appendix A8 confirm that highreputation sellers may not benefit more from adopting the product review system than low-reputation sellers.

## Strategy for the Contextual Effect of Reference Point

Although our results suggest that for high-reputation sellers, adopting the product review system does not seem beneficial for improving sales, we still observe that a significant number of top-rated sellers start using the product review system. Another interesting question is: What can the platform do to help high-reputation sellers mitigate the potential negative impact of the negative expectancy violation and benefit from using the product review system? To alleviate the contextual effect of the reference point for high-reputation sellers, we investigate whether a more dynamic reputation system, where the seller’s reputation can improve and deteriorate more frequently, may affect a seller’s performance after adopting the product review system. The rationale is that since the seller’s reputation imposes a reference effect on the impact of product reviews, when a seller’s reputation level changes, the reference effect can be adjusted accordingly, which may affect the impact of product reviews on performance.

In our context, the top-rated seller (TRS) badge is a dynamic reputation system in which a seller’s badge can be periodically obtained or taken away. With such a dynamic reputation system, a seller can thus signal different reputation levels at different time periods. Therefore, its reference effect may be adjusted accordingly. In other words, a high-reputation seller may not be associated with high expectations among consumers at all times; losing the top-rated badge could provide an opportunity for the seller to benefit from positive reviews in the context of a lower reputation level. To examine whether changes in reputation levels affect a high-reputation seller’s performance, we estimated a three-way interaction term, $A d o p t i o n _ { i j } { \times } A f t e r _ { i j t } { \times }$ Ever\_Changed<sub>ijt</sub>, in light of Equations (1a-1d). If top-rated sellers have ever lost the badge, the Ever\_Changed<sub>ijt</sub> variable equals 1, and 0 otherwise. Interestingly, our results show that the impact of receiving product reviews, either positive or negative, varies among sellers that have or have not lost their top-rated badge in the past (Table 5). For sellers that have lost their top-rated seller badge during our data collection period, receiving product reviews had a significant positive effect on sales in both models. Compared to sellers that have never lost their top-rated badge, sellers that have lost their top-rated badge in the past can mitigate the negative impact from the negative expectancy violation on sales with negative reviews and enjoy a positive impact from receiving positive reviews. One possible explanation is that when sellers lose their top-rated badge, consumers may reset or lower the perceived expectation of the seller’s quality and the reference point is thus adjusted accordingly. As a result, consumers may experience a reduced negative expectancy violation or even a positive violation with product reviews, which in turn helps increase product sales.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Research has identified the critical role of eWOM systems, including product reviews and seller reputation systems, on product sales (Cabral & Hortacsu, 2010; Chen & Xie, 2008; Hui et al., 2016). However, most of the existing studies have examined the impact of different eWOM systems separately and found mixed results, especially on the effect of product reviews (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Ho-Dac et al., 2013; Resnick et al., 2006). In contrast to prior studies, this study empirically examines the joint effects of dual eWOM systems and attempts to quantify the unique interaction effects between the two systems. Drawing on reference point theory, we find that the magnitude of product review effects may depend on the level of seller reputation, which serves as a reference point for consumers. On the one hand, negative reviews cause a loss in sales for high-reputation sellers but are not as detrimental for low-reputation sellers. On the other hand, positive reviews can boost sales for low-reputation sellers but cannot do the same for high-reputation sellers. These results further indicate that highreputation sellers on eBay may suffer from adopting the new product review system. Accordingly, our extended analysis explores what strategy may help high-reputation sellers reduce the negative expectancy violation effect on their sales performance. Specifically, we find that the top-rated seller badge as a dynamic reputation system can reconstruct reference points and change consumers’ expectations, allowing sellers that have lost their top-rated seller badge to mitigate the negative impact of receiving negative reviews and benefit from receiving positive reviews.

Our study makes significant contributions to both theory and practice. Theoretically, this study is among the first to empirically study the joint effects of two eWOM systems, which opens an important avenue for future research to explore these interesting joint effects. It extends reference point theory to the context of eWOM systems in online marketplaces. Building upon reference point theory, we quantify the reference effect of seller reputation on the relationship between online reviews and consumer purchase decisions. With dual eWOM systems, if the message is consistent, having two sources does not significantly affect product sales. However, receiving conflicting information may have differential impacts, depending on the level of seller reputation as a reference point. In contrast to the dissonancerelated explanations that decision-making processes tend to reduce discomfort emerging from inconsistent evidence and strive for consistency (Festinger, 1957), we find a prominent reference effect whereby consumers may use the initial information as a baseline when judging the degree of the discrepancy vis-à-vis the additional information. We add new insights to the reference point literature and enrich the understanding of the interaction within dual eWOM systems.

Despite the extensive studies on eWOM and its effects on product sales (e.g., Chintagunta et al., 2010; Dellarocas, 2003; Li & Hitt, 2008), the interaction effect between multiple eWOM systems and how that may vary is not fully understood (Wang et al., 2016). After eBay introduced the product review function, the presence of both product reviews and seller reputation systems created an ideal empirical setting to study the joint effects of the two systems. By exploring the joint effects between seller reputation (i.e., reputation grade, most recent feedback, or top-rated badge) and product reviews, our study advances the existing e-commerce research on the effectiveness of product reviews by explicitly specifying seller reputation as an important moderating condition. For example, a high reputation does not overcome the adverse effects of negative reviews but may further harm product sales because of the negative expectancy violation effect. In contrast, lowreputation sellers have less to lose from receiving negative reviews and can, in fact, gain comparatively larger increases in sales from receiving positive reviews. Our study reveals a nonmonotonic relationship between eWOM and product sales. With a shift of attention from the “isolated” perspective to the “connective” perspective, we can provide a more holistic view on eWOM effectiveness.

Furthermore, identifying seller reputation as a moderating condition also provides insights into the mixed findings in the eWOM literature. Although the extant studies consider some context-dependent variables, such as product category, life cycle, and platform type, to explain the equivocal results (Babić et al., 2016; Floyd et al., 2014; You et al., 2015), they rarely consider the contingent factors of different seller reputation systems. We show that the mixed findings on the effectiveness of positive or negative reviews on sales might result from the seller’s reputation reference effect. As such dual eWOM systems become increasingly common among online marketplaces (e.g., Amazon marketplace, Wayfair, Airbnb, and Taobao), consumers are likely to encounter both types of information when making purchase decisions. We offer a unique perspective to understand the effect of eWOM systems when multiple systems coexist.

Practically, we offer meaningful implications and guidelines for sellers and platforms seeking to understand and benefit from dual eWOM systems. For sellers, our results suggest opportunities for low-reputation sellers or sellers without an established reputation to improve sales performance with product reviews. We find that low-reputation sellers may benefit from using online review systems the most and are not as affected by receiving negative reviews. Low-reputation sellers and new sellers that have not yet established a reputation should diligently encourage their consumers to provide product reviews. For example, sellers may offer incentives to reward consumers who post a product review. However, high-reputation sellers may want to be more cautious when inviting consumers to review. High-reputation sellers face more detrimental consequences associated with receiving negative reviews. As a result, it may not be as beneficial for high-reputation sellers to encourage reviews.

For platforms, this research calls for rethinking the design of online eWOM systems. Although high-reputation sellers may not benefit from adopting product review systems in general, they can mitigate the detrimental impact of negative reviews and improve product sales through using a dynamic reputation system. This result provides important guidance for online platforms to better design the seller reputation systems. Many online platforms are still using reputation systems based on cumulative seller scores, which do not change often. For example, Amazon Marketplace and Wayfair use a reputation system based on accumulated feedback scores, which vary little over short time periods. We suggest that these platforms should incorporate a more dynamic reputation system in which the seller’s reputation can change more quickly. Dynamic reputation systems may not only help sellers overcome the temporal effect of a static system (Liu et al., 2016) but may also mitigate the reference effect for highreputation sellers, which would in turn benefit platforms. Additionally, we find that inconsistency between seller reputation and product reviews can be detrimental for highreputation sellers. Online platforms should devise intelligence tools to help sellers identify products that are rated negatively by consumers so that sellers can take prompt actions to address consumers’ complaints. Such tools can be especially valuable for high-reputation sellers to monitor the review dynamics of their products and red-flag products receiving negative reviews.

As with all research, this study is not without limitations. First, we find that low-reputation sellers may benefit from receiving positive product reviews, compared with those who have not adopted the product review system. This finding seems counterintuitive, as one could argue that consumers would prefer to shop from high-reputation sellers if possible. While we adopt multiple methods to control for seller-level effects when estimating the reference-point effect of seller reputation on product reviews, it may still be possible that low-reputation sellers who have adopted the product review system happen to only sell rare or niche products that consumers are not able to find elsewhere. As a result, these low-reputation sellers may experience an increase in sales because consumers are forced to gamble on low-reputation sellers because of the lack of better options. Although we did not observe this effect in our data set, we encourage future research to investigate this interesting scenario. Second, while consumers may be more likely to use seller reputation as a reference point, since it provides contextual information as a baseline, our study cannot rule out the sequential effect of how consumers may choose a reference point using the empirical setting on eBay.

Therefore, our findings are more applicable to eBay-like platforms, where seller reputation may have a longer presence than product reviews on the platform. It would be interesting for future research to examine other scenarios in which product review and/or seller reputation systems are introduced at different stages and to evaluate whether consumers weigh them differently. Finally, our data are at the aggregate level rather than at the level of individual consumers. We did not observe whether specific individuals realize the discrepancy between eWOM systems nor did we observe the reactions of particular types of consumers. Additional research could delineate the role of consumer heterogeneity in the reference effect of eWOM systems.

## Appendix A

## Ruling Out Pre-Treatment Trends

Although our baseline model results show that, in general, online sellers adoption of the product review system is beneficial for improving their product sales (Table A1), there may be a heterogeneity that is responsible for the observed outcomes before eBay introduced the product review system. If the “parallel path” assumption—i.e., the observed effect did not occur during pre-treatment—cannot be satisfied, our subsequent analyses based on the baseline model may not be valid. Therefore, we follow Angrist and Pischke’s work (2008) and check this critical assumption using the relative time model proposed by Autor (2003):

$$
l o g \left(S a l e s _ {i j t}\right) = \delta_ {0} + \delta_ {1} A f t e r _ {i j t} + \sum_ {\tau = 0} ^ {m} \delta_ {2, - \tau} \left(A d o p t i o n _ {i j} \times A f t e r _ {i j t - \tau}\right) + \sum_ {\tau = 1} ^ {n} \delta_ {3, + \tau} \left(A d o p t i o n _ {i j} \times A f t e r _ {i j t + \tau}\right) + \Theta X _ {i j t} + v _ {i j} + \mu_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t}, (A 1)
$$

where the right-hand side allows for m = 9 lags (i.e., post-treatment effects) and n = 3 leads (i.e., anticipatory effects). The results of the relative time model are reported in Table A2.

Our results show that there was no specific trend in product sales within three weeks before eBay introduced the product review system. A significant increasing effect on product sales existed in the first few weeks after the system change, excluding the impact of pre-treatment trends as a confounding factor. Moreover, we visualize the parallel trends in subgroup analyses (i.e., high-reputation vs. low-reputation). Figure A1 shows that the past values of Adoption<sub>ij</sub> × After<sub>ijt</sub> can predict log(Sales<sub>ijt</sub>) but the future values of Adoption<sub>ij</sub> × After<sub>ijt</sub> cannot. It corroborates our findings that while highreputation sellers are willing to use the product review system, their sales performance may be worse than that of low-reputation sellers.

## Propensity Score Matching and Results

Following the literature (Rishika et al., 2013; Rubin, 2006), we conducted the PSM procedure by utilizing the pre-adoption period data. To avoid a potential ordering effect, we first sorted all the treated and control sellers in random order. In that case, running a logistic regression predicted seller adoption behaviors. The results of the logistic regression are reported in Table C1. All covariates are significantly associated with the seller’s adoption indicating powerful propensity scores for the treatment and control sellers. Next, we used kernel matching (Heckman et al., 1998) to match these two seller groups in pairs that resemble each other. Finally, we found 6,063 seller-product pairs.

Furthermore, we performed several checks to ensure the quality of the PSM procedure. First, we plotted the density distributions of the predicted propensity scores (Lecher, 2002). Figure C1 shows a vast difference before matching and substantial overlap after matching. We found evidence that supports the underlying assumptions of the matching process. Second, we further tested whether the variables of the matched treatment and control groups were balanced. As shown in Table C2, the covariates used for PSM are not significantly different across the two groups post-matching. The corresponding variance ratio (dividing the variance of the treatment by that of the control) is also close to one, implying statistical balance.

## Sensitivity Analysis for Checking Hidden Bias

To examine whether unobserved variables overly affect the simultaneous assignment into treatment (i.e., using product reviews) and product sales, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using Rosenbaum bounds (Becker & Caliendo, 2007). The basic idea was to manipulate the estimated odds of receiving treatment and see how much the estimated treatment effects vary. Table D1 presents the results of Rosenbaum bounds using the Mantel-Haenszel test and Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test. Gamma is the odds of differential assignment due to unobserved factors. With the increase of Gamma from 1 to 5, the p-values in both tests are still zero, indicating that our estimation is free of unobserved selection bias. A Gamma value of 5 is well above the values used in the prior literature (Manchanda et al., 2015; Rishika et al., 2013).
