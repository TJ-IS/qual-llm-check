# A Theory of Multilevel Information Privacy Management for the Digital Era

Abstract. In the digital era, it is increasingly important to understand how privacy de cisions are made because information is frequently perceived as a commodity that is mismanaged. The preponderance of privacy literature focuses on individual-level information privacy concern and personal self-disclosure decisions. We propose that a more versatile multilevel description is required to enable exploration of complex privacy de cisions that involve co-owned (i.e., group) information in increasingly sophisticated digita environments. We define the concepts of group and individual information privacy, “we privacy” and “I-privacy” respectively, as the ability of an individual or group to construct, regulate, and apply the rules for managing their information and interaction with others. We develop the theory of multilevel information privacy (TMIP), which uses the theory of communication privacy management and the developmental theory of privacy as foundations for a social rule-based (i.e., normative) process of making privacy decisions that evolve over time with experience. The TMIP contributes to the privacy literature b drawing from prominent social psychology theories of group behavior (i.e., social identity and self-categorization theories) to explain how privacy decisions can be made by in dividuals or groups (i.e., social units) or social units acting as members of a particular group. We contend that technology complicates the privacy decision-making process by adding unique environmental characteristics that can influence the social identity assumed for a particular privacy decision, the estimation of the cost-benefit components of the privacy calculus, and the application and evolution of the norms that define the rules for information and interaction management. We discuss the implications of the TMIP for information systems research and provide a research agenda.

## Introduction

In the digital era, where the data one generates can be viewed as a commodity (Smith et al. 2011), it is increasingly important to understand how multilevel (i.e., individual and group) information privacy decisions are made. One particularly challenging aspect of multilevel information privacy decisions (MIPDs) is that multiple stakeholders may share ownership of a particular piece of information (Petronio 2002). Consider, for example, photos taken by a university photographer of students and the professor during a class. Several entities may perceive ownership of these photos—the students and the professor whose images were captured; the university photographer who took the photos; the university that now uses the images as stock photos; the university department that houses the course that was photographed and uses the photos in its marketing materials; and the college marketing team who uses the photos in magazines, social media posts, and on websites. All of these individuals and groups share ownership of the photos and will make decisions on how to use them that may impact other owners. For example, university relations could decide to use one of the photos in an email sent to the entire university, which may surprise the professor who receives emails from colleagues who saw the photo.

Whether made by an individual or a group, an MIPD involves two components: (1) the information being considered for release (e.g., the class photo) and (2) the audience that will receive it (e.g., all individuals with a campus email address). The MIPD is guided by implicit or explicit rules in the form of personal or group privacy norms. That is, the social unit develops rules that guide how that information is handled and to whom it can be disclosed

For example, the university may post rules regarding the use of its stock photos and have a web form that can be filled out to request to use one. In this case, the rules are explicit (i.e., written down), and procedures are in place for obtaining approved use of the information (i.e., photo). Therefore, if the professor’s department wants to use the photo on its website, there are written rules that the department can consult to determine approved uses of the university’s stock photos and a form for the department to fill out to obtain permission. If the professor’s department decides to use one of the stock photos on the departmental website, and obtains permission from university relations to do so, then the department is making a privacy decision on behalf of the university, the department, the professor, and the students in the photo following the explicit norms of the university. The professor, whose image is now on the department’s website, may not personally agree with the decision made (i.e., the decision may violate the professor’s personal privacy norms). It may upset the professor that the photo is being used on a website that is easily accessible to anyone in the world, because although he or she works at the university, people who are not colleagues can now see that image. The use of the photo in this example illustrates how complex privacy decisions can be when information is co-owned, different rule sets (i.e., norms) for its use exist, and technological characteristics change possible perceptions and consequences of MIPDs made online. In this paper, we propose a theory of multilevel information privacy (TMIP) that organizes such complex MIPDs into a process model.

Most of the information systems (IS) literature refers to information privacy as a form of control over the use of one’s personal information (e.g., Bélanger and Crossler 2011) and focuses on individual-level information privacy concern and willingness to disclose information. Although some classic privacy research describes the existence of group privacy (Westin 1967, Altman 1974, Laufer and Wolfe 1977, Margulis 1977), the group level of analysis has largely been neglected in the privacy literature (Bélanger and Xu 2015). Similarly, the theories proposed to explain information privacy in the IS literature have largely focused on defining the dimensions of individual information privacy concern (e.g., Hong and Thong 2013), and explaining individual-level privacy decision making using rational choice frameworks that consider a privacy decision to be a rational decision made by weighing the benefits and costs of disclosing personal information (e.g., Smith et al. 2011). An extension to the rational choice framework has been proposed that draws from information processing theories in psychology to consider the information individuals use to help guide privacy decisions (Dinev et al. 2015), but still at the individual level. In neglecting the multilevel nature of privacy decisions, prior work in IS does not fully consider whose rules or norms are guiding a particular privacy decision. IS researchers have thus noted the importance of studying multilevel information privacy and issued calls for research to fill this gap (e.g., Bélanger and Crossler 2011, Smith et al. 2011), which our paper answers.

The theory of communication privacy management (CPM) in the communications literature considers coownership of information, boundaries inclusive of those individuals who can claim ownership of particular information, and the development of rules to manage the boundaries (Petronio 2002). CPM thus provides a foundation on which to develop a normative theory of multilevel information privacy. However, CPM does not offer a social psychological explanation of how individuals navigate among the rule sets (i.e., norms) available to guide a particular decision and also does not consider the influence of the technological environment in which privacy decisions are now frequently being made. In developing the TMIP, we draw from research in IS and communications, but we extend their thinking by incorporating social psychology theories of group behavior (i.e., social identity theory (SIT) and self-categorization theories (SCTs)) to explain how social norms can guide MIPDs. Moreover, because many of today’s information privacy decisions are made in or with consideration of digital spaces, we integrate the online environment into the TMIP to illustrate how technology shapes the MIPD-making process. The online environmental characteristics are important factors in many of today’s privacy decisions because they complicate the understanding of the space in which the decision is taking place and this can lead to deleterious consequences and public scandal (e.g., the Cambridge Analytica scandal<sup>1</sup>).

In the following sections, we first provide theoretical background to establish privacy as a multilevel concept. We then introduce the TMIP and develop it by explaining the theoretical foundation for each element of the framework. We conclude with a research agenda for IS researchers that maps the elements of the TMIP to future research in technological environments.

## Multilevel Information Privacy

The IS literature has established privacy as an individual concept (e.g., Bélanger and Crossler 2011, Smith et al. 2011), and the seminal privacy literature in the social sciences provides theoretical foundation for group-level privacy (e.g., Altman 1974, Laufer and Wolfe 1977). Westin (1967) argues that privacy is “the claim of individuals, groups or institutions to determine for themselves when, how and to what extent information about themselves is communicated to others” (p. 7). This definition clearly includes multiple social units of analysis for which a concept of privacy can exist. Moreover, many of the classical writings recognize privacy as a group-level concept (Westin 1967, Altman 1974, Laufer and Wolfe 1977, Margulis 1977). Altman (1974), citing Westin (1967), describes how a primary feature of privacy is that it can involve different social units, including individuals and groups. Arguing that privacy is an interpersonal event, he further describes examples where an individual or group may avoid interaction with another individual or group, suggesting, “an important area of analysis concerns differences in privacy dynamics for various social units” (Altman 1974, p. 11).

In the developmental theory of privacy, Laufer and Wolfe (1977) frame privacy as being able to make choices regarding information and interaction management. They state that how much control an individual has over the privacy choices he or she makes depends on the interpersonal context. Further, they describe how choice involves “setting up boundaries between the self and other [and that] in situations of three-or-more persons, group boundaries become the focus of attention” (Laufer and Wolfe 1977, p. 38). In group situations, they suggest that

information and interaction management move toward the concerns and purposes of the group and away from the interpersonal characteristics of the dyad [and] collective norms begin to govern choice over degree, form, and extent of information and interaction management. (Laufer and Wolfe 1977, pp. 38–39)

In their reviews of information privacy research, Smith et al. (2011) and Bélanger and Crossler (2011) describe that one deficit in empirical IS research is the lack of research at a group level of analysis. However, they are not in complete agreement regarding the conceptualization of group privacy. Bélanger and Crossler (2011) take the position that group privacy as a concept can be defined, whereas Smith et al. (2011) refrain from associating the concept of privacy with groups, but rather suggest that group norms may guide group information disclosure decisions. These views are not incongruous if control of individual or group privacy is realized through rules (i.e., norms) that guide information and interaction (i.e., privacy) management. To explain, control is a critical component of most privacy definitions (Smith et al. 2011), and in fact, Bélanger and Crossler (2011) define information privacy as an individual’s desire to control their information. However, the mechanism through which control is realized is not often discussed in the IS literature. In discussing group-level privacy,

Smith et al. (2011) suggest that it is “likely that small groups differ in norms regarding protection of the group’s own information [. . . and that] a single individual likely belongs to more than one group, so (s) he may adhere to different norms regarding privacy as (s)he travels between groups” (p. 1007). In this statement, Smith et al. (2011) provide for the possibility that norms regulate information privacy and that people can adopt different rule sets as they switch groups. CPM, which we also use for theoretical foundation, similarly describes privacy being managed through rules that are developed for different groups (Petronio 2002). Group privacy can thus be described as the ability of the group, through the development and application of norms or rules, to regulate what information is released to which others (Westin 1967, Laufer and Wolfe 1977, Smith et al. 2011). We build on this idea as we develop the TMIP.

Prior privacy literature in multiple disciplines has thus described privacy as a multilevel concept. Privacy as a multilevel phenomenon is further reinforced by calls in the IS literature to study it at the group level of analysis (Bélanger and Crossler 2011, Smith et al. 2011). We adopt the perspective of information privacy as the ability of the social unit to control it information and interaction (Smith et al. 2011), and consider control to reflect the ability of the social uni to manage its privacy through the development, regulation, and application of rules or information privacy norms (IPNs). For use in the TMIP, we add specificity to the definition of multilevel information privacy in three critical areas. First, we follow Laufer and Wolfe (1977) and recent IS literature (Hong and Thong 2013, James et al. 2015) to propose that the management of privacy includes the management of both the social unit’s information and interaction Second, drawing from Smith et al. (2011), we propose that control over a social unit’s information and interaction is regulated by the social unit’s IPNs (i.e., rules for managing the social unit’s information and interaction). Third, we propose that th social unit can be an individual or a group, and that both individuals and groups can have IPNs that guide the management of their information and interaction (Westin 1967, Laufer and Wolfe 1977, Smith et al. 2011). Therefore, we define multilevel information privacy as the ability of an individual (I-privacy) or group (we-privacy) to construct, regulate, and apply the rules (i.e., norms) for managing their information and interaction with others. Specifically, we define two levels of information privacy—individual information privacy and group information privacy. I-privacy (i.e., individual information privacy) is the ability of an individual to construct, regulate, and apply the rules (i.e., norms) for managing his or her information and interaction with others. We-privacy (i.e., group information privacy) is the ability of groups to construct, regulate, and apply the rules (i.e., norms) for managing their information and interaction with others. These definitions, along with others that are established as we describe the theoretical foundations for the TMIP, are provided in Table A1 of Online Appendix A. With information privacy established and defined as a multilevel concept, we now turn to the development of the TMIP.

## The Theory of Multilevel Information Privacy

We introduce the theoretical framework for the TMIP that explains the MIPD-making process in Figure 1. An MIPD involves the application of personal or group IPNs to guide what information to release to which others. In the TMIP, the MIPD process is normative in the sense that individuals and groups draw from the activated set of information privacy rules (i.e., norms) to guide an MIPD. Drawing from the developmental theory of privacy (Laufer and Wolfe 1977), we propose that individuals and groups have rules (i.e., IPNs) to manage both their information and their interaction with others.

Further, we acknowledge that individuals can belong to multiple groups and thus the rule set (i.e., IPNs) activated to guide a specific MIPD is one of multiple rule sets (i.e., their own and those of any groups with which they identify) associated with the social identities that are accessible at the time that MIPD is being made. Which set of IPNs guide a particular MIPD is determined by the social identity that is salient at the moment the MIPD is being made (Turner and Reynolds 2012), and the salient social identity is influenced by the environment in which the MIPD is being made. The normative privacy decision, driven by the norms of the salient social identity, is made unless the cost-benefit tradeoff (i.e., privacy calculus) favors the counter-normative privacy decision. The ultimate MIPD determines the observed multilevel information privacy behavior (MIPB). The MIPB may result in positive or negative feedback, which over time may lead to refinement of IPNs. Therefore, due to the temporal nature of an MIPD in the TMIP, a different MIPD could be made by the same individual or group in a similar situation at a later date if the IPNs of that social identity have been refined, environmental characteristics change (e.g., technological change) that stimulate a different social identity to be assumed, or the cost-benefit calculation changes.

To illustrate the TMIP decision process shown in Figure 1, consider the following example. Imagine a female patient who has diabetes is sitting in an examination room of her primary care physician’s office in the United States with a nurse who is taking information from the patient and entering it into the software that manages the physician’s electronic medical records (EMRs). The offline environmental characteristics in this situation include being at the physician’s office (physical location), with a nurse (others physically present), textual health data are collected, and these data are co-owned by at least the physician, nurse, and patient. The information is being input into an EMR system (digital media) as text (information format) that can be accessed virtually by other entities that are not in the examination room (e.g., the physician). Some or all of these environmental characteristics could stimulate a “patient social identity,” in which case the privacy decisions of the patient (e.g., what information she tells the nurse) could be guided by “patient privacy norms,” some of which may be explicit requirements laid out in the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). Thus, the information privacy decisions made by the woman will be guided by the IPNs of the salient social identity—patient. That is, she makes the information privacy decision with the mindset “this is what a patient would do.”

Let us also imagine that the woman in our example belongs to a virtual health community (VHC) for diabetics. After her physician’s appointment, she goes home (physical location) and logs on to her diabetic VHC (social media) to talk with other people with diabetes (virtual participants) about the challenges they are facing with their disease (text health information co-owned by the VHC group). Some or all of these environmental characteristics could stimulate a “diabetic VHC” social identity, and the woman could assume the implicit IPNs that guide disclosure decisions on that particular diabetic VHC. That is, she now makes information privacy decisions with the mindset “this is what a diabetic VHC member would do.” The implicit norms in the diabetic VHC arose in part due to the characteristics of the online environment in which the information disclosures are made (e.g., the virtual people can be anyone with an internet connection who joins the VHC). Finally, imagine that the woman was told by the nurse at her appointment that she needed to lose weight to help manage her diabetes. She decides to relate this advice on the diabetic VHC and another member replies to her posts with a meme that demeans overweight individuals, which upsets many of the VHC’s members. This unpleasant consequence of the privacy decision may result in the diabetic VHC deciding to remove the offending post (i.e., a group MIPD) and to institute content moderators to avoid such an occurrence in the future (i.e., group norm refinement).

Both of the scenarios described involve complex privacy decisions regarding co-owned health information, but in different environments guided by different IPNs associated with different salient socia identities. The scenarios illustrate how a shift in online or offline environmental characteristics can influence the social identity that is salient and which of that identity’s IPNs guide the specific information privacy decision, as well as how experiential feedback could lead to the refinement of the IPNs of an individual or group (i.e., social unit). The TMIP explains why a social unit may make a particular MIPD at a specific point in time and in a given context to help researchers better understand complex information privacy decisions across levels of analysis. The TMIP also tightly integrates the role of technology in complex MIPDs. Specifically, we propose that technology can influence the social identity that is assumed, the specific rules from an individual or group’s IPNs that are applied to guide a specific MIPD, and the estimation of the cost-benefit calculus. We also propose that experiential feedback from making MIPDs in evolving technological environments may lead to norm refinement over time.

As illustrated in Figure 1, the theoretical framework for the TMIP includes the following elements: (1) the MIPD (a) that directs the MIPB (b); (2) the environment in which that MIPD is taking place; (3) the social identity that is assumed, which is influenced by the characteristics of the environment; (4) the privacy calculus, which may override the normative MIPD; and (5) the IPNs of the salient social identity that guide the MIPD. The environmental characteristics influence which social identity is salient, but they also determine which rules from the activated IPNs associated with the salient social identity are applied to guide the MIPD and the estimation of the cost and benefit components of the privacy calculus. The initial IPNs (5a) of a social unit may be refined over time through experiential feedback (5b), and the cost and benefit components of the privacy calculus may also be adjusted through experiential feedback. Time (5c), experienced by the decision maker as growing or aging and by the sociocultural system as historical or technological development, may result in changes to the environmental characteristics, which may culminate in new experiential feedback (5b) that may in turn reshape IPNs or adjust the cost or benefit components of the privacy calculus. Each of the following subsections provides the theoretical foundation for an element of the TMIP, and the numbering reflects the numbering of the theoretical element of the TMIP in Figure 1.

## Element 1: The Multilevel Information Privacy Decision (a) and Behavior (b)

Drawing from Laufer and Wolfe (1977), we propose two primary components of an MIPD: (1) information and (2) interaction. The first component defines the information that is to be disclosed, and the second defines the recipient of the information. Both of these components can be managed through rule sets (i.e., IPNs) that are developed and maintained by a social unit. We thus define an MIPD as the application of the salient social identity’s IPNs to guide what information to release to which others, where IPNs are the implicit or explicit rules constructed and maintained by the social unit (i.e., individual or group) for managing information and interaction. The MIPD results in an MIPB, which we define as the action taken (i.e., what information is given to which others) that reflects the MIPD arrived at by the social unit.

Figure 2 presents some explicit examples of an MIPD. On the left side of Figure 2 is Facebook, an online social network (OSN) for the general public. On the right side of Figure 2 is Slack, an OSN specifically designed for organizational use. In some online environments, such as those in Figure 2, the information and interaction management subcomponents of an MIPD can be clearly seen in the interface. In the Facebook example, John Doe has decided to post two pieces of information (i.e., a comment about his class and a picture of his class). John has also decided to share his post with the group “Friends.”

Facebook allows you to create customized groups (i.e., subsets of your Facebook friends), so another choice would be for John to share the picture with custom groups he created to include only his family or just the other people also taking the information visu alization course. In the Slack example, people have posted information (e.g., calendar events, documents) to a “#culture” channel (i.e., group) within their organization (Acme). In both examples, the recipient of the information was chosen (i.e., Friends and #culture) and the information to be disclosed was chosen (e.g., pictures, documents, comments). Once the person submits the post, the MIPD becomes the MIPB.

## Element 2: The Of<sup>fl</sup>ine and Online Environment

In the TMIP, the environmental characteristics (element 2) influence the social identity that is assumed (element 3). There are three critical components that define the environment in which an MIPD is taking place: (1) location (physical space, virtual space, or both), (2) people (physically or virtually present in the environment), and (3) information and its primary characteristics of type, format, and ownership. We acknowledge that there may be other environmental characteristics (e.g., other nonanimate objects in the room), but we restrict our discussion to the three key components that have the greatest impact on influencing the social identity assumed, determining which of the social identity’s IPNs guide a particular MIPD, and estimating the cost-benefit components of the privacy calculus. This allows us to concentrate on how the use of technology, especially the internet, affects this overarching environmental landscape.

Before the advent of the computer, people were making privacy decisions in some physical location (e.g., at home or the office) with some others present (e.g., a spouse or colleague). The others present may not be the primary target of the disclosure, but if they are in earshot, they also became custodians (i.e., owners) of the information. These environmental conditions (i.e., physical location and physical others) can influence the social identity that is assumed. For example, consider an alcoholic that is attending an Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) meeting in a church basement. During the meeting, individuals are sharing stories of their addiction with their fellow AA members who are physically present at the same physical location. The norms of AA prohibit those stories from being shared outside of that meeting. The physical environment (i.e., the church basement where the meetings are normally held), the other AA members who are physically present, and the information exchanged (i.e., stories of struggles with alcoholism) are environmental characteristics that are likely to stimulate the members’ AA social identity to be salient and thus the AA IPNs to be activated. The environmental characteristics may also influence which of the AA group’s IPNs apply in that situation. For example, perhaps the AA group has instituted a rule that everyone turns their cellphones off during meetings. Moreover, the estimation of the privacy calculus could be influenced, for example, by factoring into the cost of a particular MIPD a negative effect on the sobriety of one of the members present at that particular meeting. The physical location and physical people are still environmental characteristics once technology is inserted into the environment. That is, the social unit making the MIPD is still doing so from some physical location, possibly with some others physically present, although the latter is no longer a requirement. Thus, we place physical location and physical people under online and offline environmental characteristics.

Prior to the advent of recording technologies, exchanged information was spoken only. As humanity progressed, information was written down in hardcopy, typed into digital formats, and audio, still images, and video were recorded in analog and digital formats. In the developmental theory of privacy, Laufer and Wolfe (1977) describe how as early as the 1970s there were concerns about advanced computer technology making it difficult for individuals to manage personal information. Specifically, they discuss record keeping, which has a huge impact on privacy because it captures the information in an accurate and easily transferable format. Of course, record keeping occurred prior to the introduction of computer technology but has rapidly advanced in the digital era. Information can thus be spoken and digitally recorded audio, but it also may be digital or nondigital text and images, as well as video. We refer to this environmental characteristic as information format and classify it under online and offline environmental characteristics because record keeping formats can be digital or nondigital, and moreover, the digital records can be online or offline. We argue that information format, along with information type and information ownership are all online and offline environmental characteristics that can influence which social identity is assumed, which of the salient social identity’s IPNs are used to guide a particular MIPD, and the cost-benefit components of the privacy calculus. Information ownership is a concept that we appropriate from CPM and it is complex in nature. We thus discuss the theoretical foundation for it in more detail in the next subsection.

As an example of how information environmental characteristics may influence the social identity assumed, which of the salient social identity’s IPNs are used to guide a particular MIPD, and the privacy calculus components, let us return to our earlier ex ample of a patient in the physician’s office whose health data are being collected by a nurse and entered into the EMR system. The information format is digital text that is being entered into an EMR system. The information type is health and the nurse, physician, clinic, and patient may all be perceived to have some ownership claim of the data that are disclosed. The information environmental characteristics (e.g., health information that patient and clinic co-own) could stimulate the individual to think and behave as a “patient.” The relevant IPNs of the salient patient social identity would then guide MIPDs, and the patient may decide, for example, to disclose sensitive health information to the nurse because that is what a patient does. Finally, having health information entered into the EMR system (i.e., health information in digital text) by the nurse could result in a benefit of improved healthcare because the patient’s health records are more easily accessible to healthcare providers. However, EMRs are vulnerable to information security breaches in ways paper records are not, which could change the cost component.

We contend that the advent of the internet calls for the addition of two other critical environmental characteristics: digital media and virtual people. In traditional offline privacy decision making, the location was the actual physical space occupied at the time the MIPD was being made. In an online privacy decision, a social unit is making an MIPD in a virtual space as well as a physical space. The most obvious categorization of virtual space is by digital media type, for example, text message, email, or social media (McFarland and Ployhart 2015). Each digital media type can be viewed as a virtual space. Similarly, in addition to any people who may be physically present when an MIPD is being made, there are also people present in the virtual space. We posit that the digital media and virtual people environmental characteristics can influence what social identity is salient, what IPNs of that identity are employed, and the costbenefit components of the privacy calculus. This conforms to the logic of prior IS studies that have discussed the impact of technology on identity. For instance, Lamb and Kling (2003, p. 201) described the influence of digital technologies on identity as “social actors interact with variously constituted others to form the basis of social institutions and identities [and that] technologies, particularly ICTs, are integral to these interactions and so shape identity and institutions.” Moreover, IS researchers have used artifact design to encourage social identity in online communities (Ren et al. 2007, 2012). ## Information Ownership in the TMIP: An Extension of CPM

We use CPM as one of the theoretical foundations of TMIP, and it “uses the metaphor of boundaries to illustrate that, although there may be a flow of private information to others, borders mark ownership lines so issues of control are clearly understood” (Petronio 2002, p. 3). CPM thus defines information boundaries or collectives that all share knowledge of the same information and explains that these groups are formed or expanded through the disclosure of the information to someone not currently inside the information boundary. For clarity, we refer to such an arrangement using the CPM terminology of information boundary that can be defined as a group of people who are all privy to a particular piece of information and thus share ownership of it.

CPM is primarily concerned with self-disclosure and thus lacks explicitness regarding the creation of the information that is disclosed. Therefore, we extend the idea of creating information boundaries through the disclosure of information in CPM to also include creating boundaries through the creation of information, which results in the four possibilities shown in Figure 3.

The first possibility is that an individual creates some information alone; in our example, takes a selfie. As long as that individual does not share that selfie with anyone else, he or she is the sole owner of that information. Once the individual shares the selfie with others (e.g., with his or her college friends on Facebook in the second row of Figure 3), it becomes “co-owned information” through what is called the boundary linkage process in CPM. The information owners (i.e., collective, group) have rules for how coowned information (i.e., information management) and the boundary linkage process (i.e., interaction management) should be managed. In the TMIP, we refer to these rules as implicit or explicit information and interaction management norms.<sup>2</sup> If the boundary is expanded in a way that breaks the rules, CPM states that boundary turbulence has occurred.<sup>3</sup> In the third row of Figure 3, we illustrate this using IS terminology as nonconsensual third-party sharing of the selfie. Finally, a group can “cocreate” information, which in our example means they are all in the selfie that is taken (i.e., that exact group selfie could not exist if all three people were not in the photo). We propose that cocreators of information are any entities that were integral to the creation of that piece of information (i.e., the information could not have been created without those entities).

To simplify terminology, we do not use the term information boundary in the TMIP. Rather, we use the concept of information ownership, and propose that ownership of a particular piece of information is ascribed to any entity that has knowledge of the information, was integral to its creation, or is a member of a group with an ownership claim. We further differentiate between information ownership and information ownership perceptions. An entity can be perceived by the MIPD-maker to be an information owner. However, it is also possible that the MIPD-maker may not recognize all information owners. Moreover, the MIPD-maker may not assign the same weight to each ownership claim. Therefore, information ownership perceptions are environmental characteristics that may influence which social identity is assumed, which of the salient social identity’s IPNs are used to guide a particular MIPD, and the cost-benefit components of the privacy calculus, whereas information ownership straightforwardly defines which entities have an ownership claim to a particular piece of information

Therefore, in the TMIP, we consider information owners to be any entities that have knowledge of the information, were integral to the creation of the information, or are part of a group that has an ownership claim to the information. The latter part of this definition explicitly accounts for the case where not all members of a particular group are privy to a particular piece of information but can still co-own it as a consequence of membership in the group. CPM describes this type of scenario, stating: “organizations may consider certain information proprietary to a company, composing an organizational boundary [. . ., which] is built around private information belonging to the company and is collectively held by the members” (Petronio 2002, p. 90). We extend the logic in this example to its evident conclusion, which is that not all individuals in an organization may actually have knowledge of all the information the organization owns but may still be co-owners. The in formation boundary described in CPM is most often described in relation to whether a person knows the information. This does not clearly include the conceptualization of an organization whose members could be a part of the organization but not be privy to all of the organization’s information. Therefore, we extend the concept of information boundaries in CPM to more explicitly define information ownership by considering an entity to be a co-owner by virtue of membership in a group that is an owner of the information.

We also consider ownership of information to be a result of information cocreation. That is, if an entity was integral to the creation of that information it is an owner, regardless of whether it has knowledge of that information. For example, consider a video taken at a college graduation in which a professor appears without her knowledge $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , she was walking around during the ceremony and caught on film). She is unaware of her presence in the video, but because she was integral to its creation (the exact same video could not have been created without her) she has an ownership claim whether or not she knows it. Therefore, we extend the concept of information boundaries in CPM to more explicitly define information ownership by considering an entity to be a co-owner by virtue of being integral to the creation of that information. It is important to explicitly define information ownership in the TMIP in this manner because it allows for information ownership perceptions to align with actual information ownership. For example, suppose another faculty member sees the graduation video, that faculty member may perceive that the professor appearing in the video partially owns the video, and that ownership perception may cue a “faculty member” social identity. Thus, even though the professor does not know she is in the video, information privacy decisions made regarding the video may be influenced by her presence (i.e., perceptions of her ownership claim). Furthermore, consequences stemming from the use of this video may affect the professor. For example, imagine that the video is posted online and the students use the video to make a meme out of the professor that gets distributed on social media.

CPM proposes two ways in which information boundaries are extended—boundary linkage and boundary turbulence. Boundary linkage is the process of expanding the number of people within an information boundary by disclosing information to someone who did not previously know it. This action increases the number of owners of the information. It is boundary linkage only when the rules that govern privacy management dictate that the entity to whom the information is being disclosed is allowed to know that information (i.e., the privacy management rules allow that person to be added to that information boundary). An information boundary can also be extended through boundary turbulence in CPM. Boundary turbulence occurs when a person within the information boundary misinterprets the rules or simply intentionally or accidently violates them. In the case of either boundary linkage or turbulence, an MIPB results in the information ownership of a particular piece of information being modified.

## Element 3: The Salient Social Identity

In the TMIP, we propose that the salient social identity determines which social unit’s IPNs (personal or a group’s) are activated (element 5) to guide the MIPD (element 1a). To explain how different rule sets may be enacted as individuals move between groups, we draw from SIT and SCT, which provide the theoretical background to describe how individuals move from their own personal identity to assume the identity of one of the groups to which they claim membership. SIT helps us explain how individuals can identify with multiple groups, whereas SCT helps us explain how individuals can have both personal and multiple social identities and move between these identities. Together, these theories allow us to understand how a person or group can act in terms of their salient social identity at a given point in time.

SIT, first introduced in social psychology by Tajfe (1974) and Tajfel et al. (1979), contends that individuals can identify with various groups and act in terms of the group rather than the individual. The theory aims to “understand and explain how people can come to adopt and behave in terms of such social (rather than personal) identities” (Ellemers and Haslam 2012, p. 381), suggesting that one’s personal identity could differ from one’s social identities (Ellemers and Haslam 2012, Tajfel 1974). There are three psychological processes central to SIT that occur in a sequence: social categorization, social identity, and social comparison. First, an individual categorizes others into groups based on common characteristics meaningful to him or herself. The individual may then identify with various groups based on shared characteristics (e.g., a person is struggling with alcohol addiction like those in AA). Social identity is then “that part of an individual’s self-concept which derives from his knowledge of his membership of a social group (or groups) together with the emotional significance attached to that membership” (Tajfel 1974, p. 69). The individual can then compare one group to another group.

SCT extends SIT to explore how individuals can have both personal and social identities and move between them as both individuals and group members. SCT helps explain “how and when people will define themselves as individual and group entities and its implications, and examines the impact of this variability in self-perception (‘I’ to ‘we’) for understandings of mind and behavior” (Turner and Reynolds 2012, p. 399). The theory suggests that the self-categorization process is dynamic, the salient social identity is driven by context, and the movement between identities may explain group behavior. The process involves several steps. First, an individual defines him or herself as a member of a social category. Then, the individual learns or develops “the appropriate, expected, desirable behaviours that are correlated with category membership, and differentiate it from other categories (e.g., the stereotypical norm)” (Turner and Reynolds 2012, p. 406). Third, the individual assigns the norms and attributes of the social unit to him or herself by means of self-stereotyping and depersonalization (Turner and Reynolds 2012). Finally, as the individual’s group membership becomes more salient, his or her behavior becomes more normative with the group (Turner and Reynolds 2012).

Individuals can belong to multiple groups, both formal and informal, with each group having different IPNs. For example, a person may be a member of a formal group, such as a sorority, a company, a research laboratory, AA, and so forth. A person may also identify with less formal groups or categories, such as “student,” “people with diabetes,” or “family.” Importantly, group memberships (formal and informal) are often concurrent and can overlap. SIT and SCT suggest that in a particular context, however, the social identity that is salient at that time will drive the individual’s behavior because the individual will consider the salient social identity’s (i.e., personal or group) preferences for action. Drawing from SIT and SCT, we propose in the TMIP that identification with the self/group and the salience of the personal/social identity<sup>4</sup> cause the entity to activate the individual/ group’s IPNs to guide the MIPD, which without intervention (i.e., a cost-benefit analysis that favors counter-normative behavior) should lead to action in congruence with the salient social identity’s IPNs. In the TMIP, the rule sets that dictate how information and interaction are to be managed (i.e., the IPNs) are tied to the social identity.

Research has found that the salient identity can be manipulated by changing the context in which a behavior occurs (Turner et al. 1987, Turner and Reynolds 2012). In fact, researchers have explained how environmental characteristics in the decisionmaker’s social context stimulate an individual to assume a specific social identity. Hogg and Reid (2006, p. 12) state that “for a social categorization to produce normative behavior, the categorization must be psychologically salient as the basis for perception and self-conception—people must psychologically identify with their in-group in that context.” They suggest that individuals access social categorizations “because they are self-evident and perceptually salient in the situation (they are situationally accessible)” (Hogg and Reid 2006, p. 12). They further describe how individuals cycle through identities until they find one with an optimal fit:

People use accessible categories to make sense of their social context. They investigate how well the categorization accounts for similarities and differences among people (called structural or comparative fit) and how well the prototypical properties of the categorization account for why people behave as they do (called normative fit). If the fit of the categorization is poor (e.g., similarities and differences do not correspond to people’s gender or race, and people do not behave in gender or race-stereotypical ways), people cycle through other accessible categorizations (e.g., political orientation, religion, profession) until an optimal level of fit is obtained. The categorization that has optimal fit becomes psychologically salient in that context as the basis of self-categorization, group identification, and prototype-based depersonalization. It accentuates in-group similarities and intergroup differences, enhances perceived entitativity, and underpins context-relevant group and inter-group behaviors. (Hogg and Reid 2006, p. 12)

In a study of computer-mediated communication grounded in SIT, Postmes et al. (2000, p. 343) describe the same influence of context stating “in a context in which group membership is salient, members will assign these norms to themselves, employing the attributes of their social identity to define appropriate conduct for themselves in the social context.” Therefore, the characteristics of the context (i.e., environment) in which the MIPD is being made help determine which social identity is salient. We propose that the people present (physical or virtual), the location (physical or virtual space), and the characteristics of the information (format, type, and ownership perceptions) being considered for disclosure provide cues that help determine which of the available social identities is the “best fit” in a particular situation. For example, an AA meeting in a church basement (i.e., typical physical location of the meeting), surrounded by other AA members (i.e., people physically present), exchanging stories of their struggle (i.e., health information related to alcoholism) would emphasize the “AA member” social identity. Once the AA member social identity is salient, the TMIP posits that the IPNs associated with the AA member socia identity would guide MIPDs.

## Element 4: Privacy Calculus

Research has found that individuals will sometimes behave in a way that is counter to the MIPD specified by their IPNs (Awad and Krishnan 2006, Norberg et al. 2007). For example, in Figure 4, if the salient social identity is VHC community member, the normative decision is to release the data. However, the nurse may consider the costs and benefits of that MIPD. A possible benefit is that she will help the children of the VHC participants, but possible costs include violating HIPAA and enduring the subsequent fine and job loss. If the costs are perceived to outweigh the benefits, then the counter-normative decision may be made (i.e., the nurse does not release the data). Conversely, the benefits may be perceived to outweigh the costs (e.g., perhaps the nurse has seen one too many measles cases) or the costs may be misestimated by the individual (e.g., perhaps the nurse has not had adequate HIPAA training and does not know the MIPD would result in a HIPAA violation). If the cost-benefit calculation favors the normative decision, then in our example, the nurse may release the data. In fact, a situation similar to the scenario in Figure 4 has happened, which illustrates this outcome.<sup>5</sup> Therefore, in the TMIP, a cost-benefit analysis that favors the counter-normative decision may result in a counter-normative MIPD.

In the developmental theory of privacy, Laufer and Wolfe (1977) introduce the concept of the calculus of behavior, which is the foundation for the privacy calculus to examine privacy behaviors in the presence of cost-benefit tradeoffs (e.g., Dinev and Hart 2006, Jiang et al. 2013). The calculus of behavior can be viewed as an individual or group reflecting on the possible consequences, positive or negative, of en gaging in a behavior. Laufer and Wolfe (1977) describe how individuals release information when they think consequences can be managed and explain that individuals may not release information if they are unable to predict the consequences. The TMIP proposes that the salient social identity’s IPNs define the MIPD the individual or group ought to make (i.e., the normative MIPD), but that the MIPD prescribed by the IPNs is subject to a situational costbenefit analysis (i.e., the privacy calculus) that may result in the counter-normative MIPD in situations where the estimated benefit of making the counternormative MIPD is greater than the estimated cost. Therefore, following the logic of the privacy calculus, counter-normative behavior results when an indi vidual or group behaves in a way that is not in accordance with the activated IPNs of the salient social identity because the desire for a benefit overrides the normative MIPD. Counter-normative behavior resulting from the privacy calculus also provides an explanation for boundary turbulence. Boundary turbulence will occur when decision-makers intentionally execute the counter-normative MIPD to obtain a desired benefit because another social unit will gain knowledge of the information that would not have occurred had the IPNs of the salient socia identity been followed.

In the TMIP, we propose that the environmental characteristics can influence the estimation of the costs or benefits that determine the privacy calculus. For example, perhaps the chief executive officer (CEO) of a major corporation is in an AA meeting. Factoring in the reputational costs of the counternormative decision involving the disclosure of information the CEO shared in the AA meeting may change the privacy calculus, in which case the environmental characteristics (i.e., physical people) are directly impacting the estimation of the cost. Environmental characteristics may change over time with the development of new technologies (e.g., digital media), which could also result in difficulty determining costs or benefits, at least initially. In the developmental theory of privacy, Laufer and Wolfe (1977) expound on the unique role communication and record-keeping technologies can play in the privacy calculus. They explain that advances in such technologies can make it harder for individuals and groups to determine the future consequences of an information disclosure, which may lead them to discount the cost. This could result in the social unit executing the counter-normative MIPD because the benefits appear to outweigh the underestimated costs. Conversely, new technologies could introduce new benefits. For example, for individuals with rare diseases, communication with others suffering from the same affliction may have been limited or impossible prior to the VHCs that connected them. Following this logic, the TMIP posits that as environmental characteristics change over time, the estimation of the costs and benefits of a particular MIPD may become difficult, resulting in misestimations and unexpected experiential feedback. Such feedback will lead to adjustments to the cost and benefit estimates for that MIPD, which may eventually lead to norm refinement as a result of accu mulated experiential feedback.

## Element 5: Information Privacy Norms

Most IS researchers adopt the definition of information privacy as “the desire of individuals to control or have some influence over data about themselves” (Bélanger and Crossler 2011, p. 1017). Although this definition describes control as the desired outcome, it lacks specificity regarding how control is achieved and maintained. We propose that it is necessary to define the mechanism that allows for control of information privacy. CPM argues that privacy management is accomplished through rules that are developed to regulate the handling and flows of information within and between information boundaries (Petronio 2002). Norms, in the traditional sense, convey to people what “ought” to be done in a given situation (Opp 2001). Norms can be implicit—a shared understanding of accepted behavior, or explicit— written rules defining acceptable behavior (Lapinski and Rimal 2005). Thus, IPNs define a shared understanding (implicit) or written ruleset (explicit) for how information and interaction ought to be managed by a social unit. We define multilevel information privacy (I and we-privacy) as the ability of an individual or group to construct, regulate, and apply the rules (i.e., norms) for managing their information and interaction with others. Our definition thus explicitly states that the rule set (i.e., IPNs for managing information and interaction that an individual or group constructs, regulates, and applies) is the key mechanism used to control information privacy. Moreover, we propose there are two subcategories of IPNs—norms for information and interaction management. Laufer and Wolfe (1977) suggest a relationship between information and interaction management: “privacy in whatever form, presupposes the existence of others and the possibility of a relationship with them” and “the acting out of this relationship has two elements, information management and interaction management” (Laufer and Wolfe 1977, p. 33). Interaction management, according to Laufer and Wolfe (1977, p. 34) “is actually a form of noninteraction with specified other(s).” Therefore, we propose that information management norms refer to the implicit or explicit rules for managing an individual or group’s information, and interaction management norms refer to the implicit or explicit rules for managing the individual or group’s interaction with others.

We propose that at a given point in time, there may be one or more social identities available to an individual or group. Each social identity has norms for information and interaction management—IPNs. The environment influences the social identity that an individual or group assumes at that moment, and the IPNs of that social identity guide the MIPD. In the IS literature, SIT has been used to explore intention for individual or collective action on behalf of the group, called we-intention (Tsai and Bagozzi 2014). A we-intention is “a collective intention rooted in a person’s self-conception as a member of a particular group (e.g., an organization) or social category (e.g., one’s gender, one’s ethnicity), and action is conceived as either the group acting or the person acting as an agent of, or with, the group” (Bagozzi 2006, p. 18). We-intention suggests that group membership provides “collectively constructed group reasons to think, emote, and act in certain ways” (Tsai and Bagozzi 2014). Similarly, in the TMIP, individuals and groups construct and maintain IPNs that define the actions any social unit (i.e., individual or group) acting as their agent ought to take to manage their information and interaction.

CPM states that boundaries are linked in two ways (Petronio 2002). First, a boundary can be transformed from personal to collective when an individual tells another personal information. Second, a boundary can be appropriated when a person joins an existing group. In either case, someone becomes a co-owner of personal information and CPM states that each new co-owner needs to acquire or learn (either through negotiation or socialization) the rules associated with the information that has been shared (Petronio 2002). Importantly, CPM describes how when individuals enter preexisting groups (e.g., organizations, families) they are expected to learn the rules for managing the information that is collectively held by the members. In the TMIP, we formalize this further by proposing that the rules are the IPNs that are developed and maintained by the group and that when a social unit assumes the social identity of that group those IPNs are used to manage the group’s information. This allows the TMIP to cleanly explain what happens in the case of boundary turbulence (i.e., when IPNs are violated and information escapes to unintended targets or strangers).<sup>6</sup> In this case, there are two possible options: (1) the unintended target is (or becomes) part of the group and thus will use the social unit’s IPNs to manage the information if the social identity of that social unit is salient or (2) the IPNs of one of the social identities available to the new information owners may guide future MIPDs. For example, a video of a couple’s wedding dance was emailed to some of their family and friends. Unbeknownst to them, one of the recipients posted the video to YouTube (i.e., boundary turbulence) and it went viral, ending up with more than 91 million views. The song that the people in the video danced to was the property of a record company that chose to leave the video up and ultimately made money from it. The result of boundary turbulence from what was supposed to be a family and friends moment ended up being information that was managed using the IPNs of a record company,<sup>7</sup> among others. This example illustrates how information acquired by a social unit through boundary turbulence may be managed by the IPNs of the acquiring social unit (e.g., the record company manages the wedding video according to its IPNs).

When an MIPD is being made, the social unit making it could be an individual, a subset of a group, or the entire group. The decision-making social unit could be acting on its own behalf (personal identity) or on behalf of a group to which it claims membership (one of the available social identities). The MIPD made by the social unit will be guided by the IPNs of the social identity that is salient in that moment. Specifically, each individual develops an initial set of IPNs shaped by individual characteris tics, such as gender, culture, and personality. Similarly, each newly formed group develops an initial set of IPNs shaped by group characteristics, such as size, member influence, and carryover behaviors. Moreover, group composition is defined by the distributions of individual characteristics of the initial group members, and group composition can shape a group’s initial IPNs. Once the initial IPNs are established, they may be continuously refined over time. First, membership of groups may change and the addition of new members or loss of old members may change the group composition, which may lead to IPN refinement. Second, when an individual joins a new group, the IPNs of that group could lead to his or her personal norms being refined. Third, accumulated experiential feedback from MIPBs may lead to IPN refinement (e.g., every time a social unit executes an MIPB, some positive or negative feedback may result). Finally, time for a person is experienced as aging or growth and in society as historical and technological changes (Laufer and Wolfe 1977), which may also lead to norm refinement because such events may alter the environmental landscape in which future MIPDs are made and thus shape new experiential feedback.

Prior research mixes the discussion of norm development and refinement, not always providing a clear delineation between the two. Therefore, in Figure 6, we provide example individual and group characteristics from the literature that may shape the initial form of individual or group IPNs. For example, Feldman (1984) identifies four key factors that explain norm formation in the context of work teams: peer or manager influence, critical events, primacy, and carry-over behaviors. Because Feldman’s research focuses on work teams, he identified peer or manager influence as a factor influencing norm formation, which reflects the influence of group composition on norm development. Primacy reflects the first “behavior pattern that emerges in a group,” which “sets group expectations” (Feldman 1984, p. 51). In other words, if an explicit rule does not exist, the way something is done the first time the group meets may become the norm. Carry-over behaviors are those actions members of a group bring to a new group that are based on expectations they have from being in other groups. Feldman’s fourth factor describes how norms may change over time in response to critical events that make the group reassess their norms. This factor shapes norms over time as groups experience events that cause them to refine their norms, which we place within the scope of experiential feedback in the TMIP. In the TMIP, privacy events that are experienced as a consequence of executing an MIPB may lead to norm refinement over time.

In describing privacy rule development, CPM suggests that “individuals depend on five criteria to generate privacy rules for access and protection of private information, including: (a) culture, (b) gender, (c) motivations, (d) context, and (e) risk-benefit ratio” (Petronio 2002, pp. 38–39). CPM does not begin from an initial set of rules, but rather describes how new rules are constantly being developed as a result of these five criteria. We separate these five criteria into (1) individual characteristics that also determine group composition (i.e., gender and culture), and (2) evolving circumstances that are less stable (i.e., motivations, context, and risk-benefit ratio). In the TMIP, we posit that social units start with gender and cultural characteristics that can shape their initial IPNs and that the latter three of CPM’s criteria more accurately fit within norm refinement because they may occur over time. Specifically, motivations in CPM are suggested to lead to rule formation or rule enactment to satisfy particular needs (e.g., reciprocity, attraction, or to reduce ambiguity) that may arise over one’s lifetime (e.g., sharing private information to draw closer to a potential mate). Context in CPM may cause situational needs to be addressed through new privacy rule formation, for example, a trauma experience necessitating the development of new privacy rules for therapeutic reasons. The risk-benefit ratio in CPM is described as the process of estimating the risks and benefits of either revealing or concealing, which once assessed can lead to rule formation favoring the advantaged outcome. In the IS literature, the estimation of the risk and benefits is described as a privacy calculus, which in the TMIP we propose determines whether the normative MIPD will be executed. However, as previously discussed, we further posit that the estimation of the cost and the benefit components of the privacy calculus can change over time as a result of experience.

What the prior discussion of classic literature reveals is that IPNs can evolve over time as both the social unit and society evolve over time. First, socia units accumulate experiences over time (i.e., experience positive and negative feedback from MIPBs over time). Second, the larger social environment changes over time (e.g., new technologies are invented, historical events cause shifts in popular opinion). In the developmental theory of privacy, Laufer and Wolfe (1977, p. 25) state that

the individual and normative aspects of privacy are interdependent [and that] time provides the dynamic basis for the interdependence [because] the individual experiences time as growing or aging, while within the sociocultural system time is manifest as historical and technological development.

Following this logic, an MIPD is guided by the salient social identity’s IPNs for information and interaction management that can change with age/ growth (e.g., as the individual or group experiences privacy events over time) and technological/historical developments (e.g., new digital media, a major data breach in the news). Therefore, the TMIP proposes that each new individual or group develops initial rules (i.e., IPNs) for managing its information and interaction. Moreover, these initial IPNs may be refined over time (1) as a consequence of experiential feedback an individual or group accumulates from making MIPBs, and (2) as a result of privacy experiences accumulated as the entity ages and technological/historical developments occur that shift the environmental landscape producing new experiential feedback.

The perspective on time offered by Laufer and Wolfe (1977) is critical to the TMIP for two reasons. First, it allows the framework to accommodate technological development by acknowledging that such changes can modify the environmental characteristics in which an MIPD occurs. For example, consider the advent of social media, which changed the environmental characteristics (i.e., introduced a new digital media option). Social media differs from other digital media and offline information disclosure in distinct ways that have been enumerated (McFarland and Ployhart 2015; see Table 2). When social media was first introduced, and as new social media platforms are introduced or old ones change, entities experience new consequences (positive and negative) of making MIPDs under new environmental conditions, which may lead to norm refinement. Before the advent of YouTube, the couple’s wedding video in our earlier example could not have been propagated with such ease (i.e., become viral). Technological or historica developments are societal changes or events that have broader impact, but may result in unexpected experiential feedback for the social unit because they may change the environmental landscape. In other words, society gets a new social media platform (e.g., Snapchat is introduced) or a historical event of societal importance occurs (e.g., alleged Russian meddling in the U.S. presidential election through Facebook), but such developments may result in new experiential feedback for social units. In fact, the role of knowledge or media exposure on individual privacy concerns has been examined in IS privacy research (e.g., Malhotra et al. 2004). As an entity ages, it may acquire knowledge by consuming news about privacy issues or breaches, which may also lead to norm refinement.

Second, we propose in the TMIP that norm refinement can occur as a result of accumulated experiential feedback from MIPBs. That is, if a social unit executes a normative MIPB many times and it frequently results in negative experiential feedback, as a result of this accumulated experience, the social unit may consider refining its IPNs to avoid the negative outcome. Similarly, if a social unit executes a counternormative MIPB many times and it frequently results in positive experiential feedback, the social unit may consider refining its IPNs. CPM discusses the experiential refinement of privacy rules through boundary turbulence, which partially supports this logic. CPM describes boundary turbulence, which occurs when privacy rules are broken or perceived to be broken. Examples include intentional misuse or misinterpretation of privacy rules or creation of boundary linkages that violate collective privacy expectations (Petronio 2002). When boundary turbulence occurs, individuals try to “correct the problem and integrate new information into the rule system so that adjustments may be made to achieve coordination” (Petronio 2002, p. 33). The boundary turbulence process examines counter-normative behavior by type and suggests such behavior can lead to adjustment of privacy rules. In the TMIP, counter-normative behavior may result from the privacy calculus overriding the normative MIPD of the salient social identity. Thus, if the counter-normative behavior results in positive (or negative) experiential feedback over time, the TMIP proposes that this could lead to norm refinement.

Therefore, viewed through the lens of time, the motivations (e.g., building a relationship or reducing ambiguity), situational needs (e.g., recovering from trauma), and risk-benefit ratio factors discussed in CPM are life, historical, or technological (i.e., time) events that may possibly refine the social unit’s IPNs or change the estimation of either the cost or benefit of an MIPD. For example, after the Cambridge Analytica scandal, Facebook was called to testify publicly in front of the U.S. Congress. Such scandal could lead to more revealing of information than Facebook would normally do in order to help assuage public anger (i.e., the benefit of the counternormative MIPD is greater than the cost). This counternormative MIPD may be made until the organization recovers from scandal (i.e., the benefit of the counternormative decision no longer outweighs the cost), which would be a temporary change in the estimation of the costs and benefits of an MIPD. Alternatively, positive experiential feedback over time from greater public disclosure could lead to Facebook’s IPNs being permanently refined.

Finally, the TMIP also proposes that personal IPNs can shape group IPNs, and vice versa. Specifically, as membership changes (i.e., individuals enter or exit groups), individuals bring or take IPNs with them, which may lead to continuous norm refinement as the social units evolve. CPM describes rule development to be a continuous process, but in discussing the possibilities for acquisition of privacy rules, states that “although rule development is fundamental to privacy management, people do not continuously generate new privacy rules. Instead, individuals may enter preexisting privacy boundaries. They are often expected to learn ongoing rules already established by other co-owners of the information” (Petronio 2002, p. 72). TMIP modifies this logic by suggesting that when an individual joins a group, they learn the IPNs associated with that group, but by joining a new group the influence of the group’s IPNs may lead to refinement of the individual’s norms. The social psychology literature describes this process as the norms of the newly joined group leading to refinement of the individual’s norms through conformity via social influence mechanisms (Kelman 1958, Turner 1991, Cialdini and Trost 1998). Furthermore, when an individual joins a group, his or her presence changes the group’s composition. Thus, prior literature supports the TMIP’s logic that IPNs may be refined as group membership changes.

## Implications of the TMIP on IS Research

Building on solid theoretical foundations, we developed the TMIP to explain the process by which individuals and groups make MIPDs. The TMIP organizes complex MIPDs into a process model that can guide future IS researchers as they investigate increasingly complex MIPDs in a digital age defined by data analytics and socialization in virtual environments. To facilitate its use by IS researchers, we have organized the TMIP’s major logic and assumptions into Table 3. The organization of Table 3 is numerically mapped to the subsections that develop the theoretical foundation for the TMIP above, as well as to the associated elements in Figure 1 that illustrates the theoretical framework. We also provide implications for IS researchers in Table 3 presented as research questions and examples. The implications are intended to provide thought-provoking ideas regarding how to operationalize the different components of the TMIP and to enable IS researchers to incorporate elements of the TMIP into their work.

## Conclusion

This research conceptualizes and defines I-privacy and we-privacy as inclusive of interaction and information management considerations at multiple levels of analysis so that future research can extend beyond the realm of individual privacy concerns. We propose the TMIP to illustrate the nature of multilevel information privacy management. Specifically, the TMIP explicates the process through which environmental characteristics stimulate social units to assume social identities that lead to the activation of personal or group IPNs that guide MIPDs, which with consideration of cost-benefit tradeoffs, result in MIPBs.
