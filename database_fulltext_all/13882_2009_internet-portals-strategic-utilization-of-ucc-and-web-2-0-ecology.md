Keywords: Economics of user-created content Community-based search Web 2.0 Open Web

## a b s t r a c t

Some innovative portals are now utilizing user-created content (UCC) through community-based search (CBS). Surveys observe that most UCC is actually copied from many peripheral nodes. CBS not only changes portals from gateways to destinations on the web, but also directs the <sup>fl</sup>ow of contents from satellites into portals, which may further strengthen search traf<sup>fi</sup>c monopolization within the portal. Under a search portal's network centrality, we model the underlying economics of UCC production and traf<sup>fi</sup>c distribution between a portal and satellites. Our results show that a portal exercises the co-petition strategy by subsidizing traf<sup>fi</sup>c to satellites so that satellites return contents subsidization. Managerial and design implications to Web 2.0 sites are derived from our analytical results.

## 1. Introduction

A web has been viewed as a network composed of a small handful of central hubs and many peripheral nodes [1,5]. While innumerable sites exist on the web, a few dominant web hubs govern a major portion of the traf<sup>fi</sup>c. The recent advent of the open web revolutionized the way of producing and aggregating web content, which created a new type of super-stars among a galaxy of web sites. For example, in Wikipedia — a highly regarded reference on the web — content is created by a number of voluntary users' contributions, and this has been highly successful [27].

While information can be found quickly and easily using search tools, the accuracy of search results has been a challenge for web users due to the lack of available information as well as a large volume of questionable information [22]. However, the advent of Web 2.0 has delivered a unique solution engaging users in the collaborative creation of the exact information that they want and Wikipedia proves the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of this mechanism either in terms of information breadth or depth. Hence, such successful UCC (user-created content) aggregators are enjoying and sharpening the centralization of web traf<sup>fi</sup>c.

Some portals are also making an innovation in search services through this advantage of UCC. One such example is the communitybased search (CBS). Portals provide a Q&A community, where one person submits a question, and anybody who has a response shares her/his ideas by answering the question. Portals incorporate their Q&A database into traditional search results. Because the direct question and answer format creates very speci<sup>fi</sup>c and personalized information, CBS signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly satis<sup>fi</sup>es users' information needs [9,18,32]. The popularity of CBS delivered great monopoly power to a few portals in terms of traf<sup>fi</sup>c attraction and con<sup>fi</sup>nement. Naver, the <sup>fi</sup>rst portal to launch CBS, handles around 77% of internet searches originating from Korea [22], and roughly half of all inquiries are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed by the portal's Q&A database. In other words, half of in<sup>fl</sup>ow traf<sup>fi</sup>c is con<sup>fi</sup>ned in this portal even after the search has been completed (see Appendix A, Fig. 3 for details).

Now, some portals are leveraging CBS for web traf<sup>fi</sup>c. If this trend continues, portals will continue to accumulate more content, and may eventually be able to monopolize web traf<sup>fi</sup>c. With the seemingly excessive concentration of traf<sup>fi</sup>c within a few giant portals — over 75% of U.S. search traf<sup>fi</sup>c is garnered by only two search portals, Google and Yahoo, and the top four portals held 91.5% of the 2007 U.S [21], cursory observation of traf<sup>fi</sup>c distribution by CBS implies that the increased visit of information seekers and their longer stay provide more content within these portals' Q&A community.

However, further investigation on how UCC is created raises an interesting and critical research question. Originally, portals have served as the gateways of the web [13], and many satellite sites (an information site outside portals, which can be indexed by a portal's search engine and users can visit them through the links that a portal's search service provides) have thrived on the <sup>fl</sup>ow of traf<sup>fi</sup>c directed by those hubs. While this hub and spoke structure presents the out-<sup>fl</sup>ow of traf<sup>fi</sup>c from a portal to satellites, the production of UCC is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly based on the in<sup>fl</sup>ow of content from satellites to a portal.

In order to answer the questions in a portal's Q&A community, users refer to the satellites that they know better than the inquiring person knows the satellites. They un-bundle or re-bundle the information available on the web according to their expectations and preferences. Here, we note that the majority of contemporary UCC (95%) is replicated from satellites to portals, and the actual creation is limited to a small portion, under 5% [41]. Satellite sites are the ultimate sources for most UCC, not the brains of users as the name suggests.

What happens to those satellites if con<sup>fi</sup>nement of traf<sup>fi</sup>c within portals continues? Will this simply lead to a winner-takes-all scenario? Traf<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fl</sup>ow is the ultimate incentive for any site. Hence, at <sup>fi</sup>rst glance, it seems that traf<sup>fi</sup>c monopolization by portals empowered with CBS will lower the incentive of satellite sites to provide content. Furthermore, since satellites are the easiest source for UCC production, traf<sup>fi</sup>c starvation in satellites may dry out UCC in the portal. Hence, satellite sites play a dual role for portals: they are simultaneously complementors for content provision and competitors for gaining traf<sup>fi</sup>c.

By virtue of CBS, portals have become almost free riders not only in terms of content provision, but also in traf<sup>fi</sup>c con<sup>fi</sup>nement. This phenomenon brings up a serious concern about the ecosystem of Web 2.0. Will the satellites be eventually starved to death by a portal's traf<sup>fi</sup>c monopolization and will this fossilization of satellites <sup>fi</sup>nally cause the collapse of the information ecology that the Internet has so successfully built up? The collapse of satellites would deprive portals of this opportunity. In this vein, our main research objective is to identify the underlying economics of UCC production and to investigate the optimal strategy of UCC hubs (e.g. the portal with CBS) in governing web traf<sup>fi</sup>c and managing UCC. We provide managerial and design implications to Web 2.0 sites.

## 2. Motivation and literature review

## 2.1. Community-based search

Nowadays Internet users, namely information prosumers, exchange information and help each other to solve problems through so-called “social search” [4]. Social search is a service facilitating the free provision of content by users, now known by the popular term UCC, and CBS is a representative social search [22]. If a user enters an inquiry in a CBS portal (a search portal that has a Q&A database), the search engine yields relevant items from its proprietary Q&A database along with traditional search results such as news sites or web pages. Then, the user can choose either the portal's Q&A contents or an external web site. If the user chooses the Q&A contents, she/he will stay inside the portal even after inquiry. Otherwise she/he'll move to an external web site.

Naver, the leading search portal in Korea provides a good example of this [6]. Naver became the instant winner in the search market as soon as its Q&A community was introduced in 2002. Naver delivers more relevant search results than Google using CBS, at least on its home turf [23]. A simple Google search returns only certain kinds of Web pages, and a user needs to click another link to <sup>fi</sup>nd the information. Although Google attempted to develop smarter search engines that provide more “relevant” information with less search time, the “relevance” of information is “user-speci<sup>fi</sup>c” and “taskspeci<sup>fi</sup>c” [18]. Yet, the information that most search engines provide is limited to static information from similar sources [6,7].

Naver built up a proprietary database by encouraging users to ask and answer questions on anything from recipes to the composition of rocket fuel. Naver has accumulated a user-generated database of 70 million questions and answers by Jul 2007. When a user enters an inquiry in Naver, the search engine yields relevant items from its own Q&A database along with traditional search results from news sites and Web pages. Roughly half of all inquiries are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed by Naver's internal content accumulated within the Q&A database. Even though Naver was a clear laggard among search portals where leadership advantage was cited as a de<sup>fi</sup>ning success factor, it was capable of defeating global giants. In 2007, Naver handled 77% of Internet searches originating in Korea, dwar<sup>fi</sup>ng worldwide leaders Yahoo! (4.4%) and Google (1.7%) [22].

Q&A database and CBS have become a must-have feature not only for Korean search portals but also for the global portals. By the end of 2007, Yahoo!Answers has 120 million users, accumulating 400 million answers to questions [22]. Yahoo!Answers incorporates a Q&A database into its search results. Although Google closed its U.S. Q&A site in 2006, they rejoined the trend with new services in Russia and China in 2007 and Yahoo! has 26 localized Q&A sites.

With the success of CBS, portals are now monopolizing web traf<sup>fi</sup>c by absorbing and con<sup>fi</sup>ning most of it. In 2007, over 75% of U.S. search traf<sup>fi</sup>c was garnered by only two search portals — Google and Yahoo!, and Naver captured around 77% of Korean search traf<sup>fi</sup>c [6,22]. Furthermore, while traditional search services direct traf<sup>fi</sup>c to other indexed web sites, CBS con<sup>fi</sup>nes it within portals, because most inquiries <sup>fi</sup>nd solutions in the portal's proprietary Q&A database. CBS portals are thus being transformed from gateways into destinations on the web, con<sup>fi</sup>ning a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of web traf<sup>fi</sup>c [6,22]. Yet, such an interesting phenomenon has not been explored intensively so far, which delivers a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant motivation for this study.

## 2.2. Traditional portal studies

There have been many studies focusing on the general roles of portals. Dewan et al. [13,15] explained the formation of portals as a result of externality on the web: a portal with more traf<sup>fi</sup>c earns more revenue and continues to serve more consumers, providing more content. Other studies considered portal strategies for traf<sup>fi</sup>c attraction and pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization: improving search engine effectiveness [9,40], community building [37], the balancing of advertising and content provision [12,14], and enhancing portal service quality in terms of information presentation and site design [16,43]. In studies of competition between portals, portal alliances [10,17], brand extensions [31] and increases in switching cost [25] were also emphasized. Observing the <sup>fi</sup>erce competition between search portals, Gandal [19] pointed out the decline in <sup>fi</sup>rst-mover advantage for search portals.

In the midst of plentiful attention to portal strategies over competitors, Rozanski and Bollman [33] critically pointed out the under-evaluation of the business value of portal shifting to destination-oriented sites. They suggested that portals provide attractive options to ensure each user's loyalty. However, research has been biased to the opportunities given to portals, namely UCC aggregators, spotlighting them as main players in the new open web. The value of fundamental UCC supporters (e.g. satellites) remains unexplored, to the best of our knowledge.

We examine portals' strategic co-petition (cooperation and competition) with satellites. Our research is in line with a new metaphor for the health of the industry ecosystem, suggested by Moore [29]. He argued that every company should be viewed as a part of a business ecosystem, not as a simple competitor. Companies coevolve capabilities around new innovations; hence, they should balance cooperation and competition until the next round of new innovation in an ecosystem. Google Map's Mash-up service, eBay or Amazon's ‘OpenAPI’ strategy to support third parties all establish networking platforms where all users, entrepreneurs and third-party partners work together for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of all [44]. In spite of the interest and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the ecosystem of business, there is a paucity of theoretical analysis on this issue. Our study provides a model for investigating the ecology of Web 2.0.

## 3. Basic settings and model

## 3.1. Location of information providers

In our study, we model traf<sup>fi</sup>c competition between two kinds of information providers on the web, a portal and satellites. Of course, portals have various services to lure users and to accomplish their goals, i.e. search, email or connection services [28]. Among these, the search service is so dominant that portals are commonly called ‘search portals’. Focusing on this main role of portals as information searchers and providers, we do not consider some other minor services of portals in this paper. In order to model the location of these two types of players, we adopt the circular city spatial model. There are two reasons for using circular model rather than linear model. First, we borrow the creative variant of the circular city model by Balasubramanian [3] where a direct marketer is located at the center of a circular city while traditional retailers are on the circumference. The direct marketer has no physical location but has an even universal impact on the retailers. Hence locating the direct marketer at the center of the circle is quite an innovative and useful idea, which is very applicable for the portal in our study because it enjoys network centrality. Second, the instability of equilibrium by the linear model is well studied. The border locations in the linear model face different competitive conditions because they are not symmetrically placed between other locations [38]. Hence, the linear model may lead to erroneous analysis when we consider several evenly located competitors (e.g. the satellites in our model).

In our model, as depicted in Fig. 1, the circumference of a circle represents the uniform distribution of users' information needs. For simplicity of analysis, we normalize the scope of potential information needs, the size of the circle, to 1, and n (≥2) satellites actually provide information content to meet the users' needs. Each satellite site is denoted by s on the circle, the scope of information coverage of which is identically q. With the even-handed effect on all the satellites, the portal, p, is located at the center of the circle.

## 3.2. Generation of UCC

There must exist some information that is really dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to <sup>fi</sup>nd on the web, as Horowitz, the vice president of product strategy in Yahoo!, succinctly recognized. Reasons for this void can be diverse. First of all, some information may be very tacit to be coded, or some content may be very idiosyncratic so that satellite investment cannot be economically justi<sup>fi</sup>ed. In our circular model, e parts correspond to this type of information outside of satellite coverage. A larger gap allows more room for UCC, since only personalized and customized content by peer users (e.g. a Q&A community) can <sup>fi</sup>ll such a gap. Mathematical conditions for the existence of such a content gap between satellite coverage and users' needs are simply assumed such that:

$$
0 <   n q <   1.\tag{1}
$$

We assume that a portal has a Q&A community and encourages users to post questions and answers using some incentive schemes (e.g. mileage service, reward for best answers, and so on). Through the accumulation of Q&A content, a certain portion of information is internalized in a portal from the circumference (total scope of information needs). Let α be this scope of the portal's internal content endowment, namely Q&A content, which partially includes both q and e parts of information without differentiation, where

Fig. 1. Circular model for a portal and satellites.

$$
0 <   \alpha \leq 1.\tag{2}
$$

UCC is a general term for any content submitted by “digital commons” rather than web publishers. However, closer examination reveals that not all UCC is UGC (User Generated Content). We need to distinguish the answers originally created by users from the derivative information of pre-existing content [42]. Surveys claim that the majority of UCC is the material seemingly copied from other sources [41]. The participants of a Q&A community aggregate and synthesize content from other sources with their own ideas to formulate an answer to any individual question, or at least, they are inspired by the rich content from other web sources. Hence, when a new answer is created in a Q&A community, the probability of waiting depends on the volume of knowledge pre-existing on the web: the more information there is, the higher the probability of <sup>fi</sup>nding answer quickly.

## 3.3. Utility functions

Now, we describe users' utilities according to their information needs and choices of destination sites. We assume that all information seekers select a search portal as their entry point for web sur<sup>fi</sup>ng. Information can be described as a bundle of characteristics: quality, location and availability hence, users choose a destination site among search results considering both ‘transaction cost’ and ‘information quality’, namely the ‘perfection or relevance’ of information [38]. Let's assume that a user enters an inquiry on a “scienti<sup>fi</sup>c topic” in a CBS portal. The search engine yields relevant items from its own Q&A database (α) along with traditional search results indexing satellite sites (e.g. sciencemag.org, sciencedaily.com, wikipedia.org). We model that the user chooses the best option considering both the relevance of information (vertical competition) and the transaction cost for gaining the information (horizontal competition) among the cases below.

## Case 1. When the information is available on the web

This case means when the inquiry completely matches the content of α (portal) or q (satellites). Because we assume that α portion of information (in this case, α% of total information needs on the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c issue) is already available in the portal's internal Q&A database and the information from Q&A is more relevant compared to the traditional search results due to its customization process [23], the user is immediately satis<sup>fi</sup>ed by the portal's Q&A database and con<sup>fi</sup>ned within the portal with the probability of α. Such con<sup>fi</sup>nement of traf<sup>fi</sup>c in a portal shows the vertical competition between portal and satellites.

With the probability of (1 −α), the user's inquiry is not immediately answered by the portal's Q&A database. In this case, she/he has different options depending on the location of the information she/ he wants. If the inquiry falls into q ranges, it can be instantly satis<sup>fi</sup>ed by the information in a satellite. The satellite (e.g. sciencemag.org, sciencedaily.com) may be directed by the portal's traditional search service; hence the user will leave the portal and move to the satellites. We assume that a user's utility of fully and immediately answered inquiry is U.

$$
U _ {p, \alpha} = U _ {s, q} = \overline {{U}}.
$$

The <sup>fi</sup>rst subscript represents the content provider (portal or satellites), and the second subscript denotes the location of the user inquiry (note that α portion of information is already available in a portal whether the original inquiries are from q or e parts).

## Case 2. When the information is not available on the web

If she/he wants the information in e parts that are not covered by α, she/he cannot <sup>fi</sup>nd the information instantly from either satellites or the portals' Q&A database. Information unavailability lowers the utility with the imperfection of information or incurs greater transaction cost of information gaining. Let x denote the distance of one's inquiry in e range to the center of q by the nearest satellite content (see Fig.1). The user at x can visit the satellites that host the information partially relevant to her/ his inquiry (see Eq. (3)). Otherwise, the user can stay in a portal's Q&A community and submit their question, hoping that altruistic peers will respond to it with proper answers (see Eq. (4)).

$$
U _ {s, e} (x) = \overline {{U}} - t \Bigl (x - \frac {q}{2} \Bigr)\tag{3}
$$

$$
U _ {p, e} (x) = \overline {{U}} - w (1 - \alpha - \beta n q).\tag{4}
$$

## Case 2.1. When a user visits the nearest satellite

In ${ \mathrm { E q . } } ( 3 ) , ( x - { \begin{array} { l } { q } \\ { 2 } \end{array} } )$ implies the deviation in information relevancy between her/his inquiry and the answer provided by the nearest satellite. Additionally, we assume t to be the cost for <sup>fi</sup>nding such a web site. In case of physical transportation, it is assumed that the consumers know the exact location of the destination. However, the degree of information deviation is not explicitly represented to searchers, although an individual has a unique distance from a satellite based on her/his inquiry. In this situation, t, the cost of <sup>fi</sup>nding the nearest satellite can be managed by the portal's traf<sup>fi</sup>c control policy and technology. If a portal provides its conventional search results in a more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient way, for example, by providing the degree of site relevancy to each inquiry, users' time consumption to <sup>fi</sup>nd nearest satellite will be reduced. By contrast, if a portal chooses not to provide this valuable information, users will have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in instantly <sup>fi</sup>nding the most relevant site, which will drive the users to use the Q&A community. Thus when portals impose high levels of t, they can capture more traf<sup>fi</sup>c from e parts, while lower t directs more traf<sup>fi</sup>c to satellites.

However, such portals' algorithms for ef<sup>fi</sup>cient searching are limited in their ability to guide inquiries from e parts to the most relevant satellite. Although searchers may succeed in <sup>fi</sup>nding the most relevant satellite under a given level of t, an information gap still exists between their needs and the satellite information. Hence, information searchers' disutility is described by the product of t and $\textstyle ( x - { \frac { q } { 2 } } )$ in Eq. (3).

The satellite site can be either a general web site (e.g. sciencemag. org) or a UCC site (e.g. wikipedia.org) and the user will move to the satellite she/he thinks to be better. Because we are focusing on the <sup>fi</sup>nal destination of each inquiry, this is the case where the inquiry is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed not by a portals' Q&A but by a satellite site whether the satellite is a UCC site or not.

## Case 2.2. When a user asks a question in a portal's Q&A community

In this case, the user can be fully answered by the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and personalization advantage of the Q&A mechanism. However, it takes time to get a near perfect answer from peers. Many altruistic peers will answer the question. Sometimes, most of the answers can partially cover the information she/he wants so that, in order to synthesize several answers to get full information, she/he should wait for a number of answers to be accumulated. The function W denotes the disutility of such waiting. In order to characterize this function W, we need to consider the complementarity from pre-existing content to the generation of answers, as described in Section 3.2. The pre-existing content includes the portal's Q&A database and external sources available to more search-savvy users. The former is represented by α and the latter is captured by nq in our model. Hence, we assume the following characteristics of W(α, nq):

$$
\frac {\partial W}{\partial \alpha} <   0, \quad \frac {\partial W}{\partial n q} <   0.\tag{5}
$$

For simplicity of analysis, we model the linear function as $W ( \alpha , n q ) =$ $w ( 1 - \alpha - \beta n q )$ , where $w ( > 0 )$ is the marginal disutility from waiting in a portal, the parameter $\beta ( > 0 )$ captures the extent of replicated content from satellites by users, namely, content complementarities from satellites to a portal for a given w and n (Note that $\partial U _ { p , e } /$ $\partial q = n w \beta .$ . For a given n and w, β captures the marginal contribution to consumer utility when an inquiry is ful<sup>fi</sup>lled by the new answers in a portal's Q&A community). Because we assumed the scope of potential information to be 1, the creation of content in a portal happens within this scope: 1Nα+βnq.

## 3.4. Demand functions

Based on the competition between satellites and a portal, the demand is determined as follows. First, α portion of information searchers instantly choose portals. Next, among $( 1 - \alpha )$ portion of information searchers, n number of satellites also directly captures q portion of the searchers by its content provision. Hence, portals and satellites compete for only e parts of searchers from the $( 1 - \alpha )$ portion. In this case, the choice between a portal and satellites is governed by the expected size of $U _ { p , e } ( { \boldsymbol { x } } )$ and $U _ { s , e } ( x )$ , and x̃, the indifferent location between these two options, is as follows:

$$
\tilde {x} (\alpha , t, q) = \frac {w (1 - \alpha)}{t} + \left(\frac {1}{2} - \frac {w n \beta}{t}\right) q.\tag{6}
$$

Then portals will capture all inquiries for x above the threshold x, and̃ satellites get traf<sup>fi</sup>c below x̃ from e parts. Note that there are n number of e ranges. The total demand for the portal, $D _ { p } ,$ is the sum of the immediately captured α portion of searchers, and the ones who wait for help from the Q&A community service from the $( 1 - \alpha )$ portion.

$$
D _ {p} = \alpha + (1 - \alpha) \left(2 n \int_ {\tilde {x}} ^ {\frac {1}{2 n}} 1 \mathrm{d} x\right) = 1 - 2 n (1 - \alpha) \tilde {x}.\tag{7}
$$

Among the $( 1 - \alpha )$ portion of users, each satellite captures x̃ portion of inquiries from the center of q to both sides; hence, the demand for a satellite, $D _ { s } ,$ is obtained such that:

$$
D _ {s} = (1 - \alpha) \left(2 \int_ {0} ^ {\tilde {x}} 1 d x\right) = (1 - \alpha) 2 \tilde {x}.\tag{8}
$$

## 3.5. Profit functions

Because the accumulation of a Q&A database easily draws the interest of information searchers, a CBS portal can sell its advertisements to businesses [42]. Moreover, with the portal's diminishing role as a gateway, its revenue model is also evolving similar to that of direct marketers (e.g. satellites), which are paid by consumers seeking content. Under this hybrid revenue scenario, a portal's compensation depends more on the number of con<sup>fi</sup>ned users who respond to the content and ads than the total size of in-bound traf<sup>fi</sup>c which is the probable audience for ads [33]. The direct link between traf<sup>fi</sup>c and revenue is assumed to be the same for satellite sites. Hence, portals and satellites try to lure and capture more traf<sup>fi</sup>c to maximize their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. We assume that both make a pro<sup>fi</sup>t of μ per unit of traf<sup>fi</sup>c con<sup>fi</sup>nement, with a quadratic production cost for the provision of content.

$$
\pi_ {s} = \mu D _ {s} - q ^ {2}\tag{9}
$$

$$
\pi_ {p} = \mu D _ {p} - c \alpha^ {2}.\tag{10}
$$

As a platform provider, the portal <sup>fi</sup>rst invests in its platform — installation of a web server and implementation of a web site. This <sup>fi</sup>xed cost is similar to that of satellites. Hence, we ignore the <sup>fi</sup>xed cost in our model. However, portals and satellites have different marginal costs in production of content. By leveraging the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of user participation, the time taken for content to be created and spread is greatly reduced as compared to traditional information providers [40]. A portal's marginal costs are simply for managing the growing content, and are much lower than those of satellites, which must invest in the production of original content. Therefore, we assume that portals have great cost advantages over satellites for provision of the same content.

$$
c \ll 1.\tag{11}
$$

This is a well-observed economic rationale for open business models based on UCC [10,12].

In controlling the level of t, a portal must decide whether or not to provide useful information for searchers (e.g. site relevancy noti<sup>fi</sup>cation). We assume that the implementation of this service is already possible due to the state-of-the-art of the technology, which is characteristic of portals. Hence, it is not the technical issues which require IT investment, but the strategic decision-making problem. Compared to the cost of content provision, the manipulation cost of t for portals' traf<sup>fi</sup>c control policy is very minor; hence, we do not consider it in the cost function.

## 4. Results

To increase web traf<sup>fi</sup>c and lure information searchers, portals and satellites determine their optimal content provision via α and q. Moreover, satellite content provision depends on portals' traf<sup>fi</sup>c distribution policy, which is realized by t. Hence, portals should make comprehensive decisions based on α and t for optimal sharing of traf<sup>fi</sup>c with satellites. The order of the game is as follows:

At stage 1, a portal decides α and t.

At stage 2, each satellite decides q.

By backward induction, we derive the solutions. At the second stage, a satellite's pro<sup>fi</sup>t function is derived from Eqs. (6), (8) and (9) such that:

$$
\pi_ {s} = 2 \mu (1 - \alpha) \left\{\frac {w (1 - \alpha)}{t} + \left(\frac {1}{2} - \frac {w n \beta}{t}\right) q \right\} - q ^ {2}.\tag{12}
$$

## 4.4. Content dependency and investments

From the comparative statistics, we <sup>fi</sup>nd some seemingly counterintuitive and interesting results from portals' and satellites' decisions. These results reveal how the content dependency between portals and satellites critically affects their investment decisions, $\alpha ^ { * }$ and $q ^ { * }$ .

Proposition 5. Portals' content provision increases, while that of satellites decreases as the number of satellites, n increases.

It is interesting that portals increase their content provision with more satellites, content sources. A larger number of satellites mean more diverse sources for portals' content provision. However, this situation is detrimental to satellite pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, which makes satellites invest less in their content provision. Therefore, portals should invest on their own. This is consistent with the more dominating role of portals with the rise of web sites. It is reported that now cyberspace is populated with more than 80 million web sites [20], but there is no sign that portals are losing their dominance [1,5]. In fact, the opposite trend is well re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the remark, “A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention” [36] for satellites. Now, portals' CBS supplements the drain of satellites' economic incentives by utilizing UCC in an economical way.

Proposition 6. The progress of UCC technologies intensifies the content dominance of the portal.

As UCC related technology progresses, the easier instigation of user participation enables more content to be built within the portal, which results in the reduction of satellites' investment incentive. The comparative statistics on this parameter (c) shows that portals' content provision increases, while that of satellites decreases with the decrease in marginal cost (c). Hence, more easy-to-use tools for UCC and more open content as ingredients for UCC will shift the balance of content accumulation in portals and satellites.

## 5. Conclusion

Users not only create new content but also modify the existing content by replicating and synthesizing in order to provide UCC. Its huge success is well observed in the example of Wikipedia-like knowledge UCC sites. Moreover, the merit of CBS is well manifested by the surprising success of a search portal, Naver.com. We investigate CBS portals' optimal strategies for utilizing UCC. Although the portals have the opportunity of monopolizing traf<sup>fi</sup>c through CBS, we show that they should exercise the co-existence strategy by subsidizing traf<sup>fi</sup>c to satellites so that satellites return content subsidization to the portals. The contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows.

First, in a methodological perspective, we propose an innovative circular model, which investigates the centrality of web traf<sup>fi</sup>c in a portal and the interdependent relationship between a portal and its satellites. The original Salop's model analyzed the monopolistically competitive equilibrium of evenly spaced <sup>fi</sup>rms on a circle, for attracting consumers who suffer transportation costs. Although, on the surface, our model may seem to simply show the equilibrium of the portal and satellite competition, or the competition among satellites, its core feature is to capture the co-petition between a portal and satellites by exploring the interdependency in the production of the players. While a circular model has been widely employed in the studies of information goods or the Internet economy [2,3,11,24,30,39], strategic interactions among players are limited to location or prices of players.

Second, our model highlights the ecological perspective of the web economy. While studies of the Internet economy have argued the winner-take-all structure or power-law distribution of traf<sup>fi</sup>c [1,5], the ecological point of view has rarely been investigated. Our model shows how to conceptualize the complementarity (of contents) and substitutability (of traf<sup>fi</sup>c) between portals and satellites, and derives equilibrium strategies under such multifaceted relationships.

Third, our study investigates the economics of UCC, especially the strategic utilization of CBS, which is a cunning vehicle to exploit UCC production and distribution. Although UCC issues have gotten signi<sup>fi</sup>cant attention with the growth of Web 2.0, they have not been suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently explored yet, especially from the theoretical and analytical approaches from the economic perspective. We believe that our study <sup>fi</sup>lls such a gap.

Finally, we present the design and managerial implications for UCC based social networks. As a strategy to be a sustainable CBS portal, we emphasized its co-existence with satellites through traf<sup>fi</sup>c subsidization. This issue remains as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant design implication even for the non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t social networks. For example, the Wikimedia Foundation provides UCC aggregation platforms to many such social networks (e.g. wikipedia, wikibooks, wiktionary, wikisource, etc.) where the purpose of content provision is to maximize social welfare. The parameter μ (marginal revenue) in our model can be interpreted as the utility of consuming the content in those UCC networks. Then, pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization of a portal can be substituted by consumer welfare maximization for benevolent social agents such as Wikipedia.com's operator (theoretical designer). For greater consumer surplus and sustainability of the networks, chasing shortterm popularity and sharpening the centrality of web traf<sup>fi</sup>c should be avoided. We pay attention to the fact that Wiki platforms' outlinks not only strengthen the validity of the contents but also subsidize the traf<sup>fi</sup>c to the source satellites, making the UCC ecology sustainable and proli<sup>fi</sup>c.

Beyond the pure online economy, we also <sup>fi</sup>nd some strategic copetition between businesses. Even commercial vendors such as MATLAB and SAS also try to co-exist with open-source groups expecting positive effects [26], although the groups are competitors to vendors. As commercial vendors utilize their rivals' ability, the UCC hubs also have the incentive to maximize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from their competitors' growth when satellites have enough complementarity. More traf<sup>fi</sup>c to satellites creates more content on the web, which again accelerates the production of UCC. The UCC hubs need to understand this positive feedback loop.

More detailed empirical work may be required to expand the scope of research in this area and verify our claims made from our simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed analytical model. Portals and satellites use various forms of economic and technical measures to maximize their business interests, which are simply represented by theoretical parameters. Rich description and empirical validation are warranted. In addition, we assumed the monopolistic setting of the portal. Under competitive market conditions of portals, their strategic choices may be much more restricted than are modeled in this study. These are directions for further investigation.
