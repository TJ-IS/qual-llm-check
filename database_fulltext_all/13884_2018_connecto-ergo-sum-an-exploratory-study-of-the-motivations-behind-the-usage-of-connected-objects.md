## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Connectedness Connected objects Internet of things Perceived value Appropriation

## A B S T R A C T

The Internet of Things seems about to revolutionize individuals' attitudinal and behavioral patterns and theii habits. Increasingly, everyday objects will come with connected features and functions. In this context, it is crucial for companies to study motivations to use connected products and to understand the speci<sup>fi</sup>c role played by user s connectedness. On the basis of individual interviews with 43 users of various connected objects, this research explores such motivations and proposes a theoretical framework in which desirable attributes, perceived value, and psychological traits play a key role. The results are then discussed from both theoretical and managerial perspectives.

## 1. Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT) has spawned a new industry representing the meeting point between the physical and digital worlds, and makes it possible to provide internet services through everyday objects [1]. In this emerging universe, the possibilities for new products are various and adjustable to user’s needs. Connected objects are a real revolution, in terms of information management, design, and marketing. Is it not the dream of every individual to possess products that adapt to their moods and desires, in real time? At the 2020 horizon, global IoT revenues are estimated to reach 30 times those of the Internet and to include 25 billion connected objects around the world. The worldwide market in connected objects should become the next trillion dollar communication industry [2].

This market is currently organized around four types of product: (1) connected objects that carry out orders (e.g., connected co<sup>f</sup>eemakers); (2) connected objects that give access to di<sup>f</sup>erent types of information (e.g., applications with QR codes); (3) connected objects that collect and analyze data and provide users with reports enabling them to live through their quantified self [3], and which can even set them chal lenges (e.g., connected forks); and (4) smart connected objects that can act without being commanded (e.g., intelligent cars) [4]. Another segmentation of this market, more related to our research objective, is to consider their usage [5]. This segmentation distinguishes between <sup>fi</sup>tted connected objects that can sometimes recognize the user (e.g., connected locks), mobile connected objects (e.g., connected glasses), and m-health objects (ingestible, implantable, or usable in the home). These products are a genuine human revolution, increasingly enabling the real and the virtual to become one, and thus contributing to improve individuals’ quality of life [6,7].

The promising possibilities of connected objects make it important for managers to improve their understanding of the transformations that a<sup>f</sup>ect behaviors and the opportunities that these can o<sup>f</sup>er. Therefore, the issue that interests, both researchers and practitioners operating in the IoT industry, is the motivations that lead individuals to use connected objects. The main argument of this paper is that IoT devices lead to more connectedness among users. There is a common belief according to which information technologies isolate individuals and cause them to be disconnected [8]. Here, we defend the opposite thesis in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c case of IoT devices. To support this argument, we conducted an exploratory study to identify the determinants of con nectedness, and to model the process underlying this behavior.

We consider connectedness as an important sociological trend, capable of remodeling society and the relationship between individuals, objects, and other people. Very few research studies have been performed on connectedness in the framework of the IoT. This article at tempts to address this void in the literature. This paper is structured as follows. After presenting the literature review, we describe the methodology used for our qualitative research and our results, before discussing its theoretical and managerial contributions.

## 2. Connectedness: an important sociological trend

Connectedness refers to the degree to which a connected object helps users stay connected with others and create/keep social relationships. It leads to a “subjective awareness of being in close relationship with the social world” [9], p. 338). It can be achieved through a wide scope of technology uses to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll di<sup>f</sup>erent social needs in users’ daily lives [10]. It goes along with a feeling of being part of one or several groups and sharing with a multitude of people, some of them being strangers. Connectedness through connected objects allow: (1) strengthening the social link between users and those around them through conversations, content sharing, and multiplayer games [11]; (2) sharing experiences and performance; and (3) meeting and knowing new people using similar connected objects and evolving together in communities. In such communities, IoT users are able to explore and interact with other members’ interests and activities. This leads to virtual – and sometimes real-life – relationships as users may identify persons with similar interests and connect with them [12].

Connectedness can lead to a society more according to the idea of networks, involving new forms of relations, and interactions [7]. The network is emerging as a contemporary social space taking the form of a huge number of connections between people and things. This “deterri torialized,” dematerialized social space, in which information is the key stone, makes a clean break with traditional spatiotemporal references [13]. Thanks to the ubiquitous character of IoT, connectedness may occur in unexpected places and times, and may take new forms [14].

The IoT brings a deep mutation into users’ social reality, as society is considered as a huge network, itself made up of networks (family, interest, and utilitarian networks). It also impacts the political and eco nomic reality. The networks develop outside national borders. Organizations use networks and connectedness to develop commercial and <sup>fi</sup>nancial signs that structure their commercial, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, and social activity. Finally, the material and technical reality are changing because Internet, mobile phones, and connected objects have become an integral part of social life [15,16]. Connectedness is now involved in activities as diverse as trade, banking, education, social communication, interaction, leisure, health, security, and retailing [17]. The connectedness experience has several components summarized in Table 1.

Connected objects make everyday life simpler because they can bear the following three dimensions of value: utilitarian, hedonic, and social (see Table 2) as do many of today’s mobile systems [28,24,29]. From a utilitarian point of view. connected obiects offer a multitude of new services. functions and information, and enabling enhanced interaction with several commercial and non-commercial interlocutors. They can have material uses, human purposes, or therapeutic uses. These utilitarian uses manifest themselves in the rapid, ubiquitous management of semantic, historical and personal data, time management tools, openness toward more knowledge of the world, other people and oneself, applications facilitating shopping, and interactions with businesses and other users [30]. At the hedonic level, connected objects o<sup>f</sup>er fun functions [31], with games and entertain ment. They are experience-centric products, in which emotions play an increasingly important role [32]. In addition, more and more connected objects are designed to be fun to use and allow immersion into online communities and virtual universes. The hedonic and utilitarian dimensions could be closely interlinked [31]. At the social level, users can connect di rectly with others using geolocation data allowing instant social networking and socialization. Individuals can also share their experiences and contribute to merge the virtual with the real [26]. Individuals are rarely in terested in just one of the three dimensions of connectedness mentioned in Table 2 [33]. They use the multiple possibilities of the connected universe to construct for themselves a dematerialized, disincarnated extended self, broadening the social <sup>fi</sup>eld in which they can assert their digital identity (connecto ergo sum !<sup>1</sup>) through the sharing, co-construction, and narrative memory that are now available to them [13].

Despite the value brought by connectedness, this phenomenon comes also along with some negative aspects that may undermine the motivation to use connected objects. Research reports situations where technology characteristics, customer characteristics, and context speci<sup>fi</sup>c perceptions a<sup>f</sup>ect connectedness negatively [34]. For example, users may perceive risk and may have concerns about personal data security and privacy violations [35]. Other users may question or misunderstand the perceived usefulness of these devices and express concerns about them. This may imply user's resistance to their use. Resistance may also stem from risks related to perceived absence of transparency, product malfunctioning, losing control over these products, and health dangers because of radiations and harmful waves [36,37]. Schweitzer and den Hende [38] also highlight a certain number psychological e<sup>f</sup>ects and frustrations when relying on these objects: disempowerment and negative emotions. Disempowerment is related to the fear of situations, where connected objects supersede the human mind and make users dependent. Negative emotions are linked to situations where users feel fear, bad conscience, or sickness. Finally, an excess of self-control through connected objects may reduce the enjoyment related to connectedness and may a<sup>f</sup>ect users' motivation, engagement, and subjective well-being [39].

The next sections explain the methodology and the results of a qualitative research exploring how and why the IoT may contribute to more connectedness among users.

## 3. Methodology

To study the motivations related to the usage of connected objects and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c role played by users’ connectedness, we adopted an exploratory approach. Such an approach is justi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the relative scarcity of research in this <sup>fi</sup>eld and by the current low penetration of this type of product [40,41].

We conducted semi-structured interviews with users of connected objects, selected in a way to constitute a diverse sample in terms of age, sex, and profession. To avoid limiting ourselves to the motivations related to the use of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c connected object, we included respondents interested in a wide range of connected products. All our informants possess computers, smartphones, and tablets. Consequently, these devices do not appear in Table 3. We also ensured that our respondent used these products personally. We present the pro<sup>fi</sup>le of our interviewees in Table 3. We used a snowball sampling procedure to select our participants. At the same time, we tried to encompass connected objects as varied as possible to collect diverse testimonies on the topic. We continued to collect data until, we reached a theoretical saturation [42].

The aim being to produce rich data, we interviewed 43 users of connected objects. The size of this sample is in line with the standards of qualitative research in that it guarantees the variability of data and thematic saturation [43,44]. The interview guide used by the researchers included the following four topics as summarized in Fig. 1.

During the interviews, we mostly used direct questions. However, projective formulations and narratives were also included. Projective formulations enabled us to reduce the e<sup>f</sup>ect of a number of defense mechanisms relating to situations where the usage of connected objects is intimate, taboo, or embarrassing, particularly if the interviewees experienced feelings of addiction or if they did not want to admit their uneasiness with regard to technology. These formulations oriented the discussion toward users of connected objects in general, to avoid de fense mechanisms linked to excessively personal uses or opinions [45]. They give rise to general formulations re<sup>fl</sup>ecting informants’ actual perceptions. For example, to avoid social desirability biases, instead of asking direct questions about respondents’ mental blocks and inhibitions to use some connected objects, we asked questions like “Why do some people refuse to use connected objects?”. By doing so, respondents are less worried about the image they may give of themselves and will be more at ease to express even their own personal inhibitors.

Then, we have complemented these projective formulations with direct questions like “and what about you?” Narrative formulations, on the other hand, aimed to instigate discursive production that would explain in more detail the experience of users of connected objects [46]. Consequently, instead of exclusively focus on the interviewees’ perceptions and attitudes, we also invited them to share with us their experiences and to give us details about the moments during which they used their connected devices. It was then possible to have access to “turning point moments illustrating how informants lived their usage of connected devices. The discursive production allowed generating narratives about users' social life and related activities, interactions with other people, and recurrent practices related to IoT and connectedness. The interviews lasted 1 h on average (between 45 and 80 min).

The data collected were transcribed and entered in NVivo 10. An a priori nodal structure was de<sup>fi</sup>ned after a <sup>fi</sup>rst scan of the data. The structure was gradually enriched using a globally inductive process [47]. Three types of nodes progressively emerged to represent the hierarchical relationships underlying our topic: (1) set-nodes, used for later ranking and analysis; (2) nodes corresponding to more precise topics; and (3) relationships-nodes. During coding, excerpts from the interviews were ascribed to di<sup>f</sup>erent nodes. To maintain focus on the most relevant topics, we adopted a sifting process [48]. The researchers constantly discussed the results to distinguish between the essential and the secondary. NVivo also o<sup>f</sup>ers the opportunity to code relationships by creating dedicated nodes. This working method presents three advantages [49]. First, it allows identifying the relationships between the constructs of the study. Each time a testimony shows a relationship between two constructs, we have placed it in the appropriate re lationship node. Second, it also o<sup>f</sup>ers leading a re<sup>fl</sup>ection on the relationships between constructs. Nvivo invites users to de<sup>fi</sup>ne clearly the nature of these relationships and to go beyond the single case of causality relationships. We have led the re<sup>fl</sup>ection on all the relationships. Third, it allows to progressively build a framework: each of the relationships of our conceptual framework (Fig. 2) comes indeed from a relationship node. We used the NVivo Models function in parallel with di<sup>f</sup>erent types of requests: “text search” queries with proximity functions, co-occurrences of words, and Boolean searches. During the data interpretation, constant comparison enabled us to identify and revisit the literature so as to give meaning to the themes and patterns that emerged through the categories [50,51]. To ensure trustworthiness of our qualitative research, we used the criteria recommended by Flint et al. [27] (Table 4). The data reduction generated the results presented in the following section. ## 4. Findings and proposed framework

Data reduction revealed the motives behind the use of particular connected objects. However, we limit ourselves hereunder to the broad motivations of connectedness, so as to maintain the generic nature of our proposed conceptual framework. We have organized these motivations in line with the major themes revealed by our analyses. Consistent with previous work, users were revealed as being in search of added value [28,24,29]. We attempted to understand the motivations behind connectedness in more depth by concentrating on: (1) the at tributes that users are looking for in connected objects; (2) desirable attributes; (3) a number of psychological features of users; and (4) on the role of appropriation of IoT devices.

## 4.1. Users looking for value

A <sup>fi</sup>rst level of analysis revealed that the key motivations for the usage of connected objects include utilitarian, hedonic, and social values.

## 4.1.1. Utilitarian value

Our informants declared that they were looking for usefulness, materialized by a desire to achieve a precise objective that depends on the functional attributes of the connected object. They mention the technical features and qualities of the connected object from a utilitarian perspective: “Very useful, because it makes everything easier: knowing where you are, being connected with friends, simplifying everyday life… it’s really useful” (Subject 14, male, 30 years old, connected glasses). This statement and other similar ones reveal the fusional nature of connected objects. In certain cases, they even become an extension of the self, echoing the recent conceptual developments proposed by Belk [13]. People are more likely to use a connected object if it provides them with economic or informational bene<sup>fi</sup>t. It s an object that makes your life easier, it puts everything within your reach and easily accessible… It has become essential today” (Subject 35, female, 22 year old, and GPS sensor). Original forms of convenience emerge as access to people and places occurs according to new spatiotemporal patterns. If users perceive an advantage they will be prepared to use IoT devices it in everyday life [52,53]. Utilitarian value refers to the advantages of the connected object, that is, time savings, constant access to information and people, and convenience: “They’re fast. They give us access to information and tools that we couldn’t get before. I use connected objects to organize my life better; they are facilitators of everyday life” (Subject 33, male, 38 years old, Pedometer, glasses, and activity tracker). Similar to the master/slave dialectic, immersion in the connected universe can be so complete that it makes people wonder whether it is a source of freedom or of alienation. Time saving, convenience, access to information, and permanent contact with one s so cial networks represent major advantages of connected objects. This evaluation of sought bene<sup>fi</sup>ts is also an important motivation for con nectedness, Our informants were aware of the utilitarian value of all the connected objects they mentioned by. The IoT allows them an im mediate and convenient access to their social surrounding when it comes to manage daily issues and solves practical problems. ## 4.1.2. Hedonic value

The usage of connected objects appears strongly conditioned by the pleasure and emotional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that users live and share. They indeed seek pleasure, which correspond to the subjective aspect of the ex perience, including emotions, fascination, and escape [54]. Here, users focus on the intangible attributes of consumption, which result from their interaction with the object and the other members of the IoT networks. Our analysis highlights sensations generated by activitie that enable users to escape from their everyday problems or anxiety [55]: “First of all, using a Google product means you’re a real fan. It’s ‘in’ and I’m enthusiastic about wearing them” (Subject 14, male, 30 years old, connected glasses). The hedonic trait evoked here is even more intense in that it represents an opportunity to belong to a current trend created, to communicate, and be fashionable. The pleasure associated with using the object is an important motivation: “It’s so easy to use! Since I bought my connected scales, it’s much more fun to weigh myself every morning. It’s become a real ritual” (Subject 6, female, 53 years old, connected scales). The ritual mentioned here is by no means an isolated act. Indeed, the IoT has led to an enormous number of new shared practices with symbolic and expressive dimensions, and which are structured sequentially in such a way as to give rise to genuine rituals in the anthropological sense of the term [56]. These new types of connected rituals highlight the emergence of a whole series of socio-cognitive collective practices and processes that revolutionize traditional schemas, giving technology a central position within o<sup>fl</sup>ine and online communities. With regard to these new rituals, the adoption of connected objects and the user’s relationship with the object are so intense that some authors do not hesitate to speak of technological fetishism [57]. Design also contributes to hedonic value. Some respondents gain pleasure from the stimulation of their senses, revealing a sensorial approach to products and their features [58]. The pleasure is increased by the physical features of the connected object and its perceived ease of use. Manipulating the connected object is thus perceived as an overall experience that generates both rational and subjective e<sup>f</sup>ects [33]. Pleasure also stems from the possibility to instantly geolocalize and join “group members” and “friends” and “sharing diverse activities and experi ences.” As with utilitarian value, hedonic value takes di<sup>f</sup>erent forms depending on the type of connected object. With regard to installed connected objects, users seem to enjoy the creation of a lifestyle that they describe as “minimalist,” “pure,” or “futuristic”. Aestheticism and fashion as sources of value seem to be more associated with portable connected objects. Self-image (e.g., good health, anti-aging, and weight loss e<sup>f</sup>ects) is the most salient topics with regard to m-health products.

## 4.1.3. Social value

Our analysis of the interviews reveals that connectedness can re spond to a need for conformity, for belonging, or adhering to a com munity. Users considered what the members of their group think. The usage of connected objects also responds to a need for sharing, conviviality, and social links with friends and family: “I think that you also choose these objects because you want to be integrated, up-to-date, and like others” (Subject 9, male, 34 years old, connected wristlet, scales). Thi social in<sup>fl</sup>uence reveals the importance of the opinions of groups people belong to in forming users IoT continuance intention. Users tend to trust their friends’ opinions more than organizational messages: “A salesperson can influence you. A product might look good because he presents the sales arguments, when you can just do like your friends and choose the same products as them” (Subject 10, female, 24 years old, activity tracker). Interpersonal exchanges reveal to be a major motivation to use connected objects regularly as users encourage each other and create a common dynamic. Although more than with the “standard” Internet, social contagion, and buzz are at the heart of the IoT. The adoption, type, and persistence of consumption seem to depend on the behavior of the other members of the social system; people shape their behavior, beliefs, and attitudes through a process of peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence [59], i.e., the way other people in the social system think, express themselves, and behave. Several of our respondents highlight new views of connection and new forms of social links made possible by the IoT: “At first I just used the basic functions such as measuring the calories in a meal or spent when I went jogging. Then I discovered the user community, sharing ex perience, ideas, tips and mistakes to avoid. That opened up amazing possibilities. I was no longer facing my problems alone, but shared them with a lot of other people” (Subject 16, female, 32 years old, lock, connected wristlet). It should be noted that the topic of sharing and collaborative consumption [60] frequently appears in the informants’ statements and takes multiple forms, demonstrating the social value of connected ob jects. Portable connected objects seem to di<sup>f</sup>er from the two other categories of products, insofar as they are a source of ostentation and also enable users to interact continuously with their social system.

## 4.2. Desirable attributes

At a second level of analysis, we attempted to go further upstream than the search for value. Here, perceived ease of use, perceived ubiquity, and time savings were revealed as important sources of value for users.

## 4.2.1. Perceived ease of use

Perceived ease of use is the extent to which a person thinks that using a new system will be free of di<sup>fi</sup>culty or additional e<sup>f</sup>ort [61]. The more using a technology is complex, the more it is tedious [62,28]. Users have di<sup>f</sup>erent perceptions of connected objects. Perceived ease of use is a key facilitator of connectedness: “for me, a connected object must above all be easy to use and practical: easy to start using, and with appli cations that are simple to install and work well” (Subject 21, male, 55 years old, co<sup>f</sup>ee-maker alarm). Connected objects are considered a intuitive tools and easy to operate, particularly when learning how to use them: “I found it very interactive and easy to use. It’s very intuitive and you quickly learn how to work it” (Subject 7, male, 62 years old, connected sensor). However, some highlight that: “It isn’t within everybody’s reach. If it’s dificult to use, it can be a real headache. I can easily understand why some people don’t use these technologies” (Subject 43, female, 53 years old, smart fridge). Perceived ease of use depends on the e<sup>f</sup>ort that individuals think they will need to make learning to use the object and the complexity of their relationship with the device. “It’s great. As soon as I go cycling or running, I put on my Jawbone Up wristlet and of I go! It’s simple, easy and I can access my information when I like. I love it!” (Subject 13, male, 23 years old, connected wristlet). Responding to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant sociological trend, the demand for simplicity and practicality [63], and perceived ease of use appears to be another source of utilitarian value in connected objects. Compared with portable connected objects and m-health products, using installed connected objects proves more complex, and at least initially, they require more investment in time and e<sup>f</sup>ort to master.

## 4.2.2. Perceived ubiquity

This characteristic refers to the possibility of using the connected object at any time and above all, anywhere [64,1]. This is one of the most important features of connected objects. Such a perception is generated by the continuity, immediacy, and portability developed during use: “I use it every day, all the time, even when I’m not at home; I’m always connected. I don’t spend a single day without using it” (Subject 40, male, 27 years old, connected wristlet). The perceived ubiquity related to connected object removes the need to connect to the Internet and place the user in an environment that is both physical and virtual [1]. Our respondents spoke of the possibility of moving around while using their di<sup>f</sup>erent devices: “I use them all day long, in public transport, when I m at work or even when I m exercising. There s no particular moment or place” (Subject 18, female, 24 years old, connected wristlet, and scales). The ubiquitous nature seems to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the very usefulness of the connected object: “Whenever I want, I can browse the Internet, check my emails, take photos and videos, and communicate with my friends and fa mily” (Subject 33, male, 38 years old, connected glasses). Perceived ubiquity contributes to a transformation of individuals’ practices, with an increase in multi-tasking. People no longer concentrate on a single task, but on a variety of di<sup>f</sup>erent tasks. Our informants consider not just mobile objects as ubiquitous but also those that are <sup>fi</sup>xed. This is particularly the case for connected scales and fridges, which users can consult through the Internet. It is also the case for ubi-medicine products. Con<sup>fi</sup>rming the emergence of a ubiquitous paradigm, perceived ubiquity proves a source of value insofar, as it contributes to a better experience.

## 4.2.3. Time savings

Users include time savings in their decision-making. Time is a resource that people seek to minimize [65]. Time savings are one of the most important factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the use of connected objects: “For me, connected objects allow me to save a huge amount of time. They have become essential today” (Subject 8, male, 42 years old, connected light bulb). Our interviewees tend to choose between connected objects not only for their usefulness but also for the time saved by using the object. Time becomes an integral part of the decision to use the connected object: “The connected cofeemaker is really useful. It helps me to manage things, to develop habits and to save a lot of time” (Subject 21, male, 55 years old, connected co<sup>f</sup>eemaker). Time savings appear to increase the perceived value of connected objects. In some cases it is deliberately sought after and increased the appeal of the connected object: “I have very little time for myself. So I opted for connected products because they enable me to save a lot of time (Subject 43, female, 53 years old, connected wristlet, co<sup>f</sup>eemaker, smart fridge). Saving time is a key con cern. Time is considered to be such a rare resource that people are anxious to reduce their time-consuming activities; and using connected objects is one way of achieving this: “You don’t have much time to spend with others, not much time to give them, to talk to them. Nor do you have much time for yourself, time when you do nothing. Connected objects can help you to save invaluable time that you can spend on leisure activities, your family, your friends or just doing nothing and resting a bit” (Subject 27, female, 34 years old, pedometer). If we return to our initial typology, it is interesting to stress that time savings are particularly important for installed and mobile connected objects. This topic appeared less important with regard to m-health objects. In the face of increasing feelings of urgency, the search for time savings is thus a crucial factor in the use of connected objects. It also proves a source of utilitarian, hedonic, and social value.

## 4.3. Psychological traits of connected users

Our third level of analysis consists in identifying the psychological characteristics leading individuals to connectedness: technophilia and innovativeness as positive traits, and anxiety and skepticism as negative aspects of connectedness.

## 4.3.1. Technophilia

This trait is related to attraction toward technology. The <sup>fi</sup>eld of interest of technophiles goes well beyond the mere use of new tech nologies. They feel excited and curious when they are faced with a new technology, whatever its use [66]. The analysis of our interviews reveals the importance of this dimension in a context of connectivity: “I can’t wait to understand what these products can provide and the new horizons that they open up. Technology doesn’t just represent modernity and progress, but also an improvement in the quality of $\mathit { l i f e } ^ { \prime \prime }$ (Subject 29, male, 26 years old, watch, toothbrush). Moreover, IoT technologies are disruptive and often come along with changes in users’ habits and require a new capabilities and behavior patterns. More technophile users reveal to be more comfortable with this permanent evolution of their technological environment: “There are always new apps to download, new functionalities, new networks, updates and new versions. Some people may feel overtaken by events, but not tech savy users. They are at ease with all this, always aware of new possibilities and telling all the others about them” (Subject 4, female, 16 years old, wristlet, glasses). At the opposite, some interviewees fear a loss of interpersonal relations in the real world [28]. The less technophile ones develop an aversion to connected objects because of the virtual exchanges they engender: “People who don’t use these objects have an attitude where they say stop to all that and want to refocus on something that is more real and tangible. I’m afraid that all the limits between my private life and what I put on Internet, my public life, will disappear.” (Subject 43, female, 53 years old, smart fridge). The fears concerning the consequences of using connected objects include the loss of personal data or the fear of the unknown [67,68]. Consequently, technophilia is one of the principal factors that clearly distinguish those who devote themselves to connected consumption from those who refuse it.

## 4.3.2. Innovativeness

Our analysis of the interviews highlights the importance of innovativeness in motivating the use of connected objects. Consumer innovativeness re<sup>fl</sup>ects a person’s propensity to look for or accept novelty, and the impact of this tendency on buying new products [69]. Innovativeness is a personality trait or a permanent personal predis position [62]. I always keep up-to-date with what s new. I like it and it s cool, as if I was a ‘fashion victim’ … I’m always interested in high-tech” (Subject 30, female, 27 years old, and connected moneybox). “I consider myself as a ‘pioneering’ explorer of connected objects, because I always keep up-to-date with the latest products and I show them to family and friends. Novelty, innovation, creativity and fashion are my main motivations” (Subject 33, male, 38 years old, pedometer, glasses, activity tracker). Innovativeness manifests itself by searching for information about in novation or by purchasing innovative products: “I want to have the best for my daily tasks and even to pay more to get the best. I was one of the first to have a tactile tablet… I think I might buy more connected products, because that’s the way the world is going” (Subject 43, female, 53 years old, smart fridge). Innovativeness is an important phenomenon for users of connected objects. Innovative people are more likely to have risks, to accept the changes induced by connected objects, and to take advantage of their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [70]. Our corpus reveals the special status of innovative users in their social networks. The other members of their networks hardly in<sup>fl</sup>uence them when they use new connected objects. Innovative users are pioneers who appropriate and master products and their attributes, independently from the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of other people. However, this should not overshadow their place in their social environment. They are indeed very active as opinion leaders: they play a key-role explaining to others the di<sup>f</sup>erent possibilities o<sup>f</sup>ered by the IoT and re-enhancing connectedness among their communities.

The analysis of the interviews also unveils negative psychological traits related to connectedness, namely anxiety and skepticism.

## 4.3.3. Anxiety

The interviews reveal that in several situations, users live a psychological state of mind characterized by fear and apprehension. Anxiety may be related to users’ ability and willingness to use technology-related tools [71,72] but also to connectedness. This last form of anxiety stems from several factors. First, users may feel anxious in situations where there is a risk to lose connectedness: “When I forget to bring my smart-watch along, I feel very uncomfortable, you know, not being in touch with my friends and family, missing important emails or information from work colleagues… I wouldn’t feel $O K ^ { \prime \prime }$ (Subject 34, male, 52 years old, smart-watch, glasses). The anxiety also <sup>fi</sup>nds its roots in the way users manage the information they receive. The <sup>fl</sup>ow of information is uninterrupted and coming from di<sup>f</sup>erent environment (home, o<sup>fi</sup>ce, friends, associative life…). Our testimonies even show some cases information overload. The feeling of anxiety may also come from perceived ubiquity itself: connectedness translates itself by an absence of frontiers between work, family, and other social settings, which make situations more di<sup>fi</sup>cult to manage. “Sometimes when I'm in the ofice on weekends, I am also connected to home to make sure that every thing is under control. Once, just before an important meeting, my son felt down the stairs and injured his knee. So I had to phone my wife to warn her and manage the situation with her. Not always easy to manage so many things at the same time” (Subject 14, male, 30 years old, connected glasses). For one of the informants, the anxiety originates in the fear of becoming addicted to connected objects and not being able to <sup>fi</sup>nd back oneself.

## 4.3.4. Skepticism

This trait refers to users’ tendency to distrust or disbelieve a good, service, situation, commercial stimulus, or artifact (e.g., [73]. The analysis revealed a generalized feeling of apprehension related to the potential harmful consequences of connected technologies, even among heavy users. Skepticism often originates in privacy issues as our informants expressed doubts and fears about who may have access to their data and how it may be used. Even when they declare that connected objects come with strong privacy protections, they a<sup>fi</sup>rm that they fear hacking from either businesses or other individual users. “I use my connected devices very often, it’s a little bit stressful anyway… you know because of possible hackers. Not sure it is 100% secured and it may be easy for some strangers or companies to get into the system. In my moments of paranoia, I feel as if I’m watched or that it’s like Big Brother” (Subject 38, male, 38 years old, pedometer, glasses, activity tracker). Skepticism also occurs when users doubt the truthfulness of privacy policies or when they feel overloaded by the amount of information in privacy notices: “When you first used connected objects, the privacy policies are often too complicated. I don't know, but I always feel that there are hidden clauses that will allow companies to take advantage of the situation” (Subject 1, male, 27 years old, wristlet, co<sup>f</sup>ee machine, smart fridge). Skepticism does not necessarily lead to the rejection of IoT devices. However, it may inhibit their usage, and the more skeptic informants declared reducing their connection rate because of their perceived potential dangers.

## 4.4. Appropriation of the connected object

Even if it did not apply to all our respondents, one interesting result relates to the concept of appropriation of connected objects. We did not include appropriation in our framework because not all users are con cerned by this phenomenon. This concept is inspired from the mar keting literature, but we believe that it may bring interesting highlight even to IS. This concept is expressed as a dual process: a process of physical ascendancy, through the sensorial experience, physical ex ploration, gestures, and transformation; and a psychological process through the fact of making it one’s own, familiarizing oneself, gaining knowledge, and giving meaning to the manipulation of the object [74,75]. The literature highlights the major modes of appropriation, namely control, creation, and knowledge [76]. The discourse of ou informants echoes these dimensions of appropriation. First, it demonstrates control: mastering an object for personal use ensures the in strumental appropriation making its use possible. “For me, the connected watch proposes series of exercises adapted to my fitness level that I can choose. It works like a mini-coach that only looks after me” (Subject 5, male, 28 years old, watch). The user exercises his mastery by applying his demands and conditions to how and when he consumes the con: nected object. In parallel, this quasi-allegorical discourse suggests that his relationship becomes intimate, insofar as he attaches human fea tures to the object. Creation gives the person who creates property rights over the object that thus becomes a part of the “self” [77]. It appears notably in the transformation of the connected object to make it familiar and appropriate it more completely. “When connected wristlets were first sold, we recognized each other (wristlet users). I tied red ribbon on mine to diferentiate it from the others and at the gym we compared our results with our smartphones” (Subject 13, male, 23 years old, connected wristlet). Using the object doesn’t prevent its owners from being crea tive and expressing themselves, not only to make it their own but also from a social perspective to develop stronger ties with the community of users. Meanwhile, the knowledge dimension manifests itself through the acquisition of new skills when owners manipulate the connected object. “I love using my cofeemaker. As soon as I can I discover new functions. I’ve become an expert user. I’m the only one who knows how to use it at home (Subject 21, male, 55 years old, and alarm co<sup>f</sup>eemaker). Appropriation goes through knowledge of the smallest details of the object. Our study shows that knowledge takes the form of cognitive discovery, transmission, and sharing [78]. As indicated above, appro priation assumes a special relationship between the user and the con nected object, a relationship that makes its use a new kind of ritual.

## 5. Conclusion and discussion

## 5.1. Summary of results

Connectedness using the IoT leads to deep sociological and technological transformations in terms of the user’s environment. Such changes a<sup>f</sup>ect the o<sup>f</sup>ers made by businesses, their strategies, future actions, and competitive capabilities [79,80]. As such, connected objects deserve attention, and this is why we decided to explore the motivations behind their usage and more particularly connectedness. Our qualitative study enabled us to propose a conceptual framework to understand the determinants of connectedness using IoT. It revealed that the search for value − through its utilitarian, hedonic, and social dimensions − by users of connected objects is fundamental. It also allowed to emphasize some important psychological features of IoT users such as technophilia and innovativeness but also some more negative aspects of ‘connectedness’, namely anxiety and skepticism. We integrated all these factors in a model explaining the major motivations behind connectedness. Another interesting result is related to the appropriation of connected objects as a re-enforcing element of connectedness.

## 5.2. Theoretical contributions

This article has three major theoretical contributions. First, it proposed a new conceptual framework explaining the motivations behind connectedness using the IoT. From a variety of connected objects, this framework has the merit of going beyond the speci<sup>fi</sup>c features of a single connected object/category and of being more generic. Such a framework contributes to a better understanding of the motivation underlying connectedness in general.

The second theoretical contribution lies in showing that IoT brings more connectedness among users. The interviews revealed that this process operates in two ways: First, time proves to be a fundamental determinant of the decision to use connected objects, either because it is deliberately sought or because of possible savings in time-consuming activities. This has an immediate impact on connectedness. By saving time using IoT, individuals declare being able to dedicate more time to their family and close social environment. Second, perceived ubiquity and changes in spatiotemporal landmarks are major advantages pursued by users because connected objects can be used anywhere and at any time [81]. These new behaviors come along with more opportunities to be connected to one’s social system. However, this connectedness has a non-traditional form as it is performed through new communication channels and in di<sup>f</sup>erent time sequences.

The third theoretical contribution is to highlight the existence of negative aspects of connectedness using IoT. Our thematic analysis revealed two ambivalent psychological traits even among heavy users of connected objects: anxiety and skepticism. Anxiety stems from the di<sup>fi</sup>culty experienced by some users to multi-task and to manage several things at the same time. Today, more and more individuals have di<sup>fi</sup>culties <sup>fi</sup>nding work-family balance [82,83]. Our results show that with the use of connected objects, the frontiers are withering away. Connected objects even alter IoT users' relationship to time and a<sup>f</sup>ect the concept of “time <sup>fl</sup>ow” [84]. Work, family, and social relationships are no longer linked to a precise place/time. They are now ubiquitous, as individuals access them continuously with multiple devices. This new situation is at the origin of an ambivalent feeling of connectedness, not devoid of anxiety. As for skepticism, it is related to the potential intrusiveness of connected objects [36].

## 5.3. Implications for practice

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study can help practitioners − notably IoT device manufacturers, engineers, and marketing managers − to understand the importance of the three dimensions of connectedness: utilitarian, hedonic, and social. This would allow them to better adapt their o<sup>f</sup>ering to the market, especially when conceiving and commercializing connected objects. One or other of these dimensions is indeed sometimes neglected in current products. Therefore, the practitioners should ensure that the connected objects on o<sup>f</sup>er encompass all three of these dimensions of value. Our conceptual framework suggests possibilities in this respect, insofar as it reveals the links between the different factors likely to a<sup>f</sup>ect connectedness and the attributes that generate value: perceived ease of use, perceived ubiquity, and time savings. From a communicational perspective, companies should make sure that the three dimensions mentioned above are su<sup>fi</sup>ciently em phasized when targeting potential users.

This research also explains how the perceived value of the connected object is made up. The study shows that consumers use connected objects for practical reasons (time saving, practicality, access to information, communication, etc.) that partly contribute to the product’s utilitarian value [53]. While this operational dimension is present, connected objects also provide individuals with obvious hedonic value through the use of their senses and the possibility to instantly join groups, enabling them to escape from a di<sup>fi</sup>cult or stressful daily life [55]. Finally, social conformity appears to contribute to the perceived value of the product and reinforces connectedness. Two psychological determinants also play a non-negligible role − innovativeness and technophilia [28]. Our results also reveal the existence of two am bivalent psychological traits: anxiety and skepticism. These insights constitute an invitation for practitioners to take. They could do this when conceiving and designing IoT devices and when implementing the related services, particularly when de<sup>fi</sup>ning privacy policies and issues, security, and information overload. For example, tools such as instruction manuals, usage charters, anti-hacking services, and customer service may be resourceful at this level.

## 5.4. Limits and future research perspectives

The results of this study should be interpreted in light of its limitations. This research has some limitations. The <sup>fi</sup>rst limitation is related to our research setting. We conducted our qualitative study within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context: France. Results found in one country cannot always be generalized to other countries. Cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences may indeed have an impact on individuals’ connectedness and their relationships with connected devices. For example, there may be di<sup>f</sup>erences between collectivistic and individualistic cultures [85]. We encourage future research in other setting to check whether similar results may be found. Second, our sample is composed of respondents selected according to a snowball procedure. We only have data on users who chose to participate to our inquiry the survey. Consequently, the sample may not be representative of all IoT users. Besides, in order to have a good overview of the situation, our sample is composed of users of a wide variety of connected objects. Future research may re<sup>fi</sup>ne our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by recruiting users of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c category of connected objects. They may for example use the typology, we present in our introduction and focus on one of the categories.

The topic also opens avenues for additional research. It would be interesting to conceptualize and develop robust measures for individual digital connectedness. Such tools would make it possible to test the variables and relations in the conceptual framework we propose. A quantitative study may be used to validate the framework. It would also provide an opportunity to check whether the variables are valid whatever the category of connected object concerned, by using multi group models on samples of users of di<sup>f</sup>erent connected objects. Another interesting opportunity for research emerged during our analysis of the interviews: the “dark side” of connected objects, materialized in our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by anxiety and skepticism. Of course, the ultimate aim of the IoT is announced as the improvement of people’s well-being, life, and e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Nonetheless, the question remains of whether people will continue to manage their lives or whether their lives will be more or less guided by connected objects. Are we going to evolve from Homo sapiens into Homo digitalis [86]?
