# MANAGING COLLECTIVE ENTERPRISE INFORMATION SYSTEMS COMPLIANCE: A SOCIAL AND PERFORMANCE MANAGEMENT CONTEXT PERSPECTIVE<sup>1</sup>

Jingmei Zhou Department of Management Science and Engineering, School of Business, Renmin University of China, Beijing, CHINA {zhoujingmei@rmbs.ruc.edu.cn}

Yulin Fang Faculty of Business and Economics, The University of Hong Kong, Pokfulam Road, Hong Kong, SAR CHINA {ylfang@hku.hk}

Varun Grover Department of Information Systems, Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR 72701 U.S.A. {vgrover@uark.edu}

In today’s environment characterized by business dynamism and information technology (IT) advances, firms must frequently update their enterprise information systems (EIS) and their use policies to support changing business operations. In this context, users are challenged to maintain EIS compliance behavior by continuously learning new ways of using EIS. Furthermore, it is imperative to businesses that employees of a functional unit maintain EIS compliance behavior collectively, due to the interdependent nature of tasks tha the unit needs to accomplish through EIS. However, it is particularly challenging to achieve such a collective level of EIS compliance, due to the difficulty that these employees may encounter in quickly learning updated EIS. It is, therefore, vital for firms to establish effective managerial principles to ensure collective EIS compliance of a functional unit in a dynamic environment. To address this challenge, this study develops a research model to explain collective EIS compliance by integrating theoretical lens on social context and performance management context with social capital theory. It proposes that social context, an organizational environment characterized by trust and support, positively affects collective EIS compliance by developing business–IT social capital that enhances mutual learning between business and IT personnel. Furthermore, the performance management context, an organizational environment characterized by discipline and “stretch,” is seen to have a direct and beneficial effect on collective EIS compliance as well as an indirect, moderating effect on the causal chain among social contexts, business–IT social capital, and collective EIS compliance. General empirical support for this research model is provided via a multiple-sourced survey of managers and employees of 159 functional units of 53 firms that use EIS, as well as their corresponding IT unit managers. The theoretical and practical implications of these findings are discussed.

Keywords: Social context, performance management context, EIS compliance, social capital

## Introduction

In hyper-competitive and turbulent business environments, firms must rapidly and efficiently adapt their operational processes in response to environmental changes and client needs (Sambamurthy et al. 2003). Enterprise information systems (EIS), referred to as the set of information technology infrastructures, data management, application systems, and extended enterprise networks deployed to digitalize key business operations (Chen et al. 2010; Davis 2000), must be adjusted to empower this adaption, often through improvement in existing system functionalities and/or creation of new functionalities (Dong et al. 2009; Sambamurthy et al. 2003). To attain intended benefits from EIS, it is important to ensure proper use of the system by employees in the functional units reliant on EIS, an issue termed as EIS compliance (Liang et al. 2015). In a changing environment, employees must still strive to use the evolving EIS in a conforming and consistent manner so that their functional unit as a whole can rapidly stabilize evolving digital operations (Hsieh et al. 2011; Vessey and Ward 2013). The cost of noncompliance in such a situation can be high, as it could lead to failure in business operations<sup>2</sup> (Nelson 2007). Consider an EIS that manages customer-facing service work. The system is updated frequently to support or enable service work in order to achieve customer satisfaction in a highly competitive environment (Mithas et al. 2005). Employees using the system should comply with the policies while playing their different roles, including answering customer inquiries, offering information about products and promotions, processing transactions, and providing post-sales support (Sergeant and Frenkel 2000). Failure of employees to comply with EIS changes (e.g., updates in the digital codes to create a quick reply to customers queries) is detrimental to the organizational goal of delivering good customer service (Hsieh et al. 2011).

EIS compliance in a changing environment is quite challenging, however, due to the difficulty of quickly learning new ways of using EIS after it is updated. This demand for learning is especially high in dynamic environments. Indeed, even in a static environment, employees may be unwilling to conform to these work requirements as they see no personal benefit in doing so, or are unable to conform as they do not know how to properly use the systems (Liang et al. 2010; Xue et al. 2011), suggesting the difficulty (i.e., lacking motivation and ability) in learning to comply. This difficulty could accentuate when rapidly stabilizing digital operations of a functional unit in a changing environment require that all its employees exhibit EIS compliance behavior at the same time, a phenomenon we term as collective EIS compliance and define as the extent to which employees in a functional unit collectively use EIS in a compliant manner.

The extant IS literature, in particular EIS and IT security research, has investigated compliance behavior at the individual level with a focus on specific managerial interventions, notably reward (financial) and punishment (sanctions) (Bulgurcu et al. 2010; Chen et al. 2012; Liang et al. 2013; Xue et al. 2011) as well as education, training, and awareness (ETA) programs (D’Arcy et al. 2009). While this literature offers great insights, certain interventions it investigates or proposes are limited in promoting collective EIS compliance in dynamic environments. For instance, the reward and punishment approach has not been shown to be overly effective (Xue et al. 2011), as it could demotivate employees by driving them to be more selfish and competitive (Tyler and Fagan 2008; Vance et al. 2015). Periodical and post hoc training does not afford continuous learning opportunities as users are not able to follow up with the system upgrades in a dynamic environment (Baronas and Louis 1988; Sun et al. 2012; Vessey and Ward 2013). Given these limits in the IT compliance literature, it is pertinent to study how a firm can promote the collective aspects of EIS compliance in a changing environment, not just by imposing such managerial interventions, but through alternative means, such as cultivating a facilitating environment that fosters mechanisms conducive to collective EIS compliance. This study thus aims to understand what constitutes a facilitating organizational environment and how it can best promote employee collective EIS compliance.

To achieve this objective, our study largely draws on the organizational context perspective from the management literature, where organizational context is defined as the systems, processes, and beliefs that build an effective work ethic and positive engagement within the organization (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994; Tyler 2006). This includes a recognition of “hard” versus “soft” elements in the workplace (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004) that need to be carefully balanced or blended to achieve specific business goals. Soft elements refer to social context, defined as the extent to which an organization builds a trustworthy and supportive environment for employees that inspires them to mirror these behaviors within the organization (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004). Hard elements refer to the performance management context (PMC), defined as the extent to which the organization sets performance goals to motivate its members to meet expectations and strive for more ambitious goals (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004). This is a suitable perspective for our research objective because an appropriate organizational context with the soft and hard elements is found capable of promoting more collective and cooperative behaviors among employees that forward the firm’s interests (Galletta and Hufnagel 1992; LePine et al. 2002).

While the organizational context perspective is deemed as influential when an organization seeks cooperation from a collective of its employees, the EIS context is unique. Unlike other more general contexts where employees cooperate on the terms that the employer defines, changing EIS in response to dynamic environments requires continuous learning of technical knowledge (e.g., updated IT specifications) by EIS users and business knowledge (e.g., changing business needs) by the IT unit (Sun et al. 2012). In this regard, both the IT staff and EIS users, who possess the necessary domain knowledge, play an important role in influencing EIS compliance. It is unclear, however, to what extent and how the social context and PMC would account for this unique aspect of EIS when explaining collective EIS compliance. The lack of clarity on this topic leads to our key research question: To what extent and in what ways do social and performance management organizational contexts affect collective EIS compliance?

To address this research question, this study extends the organizational context literature by integrating social capital theory, a theoretical perspective well established in the IS literature to address knowledge sharing between functional and IT units (Karahanna and Preston 2013; Sun et al. 2012). Social capital theory highlights the value of social and relational resources for knowledge transfer (Adler and Kwon 2002) and how social resources can be manipulated by contextual conditions or institutional settings to achieve mutual goals (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998). We propose that social context affects collective EIS compliance through the mediation of social capital between business and IT (formally termed as business–IT social capital in this study). Furthermore, performance management context is suggested as a moderator of this mediating relationship, in addition to exerting a direct and beneficial effect on collective EIS compliance.

This research contributes to the EIS compliance, organizational context, and social capital literature in three primary areas. First, it explains how social context and PMC affect collective EIS compliance through business–IT social capital by integrating the organizational context literature and social capital theory, both new concepts to the EIS compliance literature. Second, it extends the organizational context literature to the EIS compliance context by revealing how the social and performance management contexts make independent and joint IS-specific impacts through business–IT social capital. This approach also expands upon the original organizational context literature by clarifying whether and how the two contexts complement one another (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004; Ramesh et al. 2012). Finally, this research contributes to the social capital stream of the IS literature by identifying social context and PMC as an appropriate theoretical perspective to explain the formation of business–IT social capital.

## Theoretical Background

## Conceptualizing Collective EIS Compliance

EIS compliance is commonly defined in the literature as the extent to which employees follow EIS policies and appropriately use EIS to support daily work activities (Liang et al. 2013). EIS policies are defined as formally established operational procedures, rules, guidelines, and standards, either coded in software or specified in user documents, that regulate how employees should use EIS (Galletta and Hufnagel 1992). EIS compliance behavior is different from EIS usage in that usage that does not follow EIS policy is considered noncompliant. EIS policies are abundant in practice, and compliance with them is considered important as it ensures that work routines enabled by EIS are executed as organizationally required (Jasperson et al. 2005). For instance, EIS typically sets rules and standards on data entry and search to ensure work accuracy and productivity, such as emphasizing casesensitivity of customer names and alerting not to use “%” in data queries for customer service personnel so as to achieve faster search response, or specifying type, timing, and frequency of inventory data entry for different supply chain personnel to ensure inventory accuracy and workflow efficiency.<sup>3</sup> Employee noncompliance with these rules and standards could result in decreased business performance in their respective functional units, such as poor customer satisfaction or delayed product delivery.

EIS compliance should be distinguished from IT security compliance, a topic that is also common in IS research. While they are both IT compliance behaviors (Bulgurcu et al. 2010; Liang et al. 2013), the role that users’ business knowledge plays in informing the EIS policies for compliance differs from that of security compliance. As security compliance is purported to reduce IT security risks, aiming for the benefit of assurance and safety, sometimes at the cost of business performance (Neumann 1999; Posey et al. 2014), a user’s business knowledge is not as important as IT staff’s security domain knowledge in informing IT security policies. On the contrary, EIS compliance is purported to impose best business practices, aiming for achieving business performance goals by regulating how business activities should be executed through EIS (Xue et al. 2011). Users’ business knowledge is therefore as important to informing EIS policies as IT staff’s technical knowledge. This difference highlights the need for business and IT staff to learn from each other when striving for EIS compliance. This need for mutual learning is particularly high when EIS has to be frequently revisited to accommodate emerging and fast-changing business needs, resulting in frequent changes in the requirements for EIS compliance.

This study extends EIS compliance from the individual level to the collective level by defining collective EIS compliance as the extent to which users in a functional unit collectively comply with EIS policies. We develop this concept by building on collective IS usage research in the IS literature and corroborating with collective compliance research in the organizational behavior literature. The IS literature has acknowledged that collective IS usage could exist in ways that are structurally and functionally different from individual usage (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007). Structure-wise, collective usage is not simply the sum of individual members’ usage but comprises interdependencies-in-use (i.e., interdependencies among the individuals related to their use of a system) (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007). Such interdependencies arise from the deployment of process routines that specify the ways in which group members are expected to interact (Dennis and Garfield 2003) and are thus potentially able to homogenize system usage behavior among members (Dennis et al. 2001; Gersick and Hackman 1990). In the EIS context, interdependencies-in-use are mediated through coordination and communication that occur during EIS usage among employees in a functional unit. As EIS features a high level of task interdependency among users, particularly those belonging to the same functional unit, collective EIS usage within a functional unit could emerge. Function-wise, collective EIS usage aims to support a functional unit to achieve its performance goals, while individual EIS usage is found to be important for individual performance (Jasperson et al. 2005). As it is commonly known that unit performance is not necessarily the sum of its members’ individual performance, collective EIS usage is expected to exert a distinctive functional effect at the unit level.

Extending the functional aspect, we argue that it is practically important to study employee EIS compliance behavior at the unit level because it has performance implications for the unit (not just individuals) that relies heavily on EIS to achieve its business goal. Given the interdependent nature of job tasks conducted through EIS by employees in a unit as stated earlier, failure to collectively comply (e.g., noncompliance by any individual) could deteriorate unit performance by impeding task flows from effective completion. In other words, while individual compliance is important, it would not ensure unit performance unless this compliance is achieved by the collective of the interdependent employees in the unit.

The existence and importance of collective EIS compliance can also find its theoretical support in the collective compliance research originated in the group behavior literature (Anthony 2005), including research on sports teams (Mitchell et al. 1999) and collective AIDS prevention (Heckathorn 2002). This literature suggests that collective compliance is meaningful and necessary when a group’s goal achievement depends on collective compliance with desired behavior among its group members. Collective compliance is particularly critical when noncompliance by any individual could result in collective punishment, i.e., negative consequence on the whole, not just on the focal individual (Heckathorn 1990, 2002). Collective compliance could emerge as the interdependency of group members in generating interactions that reinforce norms for compliance among group members (Heckathorn 1990). Extending this literature to the EIS context, it stands to reason that collective EIS compliance is important because the functional unit’s goal achievement depends on proper EIS usage by each and every member in the unit. While individual EIS compliance is also important, it does not ensure unit performance. Only collective EIS compliance would do this.

Albeit important, collective EIS compliance is particularly challenging to manage in a dynamic environment, because of the difficulty in collectively learning new ways of EIS use after updates, which need to occur quite frequently. Changes in EIS create challenges for employees to comply with, because they may either be unable to keep up with the changing requirement (due to lack of necessary knowledge) or feel it is too costly (time and effort consuming) to realign their usage behavior, making them less motivated to comply (Liang et al. 2010; Xue et al. 2011). This challenge is even more prominent at the unit level because, as discussed earlier, collective compliance requires simultaneous and concerted efforts by individual members in the unit. This challenge is accentuated when the demand for collective learning is ongoing due to frequent EIS updates demanded by dynamic environments.<sup>4</sup> To address this challenge, effective knowledge sharing between business and IT employees becomes necessary.

The existing compliance literature is inadequate in explaining collective EIS compliance in a dynamic environment. For instance, reward/punishment systems are identified as common sanction tactics to promote compliance to both EIS and

IT security policies (Boss et al. 2009; Bulgurcu et al. 2010; D’Arcy et al. 2009; Liang et al. 2013; Siponen et al. 2014; Xue et al. 2011). These studies suggest that achieving compliance remains as a challenging issue even when reward/ punishment policies are in place, as these practices actually have mixed effects on compliance behavior in both the EIS compliance and security contexts. Specifically, while reward and punishment have been found to be effective controls toward achieving EIS compliance, with punishment having the stronger effect (Liang et al. 2013), others have found that formal sanctions such as punishment do not have a significant effect on EIS compliance (D’Arcy et al. 2009; Xue et al. 2011). Several studies on security compliance, however, have found that reward has no effect on compliance (Boss et al. 2009; Pahnila et al. 2007; Siponen et al. 2014). The reward/ punishment system that companies often rely on for employee compliance is thus shown to have uncertain outcomes.

While this literature clearly suggests that more research is required to better understand what is behind these contradictory findings, it also points out several concerns that are, in our view, inadequate for elucidating collective EIS compliance. First, reward/punishment tactics are specific management actions enacted to reduce focal undesired employee behaviors that generally manifest as increased supervisory pressure on employees (Worthy 1950). Such formal supervision is ineffective in promoting effective collective behavior in a large population because it is weak in nurturing a selfsustaining norm of cooperation among employees (Tyler and Fagan 2008), Second, reward/punishment tactics are largely based on the control and manipulation of economic interests (Boss et al. 2009) and assumes that employees are operating with the same profit motives as employers. Manipulating employee behavior with economic interest focuses on the behavior consequences without regard to whether employees accept or recognize the firm’s goals or not, thus inducing employees to have possible negative feelings about work policies (Vance et al. 2015) and management in general. For instance, an employee’s sense of autonomy and trust may be reduced, as they may perceive that the firm makes others work for its interests without proper consideration of employee needs or concerns (Frazier and Summers 1986). Undue pressure on and manipulation of employees can also lead to undesirable emotional outcomes, such as anxiety, insecurity, and hostile relations in the workplace (Chen et al. 2012). These negative responses are obviously detrimental to cooperation.

Training is another commonly used intervention for promoting specific forms of compliance, mainly in the IT security context (Aytes and Connolly 2004; D’Arcy et al. 2009; Puhakainen and Siponen 2010). While training has also been widely applied during EIS implementation (Boynton et al.

1994; Gallivan et al. 2005; Jasperson et al. 2005), this educational process has been criticized for being post hoc and discrete (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017), and hence less effective in dynamic environments where EIS policies must frequently change. The effectiveness of training for EIS compliance in these changing settings is therefore in doubt, notably because it cannot address the demand for employees to frequently and quickly learn new ways of EIS use.

Given the limitations of the aforementioned perspectives in the existing literature, it is pertinent to draw on alternative theoretical perspectives that are appropriate for encouraging collective EIS compliance by not only considering formal heavy-handed tactics per prior research, but also through a facilitating organizational environment that encourages voluntary and proactive cooperative behavior by employees as a collective. This environment should also cater to the need of ongoing learning between IT and functional units, which is important in the EIS context. Toward this end, we refer to the notion of organizational context, specifically social and performance management contexts in the organization literature that is well established in its understanding of collective cooperative behavior of employees (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994), and augment it with social capital theory that is well contextualized in IS research for elucidating mutual learning between IT and functional units.

## Performance Management Context and Social Context

Organizational context refers to a contextual condition inside the firm enabled by a carefully selected set of systems and processes that provides employees with embedded flexibility and high performance standards (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004). The more effective organizational context could encourage employees to flourish (Cameron and Caza 2004) by motivating them to do much more “for the good of the organization” without supervision or force compared to less-useful or less-valued economic rewards (Barnard 1938, p. 200). This context generally functions by developing employees’ positive feelings such as sense of responsibility, enthusiasm, and self-empowerment about their tasks and reciprocity with their organization (Ghosal and Bartlett 1997). Considerable research confirms this approach can lead employees, as a collective, toward positive organizational behaviors such as cooperation (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994; Krosgaard et al. 2002), organizational commitment (Eisenberger et al. 1990), interpersonal trust (Six and Sorge 2008), collective learning (Bresman and Zellmer-Bruhn 2013; Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994), knowledge sharing (Carrillo et al. 2007), and improved work performance (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004; Glaser et al. 2015; Kerr and Landauer 2004).

Organizational context is manifested in both hard and soft aspects, with each playing a distinctive role. The hard side refers to performance management context (PMC), an organizational setting that includes the behavior-framing characteristics of discipline and stretch. Discipline refers to the presence of clear, open, and consistent management systems that regulate employee behavior and attitudes, which is characterized by (1) clear standards and goals with fine-grained information and responsibilities, (2) consistent sanctions with enforced accountability, and (3) systems of open, fast feedback (Arvey et al. 1984; Ghosal and Bartlett 1994, 1997). Clear standards, goals, and consistent sanctions hold employees accountable and direct their work effort, while open and frequent feedback from supervisors encourages employees to feel appreciated, thus motivating and instructing them on how to best complete their assigned tasks and meet performance goals as part of a collective effort (Sagie et al. 1996). Overall, discipline established in the work setting plays a key role in developing acceptance and commitment to the performance goals (Arvey et al. 1984; Choo 1996; Peterson et al. 2008).

Stretch refers to the context in which performance expectations of employees exceed the levels currently being achieved, instigated by (1) establishing shared ambition with the organization, (2) developing a collective identity, and (3) building personal meaning via work relations (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994). Shared ambition, collective identity, and personal meaning endow employees with a sense that they share the basic purposes of the organization (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994), thus inciting more passion, responsibility, and energy to overcome obstacles and engage in a range of necessary activities. This approach stimulates employees to pursue more ambitious objectives (Kerr and Landauer 2004; Thompson et al. 1997), from improving existing and relatively standardized tasks to accepting challenges embedded in managing new products and businesses (Kerr and Landauer 2004). Collectively, PMC characterized by discipline and stretch motivate employees to work toward the organization’s desired direction by setting high performance goals and challenging employees to exceed them (Glaser et al. 2015; Thompson et al. 1997).

It is important to note that performance management context has commonalities with specific interventions, such as reward/ punishment tactics, but these approaches differ in important ways. While these concepts of intervention have the same attribute of driving employee behavior towards a desired direction, reward and punishment are exercised by management to coerce, monitor, and evaluate behavior and outcomes against a specifically predefined set of rules primarily through manipulating economic consequences (Liang et al. 2013). PMC differs by reflecting a far broader set of supervisory activities applicable to all tasks (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994). More importantly, it differs by its focus on setting performance goals and motivating employees to reach them, rather than the consequent economic outcomes. In this sense, performance management context is more encompassing in its management actions and scope, and more focused on performance goal setting than economic interests. Indeed, this focus is justified in that, recent industry research by Forbes and MetLife on millennials indicates that the work context identified by several factors are more important to workers than economic gain or sanctions (Stahl 2019). These include working for a purpose, being treated with respect (Kohll 2018), as well as shared values and personal identity provided by the job (Ell 2017).

The soft side, or social context, is concerned with the principles of managing social relationships using employeefriendly actions, comprised of the behavior-framing characteristics of trust and support (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004). Trust is reflected by (1) equity in organizational decision processes, (2) involvement of employees in decisions and activities affecting them, and (3) staffing positions with people who possess and are seen to possess required competence (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994). A trusting social context creates a caring, more harmonious, respectful, and reciprocal atmosphere that gives employees a sense of belonging or commitment to the organization and hence encourages them to reciprocate by behaving in ways that benefit organizational interests, such as acceptance of organizational direction and decisions (Ellis and Shockley Zalabak 2001). A trusting social context can also foster bonding among employees (Ambrose and Schminke 2003; Blunsdon and Reed 2003; Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004; Six and Sorge 2008) and decrease potential interpersonal or interdepartmental politics and conflicts (Eisenberger et al. 1990). In such an environment, employees are motivated to connect with each other and act to further the interests of their collective endeavors (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004; Uslaner 2002). For instance, the mutual appreciation of competence that arises from a trusting environment (e.g., staffing positions based on people’s actual talents) could motivate employees toward selfimprovement and proactively build connections with others (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994).

Support is defined as the organizational attribute of “helporiented” rather than “control-focused” senior management. It is demonstrated by (1) allowing actors/employees to access resources outside of their own unit, (2) encouraging autonomous initiative at lower management levels, and (3) senior managers’ prioritizing guidance and help to employees over exercising authority (Eisenberger et al. 1990; Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994). Worker access to resources, autonomy, and mentorship of senior management therefore strengthens their work ethic, as employees experience a need or desire to reciprocate in appreciation of these perceived high levels of management support with positive job attitudes and behaviors, an outcome consistent with social exchange theory (Wayne et al. 1997). Such support could also induce employees to lend assistance and counsel to others throughout the organization (Ambrose and Schminke 2003; Gibson and Birkinshaw2004). A supportive context endows employees with the resources and confidence they need to have enough “social morale” to voluntarily help others, both in vertical relationships between supervisors and subordinates and in horizontal interactions across functions (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994). Organizational support at this level could lead to high social motivation among employees, thus invoking active mutual collaboration and respect (Hardy 2010). Collectively, social context characterized by trust and support for employees has been found to facilitate cooperative behavior by inspiring them to reciprocate with the organization (Wayne et al. 1997) and to enhance mutual collaboration and collective learning between units (Eisenberger et al. 1990; Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994; Six and Sorge 2008).

Despite abundant research on the role of performance management and social contexts in shaping desired cooperative behavior (Carrillo et al. 2007; Driscoll 1978; Eisenberger et al. 1990; Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994; Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004; Glaser et al. 2015; Maertz et al. 2007; Six and Sorge 2008), there has been little effort to extend this literature to IS research, especially the EIS context. Unlike other cooperative behaviors with which employees comply on terms unilaterally defined by the employer, complying with a changing EIS policy requires the exchange of IT staff’s technical knowledge and users’ business knowledge, thus giving rise to the need for learning between the functional and the IT units. This learning should occur frequently in a dynamic environment where EIS policies are often updated in response to external changes. Indeed, previous studies have suggested that EIS success can be virtually guaranteed by effective knowledge sharing between business units and IT units (He and King 2008; Jasperson et al. 2005). In recognition of the crucial role of ongoing learning between business and IT for EIS compliance, we import the perspective of performance management and social contexts to the EIS context by unveiling the EIS-specific mechanism through which they affect collective EIS compliance. Following theory contextualization guidance by Hong et al. (2013), we build on social capital theory that incorporates social capital between business and IT units as an important IS-specific mediating mechanism.

## Social Capital Theory

Social capital is defined as “the sum of the actual and potential resources embedded within and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit” (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998, p. 243). This reflects social relationships that can exist between units (e.g., functional and IT units) (Adler and Kwon 2002). Social capital is widely understood as a human resource with structural, relational, and cognitive dimensions (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998). In the IS context of our study, the structural dimension refers to “the overall pattern of connection between actors” (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998, p. 112), specifically the settings in which IT and business staff interact, such as in meetings and projects. The relational dimension focuses on “the particular relations people have” (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998, p. 114), notably the extent to which IT and functional staff trust, show respect, and reciprocate with each other (Wagner et al. 2014). The cognitive dimension captures shared codes, language, and perspectives (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998), referring to the extent to which IT and functional staff know each other’s interpretations of reality (Wagner et al. 2014). Social capital has been found to significantly contribute to value creation through knowledge exchange (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998), as the ongoing social relationships between units create knowledge exchange opportunities and subsequentlycombine knowledge for enhanced delivery of anticipated value (Robert et al. 2008; Sun et al. 2012).

Specific to the IS context, social capital between business and IT, which we formally term as business–IT social capital in this study, has been developed to theorize the effect of business–IT relationships on various IS-specific outcomes in previous IS studies. For instance, business–IT social capital is found to improve user satisfaction with IT service delivered by the IT unit, largely because it facilitates the integration of emerging business need into IT services through continuous learning between business and IT (Sun et al. 2012). Similarly, social capital between business and IT executives has been found to promote alignment between business and IT strategies through frequent knowledge sharing, ultimately providing more business value (Karahanna and Preston 2013).

Based on the established IS research on social capital, we posit that business–IT social capital, as a social mechanism for ongoing learning between business and IT, provides a sound theoretical base to understand the mediating mechanism through which social context and performance context affect collective EIS compliance in a dynamic environment, for two reasons. First, as noted earlier, this social mechanism addresses the need for ongoing learning between the functional and the IT units (Sun et al. 2012; Wagner et al. 2014), which is crucial for users to attain EIS compliance through frequent learning in a dynamic environment. And as argued earlier, social capital can continuously create value, especially in response to dynamic changes (Tsai and Ghoshal 1998) addressed herein. Social relations, formulated and accumulated through daily interactions over time, are resources that can be mobilized to create long-lasting, positive effects on employee behavior (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998). Specific to the IS context, Galletta et al. (1995) found that informal communication channels between business and IT play a powerful role in influencing user attitudes and behaviors toward IT usage. These outcomes collectively justify business–IT social capital (but not other forms of social capital) as a potentially more proximate influencer of collective EIS compliance in a dynamic environment, compared to organizational context that is general enough to explain cooperative behavior of all sorts, as done in the management literature.

Second, business–IT social capital can be cultivated in the organizational context in which exchange partners are situated. As suggested by Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998), the development of social capital is affected by institutional settings that shape the evolution of social relationships, such as the use of shared value systems in facilitating trusting relations among employees. Adler and Kwon (2002) and (Farr 2004) also proposed that social capital could be planned, organized, and manipulated by the contextual conditions of the firm. Leana and Van Buren (1999) extended this concept by showing that social capital within an organization can be built by organizational environments with norms of reciprocity, induced by management practices like shared goals and managerial support. Extending these thoughts to our research context, we see the possibility that social context, as one type of contextual condition of a firm underpinned by trust and support, may contribute to the development of business–IT social capital. These two reasons collectively speak to the plausibility of business–IT social capital as a mediating mechanism to offer more nuanced insights into how organizational context, social context in particular, affects collective EIS compliance in a dynamic context.

On a slightly different but related note, careful investigation of the literature suggests that there is a relative dearth of research that formally theorizes how social capital can be developed (Payne et al. 2011). Although prior research has identified several antecedents, such as time, space, resources, and opportunities for interaction and sociability (Adler and Kwon 2002), and has engaged in general discussion on the organizational conditions for developing intra-organizational social capital, it lacks a theoretically grounded understanding of what organizational settings could do to encourage/induce social capital between different units. We therefore believe that our theorization on how social context and performance context can affect business–IT social capital, as part of our effort to understand business–IT social capital as a mediating mechanism, has the potential to fill in this gap in the social capital literature.

## Research Model and Hypotheses Development

Basing our research model on the elaborated theoretical background noted above (Figure 1), social context is proposed as a sound mechanism to improve business–IT social capital, which in turn enables employees to collectively comply with EIS. Performance management context is proposed as a means to moderate this mediating relationship and to exert a direct effect on collective EIS compliance.

## Social Context, Business–IT Social Capital, and Collective EIS Compliance

This study posits that social context, represented by trust and support, is conducive to the development of business–IT social capital, based on two theoretical grounds. First, support as characterized by three management patterns (i.e., allowing actors to access to resources, encouraging autonomous initiative at lower levels, and help/guidance from senior functionaries) builds social capital between different units through various channels that are mutually accessible. If one unit is seen as accessible to another unit, these interactions could occur in a deeper way and with more frequency (Phillips et al. 2000; Pinto et al. 1993). Specifically, allowing actors access to resources outside of their own unit could establish a context in which leaders encourage cross-unit collaboration, whereas encouraging autonomous initiative at lower levels stimulates employees to take initiative to reach out (Gates and Egelhoff 1986). Previous studies have also acknowledged that autonomy of the firm is necessary in reducing interaction barriers among different individuals and units (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994). Help and guidance from senior functionaries describes management that plays a supportive rather than authoritarian role, and clearly distinguishes a control mode from a helping mode. This helping mode works with the enhancement of autonomy to largely increase employees’ opportunities to connect with people outside the unit.

Extending these arguments to the IS context, it stands to reason that supportive context provides accessible channels between business and IT for relationship building. Business– IT social capital is recognized as a key resource inside the firm, especially when IT is indispensable to business (Melville et al. 2004). Members in functional units rely heavily on EIS to complete their tasks, and as such, within a strong supportive context, functional unit members feel higher levels of encouragement from leaders to interact with IT units. Similarly, IT unit members are allowed by their supervisor to access the business unit and understand their requirements, which makes for better service. In a supportive environment, functional and IT unit members are also given higher levels of autonomy to take initiatives, thus bringing down communication barriers across unit boundaries. As such, business and IT have more opportunities to understand each other, thus forming better business–IT social capital through frequent interaction, shared trust, and enhanced cognition.

Second, three management patterns that characterize a trusting social context—equity, involvement, and staffing people of competence—can also build social capital through a safer or more credible channel for interaction. These three management patterns signal trust-based norms, where units in the organization are seen as reliable for mutual communication and collaboration. Specifically, the establishment of employee equity helps establish a sense that organizational politics will not be a major factor in resolving disputes during the collaboration among different units. Walton and Dutton (1969) suggest that smooth inter-unit relationships will develop in organizations that emphasize objective, credible, and impartial evidence, rather than political or competitive tendencies. Involvement means that engaging more individuals in decision making helps break down vertical and horizontal worker barriers (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994) that have previously constrained both their participation in and access to information from other units. Staffing positions with competent people builds confidence in one’s own unit as well as in the other unit’s ability to understand and respect their domains (Ein-Dor and Segev 1978). This practice thus lays the foundation for further interaction and shared understanding between units.

Extending this logic to the IS context, we argue that a trusting social context provides credible channels for building business–IT social capital. With a strong social context of trust, functional and IT unit members may believe that they will be treated equally when conflict happens during collaboration. As such, they will be more open to engage in crossunit interactions. Involving employees in operational decisions works as an interaction channel because many decisions are interdependent in relation to other important factors, especially for IT decisions. This helps them get to know each other better, thus contributing to the establishment and main tenance of improved social relationships. Furthermore, when positions are staffed with competent people, the functional unit will view their IT partners as having professional knowledge to solve their problems. In turn, the IT unit will believe that their business partners have a reasonable level of competence to provide constructive opinions and support. This mutual appreciation and professional respect lead functional and IT units to perceive each other as credible partners, which also encourages open inter-unit communication and collaboration. As trusting social context is conducive to the reliability of the interaction channels, it contributes to business–IT social capital. Based on the above arguments, we hypothesize

## H1: Social context (with trust and support) is positively related to business–IT social capital.

We argue that business–IT social capital improves collective EIS compliance in a dynamic environment by facilitating continuous learning between business and IT. With a highlevel of business–IT social capital, users in the functional unit can learn from the IT unit to best understand the system and related policy updates, whereas the IT unit can learn of emerging business needs and incorporate them into the EIS. Business–IT social capital specifically facilitates such mutual learning, both formally and informally, by affording frequent learning opportunities, improving learning willingness (Karahanna and Preston 2013), and making learning effective (Wagner et al. 2014). First, business–IT structural capital facilitates more frequent employee interactions to share business knowledge, including business requirements and feedback (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998; Robert et al. 2008) from the functional unit to the IT unit for future system improvement. Meanwhile, structural capital enables the functional unit to quickly and continuously access EIS policy updates made by IT in light of the fast-changing business environments. Second, business–IT relational capital increase the willingness to learn between the two units (Karahanna and Preston 2013; Sun et al. 2012). Relational capital encourages the functional unit to share their requirements, system suggestions, and comments with the IT unit (Tsai and Ghoshal 1998). At the same time, it motivates the functional unit to be more willing to learn and digest the significance of system and related policy updates in a trusted relationship (Karahanna and Preston 2013), thereby improving EIS compliance. Third, business–IT cognitive capital strengthens shared language and cognition between the two units, thus making the mutual learning process more effective (Robert et al. 2008). Cognitive capital is essential for improving communication quality and knowledge sharing efficiency (Adler and Kwon 2002). Since the functional and IT units have very different levels of IT and business competencies, shared cognitions make them more capable of understanding and respecting each other, whereas shared language lays the semantic basis for mutual learning. As such, when IT staff use more nontechnical approaches to inform system and policy updates and business staff have more cognitive understanding about IT, the shared cognition between IT and business enables the functional unit to learn these policy updates more efficiently, therefore enabling better compliance with EIS. Taken together, business–IT social capital addresses the difficulty of the functional unit in engaging in ongoing and swift learning of system and related policy updates, which occur frequently in the dynamic environment, thereby improving collective EIS compliance of the functional unit.

## H2: Business–IT social capital is positively related to collective EIS compliance of a functional unit.

Building upon the chains of reasoning leading to the above two hypotheses, we argue that business–IT social capital serves as a mediating mechanism underlying the influence of social context on collective EIS compliance. The literature suggests that a trusting and supportive social context creates a caring and reciprocal atmosphere, thus encouraging employees to respond with cooperative behavior, notably the acceptance of (compliance with) organizational arrangements (Ellis and Shockley Zalabak 2001). There is also empirical evidence of a robust relationship between social context and employee “obedience” to organizational rules (Krosgaard et al. 2002). As such, to the extent that collective EIS compliance is a form of cooperative behavior by employees in a functional unit, a trusting and supportive social context can be an important driving force.

As argued earlier in hypothesis 2, collective EIS compliance can also be distinctively achieved by way of building business–IT social capital. To this end, we argue that business–IT social capital is one of the mediating factors, considered as an important and necessary condition, underlying how social context makes an impact on collective EIS compliance, particularly in a dynamic environment. As argued earlier, social context featuring trust and support is seen to provide conducive institutional conditions to enable accessible and credible interaction channels between business and IT (i.e., instrumental for the development of business–IT social capital). Thus,

H3: Business–IT social capital mediates the relationship between social context (with trust and support) and collective EIS compliance.

## The Role of Performance Management Context

We propose that a performance management context with discipline and stretch is positively related to collective EIS compliance. As PMC characterized by discipline and stretch shapes a shared norm of pursuit of excellence among the employees, employees as a collective are better enabled to work toward the organization’s desired direction and motivate themselves to reach high standards (Glaser et al. 2015; Thompson et al. 1997). Extending this logic to the EIS context, we contend that, in a strong PMC context, employees are more likely to cooperate by complying with EIS. Furthermore, as EIS compliance can be difficult due to employees lack of motivation or knowledge to comply (Liang et al. 2010; Xue et al. 2011), these challenges could be more prominent when they need to comply with a “moving target” (i.e., when EIS must be updated more often in a changing business environment) by continuously learning new ways of EIS use. A strong performance management context with clear management principles on discipline and stretch could set clear, consistent, and challenging performance goals that motivate them to overcome the challenges of learning and prove their competence (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994, p. 98). By learning and accomplishing the changing ways of EIS operations, workers could also meet the organizational standard of using EIS in the proper way as an integral part of a collective effort, which can eventually contribute to improved business performance of their respective functional unit as expected in a strong performance management context. We therefore hypothesize

## H4: Performance management context (with discipline and stretch) is positively related to collective EIS compliance of a functional unit.

We further argue that PMC positively moderates the effect of business–IT social capital on collective EIS compliance, in that the learning processes involved in complying with the changing ways of using EIS based on business–IT social capital would be more effective in a stronger performance management context. When PMC is strong in using clear, consistent, and challenging performance goals, employees would be more motivated to take their work seriously, and strive to live up to or even exceed the standards set by the organization (Glaser et al. 2015; Thompson et al. 1997). Translating this into our research setting, a strong PMC would encourage employees in the functional unit to attach great importance to reaching high work standards, including addressing the challenge of complying with frequently updated EIS policies so as to competently accomplish daily tasks for their functional unit. According to Bhattacherjee and Sanford (2006), users who view an information system as being crucial to their work performance are more motivated to learn about the system. By the same token, when employees view EIS use as important to meeting organizational standards, they would be more motivated to invest the necessary cognitive effort to better learn how to comply with updated EIS policies. This motivation would lead them to scrutinize or incorporate useful information exchanged with the IT unit for better EIS compliance. In other words, the positive effect of business–IT social capital on collective EIS compliance would be stronger because business employees with stronger motivation for work excellence would more purposefully scrutinize and digest information relevant to EIS compliance from their interaction with the IT unit, thus making their learning more effective. Thus,

## H5: Performance management context (with discipline and stretch) moderates the relationship between business–IT social capital and collective EIS such that the positive relationship is stronger when performance management context is stronger.

Furthermore, we argue that performance management context (with discipline and stretch) positively moderates the effect of social context on business–IT social capital. As a social context with trust and support promotes the development of business–IT social capital by cultivating credible and accessible cross-unit interaction channels between business and IT, we posit that this effect would be stronger because employees in the functional unit would devote more effort to leverage the credible and accessible interaction channels made available through a strong social context for meaningful interactions. Ghoshal and Bartlett (1994) observed that, while a trusting and supportive environment is important for employees to build social relationships, employees make more use of this social environment in pursuing work excellence when discipline and stretch advocated in a PMC by the organization are in place that set ambitious performance goals for employees. Reversely, prior research has suggested that a strong social context with trust and support may merely create a “country club” atmosphere in which less work gets done, when employees feel less pressure to achieve ambitious objectives (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004).

Building on this literature, we argue that this positive effect of social context on business–IT social capital would be stronger within a stronger performance management context. In these circumstances, employees are more motivated to challenge themselves to meet high organizational standards (Glaser et al. 2015; Thompson et al. 1997). In our research setting, employees in the functional unit with a stronger PMC context are more motivated to achieve high performance goals for the unit, with EIS as an important means to achieve this end. As such, they will be more motivated to make EIS a useful tool to support their work performance. To the extent that business–IT social capital can help them exchange useful knowledge with IT for better performance (Sun et al. 2012), they would be more keen to develop better social relationships with IT by making good use of the interaction channels cultivated through a trusting and supportive social context. This is why we expect that social context with trust and support could result in a higher level of business–IT social capital when performance management context is stronger. We thus hypothesize

## H6: Performance management context (with discipline and stretch) moderates the relationship between social context (with trust and support) and business– IT social capital, such that the positive relationship is stronger when performance management context is stronger.

Integrating the above relationships, we propose a moderated mediation relationship. Specifically, theory is put forward herein on why business–IT social capital mediates the otherwise direct relationship between social context and collective EIS compliance. We have also theorized why the relationships between business–IT social capital and collective EIS compliance, and between social context with trust and support and business–IT social capital, are both positively moderated by PMC. These theorizing efforts collectively suggest a twostage moderated mediation relationship, in that

H7: The mediating relationship between social context (with trust and support) and collective EIS compliance through business–IT social capital is moderated by performance management context (discipline and stretch) for both the link from social context to business–IT social capital and the link from business–IT social capital to collective EIS compliance, such that the mediating relationship is stronger when performance management context is stronger.

## Methodology

## Research Setting and Participants

The research model was empirically tested through a field survey. Survey data were collected through a senior executive program of a well-known Chinese educational institution. From the executive connections in the program, we attained a sample pool of 300 departments from 100 firms across a variety of IT-intensive industries in the Greater China region. This setting is deemed appropriate to our study objective and sample for the following reasons. First, these regions represent giant, growing economies that have complex economic conditions, where most industries operate in dynamic environments due to economic transition, government intervention, and financial turbulence (He 2016; Yuan et al. 2014). Second, EIS is widely deployed in the sampled industries, where EIS compliance is an important means to ensure business operations (Liang et al. 2013). Third, the sampling targets are major functional units, including marketing/sales, procurement, and finance,<sup>5</sup> widely known as heavy EIS user departments in which proper use of EIS is extremely important.

To ensure that this study only includes firms situated in a dynamic environment, we asked the responding firms to evaluate the dynamism of their industry in terms of competitors, suppliers, consumers, and technology changes using a fivepoint Likert scale. To alleviate respondent subjective bias, we also cross-checked with the objective measure on environmental dynamism,<sup>6</sup> using volatility in industry sales and volatility in industry operating income,<sup>7</sup> both common indicators in the literature (Keats and Hitt 1988). When we compared the calculated score with the average score of all industries, the sample industries are found to have higher scores on sales volatility and operating income volatility than the average score (mean = 1.01), indicating that the sample firms qualify for testing in our research model. This process led us to a final sample of 159 functional units of 53 firms that are located in seven environmentally dynamic industries, where survey responses from multiple employees, functional unit managers, and IT managers were solicited. This data represents a response rate of 53%. The demographic results are shown in Appendix A.

Survey data were collected from three informant sources in each firm: the manager and three employees of each functional unit, and the IT unit manager. Three employees in each functional unit were contacted through the random referral of the functional unit managers. The employees were first asked to identify the information system that their units relied on before assessing their compliance to the system. The employee survey is aggregated by functional unit to measure the dependent variable (i.e., collective EIS compliance). The survey for functional manager measures not only collective EIS compliance (to cross-check with employee responses), but also social context and performance management context, as well as business–IT social capital between the focal functional unit and the IT unit. Business–IT social capital was also rated by the IT manager for cross-validation. Control variables were measured across the three sources where appropriate. Reward, punishment, and training were evaluated by employees, while others control variables (i.e., industry, history, firm size, ownership, location, and department type) were evaluated by functional managers. IT unit size and IT manager experience were evaluated by IT managers.

This data collection design is based on several key considerations. First, collecting independent and dependent variables from separate, independent sources mitigates concern about common method bias. Second, measuring business–IT social capital from both the business and IT departments allows us to evaluate the extent to which the agreements are symmetrical, an important procedure for validating constructs that involve both sides. Third, collecting the EIS compliance data of multiple employee members from one and the same unit is an essential procedure to study unit-level phenomenon, a practice widely adopted in the literature. The aggregated employee responses were further cross-checked with their functional managers to ensure validity<sup>8</sup> (Katz-Navon et al. 2005).

## Measurement Development

Collective EIS compliance: Adopting the operationalization of unit-level system usage (Easley et al. 2003), we operationalize collective EIS compliance as the aggregation of individual-level EIS compliance behaviors in a functional unit.<sup>9</sup> Individual-level EIS compliance is measured as the extent to which an employee in a functional unit complies with EIS policies, with measurement items adopted from Liang et al. (2013). For instance, one item reads: “How often do you comply with EIS-related rules and regulations?” To ensure respondents know these EIS policies, brief examples of EIS policies were given to them. For instance, accountant respondents were advised that EIS policies are standard procedures specified in the ERP rules when they use the system to manage accounting matters such as accounts receivable and payable, funds, and contracts.

Business–IT Social Capital. Business–IT social capital is measured by both the functional unit manager and IT unit manager as the extent to which functional unit and IT unit developed a solid social relationship. This construct is specified as a formative second-order construct composed of three reflective sub-constructs, consistent with the established literature (Mathwick et al. 2008). It is appropriate to model social capital as formative constructs because (1) the direction of causality is from indicators to constructs, (2) the indicators are not interchangeable, (3) there is low covariation among the indicators, and (4) the indicators have a different nomological net (Jarvis et al. 2003). The scales measuring structural capital and cognitive capital are adapted from Chiu et al. (2006), while that for relational capital are adapted from Robert et al. (2008) (e.g., to address relational capital, the statement “Our department and IT department have a close personal exchange” is used).

Social and Performance Management Contexts. These two constructs are measured by functional unit managers. According to the literature (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004; Glaser et al. 2015), managers are the most suitable respondents to surveys because they are thought to directly share and hold the firm’s management principles reflected in both social and performance management contexts.<sup>10</sup> We adopted a combination of items from the established literature for discipline and stretch (as in trust and support) to measure PMC (or social context) (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004). Specifically, social context was measured by nine items, capturing the extent to which management systems, processes, and actions provide trust and support to employees (e.g., “To give everyone sufficient authority to do their jobs well”). Performance management context was measured by seven items on the extent to which their management systems, processes, and actions encourage people at their level to achieve expected or better performance (e.g., “To issue creative challenges to their people, instead of narrowly defining tasks”). A five-point Likert scale was used to assess the items of all the aforementioned main constructs in the research model. Both social and performance management constructs are specified as firstorder reflective,<sup>11</sup> because the two aspects of each construct are closely interrelated to reflect their respective underlying construct (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994). Indeed, prior research has found that factor analysis cannot empirically distinguish the two dimensions for each construct, as tested by Gibson and Birkinshaw (2004) and retested by Glaser et al. (2015).<sup>12</sup>

Control Variables. We first controlled for three major existing factors related to EIS compliance: reward (Liang et al. 2013), punishment (Liang et al. 2013; Xue et al. 2011), and training (D’Arcy et al. 2009; Jones et al. 2008). We also controlled for organizational characteristics, including firm size, type, industry, and location. IT unit size was further controlled because it is easier for a functional unit to secure

IT assistance in firms with large IT units. Finally, we controlled for functional unit type and size.

To ensure construct validity of the adapted items, we followed the measurement development procedure recommended by Moore and Benbasat (1991). Semi-structured card sorting was conducted with two academic staff members, four doctoral students, and two senior managers to make sure that the items properly matched the definitions. Based on their feedback, the content of the measurement items and the appropriateness of language were refined. Two experienced IS scholars then reviewed and further refined the items. A translation committee approach (Van de Vijver and Leung 1997) was then used to translate the original English version to Chinese, with the help of bilingual participants. Finally, as a preliminary test of our research mode, we conducted a pilot survey with 188 part-time MBA students who are department managers.<sup>13</sup>

We conducted necessary tests to ensure that nonresponse bias and common method variance bias are not of concern in this study (see Appendix C).

## Data Analyses and Results

## Data Aggregation

Before aggregating the multiple source data of social capital and EIS compliance, we calculated an interrater agreement score for each construct. This measure ranges from 0 (“no agreement”) to 1 (“complete agreement”). Glick (1985) suggested 0.60 as the criteria for an acceptable level of interrater agreement value. Median interrater agreement was 0.71 for EIS compliance and 0.82 for social capital, suggesting adequate agreement for aggregation. We therefore averaged the matched functional unit manager and IT manager score of social capital and multiple employee members’ score of EIS compliance for further analysis.

## Data Analyses Technique

Moderated mediation was conducted with ordinary least squares (OLS) analysis in SPSS version 20 (Edwards and Lambert 2007; Muller et al. 2005). For the reflective constructs (social context, PMC, structural/relational/cognitive capital, and EIS compliance) that have multiple indicators, we first calculated a single latent factor score to represent the indicator (Yuan et al. 1997). The validity of the first-order constructs was then tested based on their original measures to make sure all the factors were valid before testing the conceptual model. Third, new indicators for social capital were computed, consisting of three latent factor scores. Last, we reexecuted the latent factor score approach and calculated the single latent indicator of each construct for further hypothesis testing.

Three further steps were then taken to examine adherence to regression assumptions’ accuracy of estimates, and robustness of the significance tests, that of norm-distribution test of error items, multicollinearity test, statistical power calculation, and heteroscedasticity-robust variance estimation (Tang and Rai 2012). Our results—a rough normal curve, small variance inflation factors (below four), and statistical power parameters of above 0.8—confirm that the research model is sufficiently robust.

## Measurement Model Evaluation

We first examined construct reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity for the reflective constructs. For reliability, the composite reliability, average variance extracted (AVE), and Cronbach’s alpha should be higher than 0.7, 0.5, and 0.7, respectively (Nunnally 1978). The results show that all reflective constructs met these criteria.

Convergent and discriminant validity was assessed through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) (Comrey and Lee 2013). Convergent validity was evaluated by checking whether indicators in the same construct had high enough correlations among themselves (Kankanhalli et al. 2005). The results show that the loadings of each item were higher than 0.7, indicating good convergent validity (Comrey and Lee 2013). For discriminant validity, all the item loadings had the strongest loadings on their corresponding construct compared with other constructs. Also, the construct correlations were lower than the square root of AVE of their respective constructs (Fornell and Larcker 1981). Finally, Table 1 shows that all the correlations among the constructs are no more than 0.5, less than the recommended level of 0.71 (MacKenzie et al. 2011). All of these test results suggest good discriminant validity.

For the formative construct—social capital—we examined latent indicator weights to establish construct validity (Petter et al. 2007). The results show that the latent indicator weights of structural capital were significant (SC1 = 0.579), while the later indicator weights of relational capital and cognitive capital were not significant (SC2 = 0.425; SC3 = 0.446). To determine if the nonsignificant indicators should be retained, we examined loadings (bivariate correlation) between indicators and their respective constructs (Cenfetelli and Bassellier 2009). The results show that loadings for the two are above 0.7 and were also statistically significant. This indicates that all the items have important absolute contributions to social capital, and hence retention of all these indicators should be adopted to maintain the completeness of the content coverage of the construct (Cenfetelli and Bassellier 2009). To evaluate the reliability of the formative constructs, multicollinearity between the three sub-constructs was examined by assessing variance inflation factor (VIF). The results show that VIFs are 2.022, 2.231 and 2.334, lower than the threshold specified by Petter et al. (2007), suggesting that multicollinearity is not a significant problem here.

## Hypotheses Testing

The hypothesized direct effects were first tested, where the effect of social context on social capital was found to be positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 5 7 4 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , thus supporting H1. The effect of social capital on collective EIS compliance was positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 4 0 3 , \rho <$ 0.001), thus supporting H2. The effect of performance management context on collective EIS compliance was positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 2 8 6 , \beta < 0 . 0 5 )$ , thus supporting H4.

To test this mediating effect of social capital, we introduced a mediation model by adding the indirect effect from social context to collective EIS compliance through social capital. First, without this indirect effect, the direct effect of social context on collective EIS compliance was positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 2 4 4 , \ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ After introducing the indirect effect by adding social capital, this direct effect became insignificant $( \beta = 0 . 1 0 1 , \rho > 0 . 1 0 )$ , suggesting a full mediation effect. To test the significance of the indirect effect, a Sobel (1982) test and a bootstrapping test were conducted. In the Sobel test, we found a significant mediating effect of social capital as shown in Table 2 $\cdot ( { \bf t } = 2 . 4 4 8 , { \bf p } <$ 0.05). In the bootstrapping test, the indirect effect of social context on EIS compliance was 0.093. LLCI was 0.005 while ULCI was 0.178, suggesting the interval does not include zero. H3 is therefore supported.

Next, we tested performance management context as a moderator. As shown in Model 6 (Table 3), the interaction effect of social capital and PMC on collective EIS compliance was positive as predicted but not significant $( \beta = 0 . 1 7 8 , \ p > 0 . 0 5 )$ H5 therefore is not supported. As seen in Model 2, the interaction effect of performance management context and social context on social capital was found to be positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 3 , \beta < 0 . 0 5 )$ , thus supporting H6. To determine whether the significant moderating effects are substantive, R-square change resulting from the interaction effect should be examined (Carte and Russell 2003), specifically through the F-test (Cohen et al. 2013). When the interaction effect was included, R<sup>2</sup> significantly increased, where the effect size (f<sup>2</sup>) was 0.08 (f<sup>2</sup> = [R<sup>2</sup> of interaction effect model – R<sup>2</sup> of main effect model]/[1 – R<sup>2</sup> of main effect model], indicating a small to medium effect size. The F-test result thus shows that the interaction effect increased R² significantly, confirming the significance of the moderating effect (Carte and Russell 2003). To illustrate the moderation effect, we drew simple slopes (Figure 2).

H7 predicts that performance management context would positively moderate the indirect effect of social context on EIS compliance via social capital, such that the indirect effect is stronger when PMC is stronger. To test this hypothesis, we conducted the bootstrapping suggested by Edwards and Lambert (2007). As shown in Table 4, in a weak PMC (mean less 1 SD), social context had a positive effect on EIS compliance $( \mathtt { b } = 0 . 2 5 8 , \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ . This means the indirect effect was not significant $( \mathtt { b } \ = \ 0 . 0 5 2 , \ \mathtt { p } \ > \ 0 . 0 5 )$ , mainly because social context could not influence the mediator— social capital $( \mathtt { b } = 0 . 1 3 9 , \mathtt { p } > 0 . 0 5 )$ . In strong performance management contexts (mean plus 1 SD), the indirect effect was significant $( \mathsf { b } = 0 . 2 4 0 , \mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ and the direct effect between social context and EIS compliance was insignificant $( \mathtt { b } \ = \ 0 . 1 0 8 , \ \mathtt { p } \ > \ 0 . 0 5 )$ , suggesting a full mediation effect through social capital. The difference between strong and weak performance management contexts in terms of indirect effect is 0.188 and therefore significant (p < 0.05). This implies that social context affects collective EIS compliance via social capital between functional and IT units, where its effect is stronger under the condition of a strong performance management, lending support to the moderated mediation effect, as proposed in H7.

## Discussion

## Theoretical Implications

The empirical results support all but one hypothesis (Figure 3), thereby generally confirming the important role of social context and performance management context in achieving collective EIS compliance and the crucial mediating role of business–IT social capital.

First, this research establishes that business–IT social capital leads to collective EIS compliance, and that social context with trust and support positively affects business–IT social capital. It also confirms the full mediating effect of business–

![](/api/attachments/XKFF6BA5/fulltext/images/1b3c15597b5806d479de5246458013697a133e7295c1e16046c03e54c901dda7.jpg)

IT social capital between social context and collective EIS compliance. Furthermore, performance management context with discipline and stretch is found to be positively related to collective EIS compliance. These findings are consistent with our expectations and provide evidence for the base model (with the direct relationships) we advanced earlier. Third, the results confirm that performance management context amplifies the relationship between social context and business–IT social capital, such that this relationship is stronger when PMC is stronger, verifying empirically our theoretical account of the complementary effect of PMC and social context. Fourth, this confirmed moderating effect, in conjunction with the confirmed mediating relationship of business–IT social capital, provides empirical evidence to the hypothesized moderated mediation effect. This outcome lends further support to our theory on how the two important organizational contexts take effect on collective EIS compliance through business–IT capital. Finally, for the control variables, reward had a positive effect on collective EIS compliance, while the effect of punishment and training were shown to be insignificant.

To better understand this moderated mediation effect, the mediating mechanisms are analyzed herein in both strong and weak performance management contexts. As shown in Table 4, when PMC is strong, both first stage (social context à social capital: b = 0.584, p < 0.01) and second stage (social capital à EIS compliance: b = 0.411, p < 0.01) effects are significant, suggesting the meaningful and substantial mediating effect of business–IT social capital. Furthermore, this mediation effect is fully in place because the direct effect of social context on collective EIS compliance is insignificant (b = 0.108, ns) when the indirect effect is included. This corroborates with our theory that business employees under a strong performance management context are motivated to achieve high performance for their functional unit by making better use of the accessible and credible interaction channels (resulting from a trusting and supportive social context) to develop collaborative social relationships with IT, which in turn lead to their better compliance with EIS.

In a weak performance management context, however, there is no mediating effect of business–IT social capital (b = 0.052, ns) because social context has little or no significant effect on business–IT social capital (b = 0.139, ns) (also see the interaction plot in Figure 2). This finding suggests that a trusting and supportive social context does not necessarily promote collective EIS compliance through business–IT social capital when PMC is weak. An interesting outcome is that social context is still found to influence EIS compliance in a direct manner (b = 0.258, p < 0.05). This finding is consistent with the original literature proposing that employees directly reciprocate through cooperative behavior in return for a trusting and supportive social context set by the employer (Van Dyne et al 1994; Bottom et al. 2006). Overall, this study’s more nuanced moderation analysis suggests that social context contributes to collective EIS compliance through the learning effect enabled by business–IT social capital when performance management context is strong, but merely through the “reciprocity” effect when performance management context is weak.

Unexpectedly, the moderating effect of performance management context on the positive link between business–IT social capital and collective EIS compliance was found to be insignificant, although differences in the strength of the positive effect under the strong versus weak performance management context do appear as expected at the descriptive level (b = 0.411 vs. b = 0.375, p < 0.05). A plausible explanation for this outcome is that the external business environments with frequent and unexpected changes may force employees to scrutinize/utilize the exchanged information derived from business–IT social capital, thereby somewhat reducing the salience of PMC. This suspicion is consistent with the study of Kemper et al. (2013) who found that, the value of social capital is stronger when external turbulence is high, mainly because it is challenging but crucial for actors to obtain and assess information to cope with market changes. This said, future research may replicate the research model in other contexts for further empirical verification.

This study also reveals some interesting results that have implications to our understanding of the effects of reward and punishment. With a result similar to the previous individuallevel studies, in our case of collective EIS compliance, the reward and punishment mechanisms show mixed effects on EIS compliance, where reward takes a positive effect on collective EIS compliance and punishment has no effect. This could be interpreted that, in a dynamic environment when users are faced with frequent learning overload, reward could motivate employees to learn, while punishment creates more pressure for the employees, generating negative feelings that impede learning (rather than motivating it). To explore the possible reasons why punishment has no effect on collective EIS compliance in the context of the core tenets developed in this paper, we run the moderating analysis of performance management context on the relationship between punishment and EIS compliance. The result shows that performance management context has a positive moderating role on the direct effect of punishment and EIS compliance (b = 0.182, p < 0.05). A possible explanation for this outcome is that, with a higher level of PMC (discipline and stretch) from the organization, punishment could be a more useful mechanism for collective compliance, because PMC adds to the necessary motivation to learn, which punishment would otherwise fail to generate. Specifically, in a strong-PMC situation, employees are given challenging performance goals (the context feature of stretch) and consistent and clear performance feedback (the context feature of discipline). These motivate people to learn by aiming high and closing the performance gap. With this motivation in place, punishment could be more effective. This argument is partially in line with a previous proposition (Chen et al. 2012) that, punishment is more effective in deterring undesirable behaviors if the punishment consistently occurs after each undesirable behavior is observed, though this argument goes beyond the previous proposition due to the more encompassing and performance goal-oriented characteristic of PMC. This finding suggests that, firms should not only set up specific control mechanisms to regulate a focal EIS compliance behavior, but also should strive to build a disciplined and stretched performance management context in which the control mechanisms operate. When we ran the moderating analysis of PMC and social context on the link between reward and collective EIS compliance, however, neither moderating effect was found to be significant. Future research could take these empirical findings as a point of departure to develop a more refined theoretical account for the plausible interaction effects between the organizational context and specific reward/punishment mechanisms.

This study makes several major theoretical contributions. First, it contributes to the EIS compliance literature by explaining collective EIS compliance through the organizational context perspective, specifically the social and performance management context perspective that is novel to IS. The extant literature to date has only focused on individual EIS compliance and largely approached it from the perspective of reward/punishment (Liang et al. 2013; Xue et al. 2011). The literature has not yet addressed collective EIS compliance despite its important role in empowering business operations, particularly in a dynamic environment. By conceptualizing and explaining collective EIS compliance, we contribute to the EIS literature by not only relegating EIS compliance to the group level, but also drawing on the novel organizational context perspective to explain this phenomenon at the group level. In particular, this study makes a contextualized theoretical contribution (Hong et al. 2013) by theorizing the extent to and the way in which social and performance management contexts meaningfully affect collective EIS compliance through IS-specific mechanisms. In doing so, our study also responds to the broader call for more research on unit-level IS usage (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007; Lapointe and Rivard 2005).

Second, this study contributes to the EIS compliance literature by theorizing the crucial role of business–IT social capital, particularly for firms in a dynamic business environment with a strong need for keeping EIS current. This social relationship approach to EIS compliance represents another theoretical advancement from the existing literature that has primarily focused on manipulation of economic interest (e.g., reward /punishment).

Third, these research findings supplement the original literature on social and performance management context by extending its applicability to IS research, particularly in the area of EIS compliance. Extant literature has primarily explained collective cooperative behavior in forms such as cooperation (Ghosal and Bartlett 1997) and organizational citizenship behavior (Van Dyne et al. 1994), but with very limited applications to the IS field. We contextualize this theoretical account to explain collective EIS compliance by theorizing and empirically examining a more IS-specific influencing mechanism. This is achieved by introducing business–IT social capital, a well-established notion in IS research, as a meaningful but conditional mediator. In doing so, nuanced insights as to how and under what conditions these two types of organizational contexts can affect collective EIS compliance is offered herein. Furthermore, the original literature implies a complementary effect between hard and soft sides of organizational context, such as through the metaphor of balanced or complementary “yin and yang” features (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994) or other conceptual discussions (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004). But little work exists that empirically examines this complementarity. Our study contributes by formally examining the interaction effect of these two organizational contexts.

Finally, our findings extend the social capital literature in two ways. One, social capital in the IS research has been used to explain user satisfaction with IT services (Sun et al. 2012), knowledge transfer (Chiu et al. 2006), alignment between business and IT strategies (Karahanna and Preston 2013), and business understanding of IT (Wagner et al. 2014). The applicability of social capital theory is expanded in this study by extending this logic to another specific IS behavior, that of EIS compliance. Two, this study also contributes to the social capital research by identifying additional antecedents of business–IT social capital. An example of this limited extant research is the proposal of Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) that organizations as institutional settings are conductive to the development of social capital, but their work only proposed several antecedents (e.g., time, interdependence, and interaction channels) without systematic theoretical development. Our study contributes by revealing that a trusting and supportive social context can shape business–IT social capital, conditioned on a disciplined and stretching performance management context, thereby enriching our understanding of how business–IT social capital can be developed or enhanced. Future research can extend this line of thought to better understand the development of social capital in other business contexts.

## Practical Implications

The findings of this study have important implications for practitioners. First, it provides guidance to managers on how to promote collective employee EIS compliance in their functional unit, particularly in a dynamically changing environment. Managers are advised to focus on developing social capital with IT for frequent social interactions, good social relationships, shared languages, and mutual trust and respect. Second, this study advises managers that, since business–IT social capital is important, they could develop this social capital by building a strong supportive and trusting social context. Managers can achieve this end by reinforcing a series of management principles that support a trusting social context by (1) ensuring equity in organizational decision processes, (2) involving employees in relevant decisions and activities, and (3) staffing positions with competent people. A supportive social context is further produced by (1) allowing actors to access resources outside of their own unit, (2) encouraging autonomous initiative at lower organizational levels, and (3) coaching management staff to prioritize guidance and help over exercising authority and control (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004).

Third, this study proposes that managers could also foster collective EIS compliance by building a strong performance management context, characterized by discipline and stretch. Following (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994), they can establish a high-discipline environment by maintaining (1) clear standards and goals with fine-grained information and responsibilities, (2) consistent sanctions with established accountability, and (3) systems of open, fast feedback. A high-stretch environment can be developed by (1) encouraging and establishing shared ambition with the organization, (2) developing a collective identity, and (3) building personal meaning at work (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994). Managers can deploy the survey instrument (items for the two contexts per Appendix B) for internal assessment of these two important organizational contexts.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations due to the scope of its investigation, which open important venues for future research. First, a notable portion of the firms in our sample are not large in size. While the theoretical tenet we develop to explain collective EIS compliance is logically generalizable to firms of larger size, future research could replicate the empirical test of the research model in other organizational settings that include larger firms. Second, while we develop our theoretical model with a focus on collective EIS compliance, future research could extend this research by replicating the model in the IS security compliance context. There is reason to expect that social context and performance management context can also elucidate security compliance behavior, due to their explanatory power toward understanding cooperative behavior in general (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1994). We suspect that there may be two distinctions for further investigation in the security context, however. One, business–IT social capital might not be as crucial to security compliance as it is to EIS compliance, as noted in our earlier theoretical discussion on the key difference between these two types of compliance. Two, whether security compliance can form a collective construct like collective EIS compliance needs to be carefully explored and discussed, based on the extent to which the IT security system is individual-centric or interdependent among multiple employees. Future studies should thus contextualize the research model to the IT security compliance context for further examination.

Third, while this study focuses on explaining EIS compliance, an important usage outcome in its own right, future research could examine alternative EIS usage outcomes that arguably could be important to business success in a dynamic environment. For example, one fruitful topic is exploratory use, defined as the extent to which individual employees explore the previously undiscovered EIS features and create new ways of using system functionalities (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005; Burton-Jones and Grange 2012; Jasperson et al. 2005). A stream of research has found that, through exploratory system use, individual employees can overcome system limitations in response to urgent situations, therefore leading to good performance and innovative solutions to problems (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005; Ke et al. 2012; Liang et al. 2015). Exploratory use has been found to be instrumental when users are experiencing work interruptions (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005; Jasperson et al. 2005; Ke et al. 2012; Liang et al. 2015), which is likely to occur in a dynamic business environment. This said, we speculate that exploratory use is more applicable to individual users employing IT to work on independent tasks, but less so for highly interdependent work that requires concerted coordinate efforts, per the EIS context of this study. We suggest that future research should examine the role of exploratory and innovative uses of EIS, either individually or collectively, in the dynamically changing environment of the current economy and rapidly developing IT systems.

## Nonresponse Bias and Common Method Bias Tests

We tested nonresponse bias by comparing differences between early and late respondents based on available demographics, including department type, firm size, industry, location, and IT unit size. The results reveal no significant differences, confirming that nonresponse bias is not a concern in this study (Segars 1997).

While gathering the independent variables and dependent variables from different and independent sources largely reduced the potential threa of common-method variance (CMV) bias, we still checked for CMV using (1) a method factor (Podsakoff et al. 2003) and (2) a marker variable (Chin et al. 2013). First, we included a method factor in the model and allowed it to load freely on all indicators, where we found the mode estimation remained unchanged after introducing the method factor. And all the method factors were insignificant, explaining a variance of 0.1% on average. The proportion of indicators explained by their focal constructs is 78.9% (Table C1). Second, as the latent method factor approach is unable to detect CMV according to Chin et al. (2012), we adopted their construct-level correction procedure to further detect and control potential CMV.
