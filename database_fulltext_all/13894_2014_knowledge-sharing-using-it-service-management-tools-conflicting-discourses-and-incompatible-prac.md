# Knowledge sharing using IT service management tools: conflicting discourses and incompatible practices

Clive R. Trusson, Neil F. Doherty\* & Donald Hislop

School of Business and Economics, Loughborough University, Loughborough LE11 3TU, UK, email: n.f.doherty@lboro.ac.uk

Abstract. ICT applications that include functionality for knowledge sharing are routinely used by IT service providers even though their implementation is known to be problematic and the reasons for such problems not well understood. To shed light on the issue, we collected data at two organisations where managers had provided IT service support workers with IT service management (ITSM) tools incorporating functionality for knowledge sharing. Using critical discourse analysis and rhetorical analysis techniques, we contrasted primary data representative of IT service support practice with other primary and publicly available secondary data re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the prevailing discourse of IT service managers. Through this analysis, we identify an apparent dissonance between ITSM managerial and worker discourses that re<sup>fl</sup>ect opposing epistemologies. Managers are optimistic about the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of ICT-based knowledge sharing, whereas the practice of workers is revealed to privilege self-reliance and interpersonal knowledge sharing. By taking a dual, management–worker, perspective, we provide fresh insight into why ICT-based knowledge sharing is problematic. As a theoretical contribution, we propose that dysfunctional intra-organisational con<sup>fl</sup>ict can arise where incompatible management and worker practices become institutionalised through the simultaneous diffusion of con<sup>fl</sup>icting discourses.

Keywords: knowledge sharing, IT service management, discourse

## INTRODUCTION

ICT-based knowledge sharing is known to be problematic (Newell, 1999; Balafas et al., 2004; Butler & Murphy, 2007). Although the reasons for this are not well understood, human behavioura and sociocultural factors are thought to be important (e.g. Lam, 2005; Hsiao et al., 2006; Huang et al., 2011). In this paper, we draw upon critical discourse analysis (CDA) and rhetorical analysis techniques to contrast data typifying a managerial discourse that is optimistic about the implementation of ICT tools for knowledge sharing with data that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the discourse and practice of workers who are provided with such tools. As such, the core research question concerning us was ‘how do workers’ experiences of using specialised tools for providing and receiving informa tion/knowledge re<sup>fl</sup>ect the prevailing managerial “knowledge sharing” discourse?

The contextual settings for this study that provides new insight into this issue are the IT divisions of two organisations: an aggregates company referred to as Stoneworks and a local authority referred to as Shire County Council (SCC). These were selected because their respective managements had structured their operations around the popular Information Technology Infrastructure Library $( | \mathsf { T } | \mathsf { L } ) ^ { \top }$ ‘best-practice’ framework for IT service management (ITSM), which strongly advocates ICT-based knowledge sharing. As well as contributing to the literature on organisational knowledge sharing, this paper contributes to the <sup>fl</sup>edgling academic critique of ITSM, which remains underexplored as a popular management phenomenon. In addressing the research question, a theory was induced that ‘dysfunctional process con<sup>fl</sup>ict results within organisations when contradictory community discourses become simultaneously established, resulting in incompatible practices. The ‘dysfunctional process con<sup>fl</sup>ict is de<sup>fi</sup>ned here as opposition, between management and workers, concerning the approach taken to accomplish worker tasks, which acts detrimentally upon organisational performance (Jehn & Mannix, 2001).

## KNOWLEDGE SHARING AND IT SERVICE MANAGEMENT

In this section, we lay the foundations for the presentation of data. Firstly, we set out the ITSM landscape, establishing it as a popular information systems (IS) phenomenon that is of growing interest to academics. We then discuss alternative approaches to knowledge sharing, with particular reference to the prevalent use of ICT tools within ITSM environments.

## IT service management

In response to the increasing importance of IT services, the knowledge-based practice of ITSM has established itself as a fashionable IS management phenomenon (Abrahamson, 1996; McNaughton et al., 2010). ITSM can be understood as a process-based approach to strategically manage complex IT services throughout their life cycles (van Bon et al., 2008; Pollard & Cater-Steel, 2009) whilst ensuring alignment with the goals of the business supported by the IT service (Taylor et al., 2007c). The most globally in<sup>fl</sup>uential of the various ITSM approaches is ITIL, a systems-thinking framework that underpins the ISO/IEC 20000 standard (van Bon et al., 2008; Kneller, 2010). The global popularity of ITIL – with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant uptake in the USA amongst large and prestigious organisations such as IBM and NASA (CER, 2010, 2011; ITILnews, 2011) – has resulted in nascent academic interest.

The ITSM practitioner literature presents a positive image as to the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of ITIL implementation (e.g. Golden, 2007; Anthes, 2008). CER (2009, p. 15) con<sup>fi</sup>dently asserted that ITIL is ‘used by thousands of companies worldwide with proven results’. In large part, the academic community has published papers broadly supportive of this discourse of positivity (e.g. Pollard & Cater-Steel, 2009; Tan et al., 2009). That said, the risks and dif<sup>fi</sup>culties associ ated with adopting ITIL have also been noted as has the lack of evidence to adequately demonstrate organisational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Pollard & Cater-Steel, 2009; Tiong et al., 2009; McNaughton et al., 2010). Other practitioner-based research has concluded that ITSM ‘best practice’ is stil immature, with organisations often lacking commitment to implementation (CER, 2010, 2011) Although ITIL is popular, a sceptical practitioner undercurrent is detectable: Lawes (2010a, 2010b) has blogged about poor governance and inconsistent and incoherent guidelines; Addy (2007, p. 5) has suggested that it has been ‘devised by bureaucrats for bureaucrats’; Holland (2012) has questioned its adaptability within a rapidly changing technological landscape; and England (2009, p. 1) has described it as a ‘fad/cult/hype phenomenon’.

## Knowledge sharing using ICTs

ITIL asserts that ‘if an organization seriously intends to implement ITSM then a fully integrated ITSM toolset will be required’ (Taylor et al., 2007a, p. 160). Although essentially these tools are work<sup>fl</sup>ow management systems (WFMS), all feature functionality to enable information/ knowledge to be shared between workers tasked with investigating service incidents. Such tools incorporate a database and knowledge-sharing functionality to be used by a worker acting in the role of either knowledge provider or knowledge receiver (Jasimuddin et al., 2012). There are then two sides to this functionality. Firstly, it enables data/information knowledge to be provided to a database via a knowledge provision process. IT service support workers, assigned IT service incidents, mandatorily provide information about each incident as it is processed through to resolution and may discretionarily provide other related data/information/knowledge for storage and potential use later in the incident management (IM) process or on a future occasion. Secondly, the functionality enables data information/knowledge to be received from the same database via a knowledge receipt process. The same workers receive previously provided data/information/knowledge so that they might use it to investigate, diagnose and resolve an incident. The ITIL guidance points to these two processes combining to provide effective knowledge-sharing capabilities, which bene<sup>fi</sup>t management in terms of worker ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and knowledge control: ‘It is important to develop the necessary database prior to the moment knowledge may be applied. Knowledge sharing should be fostered prior to, during and after the application of knowledge to the task’ (Taylor et al., 2007b, pp. 125–126). The knowledge provision process enables the development of the database, and the knowledge receipt process enables knowledge to be applied.

The use of these applications needs to be considered in the light of the debate over the capture and reuse of knowledge using ICTs (e.g. Walsham, 2001; Hislop, 2002; Grant, 2011). The disputants can be dichotomised into optimists and pessimists (Hislop, 2009). Optimists uphold a codi<sup>fi</sup>cation approach to knowledge transfer with a belief that knowledge has a distinct objective dimension that can be successfully codi<sup>fi</sup>ed and computerised (e.g. Bergeron, 2003; Kankanhalli et al., 2005). Pessimists counter that in practice knowledge cannot be divided into explicit and tacit elements and that focus should be applied to the process of synchronised knowledge transfer between workers (e.g. Mueller et al., 2010; Nicolini, 2011). The optimists typically conceptualise knowledge as discrete packages that have an organisational value when separated from their human creators. The pessimists point to knowledge sharing from one worker to another as requiring interpersonal communication to enable meaning to be effectively (re)constructed (Hislop, 2002).

This study considers the prevalence of an ITSM community discourse that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the optimistic epistemological stance. By also applying focus to the practice of IT service support workers, it addresses ITSM concerns that implementing ICT-based knowledge sharing is problematic (Service Talk, 2008). In responding to an identi<sup>fi</sup>ed need for rigorous empirical (Addy, 2007; Iden & Langeland, 2010) and case study (Winniford et al., 2009) research into ITSM, we approached the study by drawing upon credible analytical techniques compatible with the traditions of social constructionism (Berger & Luckmann, 1967).

## ANALYTICAL APPROACH

In this section, we outline our approach as being loosely structured and interpretative, particularly drawing upon CDA (e.g. Fairclough, 1993; van Dijk, 1993) and rhetorical analysis (e.g. Burke, 1969; Aristotle, 1991; Green, 2004).

## Critical discourse analysis

A discourse is taken to comprise the ‘structures, strategies and other properties’ of talk, text and communicative events that can be directly sensed or has become embedded within practice and artefacts (van Dijk, 1993, p. 250; Phillips & Jørgensen, 2002). CDA is a loosely de<sup>fi</sup>ned theory and method for addressing social issues through analysing the language that shapes and is shaped by that society, with a particular focus on power and dominance (Mayr, 2008). Here, we follow others in the IS arena (e.g. Stahl et al., 2012) by invoking CDA to address the problematic nature of ICT-based knowledge sharing within typical ITSM settings. Our interest is in the interaction of linguistic (i.e. text/talk) and non-linguistic elements (e.g. power and organisational context) affecting the practices of ITSM managers and workers. Through this analysis, the theoretical tension between the optimistic and pessimistic perspectives on knowledge sharing might be revealed in organisational life through the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of marked differences ‘between different discourses which represent different ways of understanding aspects of the world’ (Phillips & Jørgensen, 2002, p. 2). The ‘critical’ dimension draws attention to the role of discourses in maintaining unequal power relations between social groups. CDA holds that one group uses an apparently natural and acceptable discourse to persuade subordinate groups to accept their values and in doing so institutionalise their hegemonic dominance (Gramsci, 1971; Foucault, 1976; van Dijk, 1993; Mayr, 2008). We approach the study from the dichotomised perspectives of ‘management and ‘workers’, theorising that these separate groups have different historically rooted dis courses that in juxtaposition might reveal reasons as to why ICT-based knowledge sharing has proven problematic.

## Rhetorical analysis

Rhetorical analysis is a subtype of discourse analysis (and therefore CDA) concerned with the role of persuasive language in structuring social action (Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005). It has emerged as a credible method for understanding organisational phenomena (e.g. Zbaracki, 1998). We draw upon rhetorical analysis in this study to gain an understanding of the persuasive language used within the ITSM managerial community and its relationship to ITSM practice. Rhetoric is a form of language that, in communication to a recipient, is designed to have a persuasive effect upon the recipient through credibility (ethos), emotional resonance (paphos) and/or reasoning (logos) (Aristotle, 1991). With a particular focus on persuasive discourses that reveal a political or vested interest agenda, the use of rhetorical analysis in relation to popular management techniques such as ITSM ‘best practice’ involves the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and exploration of data sources that reveal embedded and recurring patterns of interests, goals and assumptions that combine to present a one-sided account that manipulates the cognitive legitimacy (i.e. appropri ateness, credibility and desirability) of the technique or speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspects of that technique (Billig, 1996; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005). Here, rhetorical analysis is used to identify recurring messages about knowledge sharing within the ITSM community.

‘New rhetorical analysis’ (e.g. Burke, 1969) extends to the consideration of how a conglom eration of rhetorical discourses from within a community of value-sharing communicators acts upon unspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed but implied recipients. Such recipients might include members of the same community, others who are drawn into the community to consistently perpetuate its implicit values and purpose and the rhetoricians themselves through unconscious self-persuasion (Burks, 1970; Sillence, 2005; Richards, 2008).

By taking a new rhetorical approach, ITSM ‘best practice’ might be reasoned to be constructed from overlapping rhetorical discourse elements that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the tensions in ‘rhetoric as form versus rhetoric as function’ or ‘product versus process’ (Jasinski, 2001, p. xxiii). The <sup>fi</sup>rst of these elements is the of<sup>fi</sup>cially sanctioned ‘best-practice’ rhetoric in the form of published guidelines (e.g. ITIL), which IT service managers might consult in their decision-making practice. This ‘static rhetoric re-forms intermittently through a process of staged evolution as ‘actors with preferences for particular practices negotiate for a dominant position by garnering resources and calling upon contemporary issues/concerns’ (Wagner et al., 2006, p. 259). Thus, this ‘of<sup>fi</sup>cial’ rhetoric emerges as durable codes of practice (e.g. ITIL version 3), authorised by the contemporaneously most powerful people within the ITSM community and re<sup>fl</sup>ective of their transient discourses.

The second element, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting rhetoric as a process, is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed within the everyday communal discourse of practising IT service managers (and others within a broader ITSM community). Such transitory rhetoric might be seen to both produce and be a product of rationalisations that combine to promote (and shape) the ‘of<sup>fi</sup>cial’ ‘best-practice’ rhetoric from which it largely originates. Thus, managers wanting to gain social approval within the ITSM managerial community might reasonably choose to identify with it by speaking similarly to its prevailing communal discourse (i.e. referring to the ‘of<sup>fi</sup>cial’ best-practice rhetoric). As Billig (1996) argued, in situations of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, individuals are inclined to accommodate the opinions and attitudes of the group and draw away from those that run counter to them. It is such communal discourse that fuels the ‘rhetoric of success’ process outlined by Zbaracki (1998, p. 602) whereby ‘managers consume a rhetoric of success. . ., use that rhetoric to develop their program, and then <sup>fi</sup>lter their experiences to present their own rhetoric of success’. Similarly, Green’s (2004) ‘rhetorical theory of diffusion’ explains that through diffusion, rhetorical discourse results in managerial practices becoming institutionalised.

A third element is what might be termed ‘parasitical’ in that it emerges from sources, such as consultancies and technology suppliers, that are pro<sup>fi</sup>t motivated to synergise with an of<sup>fi</sup>cially sanctioned rhetoric. With regard to this study, this ‘parasitical’ rhetoric takes form through (i) the inscription of ‘of<sup>fi</sup>cial’ rhetoric pertaining to technology-based knowledge sharing within the design of specialised ITSM tools (Wagner et al., 2006) and (ii) the promotional literature for these tools.

## METHODOLOGY

Whilst the highlighting of CDA and rhetorical analysis serves to emphasise the centrality of language in addressing the research question, a quantitative dimension to the method was also applied to enhance the validity of <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## Data collection method

A variety of discourse data from ITSM managerial and worker communities related to knowledge sharing was collected to present an array of voices with different vested interests, including ‘best practice’ authors, suppliers of ITSM tools, various IT service managers and consultants and IT service support workers (Table 1).

The primary data were collected at two organisations referred to as ‘Stoneworks’, the UK operation of a multinational aggregates company, and SCC, an English local government service provider. Over a period totalling approximately 130 h, ethnographic data on worker practice were collected from overt observations of workers resolving service incidents. During the observations, carried out over several visits to six teams across four of<sup>fi</sup>ce locations between January and June 2010, details were recorded of the small actions of 11 IT service support workers working on 62 incidents (Table 2). These notes were word processed promptly to become narrative representations of practice. Additionally, the same workers (or others in similar roles) were interviewed about their work and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally about knowledge sharing. By collecting a variety of primary data across multiple teams, selected to be typical of those in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by ITSM ‘best practice’, the reliability of the study’s generalisable <sup>fi</sup>ndings was enhanced (Miles & Huberman, 1994).

Stoneworks and SCC were selected as IT-dependent organisations at which a high importance had been attached to implementing ITIL. In discussions with senior IT management at both organisations, it was established that Stoneworks’ parent corporation had insisted upon ITIL being implemented and that SCC had consulted ITIL when recently restructuring its IT division. Both organisations used an IM process in accordance with the ITIL IM model, comprising sequential activities of detection, recording, classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, initial support, investigation, diagnosis, resolution and recovery (Taylor et al., 2007a; van Bon et al., 2008). The systematic observation of the workers was assisted by the structure of these prede<sup>fi</sup>ned IM processes such that there was a consistency across observations, enhancing data reliability (Bryman, 2008). Importantly, these organisations were selected because, at both, managers had imposed (different) ITIL-compliant<sup>2</sup> ITSM applications upon the workers, intending that the tools knowledge management functionalities be used for knowledge exchange within and between teams. The secondary data sources were selected as artefacts of the ITSM managerial com munity discourse for linguistic analysis, with the objective of revealing the epistemological standpoint prevailing within the community.

## Data analysis method

The collected data comprised a variety of discourse elements divided into two sets representative of ITSM managerial discursive practices and IT service support worker discourse and practice. The data sources were analysed interpretatively with a particular interest in identifying occurrences of persuasive language and enthymematic discourse (i.e. concealing deeply structured implicit premises) that pointed to epistemological perspectives on organisational knowledge sharing. Written artefacts were manually trawled for such evidence, thus revealing authors’/speakers’ subjective meanings, and by association ITSM management and worker communal meanings, about knowledge sharing and using ICTs for that purpose. The identi<sup>fi</sup>- cation of these meanings through analysis enabled voices to be heard from within the speci<sup>fi</sup>c ITSM managerial and worker communities (Marcon & Gopal, 2008). In particular, the transcriptions of the interviews were manually analysed with the objective of accessing the meanings that individuals attached to knowledge sharing and using ITSM tools that were ‘simply not amenable to observation’ (Bryman, 2008, p. 466). In analysing the data as discursive entities, we took particular interest in how both the transient communication of individuals and the durable texts sought to convey, consolidate and privilege particular meanings whilst countering or failing to acknowledge others (Alvesson & Karreman, 2000; Rapley, 2007).

The notes taken whilst observing workers were analysed to speci<sup>fi</sup>cally identify occurrences where information/knowledge was deliberately sought, received as a matter of routine and provided to others. The techniques of clustering and counting were used to detect recurring patterns (Miles & Huberman, 1994). Similar patterns and themes were manually identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from the interview transcriptions so as to construct convincing typi<sup>fi</sup>cations to re<sup>fl</sup>ect ITSM managerial and worker discourses and practices. Subsequently, by adopting the CDA perspective that discourses are central to understanding power relations, we sought to identify any dissonance between managerial and worker attitudes and practices relating to ICT-based knowledge sharing.

## KNOWLEDGE SHARING: MANAGEMENT AND WORKER PERSPECTIVES

An observation of workers’ use of ITSM tools con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the role played by technology in structuring the process by which incidents are investigated, diagnosed and resolved. Figure 1 illustrates the ICT-based knowledge-sharing process that sits within this typical structured IM process. Firstly, it models how data/information/knowledge is provided to a logical ‘incident transaction database’ as a result of workers following mandatory procedures of the IM process (marked as pathway P1). It also shows how other data/information/knowledge may be provided as a result of conscious discretionary acts of workers either within the same incident transaction database if it directly relates to an incident (P2) or within an independent logical ‘knowledge transaction database’ (KTD) if it is intended to be of generic use across incidents (P3). In differentiating between these two parts of the ITSM tool’s database, we were able to consider the worker practice in respect of providing to each part. As part of working procedurally within the IM process, workers mandatorily accessed stored information relating to services and users affected. Where workers had been assigned incidents that had previously been logged, the core data already input (e.g. description and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation) were similarly accessed as a matter of procedure (R1). Additionally, information/knowledge from historical incident records (R2) and knowledge articles (R3) might be sought from the database.

These pathways on the model re<sup>fl</sup>ect a view of knowledge sharing as involving ‘the exchange of organizational knowledge between a source and a recipient’ (Szulanski, 1996, p. 28) and that when a database is placed between the source and recipient, this exchange is rationalised into separate processes of knowledge provision and receipt (Jasimuddin et al., 2012). ## Knowledge provision: managerial perspectives

The ITIL literature is explicitly optimistic about ICT-based knowledge sharing, advocating that managers provide an effective knowledge base to improve incident resolution ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency ‘by reducing the need to rediscover knowledge’ (Taylor et al., 2007a, p. 236). The vendors of specialised ITSM tools (referred to as products A–L) are similarly consistent in their epistemo logical standpoint, making optimistic claims about their products’ knowledge-sharing functionality. The consistency that <sup>fl</sup>ows from credible academic literature through popular practitione guidelines to marketing literature for applications is in and of itself a rhetorical process (Sillence, 2005). The consistency of the argumentation of the optimistic academic authors with that of the authors of ITIL and the marketing literature for ITIL-compliant software binds them together as one coherent voice, constituting a composite argument as to the veracity of ICT-based knowledge sharing.

As well as consistently advocating objecti<sup>fi</sup>cation of worker knowledge as an implicit taken-for-granted premise within an enthymematic structure (Aristotle, 1991; Heracleous & Barrett, 2001), further consistency is found in the rationalisation of workers, with an assumption made that they will unquestionably codify information/knowledge for reuse. Within the persuasive marketing literature, these unstated, taken-for-granted premises are <sup>fi</sup>rstly applied to ‘front-line support staff. . . [who] capture knowledge resulting in an output of a service knowledge management base’ (product C). Secondly, they are applied to ‘subject matter experts. . . [who are enabled] to author, categorize and share knowledge’ (B) and ‘augment incident management records with diagnostic data’ (C). And thirdly, they are applied to the body of corporate expertise with claims of ‘the intellectual capital of the business. . . [being] increased and. . . available’ (D) and ‘[mitigating] the loss of skills and experience. .. associated with staff turnover'(G)

It is through this synergistic discourse of ‘of<sup>fi</sup>cial’ guidance and ‘parasitical’ rhetoric that managers become persuaded to implement ICT-based knowledge sharing within ITSM settings. At Stoneworks, the ITIL-trained manager Ravi noticed a cultural norm amongst ‘his’ workers to share knowledge interpersonally yet remained fully convinced by the ITIL-endorsed optimism about ICT-based knowledge sharing, remarking, ‘I think we still need to get them to buy into the whole thing of adding it on to the knowledge base.

The dif<sup>fi</sup>culty faced here by Ravi is alluded to within the ITIL literature with a solution asserted that managers reward workers for providing ‘valuable knowledge assets’ to the knowledge base:

This is a highly effective way to encourage [workers] to share their knowledge and move past the old paradigm that knowledge is power and job security and therefore needs to be hoarded. [Taylor et al., 2007d, p. 194]

Here. the authors construct a rhetorical argumentation (Perelman. 1979) that seeks to persuade the manager that workers will codify their experientially acquired knowledge if managers reward them, implicitly leading to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. They do this by constructing a complex enthymematic argument formed from the conjunction of a material premise that it is sensible to capture knowledge within an organisation and various compatible propositions, presupposed sweeping opinions or inferential premises, including that (1) all workers are predisposed to hoard knowledge; (2) all workers are motivated by management-sanctioned rewards to record knowledge using technology; (3) such knowledge can satisfactorily be codi<sup>fi</sup>ed; and (4) such codi<sup>fi</sup>ed knowledge articles are essentially valuable (Aristotle, 1991; Jasinski, 2001).

Ravi’s concerns about workers not using the tool for knowledge provision are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the concerns of another manager, Mike H. who started a discussion with other subscribers to ar ITSM group on the LinkedIn<sup>W</sup> social networking website by asking the question: ‘how do you motivate technicians to contribute the knowledge in their heads to a knowledge management solution database?’ What was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant about the 19 managerial responses he received was that none questioned the practice of tasking workers with populating the database with objective knowledge entities. There was implicit unanimity that this was ‘common sense’. Typical advice given included ‘reward the correct behaviour’ (Donna), ‘drive the behaviours that lead to them “letting go”’ (Iain), ‘get them to raise a service request for an expert to write a knowledge article’ (Andrew), ‘develop a formal system for documenting’ (Kevin E) and ‘identify writers to work with technicians’ (Mary). This ‘taken-for-granted’ management mindset was shared by the other Stoneworks IT service managers, with Graham revealing that ‘what I’m trying to do. . . is to get all of our workarounds. . . onto the knowledge base as quickly as possible’, and his manager, Karla, frustrated that workers were not routinely using the tool to share their experiential knowledge, saying:

When somebody closes an incident and there isn’t an article in the knowledge database I would like to see them create one. Most tools these days make that relatively easy.

The underlying enthymematic structures of the discourses of these practising managers, who resolutely maintain the optimistic premise concerning ICT-based knowledge sharing even in the light of their practice, remain consistent with the underlying structures of (1) the of<sup>fi</sup>cial rhetoric that assigns to the IT service manager a role of pursuing worker knowledge objecti<sup>fi</sup>cation; (2) the knowledge codi<sup>fi</sup>cation rhetoric inscribed within specialised ITSM tools; and (3) the rhetoric used in marketing those tools. It can also be seen to re<sup>fl</sup>ect Green’s (2004, p. 655) observation that ‘managers in conversation with themselves and others, rationalize the adoption of managerial practice’, in this case, the adoption of a practice that encourages the capture of worker knowledge within databases.

## Knowledge provision: worker perspectives

If a managerial discourse of workers capturing ‘expert knowledge’ was to be re<sup>fl</sup>ected in practice, one would expect workers to regularly provide discretionary knowledge articles independent of the IM process (Figure 1, P3). And yet during the observations, such activity was absent, and Stoneworks’ monthly IM reports indicate that barely one knowledge article per week was added to the knowledge base. Over a 3-month period, during which over 10 000 incidents were logged, only 15 knowledge articles were discretionarily written by just six workers (in an IT division of 72 staff), only two of whom contributed more than two articles (Table 3).

Workers complied with the mandatory requirements to provide incident data throughout the life cycle of incidents, to create a historical record of the incident and the work carried ou (Figure 1, P1). All teams were working under some degree of time pressure, and the data recorded were suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to meet the perceived requirements. The observation of incidents being worked at the initial incident-logging stage revealed that workers routinely captured data that detailed the incident. Mainly, these data were selected from drop-down menus but also included brief descriptions. If the incident was escalated to a second-line worker, this information was used as a basis for their investigations. As incidents were worked, workers routinely used the ‘update’ and/or ‘escalate’ functionality to maintain the historical record of the incident, again often selecting from drop-down menus. Where workers took opportunities to volunteer information above that mandatorily required by the tool, they often demonstrated a keen sense of what might be important for the next person working on the incident (P2). For example, when escalating an incident, Sandra (SCC) brought to her colleague’s attention that her investigations had uncovered that the incident had occurred when the system was trying to interpret data from a neighbouring county. Another worker provided a telephone number that might be useful to the worker continuing the work. Other information included details of related incidents assigned to a third party and a suggestion to assist diagnosis. On resolution, incidents were observed being routinely closed by selecting the appropriate precon<sup>fi</sup>gured resolution code. Occasionally, but by no means routinely, a note was made at this point to indicate the action taken (P2), typically making use of the ‘copy and paste’ functionality as a time-saving device (e.g. copying to the record command code used for resolution).

Roger: I’m supposedly the senior member of the team and if people need to know stuff. . . then they usually come to me.

This study <sup>fi</sup>nds that for IT service support workers, providing information/knowledge to colleagues is typically less about codi<sup>fi</sup>cation and technological retention and more about human communication, either, as with Sandra, providing at point of personal receipt or, as with Roger, providing on request. The evidence points to workers being unlikely to discretionarily spend scarce time providing a knowledge article to a centralised database for uncertain future needs.

## Knowledge receipt: managerial perspectives

An analysis of the ITIL and ISO/IEC 20000 literature and other managerial sources reveals a lesser concern for workers receiving knowledge than providing it. The Stoneworks IT service manager, Karla, argued that the knowledge base was particularly useful for new recruits but expressed her disappointment that experienced workers did not use it, explaining that ‘there are things being put there every day: things that they don’t already know’. The Stoneworks management practice was staunchly optimistic about the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of ICT-based knowledge sharing in that resources had been made available to extend the availability of the knowledge base to ‘end-users’ ‘before they log a call. . . then they won’t need to ring up (Graham). Such optimism was retained by both Karla and Graham despite being aware of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant dif<sup>fi</sup>culties from a recipient perspective in achieving the objective. Graham suggested that articles were often of poor quality and hard to follow with ‘a lot of jargon in there’, whereas Karla pointed to both the quantity and quality of the articles:

We’ve never felt con<sup>fi</sup>dent enough that we’ve got enough in there to make it available to users. The quality and breadth of things that users might expect we haven’t got. . . We’ve only got one shot at it and if it’s not right it will just turn people off and they will never go back to it.

Stoneworks’ management’s primary concern with gaining corporate ownership and control of worker knowledge, ahead of its actual use by workers, is revealed by Graham when he speaks about populating the knowledge base with instructional articles even though they are unlikely to be used:

The service desk manager doesn’t want to accept [any lengthy workaround]. . . [The service desk] have got that information there [on the knowledge base] but the person who wrote it wil still do the <sup>fi</sup>x because it takes too long for the service desk guys to use it.

Whereas facilitating knowledge provision to a database is explicit within the manageria discourse, the worker discretionally demanding knowledge from the database seems to be assumed. ITIL asserts that incident investigation ‘is likely to include. . . searching previous Incident/Problem Records and/or. . . Knowledge Databases’ (Taylor et al., 2007a, p. 52) but seems to adopt an ‘if we build it they will use it’ approach towards managing the knowledge receipt process.

Predicated by an assumption that workers will use it, the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency bene<sup>fi</sup>ts seem to be taken for granted within both this of<sup>fi</sup>cial rhetoric and the parasitical rhetoric of the ICT tool vendors. This vendor rhetoric uses enthymematic argumentation structures that conceal this assumption, along with the assumption that databases come already stocked with relevant high-quality information/knowledge, to con<sup>fi</sup>dently offer to managers seemingly valuable products. Examples of this rhetoric include

• ‘a knowledge base of easy-to-<sup>fi</sup>nd solutions’ (product B);

• ‘a single source of authoritative solutions’ (F);

• ‘a contextual knowledge base. . . for problem solving’ (J);

• ‘a comprehensive and integrated knowledge base. . . detailing. . . solutions and workarounds’ (C).

Product G's literature broadens out the benefits such that ‘a repository of corporate knowledge is provided, and product A’s literature goes further, suggesting that the database becomes an oracle that ‘empowers staff. . . by leveraging organizational wisdom’ such that they might ‘obtain the best answers in real-time’. Strip away the rhetorical premises and a reality emerges of vendors selling applications that merely provide skeletal frameworks to which information/knowledge articles might or might not be added by workers over time, which subsequently might or migh not be productively used by workers.

## Knowledge receipt: worker perspectives

The data relating to information/knowledge receipt using ICT tools question the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the optimistic managerial perspective. In practice, across the 62 incidents observed, workers used various methods to receive information/knowledge for the task at hand including using the ITSM tools (Table 4).

‘Real-time’ interpersonal communication: Considerable interpersonal knowledge seeking was observed. Workers used locally situated colleagues 13 times as readily available sources of information/knowledge. Most of the information/knowledge accessed in this way was contextual or procedural rather than technical. For example, questions asked across desks included the following: ‘Do we have problems at the moment bringing in assessment data’; ‘How many “end of year” workshops are being run’; and ‘Who is logged into the print queue? Additionally, workers telephoned other workers within personal networks 11 times to seek ou the information/knowledge they needed to progress incidents. The length and content of these conversations suggested that the information/knowledge sought by telephone was typically technical, suggesting that the knowledge seekers chose a knowledge transfer medium that facilitated a dialogue with someone they personally selected as a knowledge provider.

Via ITSM tool: On only four occasions were historical incident records discretionarily sought, and on three occasions. relevant information/knowledge was found (Figure 1. R2). On the fourth occasion, the worker commented: ‘I’m sure we’ve <sup>fi</sup>xed this before but I can’t <sup>fi</sup>nd it in our knowledge base.’ On no occasion was a non-incident-speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge article observed being discretionarily accessed from the KTD (R3). Stoneworks’ monthly IM reports indicate greater use of the KTD than the observation data suggest – one search for every nine incidents<sup>3</sup> – but still tend to support a <sup>fi</sup>nding that the KTD was largely neglected (Table 5). The apparent data inconsistency might be explained by the recruitment of workers onto Stoneworks’ service desk shortly before the 3-month period. According to Karla (Stoneworks), new recruits search the database ‘on a regular basis, doing 60, 70 searches a month’. A reasonable explanation is that the KTD contained knowledge articles of use to inexperienced workers but not to those with the experience of the observed workers.

Other methods: Internet and intranet Web pages, often via the use of search engines, were discretionarily accessed 22 times (i.e. a third of incidents observed), revealing the browser application as an important ICT for accessing technical and contextual information/knowledge. Also observed were 10 occurrences of workers referring to sources of written information including manuals, directories, technical diagrams and a whiteboard.

The observations also revealed that knowledge was not always sought when workers did not have all the information/knowledge they needed. Rather, workers used considerable problemsolving creativity as they investigated incidents. Rather than seek knowledge externally, they chose to draw upon their internal ‘stock of knowledge’ (Schutz & Luckmann, 1974). Harry’s (SCC) tangential response to being asked about the importance of knowledge sharing for IM epitomises this preference for self-reliance: ‘You’ve got to have a logical approach to fault-<sup>fi</sup>nding’.

## DISCUSSION

This qualitative study highlights the discursive processes by which a dysfunctional process con<sup>fl</sup>ict between management and workers can occur within organisations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it has been shown that a discernible management discourse that venerates ICT-based knowledge-sharing con<sup>fl</sup>icts with a simultaneous worker discourse that implicitly advocates self-reliance and pragmatic knowledge sharing; and this manifests itself in management and worker practices that are incompatible and result in dysfunctional intra-organisational con<sup>fl</sup>ict about how workers ful<sup>fi</sup>l their roles as IT service support workers.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings enable us to build upon Green’s (2004) ‘rhetorical theory of diffusion’, by demonstrating that not only does rhetoric play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the diffusion of a managerial discourse that manifests itself in institutionalised management practices but also that these practices might be undermined by a parallel diffusion of a contradictory worker discourse that manifests itself in incompatible institutionalised shop-<sup>fl</sup>oor practices. In this study, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the everyday experiences of managers and workers in IT service practice both re<sup>fl</sup>ect and produce distinct management and worker discourses, which in turn result in con<sup>fl</sup>icting institutionalised management and worker practices. Managers are more likely to engage in a discourse oriented towards the practice of strategic and tactical decision-making and thus typically be subjected to rhetoric concerning the direction of worker activity across an expanse of time. In contrast, workers are more likely to engage in an operational-level discourse for the purposes of ful<sup>fi</sup>lling time-critical needs. Thus, as Billig (1996, p. 231) noted, ‘each community possesses its own common sense’ – in this case, about how knowledge should be exchanged between workers.

Our research focus has been on how workers make use of management-imposed specialised tools for knowledge sharing; and this variance of perspective appears to be important in explaining why implementing such tools is commonly problematic for managers. With reference to Green (2004), we theorise that the institutionalisation of the management practice of implementing such tools results from an optimistic epistemological stance taking root within the ITSM manageria community, which then implicitly reinforces the credibility of this stance by <sup>fi</sup>ltering out any undesirable disputations or alternative paradigms. Through rhetorical discourses, the managerial community theoretically acts as an entity upon its own individual members and prospec tive members, <sup>fi</sup>rstly, by persuading them to identify with and disseminate a set of cohesive communal beliefs and, secondly, by pulling them away from any counterarguments that might emanate from contrarian managers and workers (Anand, 1996; Billig, 1996).

In this study, one such belief that is revealed to have become endemic amongst the community is the veracity of ‘separating’ workers from their knowledge through ICT-based codi<sup>fi</sup>cation. This belief implicitly takes form within the ITIL ‘best-practice’ advancement of the distancing, depersonalising and dissembling of workers into (1) capabilities to perform rationalised tasks and (2) codi<sup>fi</sup>ed representations of experientially acquired knowledge (Bauman, 1989; Taylor et al., 2007c). This approach has the effect of rationalising the human worker to the edges o management concern, with the workers’ everyday experiences considered to be largely inconse quential for organisational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Thus, the typical IT service manager undervalues workers personal knowledge (Siau et al., 2007), placing their dissembled knowledge centre stage as the object of value.

By contrast, the prevailing IT service support worker discourse reveals an intimate interest in operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency alongside individual concerns about their careers as IS knowledge workers in an ongoing process of developing marketable experiential histories. Following a similar pathway to the institutionalisation of management ‘best practice’, this worker discourse leads to the institutionalisation of a ‘common-sense’ worker practice that is incompatible with management ‘best-practice’ guidelines. The data reveal that central to this worker discourse is a pragmatism built upon sensitivity to work intensity. From this perspective, eschewing the management-imposed tool for ‘real-time’ interpersonal knowledge exchange and self-reliance does not carry any confrontational intent. Rather, in this study, workers, acting from within the rationalised and institutionalised thinking of their discursive community, typically chose to follow the least demanding courses of action to best cope with work overload (Handy, 1985; Kahneman, 2011). They adhered to the management will by routinely sharing information knowledge where the tool’s WFMS functionality demanded it but largely neglected the functionality for discretionarily sharing non-incident-speci<sup>fi</sup>c information/knowledge.

Taking a CDA perspective, we argue that the individual members of a managerial community, and subcommunities within that community, have unequal power relations and that it is the words of a power elite within this managerial community that dominate the managerial discourse such that managers become predisposed to being optimistic about ICT-based knowledge sharing, as illustrated by the comments of those managers interviewed and those involved in the LinkedIn<sup>W</sup> discussion. Contextually, the ‘voices’ of an ITSM power elite (conceptualised as those that are ‘heard’ most within the community via industry publications and conferences, and/or through the provision of consultancy services and ICT solutions) are most clearly heard and rhetorically asserted within the ITSM industrial sector: and these ‘voices’ powerfully act upon others within the same managerial community. It is contended that through processes of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and unconscious self-persuasion, IT service managers become a cohesive, ‘group-thinking’ community (Janis, 1972) that (1) promotes knowledge objecti<sup>fi</sup>cation through the revering of ‘best practices’ (i.e. ITIL) that originate as rhetoric from an ITSM power elite and implementation of of<sup>fi</sup>cially sanctioned (i.e. ITIL-compliant) ICT tools; (2) readily dismisses alternative perspectives to the prevailing optimism about ICT-based knowledge sharing; and (3) fails to adequately acknowledge dysfunctional con<sup>fl</sup>ict resulting from worker practices that implicitly reject that optimism. In this manner, the community of IT service managers serves the ITSM power elite who, it might be reasoned, typically have vested <sup>fi</sup>nancial interests in perpetuating the management pursuit of knowledge objecti<sup>fi</sup>cation.

The subtle manner in which alternative approaches to knowledge sharing are dismissed is apparent within ITIL’s rhetorically implicit accusation that workers failing to codify their knowledge are acting against organisational interests (Taylor et al., 2007d). Such rhetoric relies upon a belief that workers, acting out of self-interested rationality (Benkler, 2011), ‘hoard’ knowledge (e.g. Davenport & Prusak, 1998; Beaumont & Hunter, 2002; Lam, 2005). Certainly, the managerial instinct to capture and control knowledge can appear to be at odds with a worker’s instinct to enhance their labour market credentials by protecting their skills/knowledge and seizing opportunities to develop them through discovery (Crouch, 2006). Given that sharing information/ knowledge is often in practice a discretionary activity, there is clearly potential for conscious passive resistance to the managerial will from cynical. demotivated and/or competitive-minded workers with concerns about job security (Beaumont & Hunter, 2002; Lam, 2005; Selander & Henfridsson, 2012). However, the data from this study dispute the argument that workers are predisposed to hoard knowledge and instead support Dixon’s (2000, p. 7) position that people are naturally inclined to share what they know but are disinclined to do ‘a very different thing [and] . . . write something to send into a database’. The observed workers freely shared their knowledge but did not readily use the management-imposed tool for doing so. In both their talk and practice, they indicated that the complex, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge they learned through practice did not readily lend itself to language, let alone written language. As Hart (1992, pp. 18–19) noted, ‘the inability to explain high-level problem-solving activity without ambiguity is a common characteristic of experts.’ Because knowledge is innately ‘sticky’, necessitating different degrees of effort to transfer it across social contexts (Szulanski, 1996), there is a good enough reason why workers are disinclined to provide knowledge articles, and why ‘tacit knowledge has resisted operationalization’ (Ambrosini & Bowman, 2001, p. 811) without making accusations of ‘hoarding’.

Alongside this fundamental problem with codifying expert knowledge, the data suggest that IT service support workers tend to lack engagement with specialised ITSM tools beyond what the functionality makes mandatory. The reasons for this are complex and cultural (Lam, 2005), but ultimately individual and multifarious. The data support other studies that point to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of human agency within time-pressured environments for deciding what knowledge is shared, with whom and how (Gray & Durcikova, 2006; King & Marks, 2008). The data also point to IS workers recognising the career advantages of engaging with their work as selfreliant, free-spirited, curious, meaning-seeking, expertise-developing, connected and expressive craftsmen/women (Scarbrough, 1993; Snowden, 1999; Gratton, 2000; Sennett, 2009; Harper, 2010). By not engaging with the knowledge-sharing tool, they retain rights that they would otherwise cede to management. These include the rights to (1) share on the basis of reciprocation and obligation (Konstantinou & Fincham, 2010) and/or reputation (Ensign & Hébert, 2010); (2) choose from available information/knowledge sources on the basis of perceptions of quality and accessibility (O’Reilly, 1982); and (3) select a knowledge-sharing method that seems personally appropriate at that time (e.g. informal discussion, telephone dialogue and email) (Harper, 2010; Davison et al., 2013).

## CONCLUDING REMARKS

Through an analysis of the discourses and practices of separate managerial and worker communities, this study has provided fresh insight into why ICT-based knowledge sharing is problematic, reporting on a dissonance between managerial and worker discourse perspectives. In this concluding section, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y summarise the paper’s theoretical contributions before turning our attention to practical implications for ITSM and suggesting avenues for future research.

In responding to the call from academic and practitioner communities for empirically based studies of ITSM, this study has provided a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant new set of insights into the nature of ITSM practice and discourse. Starting with Green’s (2004) theorising that management practices become institutionalised through the widespread diffusion of rhetoric, we contribute to theory by illustrating that worker practices can become institutionalised in a similar way through the diffusion of a discernible discourse that becomes inscribed within those everyday practices. We also show that such discursive processes within the same institution may con<sup>fl</sup>ict and result in incompatible practices, i.e. what is taken for granted as ‘common sense’ by practising managers may con<sup>fl</sup>ict with what is taken fo granted as ‘common sense’ by practising workers. Further, although a ‘common-sense’ practice (i.e. here, promoting knowledge objecti<sup>fi</sup>cation) may gain hegemony within a managerial community through a diffusion process, this hegemony may not necessarily extend to the broader institutional community and is thus unstable (Gramsci, 1971).

By highlighting this dysfunctional con<sup>fl</sup>ict between incompatible management and worker practices, we have shed light on an important organisational issue, which if addressed might improve organisational performance. Consequently, the study’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings have speci<sup>fi</sup>c implications for ITSM practice. Firstly, IT service managers appraising suitable tools to support IT service support work should adopt a healthy scepticism towards the managerial rhetoric, taking into consideration this theorised institutional con<sup>fl</sup>ict and modifying their pre-implementation expectations accordingly. In this way, they might avoid the emergence of a gap between those expectations and a disappointing post-implementation reality (Dawson, 1996; Doherty & Lockett, 2008). This ‘expectations gap’ also has implications for the suppliers of specialised tools who risk damaging their commercial reputation by overselling bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Secondly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the managerial discourse – in depersonalising workers and implying that they act out of self, rather than organisational, interest – fails to acknowledge the full organisational value of workers’ interpersonal communication capabilities and insights into process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. As a contribution to ITSM discourse, we suggest that managers involve workers more in (1) the evolutionary social construction of collaborative and cooperative processes and practices and (2) (re)designing a hybrid knowledge-sharing strategy that acknowledges the importance of the interplay between personalisation and codi<sup>fi</sup>cation elements (Jasimuddin et al., 2012).

Additional research is needed to establish whether our theory of con<sup>fl</sup>icting discourses is helpful in understanding other areas of organisational life where there is an apparent, although possibly suppressed, con<sup>fl</sup>ict between management and worker practices. The study also invites further practice-based research into how effective knowledge-sharing practices evolve through a combination of management and worker decision-making. In this study, we found that IT service managers operate in a state of tension between empowering workers and also expecting them to use the knowledge-sharing tools they impose upon them. Further research would be useful on how this tension is dealt with in different organisations.

## Biographies

Clive Trusson is a Teaching Fellow at the School of Business and Economics, Loughborough University. He has worked extensively as an Information Systems Practitioner and is quali<sup>fi</sup>ed in IT Service Management to ITIL v.3 ‘Expert’ level. His doctoral research aims to conceptualise the IT service support worker, and his other research interests include knowledge sharing, knowledge work and the HRM aspect of information systems work

Professor Neil E. Doherty currently holds the Chair in Information Management in the School of Business and Economics, at Loughborough University. In addition to knowledge sharing, his research interests include the following: bene<sup>fi</sup>ts realisation management, IT for improved competitive positioning, information security management, and the impact and uptake of electronic commerce, He has had papers published in a range of academic journals, including European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, IEEE Transactions in Engineering Management, Journal of Business Research, European Journal of Marketing, Journal of Marketing Management, Information Systems Journal and Information & Management. He is currently serving as a Senior Editor for the Information Systems Journal and as an Associate Editor for Information Technology and People and the International Journal of Electronic Business Research.

Dr Donald Hislop is a Reader at the School of Business and Economics, at Loughborough University, where he is also the Deputy Director of the Centre for Professional Work and Society. His research interests are broadly in the areas of the socio-cultural aspects of knowledge management and mobile working. He has published articles in a wide range of management and technology journals, including: Management Learning, Journal of Information Technology, Technology Analysis & Strategic Management and the Journal of Knowledge Management. He is also the author of a textbook Knowledge Management in Organizations, (Oxford University Press), and he also edited the book Mobility and Technology in the Workplace (Routledge).
