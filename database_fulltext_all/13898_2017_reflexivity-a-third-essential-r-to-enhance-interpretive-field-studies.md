# Re<sup>fl</sup>exivity: A third essential ‘R’ to enhance interpretive <sup>fi</sup>eld studies

Julien Malaurent, David Avison

ESSEC Business School, Avenue Bernard Hirsch, Cergy-Pontoise, 95021, France

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 26 October 2015 Received in revised form 14 October 2016 Accepted 24 January 2017 Available online xxx

Keywords: Self-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity Domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity Collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity Interpretive research Case study

## A B S T R A C T

In this paper, we argue that re<sup>fl</sup>exivity should be as essential a component of interpretive studies as the much vaunted rigour and relevance. We propose a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of three forms of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity for interpretive studies: self-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity and collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. We use a case study of a project from a French public organization supporting organic farming to illustrate re<sup>fl</sup>exivity in action, highlighting its positive contribution. We suggest quality criteria to enhance re<sup>fl</sup>exive studies and show how these can be incorporated into the well-known Klein and Myers’ principles for interpretive <sup>fi</sup>eld studies in information systems.

## 1. Motivation

There has been much debate in the literature regarding the necessity for information systems (IS) research to be both rigorous and relevant—the two ‘R’s. This was particularly apparent in the late 1990s, although it is an ongoing discussion. Thus, Markus ([1] p.18) demands ‘rigorous research that describes and evaluates what is going on in practice’ to add to theoretical research. In an MIS Quarterly editorial, Lee ([2]p.viii) asked for papers ‘imbuing rigorous research with the element of relevance to managers, consultants, and other practitioners’, a call repeated in the later MIS Quarterly editorial by Straub & Ang [3]. The title of Applegate & King’s [4] editorial—‘Rigour and Relevance: Careers on the Line’— could not be clearer. There are also excellent discussions in the edited text of readings of King and Lyytinen [5] as a whole on this important debate. The research-practice gap has also been discussed recently by Mathiassen & Sandberg [6].

In this paper, we neither wish to add to this particular body of literature nor are suggesting that IS research has achieved its goals in these two requirements. Most recently, for example, Sarker et al. [7] bemoan the lack of rigour in published qualitative research. However, we agree with the general tenure of this debate—that researchers need to be both rigorous and relevant in interpretive research (which is our context)—and we draw the attention of readers to the above references (and their bibliographies). However, here we argue that there should be a third essential ‘R’, that of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. Research might be both rigorous and relevant as de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the above-mentioned works, but we suggest that without re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, we have only a limited basis on which to assess the value of interpretive studies.

Calas and Smircich [72 p.240] de<sup>fi</sup>ne re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as the ‘constant assessment of the relationship between “knowledge” and “the ways of doing knowledge”’. Alvesson & Sköldberg [8] de<sup>fi</sup>ne re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as ‘the interpretation of interpretation’ and suggest that re<sup>fl</sup>exivity is ‘the attention paid to the way different kinds of linguistic, social, political, and theoretical elements are woven together in the process of knowledge development, during which empirical material is constructed, interpreted and written’ (p.9).

In doing interpretive research, the focus of this article, we recognize the social constructions of knowledge development [9]. As Orlikowski & Baroudi [10] argued, understanding social process involves getting inside the world of those generating it, and we can never assume the interpretive researcher has a value-neutral stance. Thus, we argue that social constructions should not remain opaque to the readers. The interpretation process needs to be transparent for readers to evaluate the researcher’s process and conclusions.

Yet Sarker et al. [7] found that only 5% of published studies they reviewed in four leading IS journals between 2001 and 2012 offered any accounts about the interim struggles faced by researchers when interpreting data. According to Steedman ([11] p.53), ‘knowledge cannot be separated from the knower’, and thus, in the other 95% of the papers, we may perhaps learn only half the story, having little, if any, evidence of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity.

## 1.3. Awareness of the political–ideological character of research

This requires recognizing that the interpretations and theoretical assumptions on which both the research design is based and the research process carried out are not neutral but are impacted by political and ideological assumptions. This relates to the previous point that intends to open the black box of the researchers’ cognition for re<sup>fl</sup>ecting publicly on the way of seeing the world in which he or she is embedded.

## 1.4. Reflection in relation to the problem of representation and authority

The researcher in effect claims authority on the design and writing processes, whereas the text ‘claims’ to represent some extrinsic reality. Alvesson and Sköldberg argued that both need to be called into question. This relates to the plurality of interpretations. A researcher aims to represent extrinsic reality through text, as it is the main medium for academics [13], but this should not prevent readers to build their own interpretations of the case using the evidence provided by the researcher based on their own assumptions and mindset [14].

The strength of the above framework for re<sup>fl</sup>exivity lies in its composite nature. An alternative typology is provided by De Vaujany [15] following Archer’s framework [16].

Thus, the reader is not able to assess whether the interpretations of the researcher are potentially valid if there are no re<sup>fl</sup>ections in the text on why the researchers interpreted the data in the way that they did, with some indication of their political and ideological assumptions and other evidence about the researcher’s motivations and so on. Some of these requirements are re<sup>fl</sup>ected individually in research approaches used in IS. In the following, the <sup>fi</sup>rst reference relates to an early non-IS source and the second one is an IS application of the approach. Thus, grounded theory [17,18] highlights the importance of contact with the empirical material; action research includes re<sup>fl</sup>ection as one phase in the <sup>fi</sup>ve-phase action research cycle [19,20]; hermeneutics [21,22] highlights the awareness of the interpretive act; critical theory [23,24] suggests the clari<sup>fi</sup>cation of political–ideological contexts; and postmodernism [25,26] examines the question of representation and authority.

Calling for more re<sup>fl</sup>exivity in management research is not new. According to Weick [27], the ‘re<sup>fl</sup>exive turn’ of management scholars can be traced back to the work of Burrell & Morgan [28]. They highlighted the role played by meta-theoretical underlying assumptions in the construction of a research project and suggested questioning researchers’ interpretations to engage re<sup>fl</sup>exive discussion on the choices made by researchers during the whole research process. The importance of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity is expressed forcefully by Flood [29] who argued that, ‘without some degree of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity any research is blind and without purpose’. This enthusiasm for re<sup>fl</sup>exivity is also a leitmotiv of mode 2 management research suggested by Gibbons [30].

Although the terms ‘re<sup>fl</sup>ective’ and ‘re<sup>fl</sup>exive’ are used together (and sometimes indifferently) in the literature, we suggest they are different. For that, we refer to Hibbert et al. [73] who wrote, ‘re<sup>fl</sup>ection suggests a mirror image which affords the opportunity to engage in an observation or examination of our ways of doing. When we experience re<sup>fl</sup>ection we become observers of our own practice. Re<sup>fl</sup>exivity however, suggests a complexi<sup>fi</sup>cation of thinking and experience, or thinking about experience. Thus, we regard re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as a process of exposing or questioning our ways of doing. As such re<sup>fl</sup>exivity is related to re<sup>fl</sup>ection yet is qualitatively different from it ( . . . ) re<sup>fl</sup>exivity is more than re<sup>fl</sup>ection. What is implied is that, through questioning the bases of our interpretations, re<sup>fl</sup>exivity necessarily brings about change in the process of re<sup>fl</sup>ection – it is thereby recursive’ (p.48). To summarize, they suggest considering re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as a process of re<sup>fl</sup>ection and recursivity. Interestingly, we found in the literature that scholars progressively switched from using the term ‘re<sup>fl</sup>ective’ toward ‘re<sup>fl</sup>exive’ more widely from the early 2000s. However, this change in terminology does not necessarily mean that all kinds of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity discussed elsewhere now includes a recursive process, as suggested by Hibbert et al. [71]. In our article, we invariably use the term re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, although we acknowledge that part of the literature that we discuss (e.g. Schön [31] when he discusses the re<sup>fl</sup>ective practitioner) refers to re<sup>fl</sup>ection without implying this is a recursive process.

In this article, we intend to provide a systematic look at re<sup>fl</sup>exivity and discuss three forms that are not mutually exclusive: self-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity and collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. For each, we discuss how it might be achieved by researchers and practitioners. We then illustrate the approach through a case study. Finally, we suggest formalizing the re<sup>fl</sup>exive process and show how these guidelines can be incorporated into the Klein & Myers’ [32] principles and thus embed re<sup>fl</sup>exivity into interpretive research in IS.

## 2. Three forms of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity

## 2.1. Self-reflexivity

A re<sup>fl</sup>exive researcher re<sup>fl</sup>ects on his or her own embedded in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the way research is carried out and understands how the process of doing research shapes its outcomes [33]. In the IS discipline, re<sup>fl</sup>exivity has been particularly marked in some ethnographic accounts. For example, throughout a self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive account of her own ethnographic project studying knowledge workers, Schultze [34] discussed the in-depth characteristics of her immersion in the <sup>fi</sup>eld through descriptions of everyday life as lived by members of the <sup>fi</sup>eld, what members think about their lives in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, who the ethnographer talked to and observed, the nature of the researcher’s relationship with various categories of people in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, the response of others on the scene to the researcher’s presence, the researcher’s pre-understanding of the studied scene, the researcher’s interest in the scene, the researcher’s length of stay, and the relationship between the <sup>fi</sup>eld notes and the written-up research article. Vernacular details of her ethnography project detailing its authenticity can be found in the original text ([34] p.32). Her account can be seen as an ‘autobiographical exposition to draw readers into the text so that the assumptions and practices of the foreign culture serve as a mirror in which the reader’s own assumptions and practices are re<sup>fl</sup>ected’ (p.4).

From the self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive researcher, we now turn to the selfre<sup>fl</sup>exive practitioner. Schön [31] suggested that re<sup>fl</sup>exivity is a part of some practitioners’ daily activities. He wrote, ‘When a practitioner re<sup>fl</sup>ects in and on his practice, the possible objects of his re<sup>fl</sup>ection are as varied as the kinds of phenomena before him and the systems of knowing-in-practice that he brings to them. He may re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the tacit norms and appreciations that underlie a judgment, or on the strategies and theories implicit in a pattern of behaviour. He may re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the feeling for a situation that has led him to adopt a particular course of action, on the way in which he has framed the problem he is trying to solve, or on the role he has constructed for himself within a larger institutional context’ (p.62). He suggested a design approach to discuss how practitioners re<sup>fl</sup>ect in action by naming, framing, moving and evaluating the problems they are facing in their daily activities. The work of Mathiassen [35] in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld entitled ‘Re<sup>fl</sup>ective Systems Development’ acknowledged ‘the relation to Schön’s ideas on how professionals think in action’ (p.68). Later, he argued that when practioners are stuck, they re<sup>fl</sup>ect on their actions and ‘might <sup>fi</sup>nd completely new ways to frame the situation and impose these on the situation to see different problems and opportunities’ (p 86). If practitioners succeed in this, they might switch from a re<sup>fl</sup>ective to a re<sup>fl</sup>exive posture, given the distinction proposed by Hibbert et al. [71]. For example, a practitioner may re<sup>fl</sup>ect on practice that was particularly challenging or new. They may then refer to the practitioner journals to consider the experience of others, re<sup>fl</sup>ect further on what they did in practice and think about how they may adjust their practice in the future.

## 2.2. Domain reflexivity

Olsson et al. [36] suggested an alternative approach to that of a single researcher being self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive, that is more than one researcher collecting and/or interpreting data together. They also explained that for their interviews, while one researcher was asking the questions, the other was listening and taking notes so that a comparative interpretative discussion could take place between them after each interview. This makes it possible to compare notes and interpretations and be less vulnerable to the potential criticisms made of the self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive researcher criticising his own work.

From the practitioners’ side, Bolton [37] developed a methodology for professional development based on re<sup>fl</sup>exive writing. It is a process by which practitioners are invited to write and share their own professional issues and share these with a number of peers to consider possible endings. In a different vein, Schön [31] by examining <sup>fi</sup>ve professions (engineering, psychotherapy, architecture, management and town planning) demonstrated how activities, which could look like a kind of improvisation learned in practice, are in fact a result of collective re<sup>fl</sup>exive practices shared among peers. Studies of work that do not involve researchers actively participating are frequently referred to as practice studies, and some case studies could be included in this category (although researchers may ‘write up’ the case, unlike that espoused by Bolton above).

## 2.3. Collaborative reflexivity

Mårtensson & Lee [38] emphasized the need to recognize the existence of two distinct types of knowledge: (1) the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c expertise possessed by the academic researchers referred as ‘theoria’ and (2) the professional expertise possessed by the practitioners, referred as ‘praxia’. We need to be conscious that researchers and practitioners do not necessarily share the same mindset and rules for learning from experience. However, while performing an empirical research in real-world organizations, it is important to see both as equal in terms of status. As exemplar, Mårtensson & Lee [38], while discussing a dialogical action research project, demonstrated how a re<sup>fl</sup>exive dialogue between the two forms of knowledge helped promote mutual improvements and anchor the relevance of the empirical research. Action research is the most well-known approach in IS where the researchers and practitioners work together collaboratively [39].

Finlay [40] suggested giving our audiences the tools to criticize our accounts. Here he refers to the requirement to provide all the evidence, not simply the data collected, to assess whether our interpretations are valid. Olsson et al. [36] showed that to ensure the validity of the research project, the research team presented the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to participants and engaged in discussions to con<sup>fi</sup>rm, clarify or refute the results, and this led to some major adjustments. Heron [41] and Barry et al. [42] invited researchers to expose their underlying assumptions with peers and researched people to make use of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as a co-operative inquiry, whereas Alvesson & Sköldberg [8] discussed how we might achieve different perspectives on research by viewing the problem from different epistemological positions and suggested the production of knowledge not just from the privileged authoritative few but as an outcome of public debates and agreements. In these ‘participatory research’ or ‘social critique’ approaches, researchers and practitioners are both involved in determining the research agenda, and new interests and objectives currently excluded might be pursued [43,44].

We schematize the three main forms of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity in Fig. 1. There is also a ‘null type’ where neither practitioners nor researchers are re<sup>fl</sup>exive. As suggested above, this lack of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity makes improvement in the present project less likely. Similarly, progress in research and practitioner knowledge is likely to be slower and less well understood. We distinguish ‘selfre<sup>fl</sup>exivity’ and ‘domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity’ of researchers to those of practitioners because they may not use the same techniques and methods and their intentions might be very different. For example, researchers may use re<sup>fl</sup>exivity to analyse their interpretive research process, whereas practitioners may use re<sup>fl</sup>exivity to be more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective in their work. The only form of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity that involves both categories working together is ‘collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity’, but that does not imply that they are pursuing the same objectives while doing it.

## 3. Melibio case study

## 3.1. Introduction to the case

A French public organization, Pole Bio, created a multi-tenant project, Melibio, to support organic agriculture in the Central Massif region of France. Pole Bio managed this project for a 3-year period (2011–2014) to improve knowledge sharing in organic farming. The participants were speci<sup>fi</sup>cally interested in meadows composed of a variety of <sup>fl</sup>ora or forage crops. The project brought together a group of heterogeneous actors: researchers in biology, computer scientists, chamber of agriculture of<sup>fi</sup>cials, water supply public agencies, college teachers, agricultural experts and farmers’ associations. The project was sponsored by 17 different French public organizations. This complex situation features many actors and organizations, and a changing political and cultural environment that is not untypical of IS work. The funders were interested in this project for the support and the solutions it can provide to farmers facing climate change.

Melibio’s project had two main objectives: the <sup>fi</sup>rst one consisted of producing a decision-making model to assist <sup>fl</sup>ora seeding. This decision-making model would be embedded within an online platform to assist farmers to calculate the ideal mix for seeding <sup>fl</sup>ora in meadows. The ideal-type process is the following: farmers will enter local data into the software (such as location, soil type and weather conditions), and the system will return seeding recipes given the speci<sup>fi</sup>cities of each meadow. The second objective was the creation of a wiki-based knowledge platform to articulate both ‘scienti<sup>fi</sup>c’ and ‘practitioner’ knowledge to improve the collective expertise of the organic farming community in that region. To ful<sup>fi</sup>l these objectives, all the different actors listed above needed to collaborate to come up with a collective decision on the design of future information systems.

The project manager of Melibio, Myriam, contacted Philippe early in 2011 to join the Melibio project because the team was looking for an academic researcher, specializing in both public organizations and IS. Philippe invited Julien (the <sup>fi</sup>rst author of this paper) to join him in mid-2011. Since then, Julien has been involved in the project, attending meetings (both actual and virtual) and participating in telephone calls and in email exchanges. The second author, David, did not participate in the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork investigation but focused his attention on the re<sup>fl</sup>exive layer of the project by providing an external analysis and understanding of the behaviours and re<sup>fl</sup>ections of Julien, Philippe and the practitioners, supported by interviews and an analysis of the material. The position of David here is not dissimilar to that of Lars in the following: ‘Lars, as outsider, added re<sup>fl</sup>ective and analytical capabilities independent of the culture and traditions at Ericsson ([6]p.478).

To understand how re<sup>fl</sup>exivity emerged and took place in the Melibio project, David interviewed Julien, Myriam and Philippe. These recorded interviews lasted between 45 and 60 min. This was followed up by several less formal discussions. There are also audio recordings of interviews of 12 participants (out of 20) of the group by Julien and video recordings of the group meetings. All these recordings are available on request (for academic purposes only and following privacy and other agreements), but note that all the materials, with the exception of the interviews with the two researchers, are in French.

We now look at citations from the interviews that took place, which give exemplars of the three forms of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity that emerged progressively. However, <sup>fi</sup>rst we look at perceptions of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity by the actors involved.

## 3.2. Perceptions of reflexivity

Initially, Julien and Philippe used the metaphor of the mirror to describe re<sup>fl</sup>exivity:

It is about mirroring your own research processes: How did you do it? Why did you do it? In what conditions was it done? . . . It is a two-step process. Reflect on what happened in the field but also justify the choices made in terms of data analysis and theoretical developments. You are aiming at being transparent on your personal reflections and assumptions while collecting, interpreting and writing [Julien, researcher] and It is mirroring myself: a tool that enables me to distance myself from the matter I am working on [Philippe, researcher].

The use of a mirror as a metaphor [37] is an obvious one, and as we saw above, Schultze ([34]p.4) used it to describe her process, for re<sup>fl</sup>exivity is about the re<sup>fl</sup>ection of you as an actor in trying to make sense of what happened in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Although they are both interpretive researchers, by ‘distancing himself’, Philippe seems to be using the mirror as a way of being to some extent ‘objective’ in his appraisal of what he did, although it is more of a subjective and interpretive process as noted by Julien. Some of the practitioners interviewed also took up the mirror metaphor, for example,

This is a mirror to see how we work and try to see what works and what does not work. Then we can make an improvement process: it is a type of quality management approach. [Lina, Representative of a vetinary association]

Interestingly, Myriam, the project leader, did not like the mirro as a metaphor for being re<sup>fl</sup>exive:

When you use the mirror metaphor, I thought you meant that you were looking at yourselves as researchers through a mirror, so I did not get your point at first . . . As the group’s project is always coconstructing with you and us, I do not think this metaphor is appropriate . . . the metaphor of ping pong is more appropriate because it relates to the dynamicity of co-constructing together . . . or maybe it is more like we are all playing a game together but there are no opponents; we are all on the same side [Myriam, project leader].

Myriam’s ping-pong metaphor suggests better the ‘complexi<sup>fi</sup>cation of thinking and experience’ and the ‘recursive process’, i.e. re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, whereas the mirror is clearly an appropriate metaphor for re<sup>fl</sup>ectivity.

The two researchers saw re<sup>fl</sup>exivity positively and wished to put these ideas into practice in the Melibio project. Further, the project leader was also enthusiastic about re<sup>fl</sup>exivity having encouraged the researchers to be a kind of catalyst for what she called this ‘reflexive turn’. She saw that it might help the coconstruction of an information system that had the support of the very varied participants that might otherwise see themselves as opponents. She also thought that otherwise the process to reach a decision on the IS would be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult if not impossible by such a dispirited group with so many agendas. With re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, they would be on the ‘same side’, and the process would be easier and a good solution more likely to be achieved. Gaining external <sup>fi</sup>nancial support from research bodies to aid this re<sup>fl</sup>exive analysis also helped.

## 3.3. Self-reflexivity

When asked about why he was enthusiastic about re<sup>fl</sup>exive research, one of the researchers responded,

One way of showing the rigorous aspect of your research is to provide the audience with the material to analyse your interpretive research processes . . . in this way, readers can better assess whether your findings are reasonable . . . Providing details about the number of interviews conducted in the field and the number of days spent on site is not robust enough to be qualified as rigorous. As an interpretive researcher, you need to be able to discuss your own state of mind while in the field, your personal expectations and so on . . . When you look at the best ethnographic papers, the richness of the research does not lie in the number of interviews or days spent on-site but comes from the additional personal reflections of the researcher, analysing his or her own interpretive process. I was convinced by that argument and therefore wanted to practice this thinking in the Melibio project. [Julien, researcher]

Julien argues that it is not the numbers of interviews (to give one example) that are important but rather the use made of the data from interviews reinforced by the re<sup>fl</sup>exive process.

In the personal notes of Julien, while attending a group meeting, we can capture some of his concerns and discoveries about the group’s interactions. A typical re<sup>fl</sup>exive note concerns one participant getting upset at a meeting and Julien asks himself why:

I see conflicts between experts (represented here by Pierre) and developers. Difficulties include the control variables for the (seeding) tests . . . and Pierre becomes irritated quickly. Is it pressure? Is it a character trait? Overall, I think Pierre still wants to keep his knowledge to himself and to control the debate. It is not helpful. What’s the reason? How can we steer him towards a more positive direction?

What this evidences is Julien asking himself questions about what he sees. There are different interpretations for this negative behaviour of the expert, and Julien may decide to look for other evidence to understand this behaviour in a recursive process. In that way, it may be possible to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways in which this colleague makes a more positive contribution to the discussion in the future. Con<sup>fl</sup>ict should not be ignored but explicitly included as part of the process. With such a non-homogeneous group, these problems need to be raised and resolved to increase the likelihood that a generally agreed information system can be designed.

Interviews with individual practitioners also revealed a number of examples of them being self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive, for example,

[In the survey I carried out] I’m a little disappointed about its value considering the effort I put into it [Florence, Engineer]; There is a big job analysis, and I do not have the skills necessary . . . Maybe this model is more suitable for a counsellor or a breeder [Patrick, Government-funded agricultural researcher]; An expert system? This is probably what we wanted to do at first, but it will not work for prescribing [Paul, Agricultural engineer]; I’m more comfortable on the project management and methodology compared to the valuation dimension [Florence, Engineer]; and There are some aspects on which I participate more than others . . . [but sometimes] I do have difficulties in getting into it [Jean-Michel, Agriculture counsellor].

These examples do not show defensive positions but openness about dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, disappointments, weaknesses and strengths. Such re<sup>fl</sup>exivity gives the potential for participants to contribute most. Florence’s re<sup>fl</sup>ection may lead to a better-designed survey or data collection using a different approach (or doing something different next time), Patrick’s re<sup>fl</sup>ection may lead to someone more appropriate being approached to work on the model so that he can use his expertise more appropriately elsewhere, Paul’s observation may lead to prescribing being achieved through another information system and so on. Later, Julien and Philippe had the opportunity to convey these re<sup>fl</sup>ections to the practitioners in a joint meeting (in a way that the sources could not be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed individually).

## 3.4. Domain reflexivity

Philippe was <sup>fi</sup>rst invited by Myriam, but as he rapidly found that the group was expecting him to provide a researcher’s look on the project and contribute to its development, he decided that it would be useful for a second researcher to be involved for the development of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity at the project level and also for them to be re<sup>fl</sup>exive on their own interpretive research process:

To get an in-depth access to the Melibio project, we had to give something in return. They asked me to be active in the project and not be mere observer. And I thought that reflexivity could be a way to provide value to the participants. Being active in the project was for me incompatible with the idea of keeping a distance with the group to observe them, so that is why I asked Julien to join me in order for us to always get one researcher to observe the group that included the other researcher. And this is how this idea of reflexivity emerged progressively . . . The idea was not each of us alone being reflexive but being reflexive together, the two of us [Philippe, Researcher].

Thus, Philippe had discovered that self-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity was not enough, and more could be achieved by researchers together being re<sup>fl</sup>exive. However, in interviewing the researchers, it was evident that the boundary between self-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity and domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity was often unclear. In other words, the interviews to reveal selfre<sup>fl</sup>exivity sometimes led to a re<sup>fl</sup>exive discussion between them, i.e. domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, similar to that espoused by Olsson et al. [36]:

The way we built self-reflexivity with Philippe is interesting because it has always been led by a will to build mutual reflexivity between us. I was observing him, while he did the same for me. By questioning him about his own personal interpretations, I pushed him to reflect on his own behaviour and he did the same with me. But we then discussed this after the initial debriefing [Julien, Researcher].

The need for domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, as against self-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity or collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity alone, is perhaps best shown in the following:

The way the group of practitioners see reflexivity is different from how Philippe and I perceive it. They see it as a way to get feedback and improve themselves, while we see it as a way to open our interpretive research processes [Julien, researcher].

Both these objectives are important, and the above cite suggests that they need to be done by separate group types, in this case researchers and practitioners separately. The following gives an example of researchers’ domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity in the project:

When we were doing interviews with participants, one was asking questions and the other one was taking notes on what the interviewee said. But in the meantime, we were also sharing information about our feelings, about how to proceed further regarding the direction of the interview, about how we interpreted the answers of the interviewee, and we were also sharing information regarding the context . . . This enabled us to check if our understanding and feelings of the situation were the same or not . . . As we recorded every interaction and note between the two of us, this proved also to be a good way to reflect on this material later on [Philippe, Researcher].

Thus whilst interviewing a practitioner, the researchers were re<sup>fl</sup>ecting together on what was said and giving hints on how the interview should proceed by passing notes to each other. When the interview <sup>fi</sup>nished, they would debrief and discuss ways of approaching the interviewees and topics for the interviews that followed.

Nevertheless, Julien sees differences in their roles:

An important difference distinguishing my role with that of Philippe occurred because he entered the project 6 months earlier than me and already knew the project leader some years before that. By the time I joined, he was already integrated and engaged some specific actions with the group. Thus, I am seen much more of an outsider by the group, if not by Philippe.

Interviews with individual practitioners also revealed a number of examples of them being domain-re<sup>fl</sup>exive, for example,

Some people have an enormous weight (influence). This is related to their own qualities but also to the institutions they embody and legitimize [Anna, Chamber Of<sup>fi</sup>cial] and We need everyone involved not only in the part which is most relevant (for him or her) but the work of others as well. [Sandrine, Project manager]

Thus, practitioners could also see the value of practicing domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. The above cite suggests that one way to achieve a more democratic process might be by discussing procedural issues together so as to distribute power more evenly within the group and ideally then involve all the practitioners in all the decisions.

## 3.5. Collaborative reflexivity

In the following, the researchers show how both they and the practitioners could collaborate re<sup>fl</sup>exively together and its potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts:

We are some kind of catalyst for the group in terms of reflexivity . . . We asked the group to provide ideas, tools, methods to reflect on their practices and on the project to help us to collaborate on the design [of the IS] . . . The participants perceive us as experts in information systems, so they asked us to provide a tool, a platform, for asynchronous communication. So the demand from the group is on reflexivity but also for a tool to offer a collaborative virtual space for the group to share and exchange information and documents between meetings. . . . Proposing this reflexive approach to the group could be useful for them in other projects. The reflexive stance is a way for people with different backgrounds to work together by understanding each other. This collaborative reflexive dimension of the project is extremely relevant for global projects dealing with global issues and involving global actors since this emphasis on reflexivity can help people from different backgrounds to understand each other better [Philippe, Researcher].

The use of the term ‘catalyst’ makes clear that the researchers’ roles and those of the practitioners are different when collaborating re<sup>fl</sup>exively. The researchers offer re<sup>fl</sup>exions and comments, whereas the practitioners need to consider the issues in depth and make decisions based on this re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. The practitioners needed this external support of the researchers to help establish a smooth process and also to design a software platform to support re<sup>fl</sup>exive discussions between meetings. Many of the sentiments of Philippe were expressed by the project leader Myriam in her interview. It is clear that other practitioners also saw the need for the input from researchers:

To make progress within such a heterogeneous group . . . it is very important to get a facilitator to help ensure that everyone is moving in the same direction at the same time [Myriam, Project leader].

A particularly important contribution of the researchers as facilitators was to reveal gaps in knowledge and experience within the practitioner group. Their questioning at meetings inspired the following comments:

We should find a way to involve farmers more widely. They are the beneficiaries of the project, and the concern is to get them involved. We also must be vigilant to give them feedback at key moments [Jean-Michel, Agriculture counsellor]; The risk is that the project has several components, several sensitivities that should be taken account of [Sandrine, High school project manager]; The balance between research and development is not great and it could be better with leaders and employees in a focussed group [Stephane, Chamber of<sup>fi</sup>cial]; and We need more knowledge from the field [Patrick, Government-funded agricultural researcher].

As Mathiassen [35] argued, such situations ‘involve uncertainty, instability, uniqueness, and value-con<sup>fl</sup>ict and they require an ability to go beyond the relatively safe territory of general professional knowledge’ (p.68). The atmosphere provoked by the agreed need for re<sup>fl</sup>exivity exposed such gaps that possibly would not be revealed early enough in a more conventional project, if at all. Discussion between the different practitioners and the researchers can help reveal and resolve such situations. Alternatively, being detected and exposed by a manager in a less collaborative atmosphere may lead to individual dissatisfactions and/or divisions in the group if the manager makes decisions without such full consultation.

Mathiassen [35] differentiated his approach from the cooperative approach of Greenbaum & Kyng [45]. Mathiassen’s approach provides collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity between researchers and IT professionals, whereas Greenbaum and Kyng’s approach is based on co-operation between researchers and users. However, we can only see bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in including researchers, IT professionals and users together in collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity.

## 3.6. Discussion on the whole process

While being interviewed on the re<sup>fl</sup>exive framework that they developed with the group, Philippe acknowledged that this path from self to collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity emerged owing to the group’s interaction:

When I joined the project, I did not have any idea that the project could lead us towards such a posture. It is the interactions with the participants that make this idea of group and collaborative reflexivity emerge. It is the result of a long-term process.

It seems that this three-type schema for re<sup>fl</sup>exivity came in reaction to a ‘need’ from the group because of its complexity and diversity:

In a way, the different forms of reflexivity that we have built with the group seem to help to handle the complexity of the project involving very different participants. Participants, especially the project manager, want to capitalize on this experience. For them, we have provided a process to do reflexivity: individually, togethe with other practitioners and together with ‘outside’ researchers [ Philippe, Researcher].

Most practitioner comments suggested that the process was helpful, for example,

Reflexivity for me is a way to see and understand what happens at regular intervals [Sandrine, Chamber of<sup>fi</sup>cial]; This reflexivity dimension can help strengthen group cohesion [Patrick, Government-funded agricultural researcher]; This is a kind of mid-term evaluation, which can be very enlightening for group dynamics thereafter [Florence, Engineer]; This is a useful way for the group to question what we are doing [Sandrine, Chamber of<sup>fi</sup>cial]; This is to debate things that spontaneously we would not put at stake, and it is a way for the project to gain value [Camille, Documentalist]; It gives material to discuss the situation, to switch from a co-operative project where everyone has his own spot to truly collaborative work [Patrick]; and I also see reflexivity as a way for the group to self-control the project . . . It is a crucial insight to enhance the participative dimension that we need to maintain in the project [Myriam, Project manager].

However, some problems with the process were also revealed in re<sup>fl</sup>exive comments:

It’s hard to start. We do not make much progress when we meet [Charles, IT engineer]; At some point, decisions need to be taken, and it seems that there is no progress [Paul, Agriculture engineer]; Some people have an enormous [too much?] influence. This is related to their own qualities but also to the institutions they embody and legitimize [Anna, Chamber of<sup>fi</sup>cial]; and we need everyone to be involved [all the time] not only in the part that is most relevant (for him or her) [Sandrine, High school project manager].

There can indeed be dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in making decisions where different views are raised, which is apparent in a heterogeneous group as in Melibio, and in getting some people to participate where they feel they have more powerful colleagues expressing their views. The researchers took steps to try to resolve these problems with the group when revealed as these negative comments must be considered. In the Melibio case, the role of the two researchers as ‘catalyst’ and, to some extent, mediator would seem to be crucial. There is also a risk that the re<sup>fl</sup>exive element will add to overall project time, and it can be a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult approach to understand, appreciate and participate fully in if the subjects are used to a hierarchical and controlled environment.

As discussed in the case above, the group participants largely adopted the suggestions initiated by Philippe and Julien to develop re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. We discuss in the next section how to formalize these different forms of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity (that appeared here empirically) in a coherent framework.

## 4. Formalizing re<sup>fl</sup>exivity

4.1. Quality criteria to develop and evaluate reflexivity in interpretive studies

Having argued for interpretive research to exhibit a re<sup>fl</sup>exive turn and provided an example of its potential, we now suggest criteria, extracted from the IS and management literature, to help conduct and evaluate such studies. Following Stahl [46], we use the term ‘quality criteria’. We propose these criteria and also show how they may address the Klein & Myers’ [32] principles for interpretive research. As with that work, we focus here mainly on the researchers’ perspective.

We use the seven Klein & Myers’ [32] principles (and the additional re<sup>fl</sup>exivity dimension) as a framework for ensuring that our approach provides good guidelines for re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, rigour and relevance. We <sup>fi</sup>rst give a brief overview of the seven principles of Klein & Myers ([32]p.72). The fundamental principle of the ‘hermeneutic cycle’ suggests that all human understanding is achieved by iterating between considering the interdependent meaning of parts and the whole that they form. The principle of ‘contextualization’ suggests a critical reflection of the social and historical background of the research setting. The principle of ‘interaction between the researchers and the subjects requires a critical reflection on how the research materials (or data) were socially constructed through the interaction between the researchers and participants. The principle of ‘abstraction and generalization requires relating the idiographic details revealed by the data interpretation ( . . . ) to general concepts that describe the nature of human understanding and social action. The principle of ‘dialogical reasoning’ requires sensitivity to possible contradictions between the theoretical preconceptions guiding the research design and actual findings with subsequent cycles of revision. The principle of ‘multiple interpretations’ requires sensitivity to possible differences in interpretations among the participants as are typically expressed in multiple narratives or stories of the same sequence of events under study. Finally, the principle of ‘suspicion’ requires sensitivity to possible biases and systematic distortions in the narratives collected from the participants.

Although the Klein and Myers’ principles are not expected to be mechanistically applied in each situation, Trauth & Jessup [47] and Davidson [48], amongst others, are researchers who demonstrated the relevance of these principles to validate their own interpretive research. Other researchers have drawn complementary criteria. For example, Gopal & Prasad [49] proposed a set of criteria for evaluating symbolic interactionist research, whereas IS ethnographic works [34,47,50] use the Golden-Biddle & Locke [51] criteria, which are authenticity, plausibility and criticality, to complement Klein and Myers.

In our case, however, we did not wish to add or amend the principles suggested by Klein and Myers; rather our objective is to demonstrate that a re<sup>fl</sup>exive posture could help address them. While describing the purpose of each of our criteria, we also discuss its origins and provide an example from the Melibio case study and also from the literature more generally. It is also a way for us to provide additional information about the case study to show how this research project has been carried out, the positions of the researchers, how data were collected and interpreted, and how participants and researchers re<sup>fl</sup>ected collaboratively.

We now turn to our nine quality criteria, which stress the role of researchers in doing re<sup>fl</sup>exive interpretive research and in writing re<sup>fl</sup>exive accounts:

1 Knowledge contextuality

2 Researcher’s confessional account.

3 Epistemological and theoretical struggles.

4 Vetting for multiple realities.

5 Triangulation with multiple sources of evidences.

6 Fine-grained accounts.

7 Validation/con<sup>fi</sup>rmation with participants.

8 Knowledge heterogeneity: Theoria and Praxia.

9 Access to dataset.

## 4.2. Self-reflexivity

A self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive researcher re<sup>fl</sup>ects on his or her own embedded in<sup>fl</sup>uences, on the way research is carried out, and attempts to understand how the process of doing research shapes its outcomes [33]. To do so, we invite interpretive researchers to (1) discuss the context in which the knowledge has been constructed, (2) provide a confessional account for the readers to understand the worldview in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the researcher and (3) provide detailed accounts of interim epistemological and theoretical struggles. In addition, criterion (5) (triangulation of data with multiple sources of evidences) that we classify within the domain-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity type may also be appropriate to self-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity.

## 4.2.1. Knowledge contextuality

Being self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive implies providing some knowledge contextuality about the position(s) of the researcher(s) in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and how it in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the entire interpretive research process. Whether the researcher is an external observer, participant observer, or follows any other stance, this position needs to be discussed and justi<sup>fi</sup>ed [60]. Researchers need to provide details regarding their access to the <sup>fi</sup>eld, frequency on site, data access, restrictions they faced and so on. Researchers should also be able to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on their own in<sup>fl</sup>uences regarding their presence in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, for example, ‘how did it affect the informants’ statements or the observed behaviours?’ and ‘how did the researchers handle the biases caused by their presence?’. These dif<sup>fi</sup>culties have been introduced by grounded theorists and ethnographers [52,12,51] but are less often applied to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

An important difference distinguishing my role with that of Philippe occurred because he entered the project 6 months earlier than me and already knew the project leader some years before that as she was a student of his. By the time I joined, Philippe was already integrated and had engaged some specific actions with the group. Thus, I am seen much more of an outsider by the group if not by Philippe.

The above quote from Julien, about his position with regard to Philippe and practitioners, is an occurrence of knowledge contextuality re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, which also ful<sup>fi</sup>ls the principles of ‘interaction between the researchers and the subjects’ and ‘contextualization’ of Klein & Myers [32] by providing details to the readers about the positions of each researcher on the <sup>fi</sup>eld, how and when both integrated the project, and by relating the project’s integration of Philippe to his previous connection with the project’s leader. In general, this implies providing contextual material to the readers about the researcher’s connections with the subjects under scrutiny and also, more broadly, about the context in which the study has been carried out.

## 4.2.2. Researcher’s confessional account

The authors should each also provide a confessional account to discuss their worldview in<sup>fl</sup>uences, epistemological assumptions and previous experiences in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. This can analyse how they might have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the research process [33,8]. By entering into a con<sup>fi</sup>dential narrative, authors are invited to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the research settings that impact the research construction, the design of the research question, the authors’ interest into speci<sup>fi</sup>c perspective(s) and so on. An occurrence of that occurred when Julien discussed his worldview when interrogated by David about the technology:

I am much more interested in analyzing how people interact with the new technology and how they adapt it to suit their particular needs. Other researchers might have emphasized technological aspects and that would have been a different story but certainly not ours. I teach and research in a Department of Management not a Department of Computer Science!

Along with showing re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, this quote exempli<sup>fi</sup>es the principles of ‘abstraction and generalization’ of Klein & Myers [32]. From this quote, which we regard as an idiographic detail collected during interviews, we understand that Julien is focusing his attention on the behavioural dimension of the Melibio project to observe how users adopt the technology. It might be at the detriment of other aspects, for example, system design, but it seems coherent with the fact that Julien is seeing himself as a management researcher rather than as a computer scientist. This quote exempli<sup>fi</sup>es the kinds of abstraction and generalization processes that are engaged throughout interpretive studies as it relates how an idiographic detail revealed during an interview may reveal a general concept (e.g. a behavioural approach) that describes how Julien regards the nature of the project. This criterion suggests that transparent introspective accounts of the interpretation processes with regard to the researchers’ theoretical ‘biases’ are provided [33,60].

## 4.2.3. Epistemological and theoretical struggles

When researchers opt for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c theoretical paradigm, they should be able to be re<sup>fl</sup>exive on how it will affect the research process. This could help the audience gain a better understanding of the choices made by the researchers during the entire research process including the design, data collection and interpretation phases. It requires a certain degree of sensitivity and re<sup>fl</sup>exivity from researchers on the possible contradictions between the theoretical pre-conceptions guiding the research design and the actual <sup>fi</sup>ndings (which might lead the move away from a particular framework). Reporting and discussing the theoretical struggles of interpretive empirical research could help ful<sup>fi</sup>l the principles of ‘dialogical reasoning’ by discussing the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties faced in connecting the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork material with the theoretical framework adopted and ‘abstraction and generalization’ of Klein & Myers [32] by providing vivid details of the theorization journey, including the interim struggles that may have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the <sup>fi</sup>nal formulation of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The following quote, from Philippe, is an example of those interim discussions that existed between researchers, showing how the selection of one theoretical approach was, from the researchers’ perspective, more appropriate given the general aims of the Melibio project:

We decided to use socio-materiality as a broad theoretical framework to approach the project topic, but my colleague next door would have probably used the technology-acceptance model for analyzing and working on the same project. The conclusions would have been different. However, I think that the humanist values of the Melibio project and its objectives were more aligned with our socio-material stance, though we had difficulties to make the project team understand some aspects of it. At first they saw it as very ‘academic’ and were unable to see its value to them.

In conclusion, we see much evidence of self-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity in the Melibio case study. The above examples evidence the selfre<sup>fl</sup>exivity practiced by Julien and Philippe who were acting as observers (with Philippe even more active as an action researcher) as they progressively in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the Melibio project. First, Philippe struggled to get himself accepted by the group as he was seen as an external researcher, whereas the participants were expecting him to provide a ‘concrete contribution’. Progressively, on the basis of a growing interest from the group to develop a re<sup>fl</sup>exive posture, the positions of the two researchers evolved. They were challenged by the group participants to provide material and methods for them to be self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive themselves with regard to their own behaviours and actions.

Looking at the wide-ranging literature, applications in part of these <sup>fi</sup>rst three criteria can be found in the works of Orlikowski [53], Mathiassen [35], Schultze [34] and Mitev [54]. Schultze [34] developed a rich and in-depth self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive account of her entire research process and her in<sup>fl</sup>uences while being on site and interacting with participants. Articulated around the concepts of ‘ex-pressing’, ‘monitoring’ and ‘translating’(p.27), she discussed her own data collection and interpretation processes through the following criteria: authenticity, plausibility, criticality, self-revealing writing, and interlacing ‘actual’ and confessional content. To use her own words, her account could be seen as an ‘autobiographical exposition to draw readers into the text so that the assumptions and practices of the foreign culture serve as a mirror in which the reader’s own assumptions and practices are re<sup>fl</sup>ected’ (p.4). Orlikowski [53] also delivered a self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive account to readers by discussing how ‘the iterative approach of data collection, coding, and analysis was more open-ended and generative’ (p.314) at her <sup>fi</sup>rst research site than at the second research site. She explained how the theoretical constructs that emerged from the <sup>fi</sup>rst site guided data collection in the second site. As an exemplar of the interim theoretical struggles faced by interpretive researchers, Mitev [54] discussed her dif<sup>fi</sup>culties while using actor network theory [61,62] for her dissertation project. The richness of her re<sup>fl</sup>exive account on her theoretical journey makes the reader conscious of her dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and gives strong credibility to her work as it demonstrates the empirical researcher’s struggles while trying to enhance an empirical dataset using a speci<sup>fi</sup>c theoretical framework. In providing information about his background and motivation, Mathiassen ([35]p.97) made clear in his work that it ‘is primarily neo-humanist. These include the roots of Scandinavian trade union research . . . the explicit inclusion of con<sup>fl</sup>ict’ and an aim to empower practitioners, the latter a particular feature of some others in the ‘Scandinavian School’ and other action researchers, notably Mumford [63].

## 4.3. Domain reflexivity

Olsson et al. [36] suggested a development to that of a single researcher being self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive, i.e. more than one researcher collecting and/or interpreting data together. To do so, we invite researchers to (4) develop a vetting method for enriching data interpretation, (5) triangulate data collection and interpretation with multiple sources of evidence and multiple researchers, and (6) collect <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained, longitudinal and multi-modal accounts.

## 4.3.1. Vetting for multiple realities

A common problem that has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in relation to interpretive research is that different individuals may interpret the same data differently, referred as ‘multiple realities’ by Kaplan & Duchon [55]. To address this problem, the method of vetting can be used. This is a process whereby results and interpretations are discussed with professional colleagues [64]. It means that for each source of evidence, authors should ask for feedback or interpretations from colleagues and practitioners to ensure the reliability and robustness of the written-up <sup>fi</sup>ndings. However, different interpretations from the same source of evidence that remain should be considered as an interesting result and not be seen as necessarily disturbing.

In the Melibio case study, this is found in the double interpretation process used by Philippe and Julien where they discussed their notes and perceptions following interviews and other meetings. This process helped ful<sup>fi</sup>l the principle of ‘suspicion’ and ‘multiple interpretations’ of Klein & Myers [32], which stresses the con<sup>fl</sup>icting and possibly misleading data and interpretations collected from the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Sometimes Philippe and Julien resolved differences of interpretation, but at other times, they highlighted differences, which remained as interesting observations to be looked at again.

## 4.3.2. Triangulation with multiple sources of evidence

As precautions need be taken to corroborate interpretations made, Miles & Huberman [9] and Yin [56] argued that triangulation across data sources (multiple informants at different levels of the <sup>fi</sup>rm and from different functions) and across data collection methods (interviews, documentation and observation) can strengthen the validity of <sup>fi</sup>ndings [53]. A constant comparison of data sources aims to force the confrontation of emerging explanations to open the way for possible alternatives. This condition is also necessary because of the potential nontraceability of secondary data if these form part of the dataset.

One example of this occurred in the Melibio project when Philippe and Julien used the same questionnaire to measure the use of the new IS in place from many different informants. System logs were used to corroborate (or refute) the stated positions of the informants (sometimes what people say they do is different from what they actually do!). This triangulation with multiple sources of evidence but also with multiple researchers collecting and interpreting the dataset helped ful<sup>fi</sup>l the principle of ‘multiple interpretations’ of Klein & Myers [32] by looking at the same sequence of events or narratives from different data sources.

## 4.3.3. Fine-grained accounts

Meticulous attention to detail and access to multiple viewpoints are essential to capture the nuances of a situation. For that, it is necessary to provide detailed descriptions of the whole research process from different perspectives and actors and over different periods of time. Therefore, longitudinal access to the <sup>fi</sup>eld is more likely to provide rich empirical datasets [57]. In addition to <sup>fi</sup>negrained and longitudinal access, we suggest the development of multi-modal datasets. The basic assumption of a multi-modal research approach [58,65] is that to study human interactions, researchers should consider different modes of complementary communication such as language, gesture, gaze and material objects. As Kress & Van Leeuwen [66] have noted, language is merely one mode among many, which may or may not take a central role in an interaction. One approach to analysing multimodal interactions is video-based ethnography [59]. This method allows the researcher to identify and carefully analyse how small activities naturally occur in real-life organizational settings.

In the Melibio case study, we were able to video-record several meetings and audio-record all collective phone calls during the entire duration of the project. This gives us a longitudinal dataset of the project to trace its evolution and turning points from different sources. The ‘readers’ can compare their interpretation of the video evidence with that of the researchers. Thus, <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained and multi-modal accounts implying multiple sources of evidence over a long period of time help ful<sup>fi</sup>l the principles of ‘hermeneutic cycle’, ‘suspicion’ and ‘multiple interpretations’ of Klein & Myers [32] by cross-checking events and narratives from multiples informants and sources but also from the perspective of multiple researchers. The objective is to make transparent the entire dataset and its interpretation process by explicating the inter-dependent meaning of each event, or source, and see how each connects or relates to the story constructed by the academic researchers.

With regard to domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as a whole, by being two researchers on site, Philippe and Julien increased the potential to triangulate <sup>fi</sup>eld evidence and confront mutually interpretative schemes. In terms of data collection, they opted for ‘in/out’ collaboration, based on Bourdieu’s [67] praise for scienti<sup>fi</sup>c re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. While Philippe was in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, collecting data, Julien remained outside to focus his attention on the interpretation of the dataset collected by Philippe, and vice versa. At <sup>fi</sup>rst, Philippe was the only researcher to collect primary data, but this evolved over time when Julien and Philippe switched from one position to another. In terms of data interpretation, for any interview or group meeting, Philippe and Julien shared their personal notes before trying to <sup>fi</sup>nd common ground for interpretation. They found this veri<sup>fi</sup>cation process across various types of data and people particularly bene<sup>fi</sup>cial because it provides multiple perspectives, supplies more information on emerging concepts, and allows for cross-checking, as well as linking self-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity to domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. In addition, with Philippe and Julien being participant observers in the case study, they regularly organized group and individual interviews that were video-recorded, whereas phone meetings were audio-recorded.

Looking at the wide-ranging literature, Olsson et al. [36] explained that for their interviews, while one researcher was asking the questions, academic peers were listening and taking notes to allow a comparative interpretative discussion between them after each interview. The work of Olsson et al. [36] can also be considered a good example of a <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained and longitudinal empirical account. They collected data from a number of sources including informal meetings, formal workshops, email correspondence and face-to-face and telephone interviews with a large number of company representatives. In terms of data triangulation, we refer to the work of Orlikowski [53]. The richness of her account lies in the variety of data sources used for comparison and for

## 4.4. Collaborative reflexivity

This form of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity implies building a re<sup>fl</sup>exive dialogue between academic researchers and practitioners with the objective to promote mutual improvements and anchor the relevance of the empirical research for both audiences. To do so, we invite researchers to (7) validate/con<sup>fi</sup>rm <sup>fi</sup>ndings with participants, (8) recognize knowledge heterogeneity and complementarity between theoria and praxia and (9) provide access to the dataset for further consultation. In addition, we suggest that criterion (6) <sup>fi</sup>negrained accounts, discussed within the ‘domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity’ type, may also apply for collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as it may help provide empirical material for developing collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity between academic researchers and practitioners.

## 4.4.1. Validation/confirmation with participants

To ensure the reliability of the full chain of evidence, we also suggest the tactic of participant checking [41,42]. To do so, feedback workshops can be organized with the project participants to present the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to the practitioners and the research participants in general and then to engage discussion to collect participants’ feedback. We see it as a valuable and reliable way for the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation and clari<sup>fi</sup>cation of the results and as inputs for further research developments if simultaneously run with other checking methods such as triangulation with multiple sources of evidence.

In the Melibio case study, we organized regular feedback sessions where we discussed our <sup>fi</sup>ndings with informants. It led to some interesting discussions as we revealed important discrepancies between our initial interpretations and their understanding of the situation being observed. It led to several cycles of discussion and revisions with informants. Ful<sup>fi</sup>lling this validation process with participants also helps ful<sup>fi</sup>l the principles of ‘multiple interpretations’ and ‘suspicion’ of Klein & Myers [32].

## 4.4.2. Knowledge heterogeneity: theoria and praxia

This principle is inspired from Mårtensson & Lee [38]. They emphasized the need to recognize both scienti<sup>fi</sup>c expertise (theoria) and professional expertise (praxia). The main assumption is that both types of knowledge may give different perspectives to the same evidence but should be seen as equal in terms of status. Ful<sup>fi</sup>lling this recognition of knowledge heterogeneity helps ful<sup>fi</sup>l the principles of ‘dialogical reasoning’ by discussing the potential gaps between empirical data and the practical concerns expressed by the informants, and the theoretical framework that might be perceived as ‘disconnected’ from their own social constructions; in addition, it helps ful<sup>fi</sup>l the principles of ‘abstraction and generalization’ of Klein & Myers [32] by providing an introspective process aiming at understanding researchers’ own reasoning for abstracting and generalizing <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The following quote from Philippe (along with that earlier discussing the researchers’ socio-materiality stance) is an example of the struggles academic researchers may face with practitioners because of their different perspectives:

It is always very striking to me to see how people in academia might observe things differently than practitioners. For example, when Myriam sees a project coordination issue when people are not using the platform, we (Julien and I) would usually focus on the potential role of technology for getting all the actors involved.

## 4.4.3. Access to dataset

We need to give our audience, and not only the participants, the tools to criticize our accounts [40]. Therefore, the last condition we suggest is to provide access to the dataset by any researcher for them to make sense of the case with their own cognitive schemes, worldview in<sup>fl</sup>uences and previous research experience and therefore open the <sup>fl</sup>oor to any further comments from other scholars regarding the interpretive study. The objective here is to see re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as a co-operative inquiry [41,42].

In the Melibio case study, we have made available all our notes, and work-in-progress drafts for all members of the project, on the collaborative platform that was used for the project co-ordination. Thus, by opening up empirical datasets to others, it helped ful<sup>fi</sup>l the principles of ‘hermeneutic cycle’, ‘multiple interpretations’ and ‘suspicion’ of Klein & Myers [32]. Our motivation is to provide the material evidence from which we built our own understanding of the case.

Allowing the accessibility of the dataset to a wider public might lead to developmental discussions and multiple interpretations. However, this needs to be guided by a set of boundary conditions addressing the diffusion and use of the dataset and permission from the company or companies involved in the research project. This is a sensitive issue, and we suggest a number of guidelines. The <sup>fi</sup>rst condition is oriented towards peers that are potentially interested in deepening their understanding of the case. Whether they want to build their own understanding of the work or to propose a new interpretation, they should state their motivations. The second guideline concerns the con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and the nonproliferation (and of course distortion) of the dataset. As many empirical studies are carried out within real organizational settings, it is crucial to respect the con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality of the dataset collected by the initial researcher. A third guideline would be to open ways for commentary sessions through journal websites or further issues of the journal that published the initial contribution.

In the Melibio case study, the group’s participants recognized knowledge heterogeneity at two levels. First, in between practitioners, there are distinctions between scienti<sup>fi</sup>c experts and farmers. However, both categories eventually acknowledged the existence of each other’s knowledge and were encouraged to share information owing to the role played by Philippe and Julien to develop domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. There was also a distinction between the knowledge of practitioners and academic researchers. Philippe and Julien brought something new to the group by arguing for re<sup>fl</sup>exivity by the entire group. As demonstrated in the discussion of the case, collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity not only enhances the researchers’ perspective but also provides material to debate things between practitioners and academic researchers. As argued by Patrick, a government-funded agricultural researcher, ‘It gives material to discuss the situation, to switch from a co-operative project where everyone has his own spot to truly collaborative work’. Clearly, both researchers and practitioners recognized the value of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity but for different reasons and had different expectations. One way to communicate, validate, con<sup>fi</sup>rm or refute narratives and <sup>fi</sup>ndings with participants was achieved through an online collaborative platform through which Philippe and Julien shared a number of documents regarding their investigation. Regularly, they were also asked to report <sup>fi</sup>ndings and progress to the group during face-to-face meetings. This online platform in which interview data, videos and so on are stored is accessible on request for scholars to further interpret our dataset, assuming they are willing to commit to the principles of con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and nonproliferation discussed above.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

Having argued for re<sup>fl</sup>exivity in interpretive research, it is clearly appropriate for us to be re<sup>fl</sup>ective regarding the present study. As action researchers in other research projects [68–71], we are used to practice re<sup>fl</sup>ection as part of the Susman & Evered [19] action research cycle and see its value in guiding, thinking and practice, although only Philippe’ role was one of action researcher in the Melibio case. We acknowledge that we were inspired by the Alvesson & Sköldberg [8] text in two main ways: <sup>fi</sup>rst, suggesting that re<sup>fl</sup>exivity is more complex but potentially more valuable than re<sup>fl</sup>ectivity alone and, second, by suggesting that re<sup>fl</sup>exivity brings bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to interpretive studies as a whole [yet noting Sarker et al. [7]’s study that found only 5% of published studies they reviewed offered any accounts about the interim struggles faced by researchers when interpreting data].

Case study research is a well-used interpretive approach in IS; thus, we searched for a potential site for our study. The opportunity to research the Melibio case was somewhat serendipitous and fortunate. Philippe was invited by Myriam as a university researcher to provide an outsider’s expertise to the project (Myriam had been a student of Philippe). Following meetings with the project team, it was not long before Philippe became more fully involved, acting as an action researcher within the group. Within 6 months, Philippe invited Julien to play more the role of an outside researcher working with the team (again, they had known each other at university). Re<sup>fl</sup>exivity had now become an important feature of the project and needed to be part of the project report to ensure <sup>fi</sup>nancial support and contribute to its success. David (a colleague of Julien) was invited by Philippe and Julien to help analyse the re<sup>fl</sup>exive processes.

In our focus on re<sup>fl</sup>exivity in the case study, we were convinced by Myriam’s ping-pong metaphor as it distinguished well the difference between the mirror metaphor of re<sup>fl</sup>ectivity with its recursive activities associated with thinking about experience. We toyed with the idea of seeing re<sup>fl</sup>exivity as being within a hall of mirrors but were concerned that the metaphor might emphasize confusion rather than enlightenment, although confusion does certainly exist at times in the process. It was some time before we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the three forms of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, although in retrospect, this was very evident in the case. Again through many iterations, we proposed nine quality criteria in doing re<sup>fl</sup>exive interpretive research and writing re<sup>fl</sup>exive accounts. This required an in-depth look at wide-ranging literature and a somewhat iterative process in seeing how identi<sup>fi</sup>ed criteria might <sup>fi</sup>t with the Melibio case.

Finally, we needed to situate our observations about the potential of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity in interpretive research studies and our quality criteria in the literature. Klein & Myers [32] provided a rigorous and well-used set of guidelines for interpretive research in IS, and we set about investigating whether there was a <sup>fi</sup>t between our nine quality criteria in doing re<sup>fl</sup>exive interpretive research and in writing re<sup>fl</sup>exive accounts following the Klein and Myers’ principles. We have evidenced in Table 1 that there is such a <sup>fi</sup>t, although this followed several discussions and iterations. Thus, these should be seen as ‘guidelines within guidelines’ and not be seen as incorrect if this structure is not followed in every case of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity in practice. As with the Klein and Myers’ principles, our criteria and contribution focus on the researchers’ perspective rather than that of practitioners. This latter was more the focus of Philippe.

In conclusion, we have argued that re<sup>fl</sup>exivity should be as essential a part of interpretive studies as rigour and relevance. The raw data and the interpretation are not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient; the re<sup>fl</sup>exive turn is a key part of the evidence. As a theoretical contribution, we have proposed a framework to support such studies. We suggest three forms of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, self-re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, domain re<sup>fl</sup>exivity and collaborative re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, which re<sup>fl</sup>ect the proportion of actors involved and whether both researchers and practitioners participate. In formalizing re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, we proposed nine quality criteria that will support the re<sup>fl</sup>exive researcher, and we show how they <sup>fi</sup>t in with the Klein and Myers’ principles for interpretive research. Through the Melibio case study, we have shown that the need for re<sup>fl</sup>exivity came from the participants and was fostered through an interaction between practitioners and researchers. We have provided examples where the authors were participating with the practitioners in the different forms of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity conceptualized in our framework. The case showed that re<sup>fl</sup>exivity helped <sup>fi</sup>nd a way forward that practitioners were comfortable with despite the various self-interests, which would have otherwise made a solution dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Thus, this work may open new avenues for addressing the issues of heterogeneous groups of actors and enhance the dialogue between practitioners and researchers.
