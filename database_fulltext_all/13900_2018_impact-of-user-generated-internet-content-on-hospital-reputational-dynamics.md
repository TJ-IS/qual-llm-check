# Impact of User-Generated Internet Content on Hospital Reputational Dynamics

ANTON IVANOV AND RAJ SHARMAN

ANTON IVANOV (antoniva@illinois.edu; corresponding author) is a Research Associate in the Gies College of Business, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. He received his Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from the State University of New York at Buffalo. Dr. Ivanov’s research stands at the intersection of information systems, data science, and healthcare analytics with an emphasis on user-generated content.

RAJ SHARMAN (rsharman@buffalo.edu) is a Professor in the Management Science and Systems Department at the State University of New York at Buffalo. He has also served as the Director of Management Science for the University at Buffalo’s Management Information System program. He received his Ph.D. in Computer Science from Louisiana State University. Dr. Sharman’s expertise lies in the areas of patient safety and HealthIT, information assurance and the use of biologically inspired computer security models, and disaster response management. He has published widely in scholarly journals and is the recipient of several grants from university and external agencies, including the National Science Foundation.

ABSTRACT: In pursuit of notable reputations, organizations commonly engage their stakeholders using various online mechanisms. While “reputation signaling” via online user engagement is commonplace, its implications in the context of usergenerated content (UGC) remain largely unaddressed. This study empirically explores the impact of UGC on hospital reputational dynamics. A unique panel dataset was analyzed using a lagged model approach to determine the effects of signals of quality, awareness, and content variance on hospital prominence and quality as well as the impact of reputation on financial performance. The findings suggest that quality signals significantly impact patient-perceived quality ratings, and signals of awareness and content variance significantly impact prominence rating. Also, in contrast to prominence, perceived quality significantly and negatively impactsfinancial performance. Given the ready availability of user-generated feedback, these findings can inform patient-provider online engagement strategies and related budget allocations. Furthermore, they highlight the unique role of Facebook ratings as an antecedent to quality and prominence dimensions of reputation.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: hospital reputation, healthcare, online prominence, quality signals, content variance, user-generated content, hospital utilization, financial performance, online engagement.

Reputation is viewed as a valuable intangible asset that is associated with sustainable competitive advantages and contributes to differences in organizational performance [49]. In healthcare contexts, an outstanding reputation is known to have a significant impact on patients’ hospital selection process [22, 23, 46, 55, 57, 65]. In pursuit of notable reputations, healthcare providers commonly engage their stakeholders using various online mechanisms [51]. For example, social networking (Facebook, Google Plus), media sharing (YouTube, Instagram), microblogging (Twitter), and knowledge/information aggregation (websites) are all commonly used by U.S. hospitals to engage with stakeholders [16]. Online engagement can help reduce information asymmetry between providers and other stakeholders, including patients. Moreover, it facilitates the creation of user-generated content (UGC)—that is, feedback provided by online users in response to signals communicated by providers. Such feedback is important as it gives future online users cues regarding healthcare providers’ quality and performance characteristics. While “reputation signaling” via online user engagement is commonplace, its implications in the context of UGC remain largely unaddressed.

Accordingly, our study explores the relationships between cancer-treatment hospitals’ reputations (perceived quality and prominence), their user-generated antecedents (signals of quality, awareness, and content variance), and their financial performance outcomes (approximated using Medicare payments for fee-for-service care). From a theoretical perspective, this paper adapts the work of Rindova and colleagues [49] to explain how UGC impacts the reputation and generated revenue of cancer hospitals. By studying these complex reputational dynamics, we make three significant contributions. First, we clarify the role of UGC in hospital reputational dynamics by adapting and testing a two-dimensional model of reputation, its antecedents, and its consequences [49]. Second, we propose new ways to operationalize antecedents of reputation that occur as a result of online user actions. Third, we offer new managerial implications by way of our findings, as they suggest efficient, yet inexpensive solutions to increase reputation by means of Internet reputation management.

## Background

## Perspectives on Reputation

A review of research on organizational reputation in management, economics, sociology, and marketing literature reveals that two schools of thought primarily inform the construct’s definition [62]. Scholars studying reputation from an economics perspective tend to define it as the observers’ expectations or estimations of a specific organizational attribute [6, 35, 40, 49]. Scholars drawing on institutional theory present a different characterization, defining reputation as a global impression or the ways in which a collective (e.g., a stakeholder group or multiple stakeholder groups in the organizational field) perceives a firm, often as a consequence of information exchange and social influence [4, 36, 47, 49, 56].

The theoretical foundation for this paper is based on the influential study in the strategic management literature that proposed a two-dimensional perspective in which reputation is characterized as an amalgamation of quality and prominence dimensions [49] (original model summarized in Appendix A). Perceived quality dimension captures “the degree to which stakeholders evaluate an organization positively on a specific attribute, such as ability to produce quality products”; in contrast, prominence dimension captures “the degree to which an organization receives large-scale collective recognition in its organizational field” [49].

We adapt the two-dimensional model of reputation [49] to investigate the antecedents and consequences of reputational dimensions in the context of UGC. To facilitate our theoretical elaborations, Figure 1 summarizes the relationships under consideration.

## User-Generated Antecedents of Reputation

The reputational dimensions of quality and prominence are likely to have different antecedents. Perception of quality is likely to be influenced by signals (information) about organizations’ underlying capabilities to produce quality goods or services [42]. In contrast, prominence is likely to be influenced by the choices of other stakeholders whose attention to an organization may be seen as a form of endorsement [41].

Complementing prior work in the area of reputation management, we study reputational dynamics in the context of UGC. While UGC generally refers to a broad category of online user activities (e.g., social media or blog posts, wikis, videos, comments, reviews), we exclusively focus on the feedback provided by online users in response to online engagement efforts initiated by another party:

namely, hospitals. Examples of such user-generated feedback include “liking” a Facebook post, “following” a Twitter account, “viewing” a YouTube video, or giving a “five-star” rating on Google, among others. The management information systems literature [10, 15, 32, 39, 58] emphasizes three types of signals that emerge from such user-generated feedback: persuasive (associated with opinions that encourage or discourage consumers to purchase a product or service), awareness (associated with actions that prompt other consumers to become aware of a product or service), and content variance (associated with capturing heterogeneity in user opinions).

Persuasive feedback is typically associated with online user ratings and reviews. Prior studies have delineated its associations with consumer purchase decisions and firm pricing strategies [33], product returns [52], and analyst expectations regarding a fund’s future performance [2]. In healthcare contexts, signals of quality contained in commercial website ratings (e.g., Yelp) appear to have a significant relationship with traditional hospital performance measures made available by Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (HCAHPS) [3]. Additionally, Facebook star ratings and reviews are significantly related to readmission rates [14] and patient willingness to recommend a hospital [21]. These associations suggest that the quality dimension of hospital reputation will be influenced by persuasive feedback that contains quality evaluations of hospitals productive assets, such as doctors, nurses, additional staff, or facilities. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

## Hypothesis 1: The higher the quality evaluations contained in persuasive feedback, the higher the hospital’s perceived quality.

In contrast to persuasive feedback, awareness feedback is likely to be associated with broad exposure and visibility [38]. Following a “social influence” logic, increased exposure and visibility might lead some organizations to gain disproportionate amounts of public attention and support on the basis of rather general and nonspecific impressions and beliefs [28]. Such organizations may become prominent within their professional field and be selected by stakeholders, even in the absence of stakeholders’ specific judgments about their ability to produce quality goods or services [49]. For instance, number of Twitter followers has been shown to be positively related to researcher citation count [66] and journal impact factor [24]. Another survey revealed that the primary reason for Twitter use among healthcare providers was to enhance visibility and reputation [7]. It was further shown that content contribution through YouTube was mainly driven by contributors’ desire for exposure and reputation [59]. By offering “ready-made” metrics of hospitals’ relative standing, an aggregate of awareness measures is likely to reduce stakeholders’ need to evaluate the attributes and quality of an organization directly and individually [48]. Thus, stakeholders can use awareness signals as an overall evaluation of whether an organization is among the leaders in its industry. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2: The higher the extent of awareness, the higher the hospital’s prominence.

Finally, content variance generally refers to the contextual polarity or evaluative tone of the content shared by a focal entity. In today’s online environments, quality content is especially important because content that embodies pleasing character traits (trustworthiness, reliability) and conforms to practices deemed locally and culturally desirable can attract more stakeholders [34, 62]. Notably, the emergence of social media has transformed the conceptualization of content variance in a dramatic fashion. Consider, for instance, the widely used Facebook “like” button that expresses a favorable impression and approval of the content shared in a post. Its distinctive advantage over alternative feedback systems is that it collects the opinions of known groups of acquaintances [31] and can be used to infer details, such as intelligence, personality, or political views [25, 26]. In the healthcare context, likes have shown a strong negative association with 30-day mortality rates and positive association with patient recommendations [60] as well as significant value in predicting the most examined health outcomes and behaviors [13]. Through the process of attribute priming, through which decisions are often largely determined by input not consciously categorized, the cumulative effect of content variance or evaluative tone shapes the attitudes and opinions of online users, causing prominent organizations to stand out in their minds [5]. Moreover, the fact that someone “likes” the shared content creates a contextual effect whereby the symbolic environment surrounding the object provides future viewers with cues about its interpretation [29]. We therefore hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: The higher the variance of the content shared by the hospital, the higher its prominence.

## Consequences of Reputation

Although different in their nature, the quality and prominence dimensions of reputation are related in that both reflect the common concerns of consumers by identifying providers of high-quality goods or services [28, 49]. Indeed, organizations perceived as high quality by customers are more likely to be frequently mentioned and re-patronized, and thus attract more new customers in the future [49]. Therefore, perceptions of quality can contribute to increased organizational prominence, leading us to hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 4: The higher the hospital’s perceived quality, the greater its prominence in the professional field.

In addition to quality and prominence, we examine financial performance, which has been established as one of the major outcomes of organizational reputation [12, 50]. Because reputation reduces stakeholder uncertainty about the value of future exchanges, favorable reputation can, for instance, induce buyers to pay a price premium [49] or serve as an important asset in protecting firm value [18]. In the case of quality, better financial performance can be viewed “either as a return on reputation or as an incentive payment to induce quality maintenance” [54]. Moreover, when making economic choices, stakeholders are likely to favor prominent organizations because prominence reflects the “majority vote,” which can provide some assurance to buyers and other relevant parties regarding the efficacy of buyer choices [28, 48]. Based on extant theory, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5: The higher the hospital’s perceived quality, the higher the payments associated with its services.

Hypothesis 6: The greater the hospital’s prominence, the higher the payments associated with its services.

## Method

## Sample and Data

Our sample of high-acuity cancer-treatment hospitals was drawn from the US News and World Report (USNWR) Best Hospitals listing, one of the oldest and most widely recognized hospital rating systems for patients [45]. The panel data for this study came from multiple publicly available sources, including USNWR, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS), the Hospital Compare Database, Facebook, Google, Twitter, YouTube, Alexa, the Hospital Safety Score, and Castle Connolly’s Top Doctors. We collected data manually from each respective source by searching for the hospital’s name and location (city and zip code), thereby ensuring data validity and eliminating duplicates.

Data collection for the entire sample was executed over two one-week periods, first in July 2014 and again in July 2016. All data were merged to create a unique dataset that resulted in N = 495 (listwise) hospitals. As multiple datasets were merged, each of which had missing data for certain hospitals, the descriptive statistics of the estimation sample for the outcome variables are likely to differ from those found in other published work. Descriptive statistics and operationalization of the variables are given in Table 1, and additional details on data collection are provided in Appendix B.

## Measurement: Outcomes

To measure prominence, we used the USNWR Best Hospitals rating score (continuous), which reflects hospital performance in three interlocked dimensions of health care: structure, process, and outcomes [9, 20]. To measure hospital patient-perceived quality, we used a summary star rating provided by Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (HCAHPS) [43, 44] across all sites, as cancer-specific data were unavailable. Finally, price- and risk-adjusted data on Medicare payments were obtained from the Hospital Compare Database, covering the 2014 to 2017 reporting period. Since cancer-specific payment information is unavailable for public use, we approximated Medicare payments at the hospital level using aggregated public data from heart attack, heart failure, and pneumonia payments [8].

## Measurement: Independent Variables

To measure quality (or resource) signals, we used Facebook and Google star ratings. Although both rating systems have a similar nature (i.e., they provide evaluations based on past users’ experiences) and demonstrate somewhat similar descriptive statistics, they are only moderately correlated. Differences can be attributed to Google evaluations being linked to reviewers through email, whereas Facebook reviews are linked to actual user profiles. Thus, reviews provided by Google are “less personalized” and keep the reviewer’s identity hidden. In contrast, any opinion shared on Facebook is “connected” to a real individual, such that individual characteristics could be observed and interpreted by inquisitive online users.

To measure extent of awareness, we used an aggregate of several awareness measures that occur as a consequence of online user actions: (1) number of Twitter followers for each entity’s Twitter business account; (2) number of YouTube views of videos posted by the entity on its business account; and (3) Alexa global rank, an estimate of website rank that is calculated using a combination of the average number of daily visitors to and pageviews of the entity’s site. Given that the latent construct of awareness is an aggregate of three correlated items, we employed principal components analysis (PCA) to reduce the dimensions. First, we transformed the items by taking a natural log to improve component analytic results. Second, we examined inter-item correlations that were above the 0.3 threshold (e.g., 0.55, 0.51, 0.57). Third, we employed the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy, resulting in satisfactory values above the 0.5 threshold (e.g., 0.72, 0.68, 0.70). Fourth, we estimated internal consistency using Cronbach’s α, which resulted in a satisfactory coefficient of 0.78. Fifth, we examined the components’ coefficients (0.82, 0.82, 0.83), which were considered “excellent” above the 0.7 cut-off. For further analysis, we retained one component with an eigenvalue of 2.09, which accounted for 70% of variation.

Finally, we measured content variance by the aggregated number of Facebook likes for content that the focal hospital shared with its online stakeholders on its Facebook page. Given the high skewness (21.5) and kurtosis (476) of the data, we further transformed this variable by using its natural logarithm (ln) to improve the model fit statistic.

## Measurement: Control Variables

To explore focal effects, we controlled for a number of influential observed confounders: affiliation with high-status actors, which was identified as an influential antecedent of prominence dimension [49]; patient safety, a correlate of hospital reputation [45] as determined by third-party evaluators; hospital size; and extent of hospital and user online engagement (Table 1).

## Statistical Analysis

To test the proposed relationships simultaneously, we estimated a path-model (Figure 2). To do so, we used structural equation modeling (SEM), which allows researchers to simultaneously examine a series of dependent relationships and analyze multiple dependent variables. We took a lagged effects modeling approach to estimate focal effects, a choice rooted in our assumption that it takes time for signals (information) contained in different types of UGC to propagate across various stakeholder groups so that they can influence either of the two reputational dimensions. Therefore, we consider explanatory variables collected at time T–2 (2014) indicative of reputations at time T (2016).

The estimation model depicted in Figure 2 incorporates four exogenous explanatory variables and three endogenous variables. To provide deeper insights, we included non-hypothesized relationships (dashed lines) in addition to hypothesized relationships (solid lines). Each variable was modeled as a single indicator and presumed to contain no measurement errors. Given that social media metrics are often “interconnected,” the corresponding exogenous variables were allowed to co-vary in the estimation of the model. To estimate our model, we used the

## Discussion and Conclusion

The results of this study have several important managerial implications. As the healthcare industry continues to transform itself, healthcare providers need to be ambidextrous and nimble as their survival and success depend upon adapting to new roles in a transparent, digitally oriented, consumer-centric future. In this context, online user feedback is of the utmost importance given that it provides efficient yet inexpensive solutions for reputation management and social interaction with online stakeholders. To facilitate understanding of how managers can efficiently utilize such solutions in practice, we offer the following recommendations.

## Know Your Ratings and Raters

From an operations standpoint, signals of quality could serve as free indicators of service quality and patient satisfaction. In some cases, even one negative evaluation might substantially decrease the overall star rating for a hospital. Moreover, a recent study found that customers who have complained in the past and customers who complain about process-related rather than outcome-related issues are less likely to feel satisfied at the end of their interaction with an organization [17]. To deal with such situations, we suggest taking a cue from service recovery models and contacting the user who left the negative review to directly resolve the issue. This can be done either privately (e.g., via email or personal message) or publicly (e.g., through a public comment response to the negative review). The theory behind this response is that a service failure presents an opportunity to convert a dissatisfied customer or patient into a loyal one who may then share positive remarks regarding how he or she was treated with others, whether by word of mouth or online. This chain of events can create strong positive perceptions about a hospital.

Given the differences in individual search strategies [53], we recommend exploring multiple sources of patient-generated information with similar purposes (e.g., Healthgrades, Vitals, RateMDs, Angie’s List, Yelp). To address false or deceptive reviews on these websites, hospitals can use state-of-the-art supervised machinelearning techniques, which are capable of ensuring the credibility of online information and thus positively affect consumer confidence [27].

Furthermore, hospitals should recognize the differences in the ways Facebook and Google rating systems identify raters. While the former relies on individuals’ actual accounts to identify them, the latter relies solely on users’ email addresses accompanied by an unverified name. “Email identities” are more anonymous and provide less information about the reviewer’s characteristics, which in turn undermines readers’ trust in the review. Our findings support this assumption by identifying the greater magnitude of the impact of Facebook ratings on quality compared to Google ratings. We encourage practitioners to apply this finding by attributing less weight to or potentially excluding evaluations provided through questionable or suspicious accounts.

## Increase Awareness and Visibility

By sharing content through owned social media accounts and hospital websites, entities are able to establish lasting contact with their online audiences. Sources that receive substantial amounts of attention become known to many people and, in turn, better fulfill the reputation signaling role compared to less well-known sources. In contrast, entities that fail to attract significant user attention will not be able to gain advantages (e.g., visibility, top of mind awareness, high rate of content dissemination, popularity) through their online endeavors. We suggest that such entities develop comprehensive online engagement strategies aimed at becoming “follow-worthy” by (1) composing distinctive, idiosyncratic, effective, and provoking content; (2) signaling it using mindful dissemination strategies; (3) using comments and hashtags; and (4) affiliating with influential accounts. We also encourage healthcare professionals to increase their engagement through video sharing as it has been shown to have a substantial positive impact on online customer engagement in recent years. In this light, one study notes that strategic allocation of resources to online social media channels should feature a mixture of both earned and owned social media [64]. In other words, providers are encouraged to generate (and signal) their own content and at the same time collect substantial amounts of feedback generated by online users in response to the signals they communicate.

## Create Favorable (“Like-able”) Content

From a marketing perspective, mindful and competent content sharing through Facebook will substantially contribute to increased prominence. Specifically, the aggregated evaluation of content shared by a hospital in the form of likes will (1) positively impact its prominence in the minds of perceivers through attribute priming and emotional appeals and (2) considerably impact the dissemination of the shared content among users of Facebook and other social networking platforms. To capitalize on the relationship that such platforms can foster, hospitals should engage in content sharing by providing users with content that is adequate, competent, properly positioned, compelling, fulfilling, and convenient. Moreover, the shared content should be just and trustworthy, as such messages positively impact IT system reuse [61]. Ideally, content should be of “mixed” type, including text, image(s), video(s), and link(s), as appropriate. Further, a recent study points to the benefits of content that combines informative characteristics with brand personality-related content [30]. A disorganized approach to sharing content, in contrast, will lead to a low degree of content variance, indicating low content dissemination as well as inefficiency in signaling the hospital’s characteristics to potential and current patients. Both of these factors are likely to result in the hospital having a weak prominence position relative to its competitors.

The present study is not without its limitations, which might be addressed in future research. First, we were able to provide results for cancer-treatment hospitals only. It would be useful to determine whether these results are generalizable to other types of hospitals or industry segments. Second, the Facebook data considered in our study allowed us to examine content variance represented by positive evaluative tone only. In reality, users’ perceptions could be negative. Further complicating matters, Facebook recently introduced a new version of the like button that allows users to express more emotions: “love,” “haha,” “wow,” “sad,” and “angry.” We encourage future studies to identify types and components of content that will result in the greatest number of likes, loves, or wows and lowest number of sad or angry reactions.

In addition, given the lack of agreement among national hospital rating systems, future studies should consider data from other raters, such as Consumer Reports or Leapfrog. Also, as we merged multiple datasets, our sample had a number of missing values, particularly for the hospitals in the upper quantiles of the USNWR rating distribution (95th and above), limiting representativeness. Furthermore, we approximated financial performance with a total of Medicare payments using aggregated public data on heart attack, heart failure, and pneumonia payments. Future studies are encouraged to explore new sources that provide cancer-specific data. Finally, although we made a significant effort to establish causal relationships using the prominent two-dimensional model of reputation while controlling for multiple confounding effects, there could still be endogeneity caused by omitted variable bias.

## Appendix A: Summary of Original Model and Estimates

The original model for the antecedents and consequences of organizational reputation proposed by Rindova et al. [49] consists of two stages. In the first stage, organizational reputations, conceptualized as perceived quality and prominence dimensions, are predicted using several antecedents: (1) resource signals, such as quality of inputs (H1) and quality of productive assets (H2); (2) certifications from institutional intermediaries, such as media rankings (H3) and certifications of achievement (H4); and (3) affiliation with high-status actors (H5). Additionally, the prominence dimension is predicted using perceived quality (H6). In the second stage, perceived quality and prominence are used to predict the consequences of organizational reputation defined as price premium (H7 and H8, respectively).

## Appendix D: Robustness Check

To ensure the robustness of estimates obtained using structural-equation modeling and the maximum-likelihood estimator, we used KRLS, a novel machine learning method [11, 19]. Our selection is justified for the following reasons.

First, we sought to minimize the influence of bad leverage points given the relatively skewed distribution of user-generated predictors of interests. KRLS can be used to diminish the influence of leverage points by minimizing over-fitting and reducing the variance and fragility of estimates; thus, KRLS is far more robust in handling bad leverage points than, for instance, ordinary least squares (OLS) [19].

Second, in light of the multitude of regressors included in the model, we sought to minimize any bias associated with complex nonlinearities or interactions. The KRLS estimator addresses this issue by accurately learning the target function from the actual data rather than “guessing” the correct functional form by adding additional terms [11].
