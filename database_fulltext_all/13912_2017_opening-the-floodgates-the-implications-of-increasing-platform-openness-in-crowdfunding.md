# Opening the floodgates: the implications of increasing platform openness in crowdfunding

## Abstract

Managing platform ecosystems requires providers to govern the permeability of platform boundaries in order to strike a balance between openness and control. But is it worthwhile for platform providers to conduct screening processes for third-party offerings, or should they rather apply a laissez-faire approach and let the market determine winners and losers? In this study, we compare the market conditions on Kickstarter, one of the world’s largest crowdfunding platforms, before and after a policy change that relaxed the previously stringent screening process for new campaigns. By analyzing over 230,000 crowdfunding campaigns with a combined funding of over \$1.9 billion that cover a 4-year period around the policy change, we find that increasing platform openness was a doubleedged sword for the platform’s ecosystem. While Kickstarter’s revenue from commissions surged after the policy change due to an increase in the number of campaigns available on the platform, funding conditions for project creators and backers deteriorated. Project creators have to cope with lower success rates and an intensified competition due to a growing disproportion of backers to campaigns, while backers face higher uncertainties as project creators invest less in reducing information asymmetries. Our evidence from this natural experiment therefore suggests that increasing platform openness for third-party offerings can destabilize a platform’s ecosystem. We thus extend the literature on platform openness and on the implications of policy changes on platform ecosystems. Our findings also provide practical insights for different platform stakeholders into possible ‘‘ripple effects’’ that are triggered when policy changes alter platform openness. ## Introduction

n recent years, platforms have emerged as a prevalent organizational model in many markets and industries. Platforms differ from traditional service providers in that they do not offer products or services by themselves and directly to their customers (i.e., end users), but almost exclusively through what are referred to as complementors, who develop and deliver the respective content (e.g., apps, add-ons, plug-ins, modules, or extensions) via the platform (Tiwana et al., 2010). Prominent examples for such platforms include app stores such as the Apple App Store, which bring together app developers and smartphone users, and gaming platforms such as Steam, which bring together game developers and gamers. As these platforms are two- or multi-sided markets (cf., Economides and Katsamakas, 2006; Eisenmann et al., 2006), they are characterized by distinct cross-side network effects, since each side derives positive externalities from the participation of the respective other group (Ondrus et al., 2015; Parker and Van Alstyne, 2005; Rysman, 2009). That is, to stimulate network effects, only a platform that attracts sellers (complementors) will attract buyers (end users) and vice versa. Creating favorable conditions for these network effects to emerge is thus at the core of platform management, and platform providers face the enduring challenge to provide sufficient incentives for complementors to create high-quality and innovative content for the platform in order to create and maintain a thriving platform ecosystem (Bakos and Katsamakas, 2004; Benlian et al., 2015; Cusumano, 2010a; Rochet and Tirole, 2003).

In this respect, it is a particular challenge for platform providers to determine the appropriate level of platform openness, which is the degree to which platform providers grant access to outsiders, ‘‘thereby opening up markets for complementary innovation around the platform, and giving up some control over the platform itself’’ (Boudreau, 2010, p. 1849). Though the concept of platform openness has mainly been discussed in the context of technology platforms and, especially, computing platforms (e.g., Boudreau, 2010; Eisenmann et al., 2009; West, 2003), the concept gains a newfound relevance as the notion of platforms is expanding to, for example, open source (e.g., add-ons for the Mozilla Firefox web browser; Tiwana, 2015), open innovation (e.g., crowdsourcing platforms as a means for IT-enabled open innovation; Schlagwein and Bjørn-Andersen, 2014), and open capital (e.g., using crowdfunding platforms to raise funds; Belleflamme et al., 2014).

Prior research on platform openness has identified a key trade-off known as diversity versus control (Boudreau, 2010). On the one hand, opening a platform to third-party complementors, such as developers who contribute smartphone apps, can increase the diversity of available applications and, as a result, the respective platform is able to offer more options to end users, potentially creating a competitive advantage over rival platforms (Tiwana, 2014). On the other hand, opening a platform to complementary innovation can result in a lack of control over the complementors and their contributions. As a result, platform providers might no longer be able to align their interests and strategies with those of the complementors (Boudreau, 2012). For example, during the Atari shock in the 1980s, Atari’s platform was flooded with low-quality video games due to its inability to control quality, which was a major factor in contributing to its decline (Coughlan, 2004). Therefore, with more players entering a platform, managing the diverse intentions and interests becomes more complex (Almirall and Casadesus-Masanell, 2010). Furthermore, platform providers need to be cautious, as the unilateral growth of a particular market side can cause a disequilibrium within the platform ecosystem, potentially leading to a stronger competition for the attention of end users and less favorable conditions for complementors overall.

In this context, West (2003) highlighted that platform openness is not a dichotomous construct but rather a continuum from completely closed to completely open (cf., Schlagwein et al., 2010). Therefore, partially open platforms can exist along a continuum where the platform providers have made different choices about platform governance in respect of decision rights, ownership, and control (Boudreau and Hagiu, 2009; Tiwana et al., 2010). Although previous research has recognized the importance of specifying and implementing an appropriate degree of platform openness (e.g., Boudreau, 2010, 2012; Eisenmann et al., 2009; Ondrus et al., 2015; Parker and Van Alstyne, 2010; West, 2003), these studies examined the effects of opening a platform mainly from an ownership and technology perspective (e.g., how much equity in the platform (owner) is held by complementors or how liberal is the platform owner’s licensing program) and less from a policy perspective (e.g., how do official rules and guidelines shape the permeability of platform boundaries). As such, there is still a dearth of research studies that examine the intricate effects of changes in platform access policies on key platform stakeholders and the ecosystem overall (Eisenmann et al., 2009).

In this study, we explore platform openness from a policy perspective in the crowdfunding context. Crowdfunding allows individuals or organizations to raise funds for a diversity of projects by receiving small financial contributions from a large number of individual investors (hereafter, simply backers). Following a tradition of exploratory studies about policy changes in platform settings (e.g., Claussen et al., 2013; Tucker, 2014; Ye et al., 2014), we study the effects of a policy change (PC) with respect to platform openness for project creators (i.e., the complementors) on Kickstarter, one of the largest reward-based crowdfunding platforms worldwide. Since its launch in 2009, over 12 million individuals have pledged almost \$2.9 billion on Kickstarter, successfully funding close to 120,000 projects (Kickstarter, 2017).

Prior to the PC, each new campaign<sup>1</sup> submitted by potential complementors was evaluated manually by Kickstarter staff based on a comprehensive set of guidelines. In June 2014, however, Kickstarter altered its strategy by implementing a PC regarding their screening and approval process. The PC entailed two major changes (Strickler, 2014): First, the manual screening process (hereafter referred to as input control) for potential campaigns was replaced with an algorithmic process, which only verifies that the campaign fulfills basic requirements (e.g., has a description and offers appropriate rewards). Second, the previously elaborate list of guidelines for projects that can seek funding via the platforms was reduced to three basic principles: Projects must be shareable, honest, and within the confines of reward-based crowdfunding (e.g., no charity) (Strickler, 2014).

The PC was motivated by the expectation that it would make the platform easier to access and open it up to new kinds of projects (Strickler, 2014), as prior to the change, several articles (Hurst, 2012; Jeffries, 2014; Kelion, 2014) had reported Kickstarter’s loss of a few lucrative projects to competing platforms such as Indiegogo due to its strict input control policies. Considering the aforementioned trade-off between diversity and control in respect of platform openness, the intricate effects that the PC will have at the platform as well as the campaign level are difficult to predict. Given our ability to trace the effects of Kickstarter’s PC, it offers the unique opportunity to explore the following research question:

RQ: How does relaxing formerly stringent input control mechanisms affect critical outcome variables (1) at the platform level (e.g., overall funding success rate, revenues from commissions, backer-to-campaign proportion, competition among project creators) and (2) at the campaign level (e.g., funding goals and amounts, creatorbacker communication)?

To address our research question, we analyzed a total of 232,011 Kickstarter campaigns that cover the period from June 2012 to June 2016 (i.e., 2 years before and after the PC) and received a combined funding of \$1.9 billion. Following an exploratory approach to analyze the impact of a policy change in a natural experiment-like setting, using descriptive, illustrative, and econometric evidence, our study reveals that increasing platform openness was a double-edged sword for Kickstarter’s ecosystem. At the platform level, we observe a strong increase in the average number of new campaigns per day and a rise in Kickstarter’s revenue from platform fees. However, the PC also led to a growing disproportion of backers to campaigns, resulting in a more intense competition among project creators and a decreased average success rate. At the campaign level, we see a widening gap between project creators’ funding expectations and the amount their campaigns eventually accumulate. We also find lower communication intensity between project creators and (potential) backers (e.g., fewer videos, shorter descriptions, fewer updates), although communication activities become a more important driver of campaign success after the PC.

Our study contributes to the information system (IS) literature on openness and control as well as to the still nascent literature on platform ecosystems in the following ways. First, while previous research has focused on the ownership and technology perspective to study platform openness (e.g., Boudreau, 2010; West, 2003), little research has been devoted to analyzing the effects of increasing platform openness from a policy perspective, even though scholars have repeatedly underscored the need to further unravel the delicate implications of increasing platform openness (Boudreau, 2010, 2012). Second, we link the concept of input control to platform openness in order to explore the effects of variations in platform openness at the platform and campaign level. Third, our study contributes to research that explores the implications of policy changes on platform ecosystems (Burtch et al., 2015; Claussen et al., 2013). Fourth, we contribute to research on the role of network effects in platform ecosystems (Chu and Manchanda, 2016; Parker and Van Alstyne, 2005) by showing what dynamics unfold between the two market sides when input control is relaxed. Finally, our study also demonstrates that the attenuation of an inherent quality signal at the platform level (input control) entails that platform users shift their attention to other prevalent signals (e.g., social media shares) that increase in their relative importance for users’ decision making.

## Theoretical background

Openness and control mechanisms in platform ecosystems Platform openness, which reflects the trade-off between retaining and relinquishing control over a platform, has received increasing attention among researchers and practitioners as an instrument to alter the permeability of a platform’s boundary (e.g., Benlian et al., 2015; Boudreau, 2010; Cusumano, 2010b; Ondrus et al., 2015; Wareham et al.,

2014). According to previous studies, platform openness can be shaped through various platform governance mechanisms, of which deliberate regulations and rules about access and boundary control are key components to appropriately engage other platform stakeholders (Benlian et al., 2015; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). Platform governance, which is generally defined as ‘‘who makes what decisions about a platform’’ (Tiwana et al., 2010, p. 679), can be studied from three distinct perspectives, namely decision rights, ownership, and control mechanisms. In this study, by examining the implications of relaxing input control on platforms, we focus on the latter perspective and thus follow a long tradition of IS control studies (Kirsch, 1997; Ouchi, 1979). In our context, open versus closed platforms therefore simply represent differences in input control exercised by the platform owner.

According to classical control literature, control refers to mechanisms used by controllers in the attempt to influence controlees so that they act and behave in accordance with the controller’s objectives and goals (Kirsch, 1997; Ouchi, 1979). In previous research, two main categories of control have been distinguished, namely, formal and informal control (e.g., Kirsch, 1996). Within formal control, two distinct modes, output (also referred to as outcome) and process (also referred to as behavior) control, have been observed (e.g., Eisenhardt, 1985; Ouchi, 1979). While output control requires the controlee to reach a certain goal or objective set by the controller in order to be rewarded, process control requires the controlee to adhere to specified procedures and routines during the process, and doing so is rewarded. In contrast, informal control modes do not require specific incentives to align the goals of controller and controlee, as shared norms and values exist (Kirsch et al., 2002). Within informal control, self- and clan control can be distinguished (e.g., Kirsch, 1996; Ouchi, 1979). Self-control occurs when controlees define and monitor their own goal achievement and reward or punish themselves accordingly. Clan control is similar to self-control, with the exception that a group of controlees, rather than an individual controlee, embraces shared values and commits to achieving group goals (Kirsch et al., 2002).

Though the concept of control originates from organizational theory, it has attracted considerable attention among IS scholars (e.g., Kirsch, 1997; Kirsch et al., 2002; Tiwana and Keil, 2009). However, it has only been applied in the context of platform ecosystems relatively recently and with a strong focus on software-based ecosystems (e.g., Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Goldbach et al., 2014; Wareham et al., 2014). According to Tiwana (2015), the relevance of formal and informal control mechanisms is decreasing in platform settings due to redundancy and costliness. For instance, process control is often obsolete, as platform owners are ultimately interested in the finished complement and are not directly affected by costs that complementors have to bear, because the relationship between the platform provider and complementors is not the classical principal–agent relationship (i.e., the complementor is not hired by the platform provider) (Tiwana et al., 2010). Furthermore, it has been argued that clan control requires a relatively stable ecosystem in terms of complementors and that formal and informal control mechanisms are ‘‘less viable in loosely coupled organizational structures’’ (Tiwana, 2015, p. 4). As a result, in loosely coupled ecosystems that exhibit high fluctuations in terms of complementors, like mobile app and crowdfunding platforms do, the providers often focus their efforts with respect to control mechanisms on input control. Input control can be defined as the degree to which platform owners use predefined rules and guidelines to judge whether a compliment should be allowed into the platform (Cardinal et al., 2004; Tiwana et al., 2010). Although scant literature exists that considers input control in different forms and contexts (e.g., Boudreau, 2010; Cardinal et al., 2004; Kim and Tiwana, 2014; Liu et al., 2014), prior IS control research has mainly focused on output, process, and clan control, overlooking the increasing relevance of input control for platform ecosystems (e.g., Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch et al., 2002).

Against the background of previous IS control literature, two main research gaps can be identified. First, the question of how changing input control affects platform ecosystems in general and crowdfunding platforms in particular remains largely unexplored. Second, different configurations of control mechanisms in platform ecosystems have previously not been explored longitudinally but mainly theoretically (e.g., Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013) or based on crosssectional surveys (e.g., Kirsch, 1997; Kirsch et al., 2002; Tiwana and Keil, 2009).

## Input control mechanisms in crowdfunding

Crowdfunding, which builds on the broader concept of crowdsourcing (e.g., Bayus, 2013; Huang et al., 2014; Poetz and Schreier, 2012), allows individuals or organizations to reach a monetary (project) goal by receiving small financial contributions from a large number of individuals instead of choosing the traditional approach and receiving large contributions from a small number of investors. Crowdfunding enables project creators to collect contributions from backers through an open call, mostly on the internet, without standard financial intermediaries (Mollick, 2014; Schwienbacher and Larralde, 2012). Over the last few years, a variety of different crowdfunding platforms has emerged and four distinct models of crowdfunding have been distinguished: donation-based, reward-based, lending-based, and equitybased (Kuppuswamy and Bayus, 2014). These four models mainly differ with respect to the return backers can expect from their contribution, which can either be financial, materialistic, idealistic, or philanthropic in nature (Ahlers et al., 2015). Equity- and lending-based crowdfunding markets, for instance, offer financial returns for backers, either directly through interest or indirectly through com pany equity. In reward-based crowdfunding, backers can expect non-financial tangible benefits for their investment, which have been found to be a central reason for backers to invest (Kuppuswamy and Bayus, 2014). Finally, in donationbased crowdfunding markets, backers cannot expect tangible returns and thus pledge due to altruism and warm glow (Andreoni, 2006).

Due to regulatory requirements or strategic considerations, providers of crowdfunding platforms act as controllers and apply different input control mechanisms for complementors (controlees), thereby opening or closing their platforms to varying degrees (Table 1). For example, equity-based crowdfunding platforms such as SeedInvest apply rigorous screening processes for all potential complementors in order to avoid the prohibition of general solicitation and take advantage of the JOBS Act (Harrison, 2013; U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, 2013). As this process is complex, these platforms generally offer very few investmen opportunities simultaneously. SeedInvest, for instance, claims to receive about 80 applications each week from companies looking to be funded through the platform (SeedInvest Technology, 2015b). Out of these 80 companies, only the most promising ten are presented to the screening committee made up of experts from within SeedInvest, who judge them based on their potential, value proposition, product, etc., and on how well these characteristics are conveyed to potential investors (SeedInvest Technology, 2015a). In contrast, donation-based crowdfunding platforms such as GoFundMe often do not apply any input contro mechanisms and therefore offer several hundred or thousand investment opportunities at the same time, letting the marke determine winners and losers (Wareham et al., 2014). In reward-based crowdfunding, however, strategies with respec to input control have been less distinct. For instance, Indiegogo and Kickstarter, the largest reward-based crowdfunding platforms today, have taken different approaches to input control. Indiegogo is completely open in that the platform does not apply any input control mechanisms for project creators and thus allows any individual or organization to publish a campaign on their platform. From its beginning, Kickstarter chose to apply input control with a rigorous green-lighting process, meaning that every campaign had to be approved by Kickstarter staff manually before it could be published on the platform. These approaches to input control applied by Kickstarter and Indiegogo resemble those taken by Apple and Google in the mobile app market. While Google’s Play Store, similar to Indiegogo, does not apply input control mechanisms apart from security checks, Apple’s App Store is well known for enacting strict policies to control the quality of apps. Though applying such mechanisms is costly and can lead to lower numbers of apps or campaigns being available on the platform, they have made being published on platforms such as Apple’s App Store and Kickstarter a quality signal in itself (Mitroff, 2012).

Although a considerable body of research exists on all four crowdfunding models, most of this prior work has focused on identifying informational cues (i.e., signals) considered by platform users (i.e., backers) when making investment decisions. In this respect, researchers highlighted the impor tance of geography (e.g., Agrawal et al., 2015), the project creator’s social network (e.g., Agrawal et al., 2010; Lin et al., 2013), electronic word-of-mouth (e.g., Thies et al., 2016), and social information on the platform (e.g., Kuppuswamy and Bayus, 2014). Though all these papers offer valuable contributions, no prior work has provided insights into the effects that changes in input control (and changes in control mechanisms more generally) can have on the entire platform ecosystem and its main stakeholders.

## Policy change on input control at Kickstarter

As crowdfunding platform owners mainly generate revenue through transaction-based fees,<sup>2</sup> managing the demand and supply side is at the core of platform management. Until June 2014, Kickstarter did this primarily by manually evaluating each new campaign submitted to the platform based on a comprehensive set of guidelines. This formal input control was rather unique in the context of reward-based crowdfunding, but regularly applied on software platforms such as Apple’s App Store. Despite these relatively high entry barriers for project creators, Kickstarter became one of the largest crowdfunding platforms worldwide. However, creators of lucrative projects frequently decided to publish their campaigns on a different platform (e.g., Indiegogo) after being rejected by Kickstarter due to its strict guidelines (Jeffries, 2014; Kelion, 2014). In June 2014, however, Kickstarter altered its strategy with respect to its platform openness for complementors by applying a PC to their screening and approval process for campaigns, which removed the mandatory manual screening for the majority of submitted campaigns (Strickler, 2014). The PC was announced with the following statement:

We want creators to have the support and freedom they need when building their projects. That’s why we’re introducing a feature called Launch Now. It gives creators a simple choice: go ahead and launch your project whenever you’re ready, or get feedback from one of our Community Managers first (Strickler, 2014).

In the course of the PC, the platform’s guidelines were reduced to only three simple rules that require projects that seek funding via the platforms to be shareable, honest, and within the confines of reward-based crowdfunding (e.g., no charity) (Strickler, 2014). Prior to the PC, Kickstarter did not, for example, allow projects dedicated to developing websites or apps focused on e-commerce, business, and social networking to publish a campaign and restricted the quantities of rewards in certain categories. Figure 1 shows the screening process before and after the PC. Before the PC, the screening process took between 48 and 72 h, and about 25% of submitted campaigns were declined by the Kickstarter staff because the respective project violated platform guidelines. Other project creators were asked to improve their campaigns and resubmit them at a later time. After the PC, the manual screening process was no longer mandatory for the majority of submitted campaigns, and project creators could therefore either launch their campaign directly or request feedback on it by Kickstarter staff.

Although manually excluding less promising projects from the platform is an error-prone and expensive process, the effects of moving from a strict input control with rules and guidelines to a more laissez-faire approach are difficult to predict. On the one hand, it can be expected that relaxing input control on Kickstarter brings a larger number of complementors and campaigns on board, thereby increasing diversity and innovation. On the other hand, opening the floodgates might also have negative implications for the ecosystem (Boudreau, 2012). The uncontrolled growth of the market may lead to a situation where, ultimately, the growth on one side of the market outpaces the other, leading to a disproportion of end users to complementors that might unbalance the ecosystem.

## Research approach and data

In this study, we follow the tradition of exploratory, natural experiments about policy changes in platform settings (e.g., Claussen et al., 2013; Ye et al., 2014) to deepen our understanding of the effects that an increase in platform openness has on the overall platform ecosystem and its key stakeholders (i.e., provider, project creators, and backers). Natural experiments investigate the effects of an unplanned or naturally occurring contrast between a treatment and a comparison condition that cannot be manipulated by researchers because, for example, the manipulation occurred before the effect is measured (Shadish et al., 2002). In our study, the treatment varies through the PC, which can be regarded as exogenous for complementors and end users, while being endogenous for the platform provider. We tracked and compared measures before and after the PC, allowing us to use the before measures as an estimate of the counterfactual (i.e., what would have been the outcome without the PC). By comparing the change, and controlling for alternative explanations, we are able to infer the causal effect of the PC on our focal outcomes (Remler and Van Ryzin, 2011). Over the past decade, this research approach has received considerable attention among researchers (e.g., Chen et al., 2011; Kumar and Telang, 2012; Ye et al., 2014). As with any research that relies on a natural experiment for identification, we cannot manipulate the assignment to specific conditions, which might therefore be non-randomized, but can expect greater external validity.

Using a self-developed web crawler, we collected a daily time series dataset that covers the period from June 4, 2012, to June 3, 2016, and that contains a total of 232,011 Kickstarter campaigns that started within this timeframe. The PC was enacted from June 3, 2014, onward, giving us 2 years of data before and after the PC. We chose this time span to adequately control for seasonality and time trend effects.

Our data are suitable for our research purpose for the following reasons. First, this natural experiment-like setting about a change of input control allows for similar identification as in field experiments (Claussen et al., 2013; Goldfarb and Tucker, 2011; Tucker and Zhang, 2011). Second, as we have collected data on all (i.e., successful and unsuccessful) campaigns that ran on the platform in the specific period, we are able to avoid selection or survivor biases. Finally, Kickstarter is one of the most prominent crowdfunding platforms, making the results relevant for the entire industry.

## Variables measured

We consider campaign success in our data as whether or not the campaign reached its funding goal. Even though other success criteria exist, we chose this dichotomous variable for two main reasons. First, as Kickstarter uses an all-or-nothing (AON) funding mechanism, meaning that funds and commission fees are only paid if the funding goal is reached, achieving this goal is the primary objective for all platform participants. Second, it is an established measurement for success in the crowdfunding literature (e.g., Mollick, 2014).

At the platform level, we gathered information on the transaction-based revenue per campaign the platform provider receives on average. As we acquired data on a daily level, we could furthermore calculate the amount of new campaigns per day that were launched on the platform. Based on the number of backers each campaign was able to accumulate at the end of its runtime, we were able to calculate the Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) for the distribution of backers among campaigns (competition). The HHI stems from strategic management research and is an established indicator of the amount of competition among different firms, or in our case, campaigns. We use a normalized HHI that ranges from 0 to 100, where 100 represents a monopolistic market, and a decreasing index indicates stronger competition (Hansen and Haas, 2001; Hirschman, 1964).

Drivers of success for crowdfunding campaigns, such as a video, a detailed description (description length (DL)) without spelling errors (SE), the accompanying social buzz in the form of FacebookShares (FBS) or comments, or the number of updates, have been of great interest to scholars (e.g., Ahlers et al., 2015; Mollick, 2014). The assumption is that these signals reveal the underlying quality of a project, ensuring that higher-quality projects receive more funding compared to those of a lower quality (Mollick, 2014). According to signaling theory, quality signals can only be credible if a project creator offering a low quality has higher costs acquiring them compared to a project creator offering a high quality (Connelly et al., 2011; Kirmani and Rao, 2000; Spence, 1973). Hence, prior to the PC, being allowed to publish a campaign on Kickstarter could be considered a quality signal in itself, as passing the input control hurdle was a greater challenge for low-quality projects. It can therefore be expected that weakening this inherent quality signal increases the relative importance of the remaining information about a project and that it leads to a more intense communication between project creators and backers in order to compensate for the attenuation of the inherent quality signal through a more intense information gathering.

We therefore assess the reaction of the crowd to the increased platform openness to determine how the PC affected the relative impact of the available information on the backers decisions to fund a campaign. Compared to other crowdfunding platforms, where backers often only invest in one specific project (Agrawal et al., 2015), Kickstarter has actually developed a community of backers who predominantly invest in multiple projects.<sup>3</sup> We can therefore examine how the communication about a campaign initiated by project creators and by backers changes in response to the PC and whether this information becomes a more important driver of campaign success after the PC. For the communication that is initiated by project creators, we consider whether the campaign contains a video, look at the description length, and count the number of updates the creator published during the campaign runtime. First, uploading a video is strongly recommended by Kickstarter, claiming that campaigns that do not contain a video have a much lower success rate (Kickstarter, 2016). Second, a longer and more detailed description can potentially reduce the information asymmetry between the project creator and backers more effectively than a shorter description. Third, Kickstarter recommends providing updates for (potential) backers about the project in order to build momentum and encourage them to spread the word about the progress (Kickstarter, 2016). For the communication that is initiated by (potential) backers, we consider the number of comments backers have written about a project on the campaign page and how often the campaign has been shared or talked about on Facebook. First, comments allow backers to ask questions and engage in a direct conversation with the project creator and other backers, potentially reducing information asymmetries. Second, as peer evaluation has been shown to be particularly important for goods whose value is difficult to ascertain before purchase, potential backers are able to partially compensate for the uncertainty associated with the investment decision by sharing the campaign on Facebook and by evaluating the responses of their peers (King and Balasubramanian, 1994; Thies et al., 2016).

Finally, we also consider two measures of campaign quality. First, we checked for spelling errors (SE) in the project description as a lack of proofreading implies reduced preparedness and generally lower quality (Mollick, 2014). To identify spelling errors, we matched the project description against the list of the 4260 most commonly misspelled words in Wikipedia articles<sup>4</sup> (Wikimedia Foundation, 2016). Second we determined whether a campaign was what is referred to as a ‘‘Staff Pick’’ (SP), meaning that the campaign was selected by Kickstarter staff to be featured on Kickstarter’s homepage and was added to a separate list of campaigns recommended by the platform, because it was particularly compelling with respect to the video, description, rewards, or the project idea (Kickstarter, 2016).

As we analyze different stakeholders, multiple econometric research techniques are necessary. We therefore adjust our estimator to model the respective dependent variable adequately. On the platform level, we first model the aggregated rate of success for all campaigns with a fixed-effects panel OLS estimator (Baltagi, 2008). We then use the number of campaign backers and total backers per month as our second and third dependent variables, which are overdispersed, requiring a negative binominal estimator (Cameron and Trivedi, 2013). For our fourth analysis on the platform level, we consider market competition, measured with the help of a normalized HHI, again using a fixed-effects panel OLS estimator (Baltagi, 2008). The final econometric analysis is carried out on the campaign level and models success, which is a dichotomous variable requiring a logit estimator (Cameron and Trivedi, 2005).

## Results

Following Leung and Sharkey (2014) and Claussen et al. (2013), our exploratory research method for this longitudinal, quasi-experimental setting is twofold. We first consider descriptive, illustrative, and econometric evidence for the effects of the PC at the platform level. We then continue with evidence for the effects of the PC at the campaign level. To check for robustness of our models and to rule out alternative explanations for the observed effects of the $\mathrm { P C } ,$ we conducted a number of robustness checks for the effects at the platform as well as the campaign level, which are described in detail in the respective section below.

## Effects at the platform level

Following previous exploratory studies on policy changes (e.g., Claussen et $a l . ,$ 2013), we first look at the development of the ecosystem before and after the PC based on descriptive statistics. We then proceed with econometric evidence analyzing funding success rates, campaign and total number of backings, and general market competition on the platform level. Given that the PC is exogenous for project creators and backers, we can use this setting to draw inferences from changes in these metrics once the PC was enacted.

Platform level descriptive statistics for the whole observational period, before and after the PC, are presented in Table 2, where we observe a sharp increase in the number of campaigns that are launched each day (+70.9%, p \ 0.001). On the other hand, campaigns generally have fewer backers (-28.6%, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which translates into lower success rates $( - 3 2 . 6 \% , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). This development is also reflected in lower average platform revenue per campaign, which declined from \$387.64 to \$343.73 (-11.3%, p \ 0.01). Still, overall revenue from commissions charged by the platform increased by 51.5% $( p \textless 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Figure 2 illustrates the development of platform revenue over the course of 4 years. The slope of the linear fit, for the periods before and after the PC, reflects the general market growth and appears relatively unaffected by the PC. The underlying data were averaged on a monthly basis to give a clearer representation.

The change in platform openness and the subsequently increased number of campaigns also intensified the competition among project creators, which is reflected by a significantly lower Herfindahl–Hirschman index (-10.7%, $\begin{array} { r }  p < 0 . 0 0 0 \} \ \end{array}$ ) after the PC (Hansen and Haas, 2001; Hirschman, 1964).

Figure 3 illustrates the evolution of the platform before and after the PC by depicting the success probability of campaigns and the number of new campaigns over the course of 4 years. Again, we see a sharp drop in success rates for creators and a strong increase in campaign supply on the platform. The illustration also clearly shows the holiday seasons in December, where the number of campaigns undergoes a temporary drop each year.

We will now turn to our econometric analysis of the PC’s effects at the platform level. We employ a fixed-effects specification to control for unobserved heterogeneity by estimating effects using only within-category variation. The results discussed are generally robust to random effects models, but the Hausman specification test performed suggested that fixed-effects modeling is preferred (Hausman, 1978). The following specification was then used:

$$
y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \beta x _ {i t} + \gamma z _ {i}\tag{1}
$$

where $\gamma _ { i t }$ is the dependent variable. All variables are aggregated for each category (i) in each month (t). Independent variables are represented by $\beta x _ { i t }$ , while category-specific fixed effects (a) and an error term (z) are included. Result are reported in Table 3.

Our econometric analysis at the campaign level focuses on the effects of the communication between the creator and the community. To do this, we employ a logistic regression to test how the PC affected the communication patterns on prospective backers’ pledge behavior by using the achievement of the funding goal as our dependent variable (success). Consistent with previous natural experiment settings (e.g., Claussen et al., 2015), we use a dummy variable reflecting the PC as a moderator, which turns from 0 to 1 if the respective campaign started after the input control was relaxed.

As our dependent variable, campaign success, is dichotomous, a logit regression model is applicable. Logit models specify the probability of an outcome as a function of one or more independent variables (Cameron and Trivedi, 2005). Following Long (1997), a logit model can be formalized as:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (y = 1 | x) = F (x \beta)\tag{2}
$$

where F is the cumulative distribution function (K) of the logistic distribution for the logit model (Long, 1997). The probability of observing an event given x is the cumulative density evaluated at xb.

Our baseline model (1) includes all aforementioned drivers of success. We then add the PC in model 2 in order to model the moderating effect of the PC on the relationship between communication and campaign success. We subsequently include the interaction terms with the PC for the communication that originates from the creator, such as the video, description length, and updates in model (3). We then proceed with the interaction terms for communication that arises from (potential) backers, namely FacebookShares and comments, in model (4). The interaction term then lets us discern if each indicator became a more important driver of success after the PC and thus shows whether its signaling power was enhanced or weakened after input control was relaxed. We expect that with the attenuation of the inherent quality signal after the PC, the interaction effects show a significant impact on campaign success. The incidence rate ratios reported in the model were calculated by exponentiating each variable’s coefficient (Long, 1997).

We included several controls in our model to account for alternative explanations. All numerical variables and their correlations are given in Table 5. First, we used a category dummy for all 15 project categories on Kickstarter, ranging from Art to Film, Fashion, Music, and Technology. We further implemented a time dummy for each week to control for possible seasonality effects and the general growth trend within the industry. Additional controls are the campaign duration to account for the exposure length, the natural logarithm of the pledge goal, whether the description contained spelling errors, and if the campaign was a staff pick by Kickstarter.

Based on the results of model (2) in Table 6, we can confirm the conclusion drawn from our descriptive evidence that the PC caused a decline in funding success rates. The PC decreases the probability by a factor of 0.739, with all other variables held constant, which means that a campaign was 1.35 times less likely to succeed after the PC. In the subsequent models, we see that communication generally became more important after the PC, indicated by the positive and significant coefficients of the interaction terms in models (3) and (4). The largest gain in explanatory power compared to the baseline model, indicated by the lowest BIC, was achieved in model (4), which includes the communication initiated by (potential) backers.

In summary, we have gathered strong evidence that the PC did indeed increase the supply of campaigns on the platform. However, the demand (i.e., number of backings) did not significantly increase after the PC. Additionally, we found empirical evidence that relaxing input control for project creators led backers to focus more on the remaining information about a project, though project creators invest less in communicating the favorable aspects to their projects to backers.

## Robustness checks

To check for the robustness of our models, we conducted four sets of robustness checks. All tests resulted in similar significance levels and identical directions of all relevant coefficients. First, for effects at the platform as well as the campaign level, following Leung and Sharkey (2014), we excluded all campaigns whose funding period coincided with the date the PC was enacted. Second, as Kickstarter removed certain rules with the PC, some project types (e.g., websites or apps focusing on e-commerce) were allowed onto the platform after the PC that had previously been prohibited. We excluded all campaigns of projects that would not have been possible prior to the PC based on their subcategories. Also, in the weeks following the PC, Kickstarter added two new project categories to the platform, namely Crafts and Journalism, which we also removed for an additional robustness check. Third, an alternative explanation for the increased number of campaigns and the lower success rates could be a general trend within the crowdfunding industry and the global expansion of the platform. We therefore shrank the observational period around the PC to 24, 12, 6, and 3 months to rule out this trend. Specifications show that our results persist over these shorter time frames as well. Finally, for the probit model on the campaign level, we also ran an OLS regression with the natural logarithm of the monetary campaign funding as an alternative dependent variable. Taken together, all results we obtained from the robustness checks confirmed the consistency of our findings.

## Discussion

Our analysis of the PC concerning platform openness in the Kickstarter crowdfunding setting yielded several interesting findings. First, we found that the PC had a profound impact at the platform level. According to the announcement published by Kickstarter to explain and justify the PC, one of the main goals was to allow ‘‘more diverse ideas to thrive on Kickstarter’’ (Strickler, 2014). While our results show that this goal was achieved with an increase of 71% in the number of new campaigns per day after the PC, the results also indicate that the number of backers did not grow in the same proportion, leading to a sharp decrease in the number of backers per campaign and a lower overall success rate. Our analysis therefore reveals that there were no cross-side network effects that would have propelled the growth of the number of backers as a response to the sharp increase in the number of new campaigns and project creators. Furthermore, while the increased number of campaigns seemingly absorbed the drop in per-campaign revenue, resulting in a positive net effect of the PC for the platform provider, much of this growth in platform revenue can be attributed to the overall growth of the crowdfunding market. Unsurprisingly, the decreasing success rate and the disproportion of backers to campaigns after the PC also led to a rising competition for the attention of potential backers among project creators.

Second, we analyzed how the PC affected the platform ecosystem at the campaign level. Considering the disproportion of backers to campaigns and the decreasing average success rate after the PC, it is not surprising that we see a widening gap between project creators’ funding expectations (pledge goal) and the amount their campaigns eventually accumulate (pledged amount), making the platform less appealing for complementors and backers alike. We also examined how the communication between project creators and backers was affected by the increased platform openness in terms of its intensity and importance. We find that, after the PC, project creators invest less in reducing the information asymmetry between them and (potential) backers, which becomes evident when looking at the lower ratio of videos per campaign, shorter project descriptions, and fewer updates per campaign. Though backers also communicate less directly on Kickstarter (fewer comments), we see a sharp increase in the number of Facebook shares, suggesting that backers cope with the dilution of the inherent quality signal (i.e., the less stringent quality screening through Kickstarter) by sharing the campaign on Facebook and by evaluating the responses of their peers. Though we see a decrease in communication intensity overall, our analysis revealed that all communication streams, with the exception of videos, have become more important discriminating factors for campaign success after the PC. Overall, these results show that switching from a strict input control process with rules and guidelines to a more liberal regime, which is based on the assumption that the complementors have a strong intrinsic motivation to succeed, did not encourage project creators to commit themselves more to reaching the desired goal.

## Theoretical contributions

Our study makes the following contributions to the IS openness and control literature as well as to the emerging research on platform ecosystems. First, while previous platform openness studies have largely examined the effects of opening a platform from an ownership and technology perspective (e.g., Boudreau, 2010; West, 2003), little research attention has been directed toward analyzing the effects of increasing platform openness from a policy change perspective, even though scholars have repeatedly underscored the need to study the nature and effects of platform openness from various angles. Our findings add to previous IS openness literature by advancing our understanding of the delicate implications that policy changes on input control can have for the permeability of platform boundaries and for the nature and evolutionary dynamics of platform ecosystems. Furthermore, our study contributes to the IS control and openness literatures by conceptually linking input control, as a gatekeeping mechanism, to the concept of platform openness and by exploring the implications of increasing platform openness. More specifically, we show how relaxing input control mechanisms and thus increasing platform openness affects critical performance indicators not only on an aggregate platform level, but also on a more granular level for different platform stakeholders. Taken together, our study highlights that policy changes on input control that open up platforms to larger numbers of complementors – figuratively ‘opening the floodgates’’ – may unbalance an entire platform ecosystem as well as transform its character.

Second, we add to the growing stream of research on the implications of policy changes on platform ecosystems (e.g., Burtch et al., 2015; Claussen et al., 2013) by showing how adjusting a critical platform governance mechanism can affect an entire platform ecosystem and what dynamics unfold on the part of the different stakeholders. To the best of our knowledge, our study is also the first in the nascent crowdfunding research context to examine the effects of a major and far-reaching change of input control under conditions of a natural experiment. We believe, however, that our insights are not strictly limited to this context, as input control mechanisms are a ubiquitous phenomenon in platform ecosystems overall.

Third, while prior research on platform ecosystems has shown that in consumer-to-consumer platforms strong cross-side network effects exist and that the user base of sellers and product variety have a particularly strong effect on the growth of the buyer side of the market (Chu and Manchanda, 2016), no similar effect could be observed on Kickstarter between project creators and backers as a result of increased platform openness. These results therefore suggest that a rapid but unilateral growth of a particular market side can potentially unbalance an entire platform ecosystem, showing that further research is required to identify mechanisms for fostering a healthy growth of two- or multi-sided markets.

Finally, our study also demonstrates that the attenuation of an inherent quality signal at the platform level entails that platform users shift their attention to other prevalent signals (e.g., social media shares), which show an increase in their relative importance for users’ decision making. Our findings therefore highlight that the value and importance of platform signals, which users take as decision-making cues, are fragile and vulnerable to exogenous shocks rather than static and stable over time.

## Implications for policy and practice

Beyond the theoretical contributions of this paper, we also see a variety of policy and practical implications for platform providers and complementors. For platform providers, it is important to realize that increasing the permeability of platforms by moving to less rigorous input control practices can have profound and intricate effects on the decisionmaking processes of complementors. It is therefore crucial for platform providers to develop a deep understanding of the complementors’ goals, strategies, and capabilities that might be affected by a PC and of any potential areas of conflict that might arise (Yoffie and Kwak, 2006). For example, after the PC, Kickstarter attracted a number of projects likely to be a hoax, which may be seen as a form of rebellion against the new relaxed policies (Lecher, 2014).

Prior research has found that it is a managerial challenge to exercise enough control over a platform to ensure integrity while relinquishing enough control to encourage innovation (e.g., Boudreau, 2010, 2012; Tiwana, 2015; Tiwana et al., 2010). In this respect, platform providers can either enact hard input control mechanisms based on rules and guidelines or incentivize complementors through soft stimuli (e.g., a more prominent positioning of high-quality complements on the platform). Though Kickstarter has become successful despite its strict screening processes, such mechanisms can ‘‘be counterproductive in a nascent market in which consumer preferences are not (yet) settled’’, as innovative complements might fail to comply with any established criteria (Claussen et al., 2013, p. 199). The changing proportion of backers to campaigns and the decreasing communication intensity between backers and project creators after the PC suggest that Kickstarter’s decision has the potential to backfire in the future and that the platform provider should employ softer mechanisms to encourage complementors to invest more time and effort in their campaigns. Facebook, for instance, managed to increase the average quality of third-party apps on the platform by rewarding high-quality apps with further opportunities to engage users (Claussen et al., 2013). Though Kickstarter offers a similar mechanism with the Staff Picks, there is no clear path to becoming featured by Kickstarter that would additionally incentivize project creators (cf., Kickstarter, 2015).

For complementors, the easier access to the platform seems attractive, but entails stronger competition due to the increased number of rival campaigns. Even though a complementor’s products or services in a platform ecosystem are often unique and therefore do not compete directly with one another, each complementor has to compete for the attention of end users. Consequently, the PC increases the focus on the ability of the project creators to raise awareness for their campaigns, as it is the market that separates winners from losers. This new environment requires an adjustment to the impression management skills of creators, which could include better descriptions, higher quality of videos, or professional social media campaigns. As already seen from our results, creating buzz around a project with increased social media efforts by project creators could be one way to bring new users to the platform. Further expanding the efforts to impress potential backers could then lead to an adequate growth of the investor side of the platform. Counter-balancing the increased number of campaigns with luring new backers will particularly be crucial for Kickstarter, as high campaign failure rates and low backers-to-campaign proportions can have a considerable deterrent effect on prospective project creators.

## Limitations, future research, and conclusion

While our study provides new insights and contributions to both research and practice in the context of platform openness and control mechanisms, it is exploratory in many respects, and we acknowledge several limitations that need to be considered when interpreting the results and implications. First, our data are aggregated on a campaign level, meaning that we can only observe the aggregate behavior of backers and not the choices made by individuals. Furthermore, the available data did not allow us to compare specific characteristics of backers (e.g., socio-demographics, prior crowdfunding experience) before and after the PC. Future studies could therefore focus on the backers’ perspective to determine how relaxing or even abolishing input control mechanisms and the subsequent increase in project diversity and decrease in communication intensity affect individual decision making (e.g., do prospective backers need more time to make funding decisions on a platform with relaxed input control?). Second, though we study one of the most prominent crowdfunding platforms, we only observe a specific time frame in its evolution within a still young and very dynamic market. Therefore, one should be cautious when extrapolating our findings to other, more mature platform ecosystems. Finally, although we believe that our study offers relevant insights into various ‘‘ripple effects’’ that a platform owner can trigger when changing policies in respect of (input) control, there are other options (e.g., decision rights, ownership) for platform providers to govern the permeability of platform boundaries through platform governance mechanisms. Future research may, for instance, examine the effects of platform providers delegating decision rights to platform end users. Some crowdfunding platforms, such as Startnext, already let end users decide democratically via an open green-lighting process what complements should be allowed onto the platform, instead of making the decisions themselves.

In conclusion, and based on the findings in our research setting, a PC that increases platform openness by shifting to less extensive input control can be a double-edged sword for a platform’s ecosystem. While it may increase the number and variety of complements and, consequently, increase revenues for the platform provider in the future, these benefits are counterbalanced by a shift in the proportion of end users to complements, resulting in lower revenues (or funding) on average for complementors. These adverse effects might lead complementors to turn to rival platforms with more attractive conditions in the future. We hope that our results provide impetus for further analysis of platform openness and control mechanisms in the context of platform ecosystems and give actionable recommendations to platform providers and third-party complementors.
