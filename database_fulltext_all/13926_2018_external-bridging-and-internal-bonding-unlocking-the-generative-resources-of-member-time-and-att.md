# EXTERNAL BRIDGING AND INTERNAL BONDING: UNLOCKING THE GENERATIVE RESOURCES OF MEMBER TIME AND ATTENTION SPENT IN ONLINE COMMUNITIES<sup>1</sup>

Yongsuk Kim School of Business and Mangement, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, HONG KONG {yongskim@ust.hk}

Sirkka L. Jarvenpaa

Center for Business, Technology, and Law, McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX 78712 U.S.A. {Sirkka.Jarvenpaa@mccombs.utexas.edu}

Bin Gu Department of Information Systems, W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University. Tempe, AZ 85287 U.S.A. {Bin.Gu@asu.edu}

Sharing members with other online communities can be problematic for community responsiveness, as less member time and attention become available for the focal community. However, member time and attention spent externally have the potential to generate knowledge that bolsters the focal community’s responsiveness. Little is known about when member time and attention spent externally become such generative resources. Building on and extending the knowledge collaboration theory of online communities, we examine the network conditions that enable the generative effect of member time and attention spent externally on community responsiveness. We propose a contingent model wherein a focal community’s external bridging (the extent of bridging nonredundant communities) and internal bonding (the extent of cohesion among members) jointly moderate the effects of member time and attention spent externally on community responsiveness. We theorize that external bridging provides the opportunity to access novel external knowledge and that internal bonding improves the motivation to share knowledge to assist others. Our results show that member time and attention spent externally have a positive effect on community responsiveness only in the presence of both external bridging and internal bonding. The study has important implications for advancing the boundary conditions regarding generative resources and online knowledge collaboration.

Keywords: Online communities, membership overlap, community responsiveness, knowledge collaboration theory of online communities, member time and attention, generative resource, structural holes, external bridging, internal bonding

## Introduction

Many organizations nurture organizational online communities, each with a distinct topic and specific business objectives, to assist knowledge sharing and collaboration among distributed workforces with similar interests (Gray and Ranta 2010; McDermott and Archibald 2010). For online communities to remain viable, high levels of community responsiveness must be maintained (Butler 2001; Zhang et al. 2013). Community responsiveness refers to the extent to which a focal community generates replies to information requests through members’ posting activity (Butler and Wang 2012). The more knowledge (replies) that members provide in discussion threads, the more responsive the community is and the more value is created for its members (Butler and Wang 2012). Community responsiveness is essential for attracting new members and retaining existing members (Butler 2001; Joyce and Kraut 2006; Zhang et al. 2013).

Online communities are increasingly recognized to not be isolated collectives; rather, they are subject to the influences of one another because they have overlapping, or shared, members (Butler and Wang 2012; Gu et al. 2007; Tan and Lee 2015; Wang et al. 2013). Some studies use the term membership overlap to refer to the connections between a focal community and other communities established through shared members (e.g., Wang et al. 2013; Zhu, Chen et al. 2014; Zhu, Kraut, and Kittur 2014). A focal community’s membership overlap leads member time and attention to be spent externally in other communities (Dahlander and Frederiksen 2012; Wang et al. 2013; Zhu, Chen et al. 2014; Zhu, Kraut, and Kittur 2014). Member time and attention are vital resources for increasing community responsiveness (Butler 2001). Shared members create tension among communities because members must split their time and attention across multiple communities (Wang et al. 2013).

The prevailing view in the literature is that sharing members with other communities is problematic for a focal community because a trade-off is argued to exist between member time and attention spent externally (in other communities) and community responsiveness (Wang et al. 2013; Zhu, Kraut, and Kittur 2014). Because member time and attention are finite resources, community responsiveness is believed to diminish with the community resources spent externally (Wang et al. 2013). However, this prevailing view overlooks the possibility that member time and attention spent externally can be generative—rather than constraining—for the focal community (Faraj et al. 2011) because members obtain new knowledge from other communities.

The objective of the current study is to examine the effects of member time and attention spent externally on community responsiveness through a generative view of resources and to identify the conditions under which member time and attention spent externally become generative resources and facilitate greater community responsiveness. The current study adopts a generative view of resources from the theory of knowledge collaboration in online communities (Faraj et al. 2011) and combines it with a social network perspective (Burt 2000). The knowledge collaboration theory argues that resources such as time and attention flow in and out of an online community, and, in doing so, they create both positive and negative consequences for knowledge collaboration in the community. The key to fostering community responsiveness is whether the focal community can enact a generative mechanism of the resources that turns negative consequences into positive consequences (Faraj et al. 2011). In the case of shared members, their time and attention spent outside a focal community leave less time and attention available for the focal community, but the focal community is provided with access to the external knowledge available in other communities (Dahlander and Frederiksen 2012; O’Leary et al. 2011). Member time and attention spent externally become generative resources when the focal community harnesses the tension coming from negative and positive consequences in a way that stimulates additional knowledge generation that improves its own responsiveness. However, the knowledge collaboration theory of online communities is silent on the conditions under which member time and attention spent externally can become generative resources (and the conditions under which they cannot).

This study extends the theory by combining it with a social network perspective to highlight the contingencies of the generative effect of member time and attention spent externally. Argote et al. (2003) suggest that, in addition to ability, opportunity and motivation are important elements of knowledge generation. While ability is largely endowed to individual members, both the opportunity and motivation for knowledge acquisition and sharing are subject to the structures of online communities (Burt 2000). Taking a social network approach, the study focuses on two forms of the network structure that greatly shape such opportunity and motivation: external bridging and internal bonding. The opportunity for members to identify and access novel external knowledge is provided by the external bridging of the focal community (Burt 2000). External bridging refers to the extent to which the members of a focal community collectively bridge an external network of nonredundant communities. By nonredundant communities, we mean communities that share few or no members with one another and that are thus separated by “structural holes” (Burt 1992). A focal community’s external bridging is high when its external network is sparse. The motivation of members to share knowledge to assist others in the community is boosted by the internal bonding of the focal community (Burt 2000). Internal bonding refers to the extent to which members of a focal community are cohesive (i.e., strongly connected to one another). A focal community’s internal bonding is high when its internal network (formed by member relations) is dense.

As opportunity and motivation complement each other in facilitating knowledge generation (Argote et al. 2003), the current study theorizes that a focal community’s external bridging and internal bonding jointly enable member time and attention spent externally to become generative resources. Only when external bridging and internal bonding are both high will member time and attention spent externally improve community responsiveness because members have not only abundant opportunities to acquire novel external knowledge but also strong motivation to share it with others within the community. By contrast, when external bridging or internal bonding is low, member time and attention spent externally will reduce community responsiveness because members fail to acquire novel knowledge or do not share the knowledge that they have acquired. The current study analyzed a longitudinal data set of 76 organizational online communities from a global energy company and found empirical support for the contingent model.

This study makes important contributions to the literature on online communities. First, through the theoretical lens of a generative view of resources, the study yields a new understanding that member time and attention spent externally have not only negative, as previously believed, but also positive consequences for the focal community. The study reconciles the inconsistent findings of prior studies by suggesting that the positive (or negative) effect on community responsiveness depends on the community’s generative use of member time and attention spent externally. Second, the study extends the knowledge collaboration theory by combining it with a social network perspective to identify the contingencies of the generative effect of member time and attention spent externally. By highlighting the roles of external bridging and internal bonding, the current study advances the boundary conditions for the knowledge collaboration theory and elaborates the theory in the intercommunity context. Third, the results support the contingent model that member time and attention spent externally have a positive effect on community responsiveness only in the presence of both external bridging and internal bonding. The study underscores the importance of jointly considering intercommunity and intra-community connections when examining generative resources and online knowledge collaboration.

## Theory Background and Hypotheses

## Knowledge Collaboration View of Online Communities

According to the knowledge collaboration theory in online communities (Faraj et al. 2011), resources provided by community members, such as passion, time, and ideas, potentially have both positive and negative consequences for knowledge collaboration in an online community. Knowledge collaboration is broadly defined as the acquisition, sharing, and generation of knowledge (Faraj et al. 2011). Recognizing the dynamics of community membership, Faraj and colleagues further theorize that the fluid nature of an online community creates fluctuation in resources, which intensifies the tensions within the community regarding how to handle the positive and negative consequences of the resources. The key to spurring knowledge collaboration is the community’s “generative” use (or, more precisely, that of members of the community as a whole) of fluctuating resources (i.e., their conversion into generative resources) such that negative consequences are converted into positive consequences.

Faraj et al.’s (2011) knowledge collaboration theory of online communities is intra-community-focused and does not account for the possible influence of intercommunity connections on generative resources and knowledge collaboration in the community. However, the participation of (shared) members in other communities causes a fluctuation in the member time and attention available for a focal community. Member time and attention spent externally can be problematic for the focal community, as less time and attention will be available for members to participate (negative consequences) (Wang et al. 2013). However, as members devote their time and attention to other communities, they gain access to the knowledge bases of these communities. The time and attention devoted to spanning external communities have the potential to improve the ability of the members to acquire external knowledge (positive consequences) (Dahlander and Frederiksen 2012; O’Leary et al. 2011). Greater community responsiveness will be facilitated if the time and attention spent in other communities result in the acquisition or generation of additional knowledge relevant to the existing information needs of the focal community (generative resources). Nevertheless, not all communities can improve community responsiveness in return for their member time and attention spent externally (e.g., Dahlander and Frederiksen 2012; Zhu, Kraut, and Kittur 2014). Surprisingly, the knowledge collaboration theory says little about the conditions under which member time and attention spent externally can become generative resources. We later theorize the contingencies of the generative effect of member time and attention spent externally by incorporating the social network view of the bridging and bonding network structures of online communities. Below the concepts of bridging and bonding are reviewed.

## Social Network View of Bridging and Bonding Network Structures

Social networks have been studied in various organizational (e.g., Burt 2000; Oh et al. 2004; Reagans and Zuckerman 2001) and information systems (IS) contexts (e.g., Newell et al. 2004, Singh et al. 2011; Tang et al. 2011). Two main forms of network structures are found to affect generative resources and knowledge collaboration in the network: bridging and bonding (Burt 2000). A bridging network structure refers to a sparse network wherein a focal actor bridges connections between otherwise disconnected segments, while a bonding network structure refers to a fully connected network wherein actors are strongly connected to one another (Burt 2000).

As for bridging, a focal actor can derive benefits from spanning “structural holes,” or gaps between the contacts, whether individuals or collectives, to whom the focal actor is directly connected in the network (Burt 2000). A focal actor’s network has structural holes to the extent that the contacts therein have weak or no connection with one another.

The structural hole between two groups does not mean that people in the groups are unaware of one another. It only means that people are focused on their own activities such that they do not attend to the activities of people in the other groups. Holes are buffers, like an insulator in an electric circuit. People on either side of a structural hole circulate in different flows of information.…Structural holes separate non-redundant sources of information, sources that are more additive than overlapping (Burt 2000, p. 353).

The structural hole between contacts implies that the knowledge that contacts hold is nonredundant (Burt 1992). By being connected to both contacts, a focal actor spans the structural hole and gains access to two nonredundant sources of external knowledge. Having a network of nonredundant contacts means that the focal actor is the only one connected to otherwise unconnected actors in the network. This type of sparse network structure provides the focal actor with an opportunity to access diverse novel knowledge. By contrast, a dense network, wherein every contact is connected, provides the focal actor with access to redundant knowledge.

As for bonding, benefits result from having a dense network wherein actors are cohesive because they are fully interconnected (Burt 2000). Bonding rests on the assumption that people care about their reputations as cooperative actors and their future relations in the network to which they belong (Burt 2000). A dense network increases the motivation of network actors to exhibit cooperative behaviors toward one another for two reasons (Reagans and McEvily 2003). First, people know that if they do not cooperate, the news will be quickly shared throughout their cohesive network and potentially hurt their reputations and future interactions (Coleman 1988). Because organizational actors are keen on maintaining a good reputation (Ferris et al. 2003), being perceived as uncooperative in the eyes of colleagues can be devastating for their careers within the organization. Misbehaviors can be easily spotted in online communities (Ridings et al. 2006). Such visibility can further increase the chance for strongly connected members to coordinate collective sanctions—such as a withdrawal from future interactions—against uncooperative members. Second, internal bonding promotes norms of reciprocity within the community—i.e., that favors will eventually be returned by someone (Coleman 1988; Kramer et al. 2001). People are motivated to remain cooperative when they expect that their favors will be reciprocated in the future (Coleman 1990). Reciprocity norms can be established in online communities. Faraj and Johnson (2011) find knowledge sharing, in the form of replying to a message, to be strongly driven by both direct and indirect reciprocity in online communities.

While the benefits of bridging and bonding are associated with contrasting network structures (sparse vs. dense), they do not conflict with one another. Burt (2000) notes that bridging and bonding address distinct mechanisms but that they can be brought together when studying collectives, for example, organizational online communities in our case. Reagans and Zuckerman (2001) articulate that bridging focuses on the presence or absence of structural holes that divide a social system globally, while bonding focuses on the presence or absence of relations in local interactions. In the case of an online community, bridging highlights the advantageous opportunities that a focal community can gain by spanning unconnected, thus nonredundant, communities in its external network. Bonding points to the motivational benefits that a focal community can attain by being internally cohesive.

## Prevailing View of the Effect of Member Time and Attention Spent Externally on Community Responsiveness

The prevailing view in the online community literature on membership overlap underscores the negative consequences of member time and attention spent externally for community responsiveness (Wang et al. 2013). Community activities can consume a great amount of member time and attention, and individuals can only devote limited time and attention to a limited number of activities (Ocasio 1997; Simon 1971). For example, reading threads requires a process of searching, encoding, and interpreting (Alavi and Leidner 2001; Markus 2001). Posting replies involves a complex sequence of awareness, searching, matching, formulation, and delivery (Olivera et al. 2008). As a result, individual members inevitably become selective in whether or how much time and attention to allocate when participating in online communities (Jones et al. 2004). Sharing members with other communities is thus believed to be problematic for a focal community because the members face competing demands on their finite time and attention (Wang et al. 2013; Zhu, Kraut, and Kittur 2014). Summarizing the prevailing view in the online community literature on membership overlap, it is hypothesized that the more time and attention members devote to other communities, the less time and attention are available for the focal community and, in turn, the less responsive the focal community becomes.

Hypothesis 1 (H1): The extent to which member time and attention are spent externally has a negative effect on community responsiveness.

## Generative View of the Effect of Member Time and Attention Spent Externally on Community Responsiveness

From a generative view of resources (Faraj et al. 2011), member time and attention spent externally have not only negative consequences, as previously hypothesized, but also positive consequences for the focal community. In our context, one such positive consequence is the access to novel external knowledge (e.g., Dahlander and Frederiksen 2012). The tension that these negative and positive consequences create has the potential to facilitate greater community responsiveness. The potential will be realized if members are able to acquire novel external knowledge and then share it to generate additional knowledge in the community. Argote et al. (2003) point to opportunity and motivation, in addition to ability, as crucial elements of knowledge generation. We argue that the key elements that unlock the generative resources of member time and attention spent externally are the opportunity of members to access novel external knowledge from other communities and the motivation of members to share knowledge to assist others in the focal community. Taking a social network perspective (Burt 2000), we turn to two network structural conditions that enable the opportunity and motivation of members to access and share knowledge: external bridging and internal bonding.

## A Focal Community’s External Bridging

The external network of a focal community consists of all communities to which the focal community is connected and the connections, if any, among these (external) communities. The strengths of the connections among external communities are determined by the number of shared members. The stronger the connections are, the more redundant the external communities become. The reason is that a large number of shared members blur community boundaries with similar knowledge (Butler and Wang 2012). The fewer the connections are, the sparser the external network is—the condition for a high level of external bridging. A sparse external network offers abundant opportunities for the focal community’s members to identify and access novel knowledge in return for the time and attention spent on bridging the external communities (Burt 1992). Hence, we theorize that the focal community’s external bridging, or its sparse external network structure, is a critical enabling condition for the generative effect of member time and attention spent externally.

## A Focal Community’s Internal Bonding

Another important condition for knowledge generation (i.e., replies) in the focal community is members’ motivation to share knowledge in response to informational inquiries (e.g., Wasko and Faraj 2005). Internal bonding increases members motivation to be cooperative within the community (Reagans and McEvily 2003). A community has a high level of internal bonding if most of its members have built ties with each other through prior interactions (Singh et al. 2011). Sharing knowledge to assist others represents cooperative behavior. Responding to an information inquiry incurs an opportunity cost, demanding an individual’s time and attention that could be spent on other community activities. Moreover, when an individual shares what he or she knows online, the private knowledge becomes instantly public, and he or she no longer has the advantage of knowledge differentiation. The tendency to keep differentiated knowledge to oneself is a major barrier to knowledge sharing in an organization (von Krogh 1998). Internal bonding helps overcome these barriers and promotes knowledge sharing, both offline and online, thus improving community responsiveness (Reagans and Zuckerman 2001; Singh et al. 2011). Too much internal bonding may hinder the ability of members to cultivate new relationships outside the focal community and to accept novel external knowledge (Burt 1992; Gargiulo and Benassi 2000). Yet, this tendency is unlikely to develop in online communities, in which the average level of internal cohesion tends to be low due to the large network size compared with those of traditional groups in an organization (Singh et al. 2011). Hence, we theorize that the focal community’s internal bonding, or its dense internal network structure, is a critical enabling condition for the generative effect of member time and attention spent externally. That is, internal bonding is crucial for realizing the potential benefits of external bridging (Burt 2000; Newell et al. 2004).

## The Joint Moderation Effects of External Bridging and Internal Bonding

Given the critical roles of external bridging (in shaping members’ opportunities to access novel external knowledge) and internal bonding (in rendering the motivation to share knowledge to assist others), we theorize that a focal community’s external bridging and internal bonding jointly enable member time and attention spent externally to become generative resources. As opportunity and motivation complement each other in facilitating knowledge generation (Argote et al. 2003), only when a focal community’s external bridging and internal bonding are both high will member time and attention spent externally become generative, thus facilitating greater community responsiveness. By contrast, when either or both conditions are low, member time and attention spent externally will ultimately hurt community responsiveness. Figure 1 illustrates a focal community under different conditions of external bridging and internal bonding.

When a focal community has high levels of both internal bonding and external bridging (Quadrant 1), its members have not only a strong motivation to share knowledge to assist others but also abundant opportunities to obtain novel external knowledge (Burt 2000). The motivation to be cooperative prompts members to concentrate their available time and attention on responding to the community’s existing information needs. Shared members are likely to devote some of their time and attention to searching for and locating relevant ideas and solutions available in other communities. Exposure to various ways of framing problems with approaches rooted in different “thought worlds” (Dougherty 1992) offers members opportunities to acquire and generate novel knowledge that can benefit the focal community in terms of addressing its specific information needs in new ways (Hargadon and Sutton 1997). Shared members are capable of blending existing but previously unconnected ideas by making connections across community boundaries (O’Leary et al. 2011). Furthermore, the exposure enabled by shared members to a wide range of expertise, experiences, and perspectives can further stimulate exchanges and generation of ideas in the community. Groups comprising members with diverse interests and expertise generate more ideas and discussions than do homogenous groups (Williams and O’Reilly 1998). The need to integrate diverse knowledge and reconcile different perspectives triggers active member participation (van Knippenberg et al. 2004). In support, online community research has found member participation in discussions to be positively related to the diversity of members (e.g., Kudaravalli and Faraj 2008; Ludford et al. 2004). Taken together, we theorize that member time and attention spent externally will be generative, thus facilitating greater community responsiveness when both internal bonding and external bridging are high.

When a focal community’s internal bonding is high but its external bridging is low (Quadrant 2), members are motivated to share knowledge, but the knowledge they hold (including externally acquired knowledge) is largely redundant (Burt 2000). High internal bonding will make individual members remain attentive to fellow members’ information needs (Singh et al. 2011). However, their access to redundant external knowledge will be unlikely to lead to the acquisition and generation of additional knowledge that can be shared in response to existing information needs. In this case, member time and attention spent externally can hardly be generative. We thus theorize that member time and attention spent externally will ultimately reduce community responsiveness when internal bonding is high but external bridging is low.

When a focal community’s internal bonding is low (Quadrants 3 and 4), members are not as motivated to devote their time and attention to sharing knowledge to assist others (Singh et al. 2011). Weak commitment to cooperation is likely to prompt members to divert their time and attention away from responding to existing information needs in the focal community. Members who belong to multiple communities may prioritize other communities with higher levels of internal bonding (O’Leary et al. 2011). Members may also pass on the opportunities provided by external bridging (Newell et al. 2004) or act upon them mainly for personal interests (Burt 1992). Newell et al. (2004) observed that strong bonding among project team members is necessary for integrating different pieces of knowledge acquired externally. Therefore, we theorize that member time and attention spent externally will harm community responsiveness the most when internal bonding is low.

Taken together, we propose a contingent model in which a focal community’s external bridging and internal bonding jointly enable the generative effect of member time and attention spent externally, resulting in improved community responsiveness. Member time and attention spent externally facilitate greater community responsiveness only in the presence of both external bridging and internal bonding. If either network condition is absent, member time and attention spent externally will result in reduced community responsiveness.

## Methods

## Data

In this study, the data were collected from a global energy company with a highly skilled workforce spread across more than 100 work sites worldwide. The company launched a platform of internal online communities of practice in 2005. At the time of the data collection, more than 10,000 employees (or nearly 75% of the employees that the company identified as knowledge workers) participated in approximately 100 communities. The average community had 162 (SD = 196) members, with numbers ranging from fewer than

10 to more than 1,900.<sup>2</sup> Each community was organized around a specific topic with an explicit link to the organization (e.g., chemicals, pipeline and subsea systems integrity, defect elimination, well optimization, and geomodeling).

The company approved a new community only when the community justified a strong business case that would add unique value to the company. A new community seeking approval was also required to specify the types of outcomes that it would produce (e.g., documentation of knowledgesharing cases that would lead to cost savings or revenue generation) and the types of activities that it would support (e.g., online discussions). This rigorous approval process aimed to assure that the community would not waste employees’ time without producing a business value. By aligning the nature and focus of online communities to the company’s business purpose, the company ensured that community responsiveness was linked to generating business value.

With regard to community membership, a community member was considered a user who registered as a formal member and visited the focal community at least once during the study period. Each community’s portal had a “members” section that listed its members. Every member had a profile page that displayed both membership information (e.g., the date that the person became a member and past posting records) and HR information (e.g., job title, position, location, and contact information).

Each community had its own discussion forum. Members were automatically notified via RSS feeds<sup>3</sup> about the communication activities in their communities (e.g., new discussion threads). Any employee could post on a discussion forum without being a member, but nonmembers seldom did so.<sup>4</sup> Discussion topics were strictly focused on work. Common information inquiries included seeking technical advice for problem solving or decision making and asking for procedural know-how, manuals, or site-specific practices. (Please refer to Appendix A for discussion topic samples; Appendix B provides an example of a typical discussion thread.)

The unit of analysis in this study was the community, and the unit of measurement was the community-month. The 6- month study period spanned from January 2010 to June 2010. The study focused on this period for two reasons. The first one was the maturity of the online platform. Since 2005, the number of communities had increased substantially, but this growth plateaued in mid-2009. Meanwhile, the number of employees joining a community for the first time had steadily decreased, whereas communities had increasingly shared their members with other communities. While fewer than 10% of users were members of more than one community in 2006, approximately 50% of users had multiple community memberships in 2010 (nearly 20% were members of more than three communities). The second reason was the limited availability of community traffic (visit) data used to measure the extent to which member time and attention were spent externally. The data were available only from January to June 2010 due to their massive size. Presented in an individualcommunity matrix form constructed on a daily basis, the data showed who visited which community how many times per day.

Several criteria were employed in the selection of the sample from the dataset. Prior research has shown that many online communities have little or no posting activity (e.g., Wang et al. 2013) and that a significant number of users never or seldom post a message in a community (Nonnecke and Preece 2000; Ridings et al. 2006). Therefore, in online community studies, researchers commonly focus only on active members (e.g., Faraj and Johnson 2011; Wang et al. 2013). Accordingly, communities with, on average, less than one discussion thread per month and individuals with no discussion participation record over the 6-month observation period were excluded from the analysis. In short, the sample was composed of 76 communities and 3,190 users with, on average, 3.07 community memberships (as of June 2010), resulting in the construction of a community-month panel data set of six periods (456 observations in total).

## Measures

## Key Variables

Community Responsiveness. Consistent with Butler and Wang (2012), this study calculated the average number of replies per discussion thread in a focal community in month t.<sup>5</sup> The intention here is not to claim that more replies always generate more information-related benefits. Nevertheless, community responsiveness is essential for the viability of a community because it greatly affects member attraction and retention (Butler 2001; Joyce and Kraut 2006; Zhang et al. 2013). Individual members who receive replies to their information requests are more likely to remain and participate in the community in the future because they feel that they have received attention or help from their communities (Joyce and Kraut 2006; Zhang et al. 2013). Furthermore, members of organizational online communities can be reasonably assumed to devote ample time and attention to quality and relevance when composing replies. Our interviews with several employees confirmed that people gave careful thought to what they were writing before posting their replies to online communities, as they were highly conscious of how their replies might be perceived by peers and senior managers. In short, an organizational online community can be reasonably assumed to gain more benefits from having a higher, rather than lower, level of community responsiveness.

Member Time and Attention Spent Externally. The extent to which members of a focal community allocate time and attention to other communities was measured as the relative portion of community visits made by members outside the focal community in month t. The number of times that a member visited a community was assumed to be highly associated with the amount of time and attention that he or she devoted to the community. For each member, the number of times that he or she visited the focal community and the number of times that he or she visited any community, including the focal community, each day were counted. Individual-level data were then aggregated at the communitymonth level to produce the ratio of focal community visits to all community visits in a given month. Finally, this proportion value was reversed by subtracting it from 1 to capture the proportion of community visits made by members outside the focal community. The closer the result was to 1, the greater the portion of member time and attention spent externally.

External Bridging. To capture the redundancy that exists among network contacts, Burt (1992) developed a measure of “lack of structural holes,” $\mathbf { \nabla } ^ { \circ } C _ { i t } ,$ based on calculating the extent to which actors in a given network i are strongly connected to one another in time t. The measure has been widely used in both IS research (e.g., Singh et al. 2011; Wu 2013) and organizational research (e.g., Gargiulo and Benassi 2000; Reagans et al. 2004). Our study adapted Burt’s measure to calculate a focal community’s external bridging. The strength of the connection between two communities was determined by the number of their shared members. $C _ { i t }$ is the measure of the lack of structural holes spanned by focal community i in month t, ranging from 0 to 1. $c _ { i j t }$ has two components, one $( p _ { i j t } )$ measuring the direct connection between focal community i and community j and the other $( \sum _ { q = 1 } ^ { n } p _ { i q t } p _ { q j t } )$ measuring the indirect connections between the two communities via community q (Burt 1992). In essence, the first component captures the number of communities as sources of external knowledge that focal community i could access, while the second component captures the degree of knowledge redundancy among these communities. In the first component, the stronger the connection that focal community i had with community j, the more likely focal community i would gain access to the knowledge base of community j. In the second component, the stronger the connection that community j had with the communities already connected to focal community i (e.g., community $q ) ,$ , the more likely community j would be a redundant source of external knowledge for focal community i (i.e., a lack of structural holes).

Burt (1992) suggested calculating the proportional strength rather than the absolute strength of the connection between network actors, as shown below, such that $C _ { i t }$ varies from 0 (i.e., rich in structural holes) to 1 (i.e., no structural holes).

$$
p _ {i j t} = \frac {Z _ {i j t} + Z _ {j i t}}{\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n} \left(Z _ {i k t} + Z _ {k i t}\right)}, i \neq k\tag{[3]}
$$

where $Z _ { i j t }$ was calculated by summing the number of shared members between communities i and j as of month t.

Internal Bonding. Consistent with recent IS research examining the internal cohesion of open source development groups (Singh et al. 2011), density was used to measure the extent to which members of a focal community were strongly connected to one another. Density is one of the most commonly used measures of cohesion in network research (Borgatti et al. 2013). It is calculated by taking the sum of ties divided by the number of possible ties (i.e., the average strength of ties actually present across all possible ties in the focal community). Communication or interaction frequency is ordinarily used to calculate the tie strength between network actors (e.g., Reagans and Zuckerman 2001; Uzzi 1999; Wu 2013).

In this study, the tie strength between two individual members was calculated based on the total number of messages that they exchanged on discussion forums. For example, 1 was assigned to a given pair of members if one of the members replied to the other’s message. Because online relationships can be fragile, this study followed the approach of Wu (2013), who used a 6-month sliding window (with a 1-month step size) of prior electronic communication to capture the strengths of online ties in the current month. The total number of messages exchanged between each pair of community members within 6 months was counted prior to the current month t and was then divided by the total number of pairs existing in the focal community in month t. Therefore, although our study period spanned from January to June 2010, discussion thread data going back as far as July 2009 was used to measure internal bonding. For instance, a community’s internal bonding in January 2010 was measured based on the member-to-member interactions that occurred from July to December 2009.

## Control Variables

A number of community-specific and discussion threadspecific variables were controlled for.

Community Size and Community Age. Prior research has suggested that both the size and the age of a focal community are likely to affect community responsiveness (Butler 2001; Wang et al. 2013). The size of a focal community was calculated by the number of members in a given month. The age of a focal community was calculated by the number of the quarters elapsed since the creation of the community.

Functional Diversity. The extent to which community members have various functional backgrounds can affect the outputs of a focal community (e.g., Joshi and Roh 2009; Williams and O’Reilly 1998). This study controlled for the functional diversity of a focal community by accounting for the variety of business units represented by its members. Functional diversity was computed using Blau’s (1977) measure:

$$
F u n c t i o n a l \_ D i v e r s i t y _ {i t} = 1 - \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} p _ {j i t} ^ {2}\tag{[4]}
$$

where n is the total number of business units existing in the organization and $p _ { i j t }$ is the proportion of members of community i who belonged to business unit j as of month t.

The studied company was a multi-unit organization with multiple layers. It had seven major functional areas (e.g., operations, planning and strategy, technology), each consisting of sub-functions. Under 25 sub-functions, 131 nested functions existed in total. The second functional level (i.e., 25 sub-functions) was chosen as the basis for functional diversity because employees commonly transferred from one group to another within the same sub-function but rarely transferred from one sub-function to another. The functional diversity of a focal community was minimized (i.e., close to 0) when most members were affiliated with the same business unit and maximized (i.e., close to 1) when most were from different business units.<sup>6</sup>

Location Diversity. Employees were distributed across 138 locations (at the city level). Again, Blau’s (1977) index was used to calculate the location diversity of the focal community.

Number of Discussion Threads. Prior research highlights the cognitive challenge of individuals who confront multiple information requests (e.g., Jones et al. 2004). The more discussion threads, the fewer replies community members are able to provide to each thread. This study controlled for the number of discussion threads initiated in a focal community in month t.

Announcement. Some discussion threads were intended to disseminate information rather than to initiate discussion. A discussion thread initiator was required to choose the “information only” thread label if the purpose of the thread was to announce information. This type of thread unsurprisingly drew fewer replies. This study controlled for the proportion of the threads labeled “information only” in a focal community in month t.

Simple Inquiry. Some discussion threads merely sought yes/ no answers, references, or existing documents (e.g., reports, manuals, or inventory lists). Such simple inquiries seldom drew replies after the first (or second) response fulfilled the request (e.g., “Attached is the installation manual for…”). The study thus accounted for this type of discussion threads. The study controlled for the proportion of the threads identified as simple inquiries in a focal community in month $t . ^ { 7 }$

Joint Discussion. Members of a focal community could initiate a joint discussion thread that was shared with other communities (see footnote 4). Such joint discussion threads often received replies from nonmembers. The study controlled for the proportion of the discussion threads of a focal community that were shared with other communities in month t.<sup>8</sup>

## Robustness Checks

Several robustness checks were performed on these results. First, some replies might inflate community responsiveness but add little value to discussions (e.g., Zhang et al. 2013). For instance, thread initiators might post “replies” that merely repeat their original questions or express their appreciation for the replies provided. A person might post multiple replies that could have been merged into one. The optimal way to address the concern would involve conducting a content analysis of all replies. Unfortunately, because of the company’s policy, access to the full contents of the discussion threads in the data set was not granted. Instead, two other measures of community responsiveness were constructed— one that excluded messages provided by thread initiators and another that measured the average number of unique discussion participants (repliers) per thread instead of the number of replies. Both measures were highly correlated with the original measure of community responsiveness. Neither measure was found to change the overall results. Second, a possible omission in the measure of community responsiveness was the number of replies provided in the next month for the threads initiated in the current month. Given that most replies were made within 48 hours of a thread’s creation, community responsiveness for month t was remeasured by including the replies posted on the first 2 days of month t+1. Third, the model was rerun using external bridging measured in month t–1 instead of month t to control for a possible reverse causal effect of community responsiveness on a focal community’s external bridging. Fourth, internal bonding was remeasured using binary data instead of tie strength. Internal bonding was also remeasured using different sliding time windows, ranging from 3 months to 5 months. Fifth, analysis was performed using an alternative measure of internal bonding based on the average degree of bonding in the focal community (i.e., the number of ties that each member had in a focal community). Because density, the current measure of internal bonding, was negatively correlated with community size, one must exercise caution when comparing the density of a small community to that of a large community. Average degree is often preferred to density for this reason (Borgatti et al. 2013). Although using a fixed-effects model significantly mitigated this concern, an alternative test was performed using the average degree of a focal community. The main results remained qualitatively unchanged throughout the robustness tests, thus increasing confidence in our findings.

## Discussion and Implications

Online communities are no longer viewed as isolated collectives but rather as groups embedded in a broader online ecology. In an intercommunity context, one community influences another community. One way that this influence may occur is through shared members who split their finite time and attention across communities (Gu et al. 2007). The prevailing view in the online community literature suggests that sharing members with other communities will harm a focal community’s responsiveness because less member time and attention will be available for the focal community (Wang et al. 2013; Zhu, Kraut, and Kittur 2014). However, this view overlooks the possible generative effect of member time and attention spent externally, which can bolster community responsiveness through the sharing of externally acquired knowledge within the focal community. The results suggest that this generative effect only occurs in the presence of both external bridging and internal bonding.

## Theoretical Implications

This study contributes to the literature on online communities by complementing the existing theories. The current theory on online community membership suggests that a focal community’s membership overlap determines the extent of member time and attention spent externally in other communities (Wang et al. 2013; Zhu, Chen et al. 2014; Zhu, Kraut, and Kittur 2014). The prevailing view in this theory is that sharing members with other communities is problematic, as a trade-off is believed to exist between member time and attention spent externally and the community’s own responsiveness. Wang et al. (2013) find empirical support for their hypothesis that sharing members with other communities negatively affects the level of knowledge collaboration (based on the change in the number of contributors) in a focal community. However, the literature has also produced inconsistent findings without reconciling them. To their surprise, Zhu, Kraut, and Kittur (2014) find that sharing members with other communities has a positive effect on a focal community’s knowledge collaboration (based on the number of posts). The current study reconciles the negative and positive effects of member time and attention spent externally by building on the knowledge collaboration theory of online communities (Faraj et al. 2011). Member time and attention spent externally produce not only negative consequences (less time and attention available), as previously believed, but also positive consequences (access to external knowledge) for the focal community. Whether member time and attention spent externally ultimately harm or improve community responsiveness depends on whether the focal community can harness the tension of the negative and positive consequences in a way that generates additional knowledge. The implication for researchers is that future work on online communities should shift attention away from viewing resources either negatively or positively and toward considering their positive and negative consequences and the conditions under which positive consequences outweigh negative consequences (and vice versa).

In addition to reframing the effects of resources associated with membership overlap through the theoretical lens of the knowledge collaboration theory (Faraj et al. 2011), this study contributes to the theory by advancing the boundary conditions for the theory in the intercommunity context. The current study combines the theory with a social network perspective (Burt 2000) to highlight the contingencies of the generative effect of member time and attention spent externally. Specifically, the current study identifies external bridging and internal bonding as the network conditions that jointly enable the focal community’s generative use of member time and attention spent externally, thus facilitating greater community responsiveness. A focal community’s external network structure has a profound influence on members’ opportunities to access novel external knowledge in return for the time and attention spent externally, while the social structure within the focal community shapes the motivation of members to engage in knowledge collaboration (i.e., sharing) in the focal community. Together, both network structures form the boundary conditions of the generative effect of member time and attention spent in the intercommunity context.

Furthermore, the contingencies substantively elaborate the knowledge collaboration theory with respect to the role of network structures. While the theory emphasizes the emergent and embedded nature of member actions in online communities and deemphasizes the influence of structures (Faraj et al. 2011), the contingencies suggest how member interactions and relations aggregate to create structural dynamics in online communities—external bridging and internal bonding—that influence the community’s generative use of resources in a consistent and predictable manner.

Finally, the findings contribute to the literature on online communities by underscoring the importance of both intercommunity and intra-community connections as sources of social capital that facilitate online knowledge collaboration. In terms of intercommunity connections, Dahlander and Frederiksen (2012) found that the number of external communities spanned increased the likelihood of a person contributing novel knowledge (to the focal music software community), but they also found that the relationship was valid in the case of technical external communities but not in the case of artistic external communities. They explain this inconsistency by focusing on the nature of the communities: technical communities tend to be novel and innovative, whereas artistic communities are less so. The authors emphasize that the type of external community matters when members span external communities to acquire novel knowledge. The current study suggests that the extent to which the communities spanned are nonredundant can also be consequential for the generative effect of member time and attention. Hence, online community researchers are encouraged to consider intercommunity connections through various dimensions. However, even more important is jointly considering the intercommunity and intra-community connections when examining generative resources and online knowledge collaboration. Considering only intercommunity or intracommunity connections could result in major gaps in understanding how resources become generative in online communities. For example, ignoring the internal social structure of a community could miss how generative opportunities can be transformed into knowledge collaboration in the community.

In terms of intra-community connections, Faraj and Johnson (2011) demonstrate that knowledge sharing is driven by reciprocity at the dyad and triad levels within communities. However, they do not consider the possible influence of the greater intercommunity context in which the communities are embedded. Singh et al. (2011) examine external cohesion (similar to external bridging) and internal cohesion (similar to internal bonding) and their individual effects on knowledge collaboration in open source projects, but they do not consider their joint effects. Importantly, Dahlander and Frederiksen examine how spanning multiple external communities (external network connection) and occupying a core position in the core/peripheral structure of a focal community (internal network condition) individually and jointly affect the likelihood of a member contributing novel knowledge in the focal community. The authors find that, while both network conditions positively affect individual knowledge contribution, a person cannot achieve a high level in both conditions. Dahlander and Frederiksen suggest that studying one network condition alone or both network conditions (internal and external) without accounting for their interrelatedness could neglect an important mechanism that influences knowledge collaboration in online communities. Although their study differs from this study in many ways, including the unit of analysis (individual versus community), the measures of network conditions, and the outcomes, the current findings fully support the important point that they raise.

## Practical Implications

The results suggest that caution should be exercised with respect to the recommendation of Wang et al. (2013) that community managers should restrict, via policy or technological means, their members from participating in other communities. Given the limited availability of time and attention, community managers may want their members to spend all of their available time and attention within the community. The current study demonstrates that a community can actually benefit from the member time and attention spent in other communities. A community will gain a positive return on member time and attention spent externally when it is internally cohesive and shares members with nonredundant communities. Resources would be wasted if members devoted their time and attention to multiple redundant communities or lacked the motivation to share externally acquired knowledge with peers.

This study demonstrates the merit of taking a social network approach to examine the contingent relationships between member time and attention spent externally and community responsiveness. A network analysis of community data including membership, site visits, and posts helps discover groups of communities that are over-connected (and thus redundant). It can also identify individual communities that are internally cohesive and those that are not. Community managers need to consider how to improve the external bridging and internal bonding of their communities. Even if a manager has little direct control over the membership overlap between other communities, the manager can influence the connections between her community and other communities through incentives, for example, promoting certain (nonredundant) communities to members while deemphasizing (redundant) others. In addition, a community manager should keep in mind that her community must remain internally cohesive to maintain a high level of community responsiveness. Internal bonding helps members remain cooperative and committed, even when they face constant demands from other communities. The development of internal bonding requires more than a group of active core members. A community manager must ensure that all members of the community are tied to one another.

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

We note several limitations and suggest directions for future research. First, because the current study examined online communities used within a single company, future research should examine the generalizability of the findings in other organizational settings. A longitudinal analytical approach was taken to control for unobservable community-specific factors, but some company-specific factors may have also influenced the results. Although not formally factored into individuals’ performance evaluations, employees’ contributions to organizational online communities were positively recognized in the company that we studied. Not all companies share the same positive attitudes toward participation in organizational online communities.

Another limitation is that the quality of responses was not measured. Access to the full contents of the discussion threads in the data set was not granted because of the company’s policy. The study relied on the assumption that members derive greater benefits from a community that is more responsive. The non-anonymous context of organizational online communities helps ensure that members provide quality replies, as previously discussed. Nevertheless, a direct measure of the quality aspect of community responsiveness would have been ideal. In addition, although novel knowledge is generally beneficial, too much novelty can harm the quality of responses. It is not simply any type of external knowledge that renders informational benefits but rather external knowledge that can be understood and reliably integrated with members’ preexisting knowledge. Overly original external knowledge deviates too far from preexisting knowledge; it is likely to raise many questions and cannot be used as actionable knowledge. Future research can conduct a content analysis of discussion threads to study how novel external knowledge is brokered to yield high-quality responses.

Similar to prior research (e.g., Reagans and Zuckerman 2001; Singh et al. 2011; Uzzi 1999; Wu 2013), interaction data was used to measure relational ties between members. Given its high correlation with tie strength, communication frequency has been widely used as a proxy for tie strength (Reagans and Zuckerman 2001). Nevertheless, using trace data to measure relational ties raises several validity concerns (Howison et al. 2011). It is acknowledged that this study is no exception to the limitations commonly found in research using digital trace data. For example, the measure of internal bonding did not capture offline relationships that might exist among members. Although far from perfect, this limitation was mitigated by controlling for location diversity. In addition, community visit data was used under the assumption that people acquire knowledge from a community by viewing community contents, but it was not observed whether community visits always led to content viewing that actually resulted in knowledge acquisition. Furthermore, members might acquire external knowledge from other channels available in the company, such as meetings, webinars, and professional development workshops. The study mitigated this limitation by controlling for functional diversity as well as unobserved community- and time-specific heterogeneity (using fixedeffects models).

Another related limitation is that the measure of member time and attention spent externally using community visit data did not capture how much time and attention people actually devoted to a specific community activity per visit. A visit could simply entail reading posts, which is arguably less time consuming and less effortful than writing a post. Initially, the study used a variable to capture the number of posts created by members of a focal community in other communities during a given month. The variable was very highly correlated with the variable “member time and attention spent externally,” causing a multicollinearity problem. The variable was thus omitted from the models. Because of these limitations due to our exclusive reliance on trace data, future research should collect complementary data through survey or interviews. In addition, given the complementarity of online and offline networks in the workplace (Zhang and Venkatesh 2013), researchers are advised to account for the offline relationships among members when studying their participation in online communities.

## Conclusion

Sharing members with other communities has been deemed problematic for community responsiveness, as less member time and attention will be available for the focal community. Member time and attention spent externally have the potential to generate additional knowledge for the focal community, but little is known about how the potential generative effect can be unlocked. The current study elucidates the network conditions that turn member time and attention spent externally into generative resources, thus facilitating greater community responsiveness. The empirical results suggest that a community can bolster its own responsiveness in return for member time and attention spent externally when its external bridging and internal bonding are both high.
