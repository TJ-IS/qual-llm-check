# How self-service technology experience evaluation a<sup>f</sup>ects waiting time and customer satisfaction? A moderated mediation model

Souad Djelassi<sup>a,⁎,1</sup>, Mbaye Fall Diallo<sup>b,2</sup>, Stephan Zielke<sup>c,3</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Univ. Lille (IUT “C”), SKEMA Business School, EA 4112 - LSMRC, France

<sup>b</sup> Univ. Lille (FFBC-IMMD), SKEMA Business School, EA 4112 - LSMRC, France

<sup>c</sup> University of Wuppertal, Walbusch Chair of Multi-Channel-Management, Germany

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Self-service technology SST experience Waiting time Cognitive time A<sup>f</sup>ective time Customer satisfaction

## A B S T R A C T

The e<sup>f</sup>ects of increasingly ubiquitous self-service technologies (SSTs) on customer satisfaction are ambivalent and poorly understood. In the retail sector, such technologies often provide decision support as main purpose (information terminals) or side e<sup>f</sup>ect (self-scanning). This research investigates how SST experience evaluation a<sup>f</sup>ects customer satisfaction with the store indirectly, through the mediation of waiting time satisfaction and satisfaction with SSTs, as well as whether the SST type moderates these processes. Satisfaction with SSTs strongly mediates the e<sup>f</sup>ect of SST experience evaluation on store satisfaction; both cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective waiting time satisfaction also mediate the relationship between SST experience evaluation and satisfaction with SSTs. These e<sup>f</sup>ects di<sup>f</sup>er across SST types: The mediation of satisfaction with SSTs on the SST experience evaluation–store satisfaction link is stronger for less interactive technologies (self-checkout), whereas the mediating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a<sup>f</sup>ective waiting time satisfaction on the SST experience evaluation satisfaction with SSTs relationship is stronger for more interactive technologies with supplementary decision support functions (self-scanning). If retailers or service providers aim to strengthen the link between SST experience and SST satisfaction via perceived waiting time. they should therefore consider to increase interactivity of SSTs by integrating interactive decision support functions (even if decision support is not the main purpose of the SST).

## 1. Introduction

Progress in information technology (IT) and information systems (IS) provides <sup>fi</sup>rms with more options for replacing or supplementing personal service provision with self-service technologies (SST). Many of these technologies provide decision support to consumers either as main purpose (apps, information terminals) or as a side e<sup>f</sup>ect (selfscanning). Self-scanning, for example, allows customers check product information by scanning barcodes, although the main purpose of this technology is increasing checkout e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Other technologies mostly focus on e<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains without providing major decision support (simple self-checkout).

Investments in these technologies pay o<sup>f</sup>, when customers decide to adopt the technology, when the technology provides a positive customer experience, when this experience results in satisfaction with the technology and when this e<sup>f</sup>ect spills over to the company. However, previous research mostly focusses on theories explaining IT adoption [22,59,60]. Accordingly, research on SST also mostly focuses in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factors of initial technology acceptance [14,16,35,38,62]. In contrast, less research in the IS [51,70] and SST literature [13,62,64] addressed post usage behavior. Bhattacherjee [4–6] drew attention to the role of usage satisfaction as a determinant of IT continued use. However, the role of satisfaction in this process is still not completely understood. This holds in particular for the role of customer instore experience with the technology and possible spillover e<sup>f</sup>ects from satisfaction with the technology to satisfaction with the retailer.

In retail or service contexts, perceived waiting time plays an important role in understanding the relationship between technology experience and satisfaction, as technology usage can in<sup>fl</sup>uence time perception [64]. Waiting time is an important issue for retailers, as it is one of the most common customer complaints [8] and has an impact on the intention to use SSTs [15,41]. Previous research mostly considers speed and time savings as extrinsic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of these IT [40], while it focuses less on the role of waiting time satisfaction as an outcome of SST usage and in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factor of satisfaction with the technology.

Furthermore, e<sup>f</sup>ects of technology usage experience may depend on the level of interactivity of the technology. Verhoef et al. [61] distinguish “passive” SSTs that provide appropriate information without customer interaction with the technology and SSTs, which require more active customer participation. Technologies with decision support features (e.g. self-scanning), are for example more interactive than technologies without (e.g. self-checkout). Customers using interactive technologies may attribute a positive experience more to themselves than to the <sup>fi</sup>rm, which has consequences on satisfaction [36,56]. Furthermore, using interactive technologies can <sup>fi</sup>ll time and thus in <sup>fl</sup>uence the e<sup>f</sup>ect of waiting [54]. However, little is known about these di<sup>f</sup>erences, as most previous research has focused on single technologies [16,35].

Summing up, this research addresses the gaps mentioned above in three ways:

\- First, it investigates how technology experience evaluation a<sup>f</sup>ects customer satisfaction with the technology and the store.

\- Second, it analyzes the mediating in<sup>fl</sup>uences of cognitive and affective waiting time satisfaction as consequences of technology experience.

\- Third, it studies the moderation e<sup>f</sup>ect of technology types with di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of interactivity and decision support.

This research thus brings three new contributions to IS research. First, it focuses particularly on technology experience evaluation as a main driver of satisfaction with the technology, rather than perceptions of technology attributes [62] or perceived quality [46]. It therefore follows Bhattacherjee [5], who highlighted the role of satisfaction for understanding post usage behavior. Second, it includes waiting time satisfaction as a critical mediator. Rare IS research examines time perception as an outcome of technology use [64].Third, it analyzes two kinds of SSTs in retail service settings (self-scanning and self-checkout) and it clari<sup>fi</sup>es their moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects on the proposed mediated relationships. These two SST types serve a similar purpose (speed up the shopping trip) but feature di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of interaction with the technology and decision support to customers.

In the next section, we review the literature related to technology experience and waiting time, then develop a series of hypotheses. After we present the survey procedure and measures, we detail the results. Finally, we discuss some theoretical and practical implications of this research and note some limitations and avenues for further studies.

## 2. Theoretical and conceptual framework

## 2.1. Theoretical background

As we focus on two di<sup>f</sup>erent IT systems which are two SSTs (self scanning and self-checkout), we use the term SST instead of IT or IS when developing our framework. However, in most cases theses terms can be used synonymously.

## 2.1.1. SST experience evaluation

Also known as technology-based self-service [14], SSTs are IT artifacts [68] or customer self-service IS [30] that refer to “technological interfaces that enable customers to produce a service independent of direct service employee involvement” [41] [p.50]. By using these systems, the consumer interacts with the technology to perform the ser vice, without having contact with service personnel, so that she or he becomes a partial employee [43]. The introduction of SSTs systems to services places responsibility on the customer [12] by creating in separability between the customer and the technology [49]. That is, SST changes the way the service is organized and delivered [39], im plying a new customer experience [27,30]. If consumers evaluate a SST experience favorable or unfavorable depends on the value that this experience generates. SST use experience may provide customers with utilitarian (e.g. e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, speed) or non-utilitarian value (emotional aspects) [38]. Use experiences can be good or bad, positive or negative [30]. In performing the service, the customer might evaluate SST use as a form of freedom or source of pleasure, enjoyment, autonomy, control, and independence [15,41]. Alternatively, experience with a SST may be evaluated as bad, stressful and unpleasant, especially if the consumer is not comfortable with technologies and their use [12], the use of SSTs is perceived as di<sup>fi</sup>cult, or the system fails or takes too much time [25].

## 2.1.2. Waiting time

For services for which customers are physically present, waiting is intrinsic to the service experience [58]. Waiting generally is regarded as an undesirable activity that customers must undertake to complete the service [55]. Waiting can lead to both emotional (anger, irritation, frustration) and behavioral (e.g., abandonment) responses, especially when it is costly and limits the person's ability to engage in more productive or rewarding ways to spend time [18]. Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rms try to decrease actual, objective waiting time [31], such as by expanding its capacity with additional checkout equipment and personnel. Yet, these e<sup>f</sup>ects are limited by unmanageable factors such as physical space limits or customer tra<sup>fi</sup>c during rush hours [8,55]. Because it is economically and logistically infeasible to eliminate waiting, a complementary alternative seeks to manage customers' perceptions of waiting [18,31], which can be radically di<sup>f</sup>erent from objective or clocked time [21]. Such e<sup>f</sup>orts do not necessarily a<sup>f</sup>ect the actual time spent waiting, but they make waiting seem fun or entertaining [55].

In line with Oliver [44,45], waiting time satisfaction is a post-experience, judgmental evaluation with both cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective aspects [19]. The cognitive component pertains to consumers' perception of the waiting time as acceptable, reasonable, and tolerable [19], as well as short or long [48]. The a<sup>f</sup>ective component instead re<sup>fl</sup>ects emotional reactions to the wait, such as irritation, boredom, frustration, anger, or stress [29,54], which tend to arise because customers perceive waiting as a waste of time, unproductive, or valueless [58]. Waiting satisfaction in turn strongly a<sup>f</sup>ects service evaluations and satisfaction [28,54]. Therefore, we contribute to the literature by understanding the role of waiting time satisfaction when using SST.

## 2.1.3. Customer satisfaction

Customer satisfaction corresponds to an evaluation of whether a product or service has met the person's needs and expectations [69]. It also has an a<sup>f</sup>ective dimension [66], related to the customer's judgment about whether the product or service provides a pleasurable level of consumption-related ful<sup>fi</sup>llment [45]. Customer satisfaction occupies a central position in marketing research and in organizations, because it is an important driver of re-patronage, loyalty and positive word-ofmouth behaviors [44,45] and of <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance [2].

In the domain of information systems, customer satisfaction is a key driver of system continuance intention [4,5] and behavior [6]. In the context of service IT, customer satisfaction may be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by two dimensions: satisfaction with the service provider and satisfaction with the system or the technology itself [52,68]. As highlighted by Song et al. [52], service-related satisfaction (here satisfaction with the store) re<sup>fl</sup>ects the users' feelings about his/her interaction with the service which includes multiple instances (e.g. product assortment, service quality, security, etc.). The satisfaction toward the SST stems from the customers' evaluation of the SST use. The satisfaction with the SST itself may be an important determinant of user satisfaction with the service. Therefore, it is important to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate the two kinds of satisfaction.

Most previous research in information systems and SSTs <sup>fi</sup>eld has essentially focused on one or the other of the both kinds of customer satisfaction. For example, Xu et al. [68] show the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of utilitarian and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts on customer satisfaction with the experience of using a mobile application. Our research extends their work by integrating service-related satisfaction to provide further insights.

Furthermore, in line with Song et al. [52] and Weijters et al. [64], we also explore the potential relationship between satisfaction with the system (SST) and satisfaction with the service provider (the store).

As far as we know, only few studies [38] have considered both the customer satisfaction with the SST and with the service provider (here the store or the retailer) and the relationship between them. Therefore, we believe that it is necessary and relevant to study this relationship for better understanding user behaviors. This research contributes to IS research by addressing this issue.

## 2.2.1. SST experience evaluation, SST satisfaction, and store satisfaction

The experienced con<sup>fi</sup>rmation or discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation of expectations is a key in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factor of satisfaction with an IS [4–6]. We therefore also expect a positive link between SST experience evaluation and SST satisfaction. Customers can experience strong emotions (e.g., delight, disappointment) after using a SST, depending on its di<sup>fi</sup>culty or ease of use [62]. If the consumer evaluates the SST experience as pleasurable and enjoyable, the customer should be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the SST, because enjoyment, as a hedonic aspect of self-service IS use [30,64], is a key determinant of customers' satisfaction [49]. In turn, customers' satisfaction with the SST might in<sup>fl</sup>uence their satisfaction toward the service provider. Customers' perceptions of the SST encounter even might spillover to the entire service [49]. Robertson et al. [49] de monstrate that SST satisfaction in<sup>fl</sup>uences trust in the service provider, and in their study of self-scanning technologies, Marzocchi and Zammit [38] show that satisfaction with self-scanning use has a positive impact on retail satisfaction. Thus, we predict:

H1. Customer satisfaction with SST mediates the relationship between SST experience evaluation and customer satisfaction with the store.

2.2.2. SST experience evaluation, waiting time satisfaction, and SST satisfaction

The decision to use a SST, especially in stores, is mainly motivated by avoiding long waiting lines [33]. Dabholkar et al.'s [16] and Authors [17] studies reveal that customers prefer SSTs (self-scanning and self checkout) when waiting time in the regular checkout is long. Hence, waiting time savings represent an important advantage of SST use [14,41].

Previous research reveals a link between the service experience and perceived waiting time [34]. In particular, positive (negative) a<sup>f</sup>ect reduces (increases) perceived waiting time [3]. In pleasant situations, customers attach less importance to time and perceive waiting time as shorter. In unpleasant situations, they would focus more on time and seek to escape the situation, so they perceive longer waiting times. Weijters et al. [64] demonstrate that SST use reduces actual and perceived waiting time in store. We accordingly deduce that waiting time satisfaction might result from SST use, especially if the use involves a good experience evaluation for the consumer. In this sense, consumers may <sup>fi</sup>nd the waiting time more acceptable or less stressful. However, an SST experience judged unfavorable likely leads to dissatisfaction with the waiting time, which seems stressful and unpleasant.

Waiting time satisfaction also in<sup>fl</sup>uences customer satisfaction [71], such as when long wait times produce dissatisfaction, discontent, and negative evaluations of the service [28]. For example, Durrande-Moreau and Usunier [19] demonstrate that waiting acceptability (i.e., cognitive waiting time) has a positive impact on service satisfaction, and other studies show more generally that a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to the wait in<sup>fl</sup>uence overall service evaluations [28,29]. In a retailing setting, Pruyn and Smidts [48] note that both a<sup>f</sup>ective and cognitive waiting time in<sup>fl</sup>uence service satisfaction. The speed of the transaction in<sup>fl</sup>uences overall satisfaction with SST use [11], whereas waiting time has a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on expected SST evaluations [14]. In a related stream, Tom and Lucey [55] show that waiting time a<sup>f</sup>ects customer satisfac tion with the checker and with the store. In the context of SST, customers may decide to use a technology, because they expect to save time at the checkout. Hence, if the experience use is good without incidents leading to perceived waiting time extension, the SST use meets in this case customers' expectations in terms of waiting time which leads to waiting time satisfaction and <sup>fi</sup>nally satisfaction with the technology.

Therefore, we anticipate that SST use experience a<sup>f</sup>ects cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective waiting time satisfaction, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences customers' satisfaction with SSTs. Formally,

H2. (a) Cognitive and (b) a<sup>f</sup>ective waiting time satisfaction mediate the relationship between the SST experience evaluation and customer satisfaction with SSTs.

## 2.2.3. Moderating efect of SST technologies

Service providers use a wide variety of SSTs, and the nature of the customer experience di<sup>f</sup>ers across these SST types [50]. These distinct impacts of various categories of SSTs are only sparsely re<sup>fl</sup>ected in prior research though. Verhoef et al. [61] discuss theoretically the need to distinguish di<sup>f</sup>erent SSTs based on the degree of the interaction with the technology. In a retail context, self-scanning and self-checkout imply di<sup>f</sup>erent degrees of customer participation. They consist of different experiences with the SST that might a<sup>f</sup>ect customer behavior di<sup>f</sup>erently too. First, self-scanning devices incorporate optical readers that can scan product bar codes and display information, such as price, quantity, and type [38]. Customers scan their products while shopping, such that they gain a precise idea about and control over how much they have spent. When they <sup>fi</sup>nish, customers simply pay the amount due, without placing products on the checkout counter, such that there is less waiting involved. Second, self-checkout enables customers to place their merchandise on the counter and scan the items on their own, at the end of their shopping trip and after waiting in a checkout line. Comparing these SSTs, we note that customers are more active and have more control when they use self-scanning rather than self checkout. Customer participation and interactivity also appear more important for self-scanning since the customer is in interaction with the SST throughout his/her shopping. Furthermore, self-scanning can be considered as a consumer decision support system, as the interactive scanning function provide customers with information that is helpful for making purchase decisions. Previous research has accordingly shown that scanning devices can improve consideration set quality in purchase decisions [57].

In line with attribution theory [63], the technology type can moderate the relationship between customer experience and satisfaction. Customers search for explanations of situations that they observe [32], such as good or bad SST performance. If they attribute the situation to the store, rather than themselves, it can a<sup>f</sup>ect their satisfaction. For example, Machleit and Mantel [36] show that when customers attribute their emotions to the store, rather than themselves, the e<sup>f</sup>ects on their satisfaction are stronger. Tsiros et al. [56] observe that the relationship between expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and satisfaction also increases when customers make stable attributions to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. If customers attribute their SST experience to either the technology or the store, the link between their experience and satisfaction should be stronger. If instead they attribute it to themselves, we expect a weaker relationship. Next, more interactive technologies (i.e., self-scanning) should produce stronger internal (customer) attributions, whereas less interactive technologies (self-checkout) could induce stronger external (<sup>fi</sup>rm) attributions, with di<sup>f</sup>ering e<sup>f</sup>ects of experience on satisfaction. Based on these arguments, we predict:

H3. The mediating e<sup>f</sup>ect of customer satisfaction with SSTs on the relationship between SST experience evaluation and customer satisfaction with the store is stronger for self-checkout than for selfscanning.

Waiting time satisfaction also might have di<sup>f</sup>erent e<sup>f</sup>ects across technology types. Self-scanning customers have already scanned the barcodes, so they only pay the amount due when they <sup>fi</sup>nish shopping by passing through a checkout lane dedicated to them. They thus may spend more time using the technology but experience a much shorter waiting time at checkout. In contrast, self-checkout customers must scan and pay for each item they want to purchase, a task that can take substantial time. If there is a problem with any product, self-checkout also demands assistance from a service employee, rather than resolving the product scanning errors all at once. Therefore, self-checkout use might extend waiting times, as well as make them more unpleasant and stressful compared with self-scanning.

In retrospective time assessments, customers judge their waiting time as shorter if the progress is faster at the end of a particular time interval [34]. Thus, customers should judge the time spent using selfscanning as shorter than time spent using self-checkouts. Moreover, <sup>fi</sup>lled time has a positive impact on the wait experience [54] and can reduce negative a<sup>f</sup>ective reactions to waiting. Self-scanning consumers are occupied with scanning the barcodes of items throughout their shopping trip, and this active participation may cause them to pay less attention to shopping time [54] or the wait at the checkout counter. We therefore anticipate that waiting time dissatisfaction is more prevalent in customer experiences with self-checkout than with self-scanning, such that we formulate the following hypotheses:

H4. The mediating e<sup>f</sup>ect of (a) cognitive and (b) a<sup>f</sup>ective waiting time satisfaction in the relationship between SST experience evaluation and customer satisfaction with SST is stronger for self-checkout than for self-scanning.

## 3. Research methodology

The research methodology strategy is based on measurement theory and structural equation modeling as theorized by [20,23,47]. Thus, we build the research model from theory, then assessed the measurement models (reliability and validity) and the structural models (hypotheses), while ensuring absence of common method bias (robust <sup>fi</sup>ndings).

## 3.1. Context and data collection

For the data collection, we targeted a large city in northern France, where SSTs are well established. The questionnaire was administered face-to-face by trained investigators in a mall intercept survey, conducted in French mass retail chains with similar store management policies (e.g., Carrefour, Auchan, Leclerc). Respondents were intercepted at the exit of retail stores, just after using SSTs. Therefore, we gathered information in a real retail setting and immediately following customers' use of the SSTs. The trained investigators sought respondents who were at least 18 years of age and fully or partially in charge of household purchases. The data collection spanned multiple times and di<sup>f</sup>erent weekdays.

Overall, we obtained 714 usable questionnaires. To avoid respondent fatigue and ensure adequate responses, each participant evaluated the single SST they had just used. A screening question ensured that respondents had experience with the focal store and the SST, whether self-checkout (N = 377) or self-scanning (N = 337). Although we did not impose quotas for the data collection, the respondents were well distributed in terms of sociodemographic variables (see Table 1) Furthermore, the respondents indicated di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of SST usage frequency in the focal store: 13% for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, 17.4% very rarely, 23.7% rarely, 24.9% often, and 21% very often.

## 3.2. Measurement scales

Respondents answered the questionnaire at the end of their shop ping trip, at checkout counters dedicated to users of self-scanning or self-checkout options. The survey instrument was developed on the basis of well-established scales (see Appendix 1). Because these scales come from di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts, we used back translation to ensure clear understanding of the items in the context investigated. The scales were translated from English to French and vice versa. This process allows to identify understanding issues and achieve accuracy. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we took three main steps in line with Hambleton [24]: (1) selected experienced translators; (2) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and minimized cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences by using clear and simple words; and (3) found equivalent words or phrases when necessary.

SST experience evaluation was measured by asking respondents to assess their overall experience with actual SST usage, using a sevenpoint semantic di<sup>f</sup>erential scale with four endpoints: good/bad , “pleasant/unpleasant”, “harmful/bene<sup>fi</sup>cial” and “favorable/unfavorable” [15]. To measure cognitive waiting time satisfaction, we em ployed a seven-point semantic di<sup>f</sup>erential scale with three items: “completely unreasonable/completely reasonable”, “very long/very short” and “completely unacceptable/completely acceptable” [19,29]. A<sup>f</sup>ective waiting time satisfaction used another seven-point Likert scale, with <sup>fi</sup>ve items from Taylor [54] and Hui and Tse [29], on which participants indicated how irritated, stressed, frustrated, annoyed, and angry they felt. We operationalized customer satisfaction toward the SST with a seven-point semantic di<sup>f</sup>erential scale adapted from Westbrook [65] (“not at all satis<sup>fi</sup>ed/completely satis<sup>fi</sup>ed”). This scale is widely used in established IS research [67], even though other scales have also been used over the last years. Finally, four items adapted from Oliver [44] provided the measure of store satisfaction. We also included control variables (sociodemographics and frequency of SST usage) to check for cofounding e<sup>f</sup>ects [7,72].

## 5. Conclusion, discussion, and implications

## 5.1. Discussion

Information system research has mainly discussed determinants of information technologies usage [30,35,68], consumer attitudes [11,15] and resistance toward using such technologies [9,26]. This study focuses rather on psychological consequences of information system use in retail settings. It completes previous research and contributes to a better understanding of the adoption process of an IT. We con<sup>fi</sup>rm the relationships between SST experience evaluation, SST satisfaction, cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective waiting time satisfaction, and store satisfaction.

We show that the level of interactivity (and decision support potential) of a technology moderates these e<sup>f</sup>ects. We con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the mediating e<sup>f</sup>ect of satisfaction with SST system on the relationship between SST experience evaluation and customer satisfaction toward the store is stronger for self-checkout than for self-scanning. The explanation re<sup>fl</sup>ects the interactive nature of self-scanning and stronger internal attributions for the success or failure of the technology usage. Internal attributions weaken the link between experience and satisfac tion, while external attributions strengthen it [36,56]. Because customers have more control over the self-scanning experience, compared with the self-checkout one, they may attribute the experience more to themselves, resulting in a weaker link with satisfaction. Using self checkout is less interactive, i.e. it implies less interaction between the customer and the technology. Customers might attribute the experience to the simpler technology, such that it is easier for customers to form a satisfaction rating based on their experience.

Contrary with our expectation that cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective waiting time satisfaction would mediate the relationship between SST experience evaluation and satisfaction with the SST more strongly for self-checkout, we observed no such e<sup>f</sup>ect for cognitive waiting time satisfaction and a reversed e<sup>f</sup>ect for a<sup>f</sup>ective waiting time satisfaction (stronger mediation for self-scanning). One explanation may be related to the fact that the interaction with the technology seemingly results in a strong experience that can bias waiting time perceptions, positively or negatively. In selfscanning, the interaction with the technology takes place throughout the shopping trip, leading to more active participation by the consumer in the service process. The interaction and customer participation <sup>fi</sup>ll the customer's time and can result in a <sup>fl</sup>ow experience, in which time passes quickly. Thus, waiting time at a checkout counter reserved for self-scanning users may be less stressful and less annoying, as Taylor [54] shows. Filled time feels shorter and leads to more positive a<sup>f</sup>ective responses than unoccupied time. For self-checkout, the experience is less complex and has less potential for creating a biased e<sup>f</sup>ect.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications for decision support

Our results contribute to the IS literature in three di<sup>f</sup>erent ways.

(1) The results underline the importance of technology experience (rather than separated attribute perceptions) as a driver of satisfaction with the technology and with its owner. Thereby, these results follow and complement research on IS post usage behavior [4–6] by drawing attention to positive spillover e<sup>f</sup>ects on the technology owner. In the context of SST, results shed new light on the consequences of SST usage and show in particular the link between SST usage and a store-level variable (i.e., store satisfaction). A positive SST usage experience has therefore a potential to in<sup>fl</sup>uence store choice decisions via store satisfaction (although, we have not explicitly analyzed this link). In other words, when the decision to use a SST results in a good experience, it meets customers' expectations about waiting time which leads to positive consequences in terms of customer satisfaction at di<sup>f</sup>erent levels and thus in<sup>fl</sup>u ences future decisions to use the technology or shop at the retailer. This process underlines that future IS research should also analyze a broader range of consequences of technology use beyond adoption, in particular spillover e<sup>f</sup>ects to providers of such technologies.

(2) Furthermore, with rare exceptions [33,64], previous research has neglected the role of time perception as a consequence of information technology usage. This research highlights that both cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective dimensions of waiting time satisfaction are important mediators of the relationship between usage experience evaluation and satisfaction with a technology. While prior studies tend to cite perceived speed as an independent in<sup>fl</sup>uence on tech nology acceptance [14,16], our results instead show that the technology experience evaluation can bias waiting time satisfaction, in positive or negative directions. Even if technologies reduce actual wait time, their impact on waiting time satisfaction depends on the experience evaluation. Except for Weijters et al. [64], we know of little research into the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of technology usage on waiting time, such that we contribute to previous work on both information technology usage and the determinants and consequences of waiting time [18,28,34,58]. Although, we analyzed SSTs in retailing, these results are also relevant in other settings, such as IS usage by employees and their impact on working time perception. Thus, it is important and relevant to emphasize the interplay of information technology experiences and time perceptions. When analyzing e<sup>f</sup>ects of IS use, IS research should therefore consider time and e<sup>fi</sup>ciency perceptions in addition to objective time savings or e<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains.

(3) Despite of the variety of IT systems o<sup>f</sup>erings, previous IS research has tended to treat them as being homogenous [50]. In contrast, our study is in line with a few prior studies that suggest signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences across SST technologies [12,49]. It follows in particular Verhoef et al.'s [61] suggestion to distinguish active and passive technologies.

Results show that the level of interaction with a technology reduces spillover e<sup>f</sup>ects to the technology provider (here the store), while it has a stronger potential to in<sup>fl</sup>uence technology satisfaction via time perception. We therefore extend prior studies by explaining the di<sup>f</sup>erences according to the level of interaction with the technology and by showing how di<sup>f</sup>erent technologies alter the relationships in our framework. IS research should therefore put more emphasis on di<sup>f</sup>erences between technology types. Research should in particular consider e<sup>f</sup>ects of interactivity (e.g. thorough integration of interactive decision support features) and in this con text the role of attributions and time perception.

Although, we compared only two technologies, results should be generalizable to other types of IS.

## 5.3. Practical implications to decision support

To reap the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information technologies, organizations need a better understanding of behaviors [68]. Retailers, for example, must create positive customer experiences with technologies to produce technology satisfaction and contribute to store satisfaction. Technologies represent not just a source of cost savings but also as an opportunity to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate from competition. Organizations should therefore evaluate the success of technologies on the basis of not just cost savings and e<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains but long-term satisfaction and loyalty e<sup>f</sup>ects as well. It might be shortsighted to eliminate technologies in response to low cost savings and usage rates.

The links in the model also show that customers must experience the technology before it can have a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on satisfaction. Therefore, it is important to approach nonusers of the technology proactively to assist them and ensure a positive <sup>fi</sup>rst impression. When IKEA introduced self-checkouts, sta<sup>f</sup> members encouraged customers in regular checkout lines to try the self-checkout, then assisted custo mers using this checkout option for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time. A more positive evaluation of the experience with an IS also has a positive impact on waiting time satisfaction, such that organizations (e.g. retailers or service providers) can improve this metric by providing a better IS experience evaluation, rather than necessarily reducing actual waiting time.

However, managers using IS also need to consider the di<sup>f</sup>erences across technology types. For more interactive or active [61] technolo gies, the link between experience evaluation and satisfaction is not as strong, probably due to attribution e<sup>f</sup>ects. Organizations therefore might strengthen this link actively; for example, they might commu nicate about their investments to provide a smooth service experience. Actual waiting time instead seems less critical for these support technologies, because the positive experience evaluation biases time perceptions and results in positive waiting time satisfaction. Hence, if <sup>fi</sup>rms aim to strengthen the link between technology experience and technology satisfaction via perceived waiting time, they should integrate more interactive elements (such as decision support features). For less interactive or passive [61] technologies though, retailers or service providers should exploit the positive link between experience and technology satisfaction. Technology experience evaluation in<sup>fl</sup>uences waiting time satisfaction less though, so objective waiting time might be more relevant and requires more management focus.

## 5.4. Further research and limitations

As we analyzed only two selected information technologies, future research might seek to replicate our study with other technologies for which waiting time is important (e.g., check-in airports, checkout in hotels, ordering in restaurants) or for DSS which can be used while waiting for or supplementing personal service (e.g. information terminals). Although we treated experience evaluation at an overall level, research on customer experience in general [27] and technology usage in particular [42] emphasizes both utilitarian and non-utilitarian dimensions of customer experience. It would be interesting to consider and distinguish these dimensions in further research. Further positive e<sup>f</sup>ects may result from the mere implementation of technologies, which signal the innovativeness and competence of the organization, independent of technology usage. In a similar vein, further studies could extend our model by di<sup>f</sup>erentiating between retrospective and prospective waiting time [34]. Future research may further extend our framework by adding links between satisfaction and upcoming decisions to reuse the technology and revisit the retailer. From the general perspective of IS, it might be also interesting to analyze if voluntary decisions to use an IS in<sup>fl</sup>uence satisfaction di<sup>f</sup>erently compared to forced decisions (in our study customers proactively decided to use the technology, while employees are often forced to use IS in organizations). Besides, we used the scale by Westbrook [65,66] to measure satisfaction with SST because it is well established. However, also other recent scales have merits and should be used in future studies to assess the generalization of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Finally, we explained moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects of technology type by di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of customer participation and interaction with the technology and related to these di<sup>f</sup>erent customer attributions. Future research should build on our results and shed additional light on such attribution e<sup>f</sup>ects, for example in controlled experiments.

Souad Djelassi is an Associate Professor of Marketing at University of Lille (Marketing Techniques Department, IUT “C”) and member of the LSMRC Lab (University of Lille-Skema Business School). Her current research, in service and retailing sectors, includes customers' participation in value co-creation through crowdsourcing, open innovation, usage of new technologies, and retail in African emerging countries. Her research has been published in several international peer reviewed journals, including Industrial Marketing Management, International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management, Recherche et Applications en marketing, Revue Française de Gestion, Revue Française de Marketing, Décisions Marketing,…, and in various conferences proceedings (CCT, EIRASS, AFM, etc.). She is a member of the French Marketing Association.
