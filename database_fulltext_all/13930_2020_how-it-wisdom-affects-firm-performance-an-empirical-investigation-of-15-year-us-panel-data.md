## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IT wisdom Meta-capability IT-enabled organizational capabilities perspective Firm performance IT business value

## A B S T R A C T

This paper introduces the concept of IT wisdom from a perspective of meta-capability and establishes the relationship between IT wisdom and firm performance. We propose that IT wisdom as a holistic meta-IT capability of a firm can strategically enable and exercise suitable IT capabilities and organizational capabilities to adapt to changing environments for improving firm performance. We test our hypotheses with panel data collected from 26 US companies over 15 years. The results of data analysis show that IT wisdom is significantly related to firm performance indicated by both accounting-based measures and market-based measures. Theoretical and prac tical implications of the findings are presented.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) business value research is considered fundamental to the contribution of the information systems (IS) dis cipline [14,44,45,70]. Many theoretical perspectives have been pro posed to reveal how “IT” can contribute to firm performance, including “IT capabilities” [8], “digital option” [51], “resource complementarity” [46,69], “IT-business alignment” [61,67], and “competitive intelligence” [72]. Despite these eforts, IS scholars still often confront with the same question – “can IT contribute to firm performance?” – which, to our knowledge, does not reach a conclusive answer.

Once encouraging efforts by Bharadwai [8] and Santhanam et al. [52] provided empirical evidence that IT can be directly related to firm performance. However, their findings were recently challenged by Chae et al. [14] who followed these two studies using more recently collected data but found no direct connection between IT and firm performance. One school of thoughts for explaining these inconsistent findings argues on the challenge of conceptualization and operationalization of “IT” (e.g., [14]). Some IS scholars thus have turned to diferent perspectives and proposed the concept of dynamic IT capability based on the dy namic capability theory [23,65] which encompasses the ability of a firm to renew its IT resources as IT capability [34,71]. Their definitions of dynamic IT capability, while understandable and alleviating the limitation of IT capability based on the Resource Based View [3], still simply equate IT resources to other resources, and thus make dynamic IT capability as merely a subcategory of dynamic capabilities.

Meanwhile, there has been a notable vigorous efort of IS scholars who describe the IT-enabled capabilities perspective where IT can develop and enable organizational capabilities leading to superior firm performance [7]. They argue that this IT-enabled capabilities perspective is related to a hierarchy of capabilities [26,48]. Along this perspective, Collis [21] suggests that there are three categories of capabilities, namely basic capabilities, dynamic capabilities, and metacapabilities. Holland [33] refers meta-capability as the capability that enables and exercises other capabilities. In the IT capability realm, we conjecture that firms can possess and should pursue a higher order IT capability itself. That is, a meta-IT capability can develop and integrate various levels of IT capabilities when needed and in turn generate and enable appropriate various orders of organizational capabilities to im prove firm performance.

As a continual efort to investigate the efect of IT on firm performance, this study proposes a perspective of IT wisdom (ITW) as a holistic meta-IT capability of a firm which can strategically enable and exercise any suitable IT capabilities and organizational capabilities for improving firm performance. We use longitudinal empirical data to demonstrate that, in a changing networked business environment, IT wisdom can be a representative measure of IT which can confirm it relationship with firm performance. Purposely, we respond to the call from existing studies on (1) “explaining the relationship between IT capability and firm performance ([14], p. 324),” as well as on (2) “examining the interplay between IT-enabled capability and networks ([20], p. 563).”

Our key central thesis is to propose the IT wisdom concept from the perspectives of meta-capability and IT-enabled capabilities as well as demonstrate the positive and direct link from IT wisdom to firm performance with a 15-year panel data. The major contribution of this paper is twofold. First, we adopt a meta-capability perspective of IT on business value. With this, we disentangle the theoretical classification and conceptualization of “IT” and propose an IT wisdom concept to further expand and clarify our understanding of IT business value. Second, as the first and exploratory attempt, we operationalize IT wisdom and collect empirical data to show evidence of the impact of IT wisdom on both accounting-based and market-based performance of a firm.

The paper is organized as follows. We first provide clarification of diferences between IT resource, IT capability, IT-enabled capability, dynamic capability, dynamic IT capability, and our proposed IT wisdom (from a perspective of meta-capability) as an expansion and improvement from existing literature. We hypothesize the relationships between IT wisdom and both accounting-based and market-based measures of firm performance. We then depict a pertinent operationalization of IT wisdom and longitudinal data collection to test our hypotheses. Finally, we present our findings and discuss implications as well as future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypothesis

## 2.1. Definitions of capabilities

According to Collis [21], there are three categories of capabilities. The first category reflects a capability to perform the basic functional activities, such as distribution logistics and marketing campaigns, and is called static or zero-order capability. The second category is dynamic capability, namely the capability to learn, adapt, change and renew over time [65]. The third category is an extension of dynamic capability and called meta-capability, defined as the capability to develop another capability for enabling an organization to conceive, choose and implement strategies [3]. Examples of meta-capability include the flexibility to shift between capabilities [31], the capability to respond to radical changes [1], and the capability to innovate the innovation that innovates the innovation [21].

## 2.1.1. Capability and dynamic capability

Capability can be thought as a collection of skills, experience, and qualifications to utilize resources eficiently and efectively. Helfat and Winter [32] stated that “an organization has a specific ‘capability’ to imply that the organization (or its constituent parts) has the capacity to perform a particular activity in a reliable and at least minimally satisfactory manner (p. 1244).” An example of the first category of capability is “ordinary” or “operational” capability, which is the type of capability to “earn a living now” [69]. With operational capabilities, a firm can produce and sell products and collect revenues from customers. However, such operational capabilities are called zero-order capabilities as they are not capable of dealing with changes, such a customer preference shifting.

In turn, dynamic capability, as an example of the second category of capability, is defined as the capability of “adapting, integrating, and reconfiguring internal and external organizational skills, resources, and functional competences to match the requirements of a changing environment ([65], p. 515).” Thus, capabilities used to handle frequent changes over products, production scale, customer population, and environments have to be dynamic and cannot rely on only operational capabilities [69].

## 2.1.2. IT capability and dynamic IT capability

IS scholars on IT business value research frequently borrow theories from other disciplines, such as strategy and marketing, and develop their own IT-based theories. A salient example is IT capability [8,10,49] which is based on the theory of resource-based view [3] and the con cept of capability [27], both borrowed from the strategy discipline. Bharadwaj [8] defines IT capability as a firm's ability to mobilize and deploy IT-based resources in combination or co-present with other organizational resources.

Further, IS scholars have started to use the concept of dynamic capability to guide IT business value studies. For example, Benitez-Amado et al. [6] refer a flexible IT infrastructure and post-merger and acquisition IT integration capability as dynamic capability in a specific merger and acquisition context. As a result, the concept of dynamic IT capability subsequently has been introduced in a few IS studies (e.g., [38,71]). However, their definition of dynamic IT capability (which includes four components: IT infrastructure, human IT resources, IT enabled intangibles, and IT reconfigurability in [71]) leads to a complicated operationalization which may attribute to their inconclusive findings at component level.

Table 1 summarizes the abovementioned concepts defined by existing literature as well as our proposed concept of “IT wisdom” (introduced in Section 2.2).

## 2.1.3. IT capability and IT-enabled capability

IT resources can be generally referred to IT assets which are defined as widely available, of-the shelf or commodity-like information tech nologies that are used to process, store, and disseminate information [68] such as IT hardware and software and IT infrastructure. On the other hand, IT capability is the ability of a firm to leverage IT resources in combination or co-present with other organizational resources to create business values. It is also important to clarify the diference between IT capability and IT-enabled capability. IT-enabled capability is the organizational capability of a firm (both dynamic and operational) that is enabled by applying IT capability with IT resources to meet business needs such as IT-enabled corporate entrepreneurship [16] or strategic flexibility [15]. Therefore, IT capability is one type, arguably very important type, of a firm's capabilities. Capabilities of a firm do not need to be IT-enabled. However, IT capability is a basis to generate IT-enabled capability of a firm. For example, Walmart has IT capability (based on IT artifacts, IT personnel, and IT knowhow along with other organizational resources and capabilities) to build its supply chain system to connect to suppliers. That is, Walmart's modern superior logistic capability is enabled by applying its IT capability and can be categorized as an IT-enabled capability. However, their past logistic capability was not IT-enabled as they started in 1962 nor they had IT capability back then.

Benitez-Amado and Walczuch [7] refer to the perspective on IT enabled capabilities where IT can develop and enable higher-order organizational capabilities which in turn lead to superior firm perfor mance. This stream of research suggests that firms have various cap abilities at diferent levels and IT capability can be an important enabler of other higher-order capabilities (e.g., [7]). In today's environment, we tend to believe that many valuable organizational capabilities are enabled by IT capability as mentioned in existing literature, including business flexibility [9], agility, digital options, and entrepreneurial alertness [51], IT-enabled absorptive capacity, poten tial capability, and realized capability [35], and IT-enabled opportunity exploration and exploitation [5]. In addition, from the IT-enabled capability perspective, Benitez-Amado and Walczuch [7] phrase the “mediation hypothesis” to suggest that IT is too far removed from firm performance for a direct efect to be detected. They also echo the “moderation hypothesis” which suggests that IT resources and cap abilities (operational, ordinary, and dynamic) need to interact other resources to afect firm performance (e.g., [4]). These perspectives help explain why there are inconsistent findings of the direct relationship between IT and firm performance (e.g., IT productivity paradox [12]).

## 2.2. Meta-IT capability

## 2.2.1. IT wisdom as a Meta-IT capability

Meta-capability refers to the capability that enables and exercises other capabilities [33]. In the environment sustainability context, Holland proposes the “Sustainable Ecological Capacity” as a metacapability which can energize and utilize all the central human cap abilities. In the human health context, Venkatapuram [66] refers meta capability as the capability which achieves a cluster of basic and interrelated capabilities and functioning. That is, the capability to be healthy is a meta-capability which reinforces the inter-dependence of almost all of the humanly capabilities and bodily functioning (e.g., senses, emotions, nutrition, movement, clothing and shelter, participation, education). This category of capability is conceptually similar to the learningto-learn capability, higher-order capability [69], and the capability enabling the continuous development of dynamic capabilities [42]. Following the argument of Collis [21], there should be a meta- or creative “capability” which can encompass IT capabilities and dynamic IT capabilities. We recognize that this holistic “capability” or “intelligence” of a firm to strategically create, renew, and utilize its IT capabilities and dynamic IT capabilities should actually be in the third category (i.e., as a type of meta-capability). That is, this meta-IT cap ability is an extension of both IT capabilities and dynamic IT cap abilities and we propose it as “IT wisdom” which can strategically create and utilize all sorts of IT capabilities enabling other higher order of organizational capabilities.

We define the concept of IT wisdom (ITW) as a holistic meta-IT capability of a firm which can strategically enable and exercise any suitable IT capabilities and organizational capabilities for improving firm performance. This IT wisdom adopts perspective of meta-capability and per spective of IT-enabled capabilities where IT wisdom can agilely create, renew, combine, and utilize necessary and appropriate IT capabilities (operational or dynamic) and generate and enable appropriate various orders of organizational capabilities to encounter changes and stay competitive. Importantly, as a meta-IT capability, IT wisdom extends above and beyond existing focus of “IT resource” and “IT capability.” Tanriverdi [63] states that firm can possess a hierarchy of variou capabilities and IT capability is more of an enabler of higher-order capabilities in the firm. This argument is consistent with the abovementioned IT-enabled capabilities perspective. Following this rationale, we propose that IT wisdom is a meta-IT capability and is a higher order of IT capability. Hence, we assert the contribution of our proposed IT wisdom definition over previous IT capability studies in which IT wisdom is in a higher-order category from IT capability or dynamic IT capability. In addition, Collis [21] implies that a higher-order capability might explain why there is no direct link between capabilities and performance. Therefore, we conjecture that IT wisdom can and should have positive and direct impact on firm performance.

## 2.2.2. IT wisdom and network environments

With this diferentiation of categories of capabilities, we also provide a foundation to clarify how diferent categories of capability can directly and positively contribute to the performance of a firm which operates in corresponding environments (e.g., static, changing, or network). That is, a mis-fit of the category of capability (that a firm pursues and possesses) and the external environment (that a firm operates within) might not lead to desirable and improved firm performance.

A network can be defined as a set of actors connected by a set of ties [11] and the actors may be roles, individuals, groups, organizations, industries, or even countries [24]. In strategy literature, ties between organizations usually refer to partnerships or strategic alliances [29]. Accordingly, a network environment indicates a situation where firms are involved in significant connections with others. The notion that network environments are characterized with dynamism is well-established. In fact, existing literature has used the term “dynamic” to describe network environments [43]. It is argued that firms operating in network environments are connected to and have alliances with other firms in order to handle dynamism [17,29,30,50].

Thus, it is evident that the more companies are connected to other companies, the more they need IT wisdom to strategically enable and exercise suitable IT capabilities (both operational and dynamic) and other organizational capabilities in order to react to changes and dy namism from the external environments, such as customer preference (or needs) shift and emergence of new technologies. The role and im pacts of IT wisdom become more crucial in the networked environment not only for coordination and cooperation of inter-firm activities, but also for management of sharing and quick learning with competition. That is, there needs a ‘fit’ between IT wisdom and the network en vironments so any improvement of firm performance can be realized.

This study relates IT wisdom to network environments for three reasons. First, network environments are characterized with dynamism and constant changes [64] and IT wisdom is required in changing environments. Second, the extent to which companies are involved in network environments (the number of connections with other companies) is measurable, which makes it suitable for us to identify companies with fit of IT wisdom and network environments. Third, we aim to extend IT business value research from investigating a single firm to focusing more on the relationships between IT and inter-firm network, which is still in the early stage of exploration in the IS field.

## 2.3. IT wisdom and firm performance

A firm's competitive advantage has often been indicated by its performance that can be assessed subjectively (e.g., using perception of respondents) [13,71] or objectively (e.g., using financial report data). The latter approach can be market-based or accounting-based measures [14,44,70]. Since subjective performance measure has known to be inconsistent [39,71] and a proxy of objective performance, this study focuses on objective performance that includes both market-based [25,28] and accounting-based measures [14,44,70].

We formalize the concept of IT wisdom as a meta-IT capability which can encompass IT capability and dynamic IT capability in an attempt to extend continuous efort in IS field to establish a relationship between IT and firm performance. We conjecture that IT wisdom can enable and exercise any suitable IT capabilities (ordinary and dynamic) and organizational capabilities of a firm which then lead to better firm performance. More specifically, in today's networked business environments where firms are connected via strategic alliances, the ones possess IT wisdom are able to produce better firm performance.

The IS field has accumulated a critical mass of studies that demonstrate a relationship between some of IT concepts/artifacts and accounting-based firm performance. For example, Stoel and Muhanna [58] found that external IT capabilities are positively related to return on sales, operating income to sales, and operating income to employees in high dynamism environment. Based on the literature review of IS studies on firm performance, this study adopts a set of six accountingbased measures, including return on assets (ROA), return on sales (ROS), operating income to assets (OIA), operating income to sales (OIS), operating income to employees (OIE), and operating expenses to sales (OEXPES) (see Appendix A for details). Past studies demonstrate that various IT capabilities can lead to improvement of firm performance according to abovementioned six accounting-based measures (e.g., [8,47,49,52]). As we propose that IT wisdom is capable of strategically enabling and exercising any suitable IT capabilities to adapt to changing environments, we expect that IT wisdom is related to these measures of firm performance. Thus, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1. IT wisdom of a firm is positively related to its accounting based firm performance in term of ROA (H1a). ROS (H1b), OIA (H1c), OIS (H1d), OIE (H1e), while it is negatively related to its OEXPES (H1f) in today's networked business environments.

For assessing a firm's market-based performance, measures such as price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio, market-to-book (MB) ratio, market value added (MVA), and Tobin's q are commonly used. In the IT business value research stream, most studies have adopted Tobin's a as the market-based firm performance) and investigated its relationship with IT media exposure [40], IT investment [2,54], dynamic IT capability and IT infrastructure [71], IT intensity [60], cross-unit IT synergy [62], and IT relatedness [63]. Most empirical results of these studies appear to support a robust relationship between IT and market-based firm performance. The major argument for the use of market-based (i.e., Tobin's q) is that IT can contribute to long-run firm performance as well as firm's intangible value, both of which cannot be appropriately captured by accounting-based measures [9]. Basically, market-based per formance represents expectations of investors for a firm's “future” accounting-based performance. In general, IT can support most, if not all, business processes and bring breakthrough on the way firms do business. The good impression of investors on IT is associated with the expectation for high performance of firms that embrace IT in some way. Therefore, it is predicted that IT wisdom creates the positive efect on market-based firm performance as with what other IT capability and dynamic IT capability did, especially in network environments where IT wisdom fits. Thus, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2. IT wisdom of a firm is positively and directly related to its market-based firm performance in today's networked business environments.

## 3. Research methodology and data analysis

## 3.1. Operationalization of IT wisdom

In existing literature, there are three basic ways to operationalize IT capabilities. The first way is to use proxies, such as IT leaders, as indicators [8,18,52]. The major problem of this method is that IT leaders were selected subjectively, and the standards seemed to be decided casually by the editors of publications [18]. The second is to develop survey-based instruments [71]. This method is still at a stage of development and no validated survey items are emerged. The third is to use media reports on firm IT initiatives as measures of firms' IT capabilities [18,19,35]. Though not perfect, this method has been argued as “the only feasible one where the data collection involves assessing IT use across multiple years” ([20], p. 562) and supported by many researchers in the IS field (e.g., [18,19,35]). Thus we adopt the third approach to facilitate our pertinent operationalization of IT wisdom. More importantly, we carefully go through relevant media reports on IT initiatives to include only those which are time-dependent, suitable, and feasible for the purpose of this study and operationalization of IT wisdom. These three considerations are described in detail below.

## 3.1.1. Time-dependent media reports

This study uses media reports of IT initiatives to assess IT wisdom of a firm. First, media reports have richer contents than news announcements since more than half of media reports are reviews of post implementation of IT initiatives rather than simply announcements of new IT initiatives. Second, the selection of IT initiatives in this study is unique since we only used the time-dependent number of IT initiatives successfully implemented and deployed by a firm as a proxy of its IT wisdom. In most existing studies, IT capability (the capability to develop and use resources) are not really distinguished from IT resources (resources themselves). For this reason, the operationalization of IT capability in these studies are not time-sensitive and all IT initiatives implemented by a firm before a certain year are accumulated as an indicator of IT capability for that year [18,20,35]. This operationalization is appropriate only for undistinguished IT resources (mixed IT capability with IT resources) but not for IT wisdom. Third, since IT wisdom is an meta-capability which enables necessary organizational capabilities to adapt to changes, and companies need to deploy IT initiatives as responses to dynamism in external environments, the number of successful IT initiatives deployed in a specific period should be a good proxy of measuring IT wisdom in that period.

Thus, we counted successful IT initiatives deployed in a specific year as an indicator of IT wisdom for that year. In addition, we methodically followed two procedures to determine whether an IT initiative could be considered as successful during the data collection. One, we looked through the media reports back and forth during the whole 15-year period to confirm whether an IT initiative was implemented successfully and has been actively utilized for the entire period. If not, we did not include it. Two, we carefully read through the content of the media reports and investigated whether the organizational capabilities enabled by these IT initiatives afect business processes such as operations, marketing & sales, service, and technology development. By demonstrating how firms actively use these IT initiatives to shape and improve business processes, we confirmed that these IT initiatives are successful. Finally, there should be a time lag between IT implementation and its efects [36] and we adopted a one-year lag that has been used in existing studies [54].

## 3.1.2. Suitability of using objective IT initiatives

Our selection of IT initiatives over other options such as IT spending or conducting surveys with human subjects can be justified in several ways. First, IT wisdom is capable of strategically creating, renewing, and utilizing suitable IT capabilities as responses to rapidly changing environments. It should not be measured simply by using IT spending since 50–80% of IT projects failed to meet their designed purposes [22]. Second, a small-size IT initiative may cause significant improvement on business processes, such as using a middleware to integrate legacy systems with modern web servers or applications from diferent vendors. In this case, the amount of IT spending can be a biased measure of IT wisdom. Third, the benefits of IT initiatives are usually realized a year later after original implementation [54]. It is hard to use surveys to trace contributions to the sources with this time lag efect. Finally, a longitudinal study design with objective data provides flexibility to conduct data analysis with diferent time lags, which is desirable for this study.

## 3.1.3. Feasibility of data collection

There are also benefits derived from using secondary data to assess IT wisdom. First, because these data are available to public, findings from previous studies can be validated using the same set of data by follow-up studies. Second, the efects of an IT initiative can be assessed in a longitudinal way. For example, after three years of its implementation, Dell decided to replace its ERP system because of mismatch between organizational culture and inflexibility of system design [57]. In this case, using longitudinal data can exclude unsuccessful IT initiatives over a multiple-year period. Finally, IT initiatives might be mentioned multiple times in the same media for diferent purposes such as news announcement or interview of IT executives, or might be reported by diferent media such as social media. We can use these sources for cross-checking and extra data validation as well as elim ination of duplications.

## 3.2. Control variables

Organizational empirical studies usually need to include firm size and industry type as control variables [9]. Firm size can be indicated by the number of employees in a firm [19]. It has been argued that large firms can enjoy economies of scales that might not be available for smaller firms [10]. One way to distinguish industries is to examine their average industry research and development (R&D) spending, which indicates the level of innovations in industries [35]. For a longitudinal study, time factor must also be considered. Thus, we added firm size, industry type, and year as control variables in our data analysis.

## 3.3. Sample selection

For this study, we went through plausible procedures to select only those feasible companies to be included in our data analysis. First, this study selected data sources covered the 15-year time period between 1994 and 2008. One reason to select 2008 as a stop point is that we found a significant reduction in both the number of IT initiatives implemented by firms and the number of alliances firms entered in 2008 in our data collection.<sup>1</sup> We believe that this big drop is due to the economic crisis in 2008. Another reason is that our data collection overlaps with similar (albeit longer than) time period of those from abovementioned pioneer studies [8,14,52]. We feel that the convergence of empirical results with diferent data collection methodologies in a similar time period can enhance our arguments.

Second, we made a list of companies who had entered any number of alliances during that period. Then we calculated the number of alliances in each year for all the companies. This number indicates the level of network involvement for these companies in that year. Third, to avoid unbalanced media attention derived from company ownership of diferent countries, we removed non-U.S. companies as well as private companies. Fourth, we checked the history of remaining companies and deleted those who had lost their identity through acquisition or merger during the period. Fifth, we selected sample companies only from industries that report at least some level of research and development (R& D) spending. Past studies have used the level of industry R&D spending as an indicator of dynamism of diferent industry types (e.g., [35]). Since we included firms from multiple industries, we followed thi practice and used industry R&D spending to capture industry type as a control variable in data analysis.

Finally, because this study includes sample companies across multiple industries, there is a concern of unbalanced representation from diferent industries; that is, some industries might be over-represented with having too many sample companies included.<sup>2</sup> To handle that concern, we avoided outlier companies in such industries applying the interquartile range (IQR) measure.<sup>3</sup> Using these criteria, we obtained 26 sample companies from 19 industries (listed in Appendix B).

## 3.4. Data collection and data coding

Similar to previous studies that used major computer magazines to collect data of firm IT initiative (e.g., [8,9,35]), this study used LexisNexis database, which includes > 4000 news sources and major computer magazines, such as Computerworld (CW), InformationWeek (IW), and eWeek, to collect data of IT initiatives deployed by the 26 companies.

We also collected data of firm strategic alliances in the 15-year time period from the SDC Platinum dataset. one of the most comprehensive alliance data sources [53,60]. Prior studies have used three- or five-year moving windows (assuming that the average alliance duration is three or five years) to handle the issue of underreported data on alliance termination [37,59]. We followed Chi et al. [19] and adopted the threeyear moving window.<sup>4</sup> Further, we used COMPUSTAT database to collect the rest of data, including firm performance, the number of employees (firm size), and the average industry R&D spending (in dustry type).

For organizational research using content analysis, data coding is critical since the process might be sufered from subjective judgements. For example, in similar studies, significant eforts have been contributed to categorizing IT initiatives [8,35]. In this study, since we do not focus on diferent types of IT initiatives, we avoid that concern. However, we had spent significant time on distinguishing diferent IT initiatives. As we adopted time-dependent media reports from major computer magazines (e.g., Computerworld, InformationWeek, and eWeek) included in the LexisNexis database, it is possible that the same IT initiatives are mentioned in diferent reports. We first carefully recorded dates of implementation of IT initiatives and other related in formation described in a media report. If subsequently we discovered similar description of an IT initiative in the same year from a diferent report, we went back the original report to check whether they were the same IT initiatives. Besides the major data collection sources included in the LexisNexis database, we also searched for information in web pages of firms, as well as their social media pages, to avoid any potential double counting.<sup>5</sup>

In this study, we investigate whether IT wisdom is needed by and best fit with firms operating in network environments and explore a first attempt to verify this with empirical data. Before data coding process, we first need to decide the appropriate level of network involvement. We adopted a “top-down” approach where we used the highest number of alliances possible after satisfying the requirement of sample size (as the number of alliances increases, the sample size of available companies reduces). There are two steps in this approach. One, referred from existing studies [19,20,35], we inferred that the possible sample size to capture the efects of IT wisdom is around 350 observations. Two, we continuously reduced the number of alliances from the highest until we reached close to that sample size of companies (across 15 years of observations). As a result, we obtained the number six, which means that sample firms included in this study had at least six strategic alliances over the entire time period.

Data coding was finished by one of the authors and three research assistants. The research assistants were senior business major students with a strong IS background evident by taking advanced IS courses or from prior IT related work experience. All of them received a training at the beginning and then were given a sample of 20 news reports for practice. Mistakes were reviewed together to eliminate any misunderstanding. To assess the reliability of using multiple judges for nominal data coding, a random sample size of 120 news reports were then selected and distributed to all the coders and found the high interrater reliability (ranged from 0.91 to 0.92).

## 3.5. Sensitivity test

We conducted two sensitivity tests. We first performed a test using diferent numbers of strategic alliance for selecting sample firms (i.e., four, five, and seven). We did not find significant diferences in the results of data analysis except for OI/A when we changed the alliance range from four to seven, which caused changes of the corresponding sample size changing from 340 to 297 (see Appendix C). The second test was conducted by selecting 26 companies from 19 industries that can cause an uneven distribution among industries. We chose only one company every time from the multiple-representing industries and did not find any significant diferences by including diferent companies from these industries.

## 3.6. Data analysis

In this study, we collected longitudinal data from multiple companies. It is common that observations within companies are more similar than observations between companies, namely the data are nested (clustered). To analyze nested data, we adopted multilevel modeling (MLM) because it is particularly well-suited to the analysis of long itudinal data [55]. Another benefit of using MLM is to enhance the generalizability of results because MLM treats clusters as if they are sampled from a larger population of clusters. To justify the use of MLM, we calculated the proportion of observed variance between units in the total observed variance (sum of the between- and within-cluster variance), or the intra-class correlation (ICC). Most ICCs for the seven firm performance indicators were over 0.40, with ROS (0.25) and Tobin's q (0.23), indicating that MLM is an appropriate method for our data analysis [55].

## 5.2. Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the IS literature in several ways. First, we adopt a meta-capability perspective of IT on business value. As Collis [21] suggests, a meta- or creative “capability” encompasses capabilities and dynamic capabilities. We adopt this perspective and refer to a holistic IT “capability” or “intelligence” of a firm being a type of meta-capability. That is, this meta-IT capability is an extension of both IT capabilities and dynamic IT cap abilities which can strategically create and utilize all sorts of IT capabilitie enabling other higher order of organizational capabilities. Collis [21] also proposes that a higher-order capability might help explain the lack of direct link between capabilities and performance. We tested this proposition empirically and confirmed a direct and positive relationship between a meta-IT capability and firm performance. Although further eforts are warranted, our study contributes to the advancement of meta-capability perspective by expending its theoretical boundaries to the area of IT business value.

Second, from the meta-capability perspective, we propose IT wisdom, and empirically validate the relationship between IT wisdom and firm performance. This new meta-IT capability concept echoes the idea proposed by Benitez-Amado et al. [6] that IT compatibility, IT connectivity, mod ularity, and IT personnel skills flexibility (which are all defined as IT cap abilities) are the key characteristics that make a firm's IT infrastructure flexible. As a meta-IT capability, IT wisdom can enable business flexibility (a dynamic managerial capability) on all three types (i.e., operational, structural, and strategic) and in turn improve performance (in a context of mergers and acquisitions). This aspect is consistent with the IT-enabled organizational capabilities perspective where IT wisdom as a meta-cap ability (base) can develop any suitable IT and higher order of organizational capabilities (including operational, dynamic or dual-purpose). Thus the proposed IT wisdom can be used as an important construct for future studies to assess the impact of IT on firm performance.

Third, we provide a solid foundation for utilizing IT wisdom in fu ture IT business value research by rendering the following evidences. Firstly, IT is a means to achieve business goals and enable organizational capabilities by delivering specific functions, such as sales and marketing; therefore, IT initiatives, which provide functions to support business processes, can be a reasonable proxy measure of IT wisdom. Secondly, under business environments where competition among companies becomes more intense, pursuing IT wisdom as a meta-IT capability becomes crucial as firms have to enable and exercise suitable IT capabilities and organizational capabilities instead of just relying on specific IT capabilities (operational, dynamic, or even dual-purpose). Thirdly, although it needs to be further validated through future replicate studies, we found that the proposed IT wisdom significantly influences on both past (looking-back, accounting-based) and future (looking-forward, market-based) performance measures.

Fourth, we provide an alternative means to resolve the inconsistent findings of the relationship between IT and firm performance. Previous studies [8,52] found significant direct relationship between IT and firm performance in early years (1991–1994), while no significant relationship during 2001–2004 [14]. With our proposed definition and pertinent operationalization of IT wisdom, our study demonstrates a consistent direct relationship between IT wisdom and firm performance across a 15-year time period (1994–2008). The consistent findings therefore further afirm that the reason of past inconsistent findings is not because of the time diference (diferent periods), but rather the conceptualization of “IT”. That is, our IT wisdom concept as a meta-IT capability and its accordance with the per spective of IT-enabled organizational capability renders a better conceptualization and operationalization of “IT”.

Finally, we relate the IT wisdom perspective to network environ ments. Although IT has provided many technical platforms to facilitate connections among organizations such as inter-organizational IT systems and B2B portals, research for investigating the efects of IT on inter-firm networks is still in the early stage. The inconsistent evidences found by previous studies on how IT interacts with network structures call more attention to this area [19]. While a network environment indicates a situation where firms are involved in cooperative connections with other firms via strategic alliances in order to reduce uncertainty and adapt to dynamic changes, our IT wisdom perspective suggests that there needs a ‘fit’ between IT wisdom and the network environments for firms to improve performance. That is, we argue that diferent external environments require and fit with diferent categories of capabilities and firms need to develop their capabilities wisely. This study provides empirical supports the presence of “fit” between IT (IT wisdom) and network environments. As IT continues to provide opportunities, such as mobile applications and cloud computing, to facilitate connections among companies, our findings can motivate researchers to further explore the relationship between IT and network environments from diferent perspectives, such as knowledge-creation.

## 5.3. Practical implications

Our findings also suggest important implications for managers. Our IT wisdom definition helps managers better appreciate and exploit the values of IT. That is, if managers have a better understanding of a hierarchical structure of capabilities and the concept of a meta-IT capability as an enabler of other IT capabilities and organizational capabilities, they can make IT investment decisions with confidence on the contribution of IT on firm performance. We expect the new concept of IT wisdom as a meta-IT capability can extend above and beyond existing focus of “IT resource” and “IT capability” and help managers transcend thinking about IT as a set of hardware and software resources or low-end capability for supporting daily operations. The better comprehension of the overall value of IT stimulates them to pursue and nurture their firms' IT wisdom and help their firms agilely create, renew, combine, and utilize necessary and appropriate IT capabilities as well as generate and enable appropriate various orders of organizational capabilities to encounter changes and stay competitive.

In addition, this study helps alleviate managers' doubts whether IT can directly contribute to firm performance. Our findings demonstrate that IT wisdom can contribute to firm performance not only through helping generate revenues and reducing costs but also improving productivity of employees. We also find that IT wisdom is significantly associated with both past and future performance. By providing supporting evidence on the direct relationship between IT wisdom and firm performance, this study thus help convince and encourage managers and IT practitioners to continuously develop IT wisdom and update IT initiatives as a means for build competitive advantage in today's hypercompetitive and dynamic environments.

On the other hand, although we demonstrate that IT wisdom can significantly afect firm performance in network environments, we suggest managers of firms operated in stable and not-connected environment should approach IT wisdom with caution. As Winter [69] points out, maintaining a dynamic capability can be very expensive and a meta-capability (e.g., IT wisdom) can be even more so. Firms should aggressively develop and pursue certain category of capability only as demanded by the unique external environment where they operate. Otherwise, with a lack of a ‘fit’ between capabilities and the external environments, firms may not be able to achieve better performance or simply waste their investments and resources. That is, firms operate in a stable, not-connected environment might not need to pursue or cannot greatly benefit from IT wisdom since other categories of IT capabilities can be suficient enough and may suit better.

## 5.4. Limitations and future research

Although our study provides useful insights, it has limitations that are inherent in our research design and data availability. First, this study proposes the concept of IT wisdom which can enable and exercise other IT capabilities and organizational capabilities. However, we recognize that there is potentially a simultaneity form of endogeneity problem between IT wisdom and IT capabilities. Therefore, IS scholars

are encouraged to explore whether some specific IT capabilities can also develop IT wisdom in a company. Second, as we explain in Section 3, even with a plausible attempt of operationalizing our IT wisdom concept and a careful and systematic process of collecting empirical data, further refinement and improvement of these two aspects should be warranted to further validate our results. Third, this study has a lim itation concerning the sample size. Unlike other disciplines, organizational studies are always limited by finding enough sample companies that meet research design constraints or within theoretical boundary. This study included 26 companies across 19 industries in a 15-year time span (1994–2008). Even though the sample size is comparable with other similar studies, such as 12 companies overs 16 years (1988–2003) [20] and 20 companies over 7 years (2000–2006) [19], it is re commended for future research to validate the findings of this study by collecting a larger sample size that guarantees stronger validity. One possibility is to include foreign companies in countries that have the similar culture and business environments with the United States. Fourth, as we mentioned, we collected data in similar time periods (up to 2008) related to past pioneer studies [8,14,52]. While we success fully demonstrate that our findings are consistent across those years, we encourage future studies to collect more up-to-date data to further validate our findings. In addition, we collected and examined data in a 15- year time period only (1994–2008). Future research with data from multiple time periods or longer time periods to further verify our findings is recommended. Finally, while the data support our research model with all seven performance measures, other financial performance measures (e.g., profit margin (PM), earnings per share (EPS)) and key performance indicators (KPIs) can also be considered to extend this research stream.

In summary, this study proposes an IT wisdom definition as a meta-IT capability in an attempt to address the relationship between IT and firm performance. Although further eforts have to be warranted to generalize our findings, our study finds that IT wisdom is significantly and directly related to seven major firm performance measures. We encourage scholars to apply the IT wisdom perspective to extended contexts to further validate and extend our findings. By doing so, this study is expected to guide researchers and practitioners on what to focus with IT investment and how to build IT wisdom.

## Appendix A. Accounting-based performance measures

ROA indicates the earned profit for each dollar of asset invested. Previous studies (e.g., [8]) have found the positive relationship between IT capability and ROA, confirming that firms can garner better profitability through their investment on IT to improve their IT capability. ROS measures the profit for each dollar of sales. The positive relation between IT capability and ROS found in previous studies (e.g., [47,49]), indicating that IT capability can help firms to earn more profit from the same amount of sales. In addition, the negative relationship between IT capability and OEXPES found in previous studies (e.g., [8,52]), confirming that IT capability can help firms reduce operating cost for the same amount of sales. OIA and OIS focus on incomes earned from operations and exclude those from other sources, such as investments. Some researchers have argued that OIA and OIS are preferred to ROA and ROS when measuring the efects of IT capability on firm financial performance since IT capability is supposed to improve the efficiency of business processes and may not play an important role in investment activities [8]. Previous studies also reveal that IT capability is positively to OIA and OIS (e.g., [47]). Finally, OIE indicates the profit earned by per employee. It is understandable that firms utilizing IT extensively may use less labors than others, thus realizing huge profits. For example, both Apple and Amazon are one of the largest firms based on their marke values, not on the number of their employees. Previous studies (e.g., [58]) found that IT capability is positively related to OIE.
