# A MATTER OF LIFE AND DEATH: EXPLORING CONCEPTUALIZATIONS OF SOCIOMATERIALITY IN THE CONTEXT OF CRITICAL CARE<sup>1</sup>

Matthew Jones Judge Business School, University of Cambridge, Trumpington Street, Cambridge CB 2 1AG UNITED KINGDOM {mrj10@cam.ac.uk}

Sociomateriality has been attracting growing attention in the Organization Studies and Information Systems literatures since 2007, with more than 140 journal articles now referring to the concept. Over 80 percent of these articles have been published since January 2011 and almost all cite the work of Orlikowski (2007, 2010; Orlikowski and Scott 2008) as the source of the concept. Only a few, however, address all of the notions that Orlikowski suggests are entailed in sociomateriality, namely materiality, inseparability, relationality, performativity, and practices, with many employing the concept quite selectively. The contribution of sociomateriality to these literatures is, therefore, still unclear. Drawing on evidence from an ongoing study of the adoption of a computer-based clinical information system in a hospital critical care unit, this paper explores whether the notions, individually and collectively, offer a distinctive and coherent account of the relationship between the social and the material that may be useful in Information Systems research. It is argued that if sociomateriality is to be more than simply a label for research employing a number of loosely related existing theoretical approaches, then studies employing the concept need to pay greater attention to the notions entailed in it and to differences in their interpretation.

Keywords: Sociomateriality, practice, empirical, adoption, interpretive

## Introduction

In disciplines such as human geography, sociology, and economics, the term sociomaterial (with or without a hyphen) has been in use since at least the 1950s. Southern (1958, p. 1643), for example, refers to “socio-material well-being”; Carver (1975, p. 47) argues that Marx viewed labor as “part of the socio-material world”; Legasto (1979, p. 229) discusses “sociomaterial tradeoffs” in economic development. The specific term sociomateriality, the focus of this special issue, in contrast, would seem to be a more recent coinage, with the first journal articles using it only being published in 2001 and, with the exception of a small handful of sociological papers, the great majority appearing after 2007. In the Organization Studies and Information Systems fields in particular, none of the more than 140 journal articles using the term that have now been published (see Appendix A) precede that date and more than 80% have appeared since January 2011. The same is true of papers in the grey literature (conference proceedings and working papers), the total number of which is of a similar order to that of journal articles. For example, there are 64 conference papers referring to sociomateriality in the AIS Electronic Library alone, more than three quarters of which are from the proceedings of conferences held in 2011 and 2012.

The significance of 2007 in the Organization Studies and Information Systems literature discussing sociomateriality would appear to relate to the publication of Wanda Orlikowski’s paper entitled “Sociomaterial Practices: Exploring Technology at Work” in Organization Studies in that year.<sup>2</sup> Although the origin of the concept is attributed by Orlikowski to Mol (2003) and Suchman (2007), all but 19 of the 144 subsequent journal articles employing the term cite one or more of Orlikowski’s papers discussing sociomateriality (specifically, Orlikowski 2007, 2010; Orlikowski and Scott 2008), and 11 of those 19 do so indirectly via another paper citing her work. The others just mention the term without citing any source. The centrality of Orlikowski’s account of sociomateriality to the understanding of the concept would, therefore, seem clear.

What is also evident from the published literature on sociomateriality, however, is that many papers are somewhat selective in their reading of Orlikowski’s work, identifying it primarily as a call for greater attention to be paid to materiality in IS and organizational research (see, for example, Barnes 2012; Choi et al. 2010), or as a reworking of existing theories. Contractor et al. (2011) describe sociomateriality as largely based on Actor Network Theory, for example, while Barley et al. (2011) propose that sociomaterial may be considered a synonym for sociotechnical.

To some extent such readings are supported by Orlikowski and Scott’s (2008, p. 446) description of sociomateriality as an “umbrella term” under which they organize research drawing on a number of theoretical traditions, among which they specifically identify actor network theory (Callon 1986; Latour 2005), Suchman’s (2007) ethnographic studies of work practices, Barad’s (2003) posthumanist performativity, and Pickering’s (1995) mangle of practice. This could be seen to suggest that sociomateriality is simply an externally applied label that groups together certain, preexisting theoretical traditions (Monteiro et al. 2012). Any research in these traditions, whether cognizant of the other theories with which Orlikowski and Scott associate them or not, could be considered examples of the approach. If this was all that was argued in Orlikowski (2007, 2010) and Orlikowski and Scott (2008), though, then it would be hard not to agree with critics who question whether sociomateriality offers anything new (Kautz and Jensen 2013; Sutton 2010).

The account offered in the three papers, however, does more than just propose a new label for existing research. It identifies a number of “notions” that are seen to be significant to “scholars of sociomateriality” (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 462) and that might be considered to be common principles of research adopting that approach. The aim of this paper is to present a critical analysis of these notions, both separately and in combination, to consider what insights they may offer for Information Systems research. Consistent with this account of sociomateriality, the analysis is empirically grounded, drawing on examples from an ongoing study of the changes in clinical practice associated with the introduction of a computer-based clinical information system (CIS) in a cardiothoracic critical care unit (CCU).

It will be argued that, while there may be some inconsistencies in the account of sociomateriality offered by Orlikowski and some specific claims in the three papers may be considered to overstate the case, the combination of the five notions offers a potentially distinctive contribution to the IS field. Given the different emphases and assumptions in the contributing literatures, however, it may be unrealistic to talk of a single unified conception of sociomateriality; rather, different variants may coexist. Some of these variants and their differences as they relate to the CIS case are identified.

## Key Notions of Sociomateriality

As described by Orlikowski and Scott (2008), the term sociomateriality attempts to define an emerging stream of research on technology in organizations that is intended to counter what is identified as the significant neglect of technology in the management literature, notwithstanding its ubiquity in contemporary organizational work. Such a new perspective, they argue, is particularly needed

given the dynamic, distributed, and interdependent nature of technologies in use today, and the multiple and unprecedented ways in which they are shaping and will continue to shape organizational realities (p. 437).

Acknowledging, furthermore, that there are many definitions of technology, between which, they suggest, it is not possible to adjudicate, they reframe the issue in terms of “a conception of materiality as integral to human activities and relationships” (p. 438).

This emerging research stream, they argue, differs from earlier accounts of the relationship between technology and organizations that viewed it either as the action of one independent entity upon another or as the emergent product of a mutually interacting ensemble. Rather, sociomateriality proposes that technology and organizations are not separate entities, but are “inextricably fused” (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 463) or “constitutively entangled” (Orlikowski 2007, p. 1437).

Although Orlikowski and Scott (pp. 455-456) suggest that the sociomaterial research stream is too new to have settled into a unified approach, they highlight three common themes that they suggest are features of such studies: a relational ontology, which sees entities (both humans and technologies) not as having inherent properties, but as acquiring them through their mutual and emergent entanglement; an emphasis on performativity whereby descriptions of reality do not merely reflect the world as it is, but intervene in it, creating the phenomena they describe; and, from this, an orientation to the practices through which the relations and boundaries between the social and the material are enacted.

Sociomateriality, as outlined by Orlikowski and Scott, may therefore be characterized as comprising five main notions: (1) a concern to (re)establish materiality as central to our understanding of contemporary organizations; (2) an ontological claim about the inextricable entanglement of the social and the material; (3) an anti-essentialist rejection of the notion that entities have inherent properties, viewing these rather as relational; (4) a view of the relations and boundaries between the social and material as being enacted rather than given; and (5) a focus on practices, rather than discourses or cognition. For the purposes of simplicity, these will be referred to as materiality, inseparability, relationality, performativity, and practices in the remainder of the paper and each will now be considered in more detail.

## Materiality

Sociomateriality is not alone in seeking to counter the perceived neglect of materiality in contemporary studies of organizations. Orlikowski herself highlighted the lack of attention to the IT artefact in IS research in an earlier paper (Orlikowski and Iacono 2001) and there is now a growing body of literature in sociology (e.g., Pels et al. 2002; Schatzki 2010) and Organization Studies (e.g., Dale 2005; Iedema 2007) that argues that a greater focus on materiality is desirable, both as a necessary corrective to the lack of attention to materiality in prevailing poststructuralist and constructivist approaches (that were themselves challenging the perceived determinism of earlier approaches) and as an opportunity to move beyond the privileging of either the social or the material.

Unfortunately, while agreed that materiality is important, the literature on sociomateriality is markedly inconsistent in what it takes this to mean. Thus, even within Orlikowski’s account, materiality is variously identified with the material, artefacts, the tangible, machine, nonhuman, and technology (and there is a similar variation in the terms used for the social aspect of sociomateriality, including the social, human, people, organizations, and work). While there may be significant overlap between these terms in particular contexts, they would not necessarily seem synonymous. Some authors, such as Leonardi (2010, 2012) and Faulkner and Runde (2011), argue that not all technologies, especially digital technologies, have a physical existence. Indeed the distinctive materiality of digital technologies is debated in a number of recent contributions in the Information Systems literature (Kallinikos et al. 2013; Yoo 2010). Treating all these terms as equivalent, therefore, would seem to be a source of potential inconsistency in discussions of sociomateriality.

Further possible inconsistency is added by differences in the meaning of materiality itself, sometimes even within the same paper. The two common definitions of materiality in the Oxford English Dictionary identify it with physicality and solidity. A number of authors writing on sociomateriality, including Orlikowski and Scott, however, use the term to refer not just to tangible “stuff,” or “objects” (Leonardi 2010), but also to intangible stuff, such as data and algorithms. In seeking to accommodate this, Leonardi (2012), following Faulkner and Runde (2011), proposes that materiality should not be equated with physicality, but rather with the persistence of the arrangement of an artifact’s physical and/or digital materials across space and time.

Nor is this the end of the story, since, as Leonardi (2010) points out, in addition to (physical) matter, the term material can also refer to practical instantiation or significance and a number of authors discussing materiality employ the term in more than one of these meanings at different points in their argument. Introna and Hayes (2011), for example, quote the statement by Barad (2003, p. 801): “Language matters. Discourse matters. Culture matters. There is an important sense in which the only thing that does not seem to matter anymore is matter.” Similar tropes are employed by Jonsson et al. (2009) and Fenwick (2010). Even where authors refer to matter or the material, there is still some ambiguity about the scope of reference.

## Inseparability

Notions of the inseparability of objects from their context are traced by Brown and Duguid (1994) to the process philosophy of Whitehead (1933). Pels et al. (2002, p. 1) talk of the current “(re)discovery” of the multiple ways in which the social and material are entangled, and Orlikowski (2007) identifies many of the same theoretical approaches that Orlikowski and Scott gather under the sociomateriality umbrella as viewing materiality as constitutive of everyday life. Sociomateriality, therefore, follows a fairly well-established position in arguing that the social and material are not independent of each other, but are inextricably related.

In Orlikowski (2007, p. 1437), however, the notion is given a specifically ontological interpretation: “there is no social that is not also material, and no material that is not also social,” as she puts it. It makes no sense, therefore, to talk of the social and the material separately from each other; they are different aspects of the same phenomenon, and their relationship is not one of unidirectional impacts or mutual interaction, but of “intra-action” (Barad 2003). Materiality is thus viewed as integral to human activity and there is no social action that does not entail material means.

Even allowing for the difficulties of expressing indissolubility, as Orlikowski and Scott observe, and the understandable desire not to deter readers with too much repetition, accounts of sociomateriality refer to the relationship between the social and the material in many different ways, not all of which necessarily carry the same ontological implications. These include entangled, inseparable, intertwined, intermingled, interpenetrated, and fused. As Jones (2013) argues, some of these terms direct attention more toward a coalescence of elements and others toward their mutual positioning. Similarly, some would seem more concerned with the combined form, or the process of bringing it about, while others would seem to have more to do with the impossibility of severing the association. Even if sociomateriality approaches could be said to subscribe to a notion of inseparability, therefore, this does not mean that they will necessarily be consistent in how this is understood.

## Relationality

Orlikowski and Scott identify sociomateriality as adopting a relational ontology “that dissolves analytical boundaries between technology and humans” (p. 455), associating this position with the work in science and technology studies of Knorr-Cetina (1997), Latour (2005), and Pickering (1995) (and, later in the paper, Barad 2003). Such an ontology they suggest, citing work in psychology by Slife (2004), proposes that “people and things only exist in relation to each other” and thus that “entities (whether humans or technologies) have no inherent properties, but acquire form, attributes, and capabilities through their interpenetration (p. 455). Further examples of the position are quoted in the work of Pickering (1993, p. 567: “material and human agencies are mutually and emergently productive of one another”) and Law (2004, p. 42: “Materials—and so realities...do not exist in and of themselves”).

While a relational ontology may be a common feature of the theoretical traditions associated with sociomateriality, it should be noted that the traditions differ in what they see as being produced through the relation. Thus the Orlikowski and Scott quote refers to the properties of entities, the Pickering quote to the existence of agency, and the Law quote to the existence of entities, each of which are arguably quite distinct from each other. To propose that human and material agency emerge in their intra-action in specific practices does not necessarily mean that “people and things only exist in relation to each other” (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 455). The latter would seem a much stronger position than the former and would imply that the existence of all matter is dependent on humans. That is to say that before its discovery in 1970, for example, there was no oil in the Forties area of the North Sea. In one sense, of course, the designation of the Forties Oil Field and the extraction of oil from it would not have happened without human action, nor would the oil trapped in the reservoir have had any social significance until it was discovered and extracted, but, as Faulkner and Runde (2011) suggest, it would seem another thing to argue that the oil only came into being in 1970.

Sociomaterial accounts also differ in how symmetrical they consider the relationship between the social and the material to be. Thus actor network theory proposes principles of “generalized symmetry” and “free association” (Callon 1986), arguing that there should be no a priori distinction between humans and nonhumans and that they should be analyzed in the same terms. Suchman (2007, p. 269), in contrast, argues that “persons and artifacts do not constitute each other in the same way,” and Pickering (1995) argues that, while human and material agency may be constitutively entwined, they are not equivalent. Sociomateriality’s adoption of a relational ontology may place it clearly on the side of what Thompson (2011) refers to as the process perspective that would reject a view of organizations as static entities with determinate properties, but it does not necessarily imply a unitary view of the alternative.

## Performativity

The concept of performativity is usually traced to speech-act theory and the idea that certain utterances have the capacity to achieve social outcomes. It has subsequently been extended by Butler (1990) to refer to the way in which gender identities serve to create the reality they purport to describe. As presented by Orlikowski and Scott, its particular significance in the context of sociomateriality lies in more recent work in social studies of finance that have sought to show how financial models enact the phenomena they seek to explain (Callon 2007; McKenzie 2006). A number of the key sociomaterial authors, such as Barad and Pickering, also offer what they describe (albeit from rather different perspectives) as performative accounts of social/material entanglement that reject the representationalist view of descriptions as mirrors of nature. Rather, as Orlikowski and Scott argue, “relations and boundaries between humans and technologies are not pregiven or fixed, but enacted in practice” (p. 462).

## Practice

There is a long-standing tradition of practice-based studies of organizations (Nicolini et al. 2003), drawing on a number of different theoretical traditions including cultural and historical activity theory (Engestrom and Middleton 1996; Miettinen and Virkkunen 2005) and the work of Bourdieu (see Emirbayer and Johnson 2008). In discussions of sociomateriality, however, the notion of practice is primarily invoked in two ways: first, in reference to the work of Suchman and Pickering and their focus on the material in everyday work and scientific practice; and second, in reference to practices as the enactment of performativity.

Although Orlikowski and Scott associate this second view of practice with the work of Reckwitz (2002), they do not elaborate the point. Reckwitz’s definition of practice, however, as “forms of bodily activities, forms of mental activities, ‘things’ and their use, a background knowledge in the form of understanding, know-how, states of emotion and motivational knowledge” (p. 249) would seem to already embrace the notion of constitutive entanglement. To talk of sociomaterial practices, as authors such as Orlikowski (2007), Sandberg and Tsoukas (2011), and Leonardi (2012) do, might be considered tautological.

If Reckwitz’s definition can be seen as prefiguring sociomateriality, it may also suggest some possible lacunae in the concept. Emotions, in particular, receive little attention in the sociomaterial literature, including the work of Orlikowski (Thompson 2012). With a few exceptions, such as Schultze and Orlikowski (2010), bodies are similarly neglected, notwithstanding the emphasis placed by Suchman, in work seen as foundational to the concept, on the embodied character of work. Sociomateriality is far from unique in this respect, however. As Reckwitz (2012) argues, the spatial and affective dimensions of social phenomena are generally neglected in much social and cultural theory, but this would seem a particular deficiency in an approach that presents itself as adopting a practice lens.

## More than the Sum of its Parts?

The five notions separately would seem insufficient to justify the designation of sociomateriality as a distinctive research approach, since none of them is unique to the concept. There has been, and is likely to continue to be, research focusing on materiality, say, or performativity that does not consider the other notions. Nor would the continuation of independent research in any of the contributing research traditions seem enough to merit claims that the concept represents something new. Actor network researchers or practice theorists, for instance, do not need to subscribe to the tenets of sociomateriality, or even perhaps to be aware of them, to pursue their own work and it is unclear what might be gained by applying an extra label to such studies after the fact. Some nonarbitrary relationship between the notions that is significant to the conduct of research and that justifies the claim that the various theoretical traditions share some common, albeit perhaps contested, ground would seem necessary. Otherwise the concept of sociomateriality risks being little more than a flag of convenience that authors use to claim some, unspecified, distinction for their work.

This is not to suggest that the five notions that have been identified here as significant to sociomateriality adequately characterize the concept, or that more, or fewer, or different notions might not do this better. To the extent that they are specifically highlighted as central ideas entailed in sociomateriality in the papers that are almost universally cited as the sources of the concept in the Organization Studies and IS journal articles that use the term, however, they would seem a plausible starting point.

If this is the case, then it would seem reasonable to consider the extent to which the journal articles engage with these notions. Are authors referring to sociomateriality understanding it in the way that Orlikowski presents it? This is not to say that there is one true vision of sociomateriality and that any deviation is unacceptable, but that if the concept is to have any coherence, then research adopting it should at least show some recognition of its tenets, if only perhaps in contesting them.

As may be seen from Table 1, however, the journal articles referring to sociomateriality that have been published to date tend to be rather selective in their view of the concept, with more than a third of them (54 out of 146, categorized as “passing mention” in Table 1) doing no more than name checking sociomateriality, generally as a potentially fruitful area for future research (e.g., Agarwal et al. 2010; Choi et al. 2010; Walsham 2012) or citing Orlikowski and Scott as an example of research addressing materiality (Muniesa et al. 2011), say, or inseparability (Germonprez et al. 2011). Even among the papers that have more than a passing mention of sociomateriality, though, only 13 (excluding papers of which Orlikowski is an author) refer to all five notions and 17 to four of them, while 35 refer to just one (usually materiality). Although there is now a growing body of empirical papers drawing on sociomateriality (37 papers, although only 5 of these were published before 2012), only 12 papers engage critically with its argument. Organizational and IS research that employs a conception of sociomateriality that is comparable to that in the literature from which it claims to be drawing or that questions this conception is therefore in short supply.

There would thus seem to be a need for research that seeks to employ the concept of sociomateriality to pay greater attention to the full range of notions involved in Orlikowski’s account, but also to be aware of the variation within it. Consistent with the assumptions of the sociomateriality approach, however, this cannot be done in the abstract, but needs to be grounded in a specific setting. To explore what this might involve, a case study of changes in clinical practice in a hospital critical care unit associated with the move from paperbased to electronic medical records, a setting in which information systems are (in both senses of the word) a matter of life and death, is presented in the next section. The case is analyzed in terms of the five notions discussed above and the implications of different interpretations of these notions are explored.

## Methodology

The data on which the case description is based were gathered as part of an ongoing study of the implementation of a computer-based clinical information system (CIS) in a 25-bed critical care unit (CCU) in a specialist cardiothoracic hospital in the United Kingdom. The study commenced about six months before the implementation of the CIS, but after the decision had been made to purchase and install the system, and involved observation of work practices in the CCU and semi-structured interviews with a sample of senior and junior doctors, nurses of all grades (from trainees to nurse practitioners), and allied health professionals (pharmacists and dieticians). Further observations and interviews were carried out about two months after the CIS was implemented and again about nine months later. All interviews were audiorecorded and video-recordings were made of staff interactions during a number of ward rounds. In total, 37 interviews were carried out, with 16 nurses, 10 junior doctors, 6 consultants (senior doctors), 2 dieticians, and 2 pharmacists. Observations were carried out on 25 days over the three periods. Additional data were gathered from local documentation, such as business cases, training materials, and reports, and from observation of meetings of the national user group for the particular software package adopted.

Data analysis was conducted in an iterative and exploratory manner. Interview transcripts, video, and observation notes were initially analyzed to identify situations in which materiality was seen to be significant in clinical practice. These were then categorized in terms of the five notions and the data reexamined to identify further examples related to each notion. When a rich set of statements and observations had been accumulated, these were then collated to build up the account presented below. This was shown to the director of the ICU and a senior nurse to check the accuracy and adequacy of the description (Seale 1999).<sup>3</sup>

## The Critical Care Setting

In the CCU in question, most patients had undergone, or were awaiting, some form of cardiac surgery, such as a coronary bypass, or implantation of an artificial heart valve or pacemaker. Such procedures were relatively routine at this hospital and, if there were no complications, patients would typically be discharged to a regular ward within three days. Because of the severity of their medical condition, however, many patients were bed bound and sedated or asleep during much of their stay in CCU.

The majority of patients were located in an open ward, divided into “bays” of six to eight beds, with the remainder in side rooms. Each bed had a dedicated nurse and each four or five beds was supervised by a senior nurse (who also looked after a patient of their own). A variety of devices were stationed around the head of each bed, including stands with bags of fluid (such as blood, saline, or parenteral nutrition), syringe drivers, and a monitor displaying the patient’s vital signs (such as heart rate, blood pressure, blood oxygenation, and body temperature) as colored lines tracking across the screen and as numbers. Lines from the fluid drips and syringe drivers fed into a central venous catheter in the patient’s chest and cables attached to sensors (on, or sometimes in, the patient’s body) also connected the patient to the monitor. In addition to receiving medication and monitoring, patients might need to be supported with breathing by a ventilator (via either a face mask or tracheal intubation), kidney function by hemofiltration, or heart/lung function by extra corporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Care of the patient involved looking after not just the patient’s body, but also these different devices on which their continuing survival depended. As this illustrates, space around the bed was quite restricted and equipment could sometimes obstruct staff in getting access to the patient’s body for examinations or to connect a new line.

Where aspects of the patient’s condition, such as blood gases and chemistry, could not be so directly monitored, nurses took samples from the patient for analysis, either on devices, such as a blood gas analyzer situated on the CCU, or in central hospital laboratories. Radiographers came onto the CCU regularly to take X-rays with mobile equipment, the images from which were stored on the hospital’s Picture Archiving Computer System (PACS). To see sufficient detail, however, images generally had to be displayed on a higher resolution screen than was available on a standard desktop PC, and only two such screens were installed on the CCU, one in the doctors’ office and the other adjacent to the central nursing station.

Prior to the introduction of the CIS, a trolley was positioned at the foot of each bed, on top of which was spread a large (A3) paper chart. Nurses took observations (“obs”) of the patient’s vital signs from the bedside monitor and from measurements they took themselves, typically hourly throughout the day, and entered the values, by hand, on the chart, plotting some of them graphically. They also recorded the administration of medicines, the patient’s fluid intake and output (working out the balance with a hand calculator), and wrote notes on the patient’s condition. The extensive layout of the record meant that almost all of the critical data were visible at one glance and data items in different parts of the record could be easily compared.

One page of this chart was completed over the course of 24 hours and was then replaced by a fresh one. The previous days’ charts were kept in a pile under the current one, but were rarely consulted as it was difficult to track data over several different sheets. Nursing notes were written on the reverse of the sheet, although it was said that doctors never looked at that side.

Placed on top of the chart (or in a rack attached to the trolley) were folders (containing, for example, printouts from the blood gas analyzer or biochemistry or pathology reports) and charts, such as the drug chart (recording all the medication prescribed) and the “plan for the day” (recording key treatment decisions made at the morning ward round). Being on loose pieces of paper or card, however, these could sometimes be misplaced, wasting staff time and causing frustration in trying to locate them. As doctors wrote prescriptions on the drug chart by hand, these were also not always legible and many lacked one or more items of essential information such as the administration route, the time to be given, the dose, or who had prescribed the drug.

Although hand-written records could sometimes be illegible, incomplete, or go missing, they also offered significant flexibility. If “obs” could not be done on time, due to other pressing commitments, they could be entered later, for example, or if a drug dose was missed because the patient was unable to receive it, this could be explained in the record. Nurses also had considerable space in the record for free text and could write extensive or concise notes as they preferred, or as time permitted.

As part of the introduction of the CIS, interfaces were developed for most, but not all, of the devices and laboratory services so that data were uploaded directly into the record, although a few still required values to be entered from device displays or print-outs. Transcription errors were thus largely eliminated and a much more detailed trace of the patient’s condition was recorded. Although nurses still had to validate the data at regular intervals, calculations, such as fluid balances, were performed automatically and data could be displayed in different formats and over any chosen time period. There was still space in the record for nursing notes to be entered, but this was more limited than on the paper chart. Most nurses also reported that typing notes was considerably slower than handwriting, with the result that they tended to write much less. While some expressed concerns at this restriction on their ability to document their care fully, others argued that more concise notes were desirable as extensive free text notes tended to be disregarded. Nurses also complained of the difficulties of using keyboards on night shifts, when lighting in the unit was dimmed to allow patients to rest, and expressed concerns about keeping keyboards clean to stop the spread of infection.

Drug charts and the “plan for the day” were incorporated in the CIS, so the old paper charts were eliminated and replaced by a computer and screen mounted on a trolley positioned at the foot of each bed. Prescribing, too, was now done through the CIS so prescriptions were always legible, complete, and traceable to a named individual. With the CIS accessible from any computer on the CCU (including the doctors’ office), there was also no longer a problem of missing records.

The particular CIS had been purchased because of its customizability and considerable efforts were spent to adapt it to fit with the CCU’s working practices and to support evidencebased care. This included designing the main screen of the CIS (“plan A”) to look similar to the old paper chart, providing a short cut for common prescriptions with pre-filled (but modifiable) boxes and checklists of standard procedures, such as admitting a patient onto the CCU. Nursing records were highly structured too, with prompts for all the data needing to be captured or processed and certain activities made obligatory, requiring nurses to tick boxes to say that they had undertaken them before allowing other data to be entered. Recognizing doctors’ clinical autonomy, the CIS did not prevent them from carrying out most actions (although it might alert them to potential allergic reactions or drug interactions), but just made it more difficult to do things that were not considered best practice. Drugs that were not on the recommended protocol, for example, were purposefully made harder (but not impossible) to prescribe.

Compared to the single large sheet of paper (and ancillary paperwork) of the pre-CIS record, the CIS data were spread over 18 different “screens,” each indicated by a tab on the CIS display. Six of these screens showed three different sets of data in two different formats, either as numbers or graphically, allowing clinicians to choose their preferred presentation. A numerical screen might have 40 different variables shown, with up to 12 (generally hourly) periods of observation visible at one time. The font size and the viewing angle of the display made it difficult for specific values to be read by more than one or two people at the same time.

With graphical displays of the data and the ability to examine and scroll through the data over time, it was much easier to explore trends in the patient’s condition than with the paper record. Although these new representations and the more detailed data available were generally seen as enhancing patient care by providing a richer picture of the patient’s situation, there was also the recognized danger of becoming too focused on the data to the potential neglect of a more holistic appreciation of their condition and hence of “treating the numbers rather than the patient.” Indeed, it was reported that on one occasion a ward round had nearly completed deciding on the treatment of a patient when it was noticed that the data they had been discussing related to the previous day.

Some nurses also found the greater volume and historical depth of data available daunting and expressed concern that they no longer felt that they knew “everything there was to know about a patient.” One senior nurse, in particular, who was expected to oversee the work of a number of colleagues, found this perceived loss of control and competence highly distressing.

Most nurses, however, welcomed (or at least tolerated) the CIS and felt that it freed them up to spend more time in direct care of their patients. In practice, however, these time savings tended to be offset by increases in the time taken to carry out other tasks, such as documentation, and new delays due to the need to log in, waiting for data to load or screens to refresh and the comprehensiveness of the documentation that the CIS enforced, with many boxes to be ticked. Nurses also commented that because not all data could now be viewed simultaneously, time was taken in clicking between different screens and that this made it harder to compare different parts of the record.

A few nurses were said to pay particular attention to ensuring that the CIS record was complete and up to date, almost, it was perceived by some, at the expense of their care of patients. Some nurses were also identified as failing to look beyond the CIS to assess their patient’s condition directly. This was not seen as a particular effect of the introduction of the CIS, however, as similar behavior was recognized as having occurred with the paper record, but the computer screen now offered a new barrier, behind which the nurse could “hide” (both literally and figuratively). Indeed some patients’ relatives joked that nurses using the CIS were playing computer games.

Other staff, in contrast, expressed concern that the CIS distracted them from their physical care of patients and could make them feel more emotionally detached, slipping into seeing the patient as a set of data rather than a person. Therefore, they made a conscious effort to take time regularly to talk to the patient, if that was possible, and if not, at least to try to make them as comfortable as possible They also stressed the importance of touching and examining the patient’s body to check that the interpretation of the patient’s condition that they drew from the record was consistent with their personal observation. There were times, however, when the detachment promoted by the CIS’s data-centric approach to care was considered to be beneficial. A senior doctor commented, for example, that it could help to ease the distress when a patient died.

One aspect of clinical practice that the CIS was seen as having particularly affected was that of the ward round. These are the formal occasions at which the progress and treatment of patients is reviewed. They serve an important role in decision making about patient care, education of junior staff, patient contact, and multiprofessional team building and communication. In the CCU, they were held four times in each 24 hour period.

On most ward rounds, senior and junior doctors, accompanied by a senior nurse and a number of allied health professionals (such as dieticians, pharmacists, and physiotherapists), would visit each patient’s bed to review the patient’s condition (as presented in the clinical record and perhaps from examination of the patient), discuss the patient’s progress (or lack of it), and agree to any changes in treatment. At the bedside they would be joined by the nurse caring for that patient and by the senior nurse responsible for supervising care in that bay. There were typically at least 6 people grouped in a semicircle around the foot of the bed and sometimes more than 20 (in the case of the morning grand ward round when several surgeons, junior doctors, and medical students might join the group).

Even with the smallest group, the ability of all ward round participants to study the patient record at the same time was limited. With the large format paper chart, however, they had potentially been able to see what area of the chart was being discussed by the main protagonists (usually the senior and junior doctors) and maybe some general trends on the graphs. The existence of some data on separate charts also enabled the possibility of parallel review and amendment of the drug chart, for example, while the observation chart was being discussed by others.

With the limited viewing angle of the CIS display, however, only a few people could see the record at all, and the screens selected would depend on the individual (usually the senior doctor) controlling the keyboard and mouse. Video evidence of ward rounds showed that other group members, unable to see what was being referred to, tended to become disengaged from discussions and to just pay attention if asked for information or their opinion and when final decisions on the patient’s treatment were being summarized before the round moved on to the next patient. With more data available from the CIS, discussions also tended to be prolonged, slowing the ward round and exacerbating the detachment of other staff. Once this was drawn to the notice of senior doctors a number of them sought to reconfigure the ward round arrangement to allow more staff to have sight of the computer screen, if not to see all the detail, and to encourage more participation. This was not a universal response, however, with some senior doctors showing little concern with engaging other staff and others, conducting ward rounds on the CCU only occasionally, and happy to let junior colleagues use the CIS for them. As a result, only doctors tended to be involved in discussions on their ward rounds and other staff were excluded.

Being unable to view or access the CIS record during the ward round, staff began to review each patient’s record before it took place. They would then spend the time in the round focusing on the decisions being made (and recording these and the actions that they would need to take as a consequence in their personal documentation—often scraps of paper kept in their pocket or, in some circumstances, any available surface that could be written on, such as their hand or a cardboard kidney dish) and then making their own changes to the CIS record after the round was over. Activities previously done in parallel were thus now being undertaken in series.

One of the ward rounds was usually conducted as a “sitting round,” that is, the doctors and the senior nurse met in the doctors’ office and discussed each patient’s case, perhaps consulting documentation or x-ray results (that could be accessed in the office, but not at each bedside). With the introduction of the CIS, a number of nurses expressed concern that there was a danger of doctors doing more and more of their work remotely, making decisions without viewing the patient and without communicating with nurses. While some doctors stressed the continuing need to see every patient before making any decision, others welcomed not having to visit the bedside every time there was a minor change to treatment requiring their authorization, as this gave them a chance to escape the interruptions, distractions and additional tasks they would be asked to do whenever they ventured onto the CCU.

While the introduction of the CIS was associated with some potentially significant changes in work practices, it should be emphasized that the overall character of clinical work in the CCU remained largely unchanged. Thus, the great majority of nursing time, whether using paper or computer-based records, was devoted to the delivery of treatment (such as preparing and administering drugs, setting up and operating equipment, and collecting samples for analysis) and to physical care of the patient (such as cleaning, changing dressings and bedding, turning the patient, or getting them in or out of bed). Similarly, for doctors, while their actions might be recorded on a computer, rather than on paper, their tasks of diagnosis, decision making, prescription of drugs, and administration of certain procedures continued much as before.

This is not to suggest, however, that the shift from paper to computer-based records was not consequential. Much of the clinical work in CCU is based on what is presented in the record. The narrative of the patient’s condition that it provides is a key influence on diagnosis and treatment decisions, and the record of these decisions, along with the narrative, guides nurses’ work.

The record also plays a crucial role as the formal account of the patient’s treatment, both informing clinical staff subsequently involved in their care and providing evidence of the care the patients have received. In this latter case, the completeness of the record may be crucial if there is ever any cause to question the adequacy of a patient’s treatment—for example, in a coroner’s investigation. As a result, for some doctors, the CIS was seen as promoting defensive medicine, ensuring that they could demonstrate that their work conformed to evidence-based practices, even if they were not convinced that this was necessarily always what was best for a particular patient.

## Analysis

Following the earlier account of the key notions of sociomateriality, the analysis will be organized initially around these. In each case, since the introduction of the CIS amounted to a change in the form (from paper to computerbased), not the existence, of the information system, the focus will be on the ways in which these notions offer insight on the practice changes associated with the new arrangements. Such a focus, it should be acknowledged, provides only a very partial account of sociomaterial intra-action in the CCU. There is much, from the relationship between the spatial layout of the unit and the communication patterns among staff to the difficulties of inserting a peripheral venous catheter in the arm of an elderly patient, that it omits. Given the aim to explore the potential contribution of sociomateriality to Information Systems research specifically, however, such exclusions may be justified, even though they necessarily narrow the analysis.

## Materiality

The introduction of the CIS in the CCU provides a number of examples of the ways in which materiality was associated with significant changes in work practices. Viewing materiality initially in terms of the physical characteristics of the record, the move from a large sheet of paper, laid horizontally, showing all the main data considered pertinent to the patient’s care, to a vertically positioned computer screen, visible by only a few people and with more data presented over a number of different screens, contributed to a reorganization of ward round activities, both in terms of the configuration of the group around the record and in the temporal ordering of work.

It was not simply the materiality of the record itself that contributed to these changes, but also the spatial layout of the unit. As shown in Figure 1, for example, the gap between beds was quite narrow and often obstructed by equipment. Apart from staff directly carrying out procedures on the patient’s body or attending to equipment, observation of the patient (and use of the record) occurred from the central area of the bay. It was there, too, that the ward round group would congregate. Even with the paper charts it was only possible for a few staff to have an unobstructed view of the record, a situation that was exacerbated with the introduction of the CIS.

The physical qualities of the technology used to store and display the record were also evident in the barrier that the computer screen interposed between the nurse and the patient (compared to the paper chart spread out on a trolley). Thus, while the computer carts were sufficiently mobile that they could be positioned such that the nurse could observe the patient while using the CIS, the relatives’ comments about nurses playing computer games suggests that at least some nurses’ use of the CIS was perceived as diverting attention from the patient. The greater attention needed by staff in entering data using a keyboard also made it harder to maintain peripheral observation of the patient, or to use the CIS in low lighting conditions at night.

Even viewing materiality in terms of physicality, however, does not mean that the record should be viewed as simply charts or screens. The decisions and “to-do lists” that staff recorded in their personal documentation, for example, were an important adjunct to the formal record in shaping work practices. An understanding of the role of materiality in the enactment of critical care practice needs to extend beyond a focus on (information) technology.

Furthermore, practice changes associated with the introduction of electronic records were not related only to the physical qualities of technology but also to a move from physical to nonphysical materiality (at least in the sense proposed by Leonardi 2012). Thus the richness of accessible data and alternative presentation formats provided by the CIS, and the remote accessibility of the record were both seen as contributing to changes in clinical practice. While, for example, clinicians could use the CIS without accessing any more detail or patient history than was available with the paper record (indeed, the front screen of the CIS was designed to replicate what had been presented on paper), or without viewing data in formats that they were not familiar with, the fact that more data, over an extended time period, could be viewed in new ways allowed new possibilities for exploring the patient record that some clinicians made extensive use of, albeit at the cost of longer ward rounds. The automatic plotting of trends and calculation of balances that were previously done manually also relieved staff of cognitive load and made it easier to track clinically significant changes in the patient’s condition.

Not all practice changes associated with nonphysical materiality of records were universally welcomed, though. Prior to the introduction of the CIS, access to the record required a visit to the patient’s bedside and then, potentially, time wasted in trying to locate mislaid paperwork. While this meant that any amendment to the record would be made in the presence of the patient, making it more likely that decisions would be checked against the visible evidence of the patients’ condition rather than relying solely on the data in the record, the time spent visiting the bedside and the attendant distractions and interruptions disrupted doctors’ work practices. With the record accessible remotely, such disruption was reduced and the problem of missing records eliminated, but so too were opportunities for observation of the patient or of informal interaction with colleagues that might be pertinent to decision making.

More than just reconfiguring existing practices, however, the introduction of the CIS may be seen as creating a new, digital materiality, a virtual patient, represented by the CIS data, that became increasingly central to care practices. Following Zuboff (1988), we may refer to this textualization of the patient’s condition as a process of “informating,” rendering the patient visible in new ways. It is not that these traces, or some subset of them, did not necessarily exist before the CIS was introduced, but the richness of detail and the integration of data in one place created a more immersive and persuasive representation toward which clinical attention was increasingly drawn. While some medical staff feared the consequences of treating the numbers rather than the patient, others argued that so long as common sense was not abandoned and reasonable checks were made that data were reliable, then the CIS provided a qualitatively better understanding of the patient than would be available from observation alone or combined with the fragmented data of the paper record.

It should be emphasized that this is not intended as a complete listing of all possible forms of materiality, nor does it consider materiality in the secondary sense of practical instantiation of significance. Rather the aim is to illustrate the potential range of phenomena that a focus on materiality might draw attention to.

## Inseparability

If it is accepted that the practice of clinical care necessarily involves embodied actors in a particular physical setting, then it would be difficult to conceive how it could be considered independently of materiality. Nor, in such a setting, would it seem feasible to consider materiality that was independent of the social, even if the relevant intra-actions may be temporally and spatially remote from the immediate context. Thus the particular layout of the unit, the particular devices that the CIS was able to interface with, or the resolution of PACS images, all of which were significant in clinical practices, may appear to have the character of material “givens” in the CCU, but were the product of decisions made by human designers at some earlier point in time.

For most staff, the performance of their work was also inseparable from technology. While this was most evident with respect to the devices, such as syringe drivers and drips, used to administer medication or to sustain vital functions, such as hemofiltration or ventilation, the operation and maintenance of which took up a considerable amount of nurses’ time, it was also the case for patient records, whether on paper or computer-based. This reflected not just the time taken in entering or validating data, but the dependence of many work activities on the evidence provided by the record, whether directly (for example, where the record indicated the timing and dosage of medication to be administered) or indirectly (for example, where the trend in a vital function might prompt a particular intervention, such as adjustment of the ventilation rate). The data held in the record was a key element in the medical staff’s understanding of the patient’s condition on which they based their actions. All work entailed material means, therefore, and it would not make sense to talk about clinical care in the CCU in terms of either of them separately.

While both the paper and computer-based records were critical to clinical practice, such that staff found it difficult, if not impossible, to carry out their work if the record was unavailable (for example, due to paperwork being mislaid or a computer crash), it was evident that dependence on the CIS was greater than with the paper record. In part this reflected the greater comprehensiveness of the CIS record and the consequent perceived integrity of the derived understanding of the patient’s condition, but also the greater reliance of work practices on the guidance and protocols provided by the CIS. Familiarity with CIS guidance, for example, was seen as inducing a dependence that made it hard to revert to “manual” procedures (staff struggled to recall how to undertake tasks without the CIS, or, in some cases, had no experience of working without it).

The CCU also constitutes a setting in which there is a considerable degree of interpenetration of humans and technology. Patients’ bodies have devices inserted into them, in many cases temporarily, as in the case of a central venous catheter, but sometimes permanently, as in the case of an implanted pacemaker or artificial heart valve. At times patients’ vital functions may be maintained by ventilators, hemofiltration, or ECMO devices, such that their survival depends on their continuing intra-action with the machine. Cyborg metaphors, as adopted by some authors on sociomateriality (e.g., Barad 2007; Nyberg 2009), would thus seem particularly appropriate in describing such human–machine hybridization, although the degree of interpenetration in the CCU would seem asymmetrical (machines penetrated patients rather than the other way round), and even permanent implants remained, in principle, extricable from the patient’s body.

This profound entanglement of clinical practice with technology, however, appeared to be associated not with a heightened perception of technology’s role in patient care, but rather with the reverse: for most staff, the CIS and the technologies of monitoring and treatment fell out of awareness in everyday use and they behaved as if they were directly viewing and acting on the patient’s body. The traces displayed in the CIS were treated as representing the actual state of the patient’s body and the actions taken with devices, such as pressing a button to start a syringe driver, were treated as causing the requisite dosage of drug to enter the patient’s bloodstream. In most cases this was a reasonable assumption, but there would occasionally be problems, such as faulty sensors or blocked lines, that would bring the technology back into focal awareness. It is at such moments of breakdown, Sandberg and Tsoukas (2011) argue, that practices are opened to scrutiny and ongoing sociomaterial entanglement can be explored.

## Relationality

That clinical practices were dependent on technology does not mean that they were determined by it; rather, it is in the emergent engagement of work and technology that the influence plays out. Following Pickering (1995), we may view this mangle of practice as a dialectic of resistance and accommodation: some things were easier or more difficult for humans or technology and the particular practices that evolve reflect the local equilibrium between these different forces. Direct interfaces between the monitor and the CIS, for example, meant that recording of vital signs was easier, while text entry was slowed, so more data were recorded, but notes tended to be terser. Staff were flexible in responding to the new documentation requirements of the CIS, but could make errors in transcribing and calculations, so staff learned how to “keep the CIS happy” even when practices diverged from these requirements, for example in a medical emergency, but greater credence was given to the computerized record. Neither humans nor technology independently of each other determined the practices of CIS use; rather, this was a relational product of their intra-action.

It was certainly the case that the introduction of the CIS was motivated by an interest in promoting more evidence-based clinical practice, and the protocols, guidelines, and alerts built into it were intended to achieve this. The extent to which these actually influenced practice, however, varied considerably. As an example, the CIS could block completion of an action unless particular data were provided, requiring entry of full details such as name, dosage, and administration route before a drug could be prescribed. In other situations, the CIS acted more as a reminder to staff to undertake certain actions, such as positioning the bed at a particular angle, evidence of the completion of which was not accessible to the CIS, and was, therefore, dependent on the staff accurately reporting the task’s completion.

The CIS could also influence work more subtly, not by enforcing particular actions, but by making certain actions easier than others. For busy staff, providing a shortcut to prescribe a standard set of recommended drugs on admission of a patient to the CCU proved effective in achieving adherence to the protocol while not restricting the doctor’s clinical autonomy to prescribe other drugs if they considered this more appropriate.

Even where the CIS could require certain actions to be taken, however, the scope of its control was limited to those that required the CIS for their completion. In practice, these related almost exclusively to the documentation of care rather than its actual delivery. Thus the CIS could enforce the completion of all details in a prescription, but the delivery of the drug to the patient depended on a nurse correctly administering the medication as prescribed. While the nurses’ diligence meant that, in most circumstances, there was a direct correspondence between the CIS prescription and the medication administered, it was not within the control of the CIS to enforce this, however much the CIS may have guided the nurses actions. The particular practices enacted are, therefore, a product of the temporally emergent dynamic between humans and technology, not of one or the other independently.

This dynamic is also evident in the way that ward rounds were modified in response to the different material characteristics of paper records and the CIS. While the positioning of the screen, the font size and the viewing angle all restricted the number of people who could see the data being discussed during the ward round, by adjusting the arrangement of staff around the foot of the bed, standing back from the computer, and inviting participation, the disengagement of staff from ward round discussions could be countered, if not wholly overcome. Whether such adjustments were seen to be necessary, however, depended on the attitude of the senior doctors leading the round, not all of whom were so concerned about engaging other staff.

While the practices enacted may be viewed as illustrating sociomaterial intra-action, whether technology and humans should be considered as playing an equal role in this dynamic is questionable. Thus, following authors such as Pickering (1995) and Suchman (2007), it might be argued that the CIS influenced, but did not enforce the nurses actions, or that the reconfiguration of ward rounds depended on the initiative of senior doctors. This is not to say that these actions were independent of the technology or the broader materiality of the setting, but that the influence of humans and technology was not symmetrical. However shaped by the entanglement of the social and material, the emergent practices were oriented by the clinicians’ intentions.

Some of the approaches brought under the sociomateriality umbrella, moreover, involve a more radical ontological commitment than that of Pickering (1995), not just to the mutual constitution of human and machine agencies, but to the “properties…form, attributes and capabilities” of entities (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 455), or even their existence (Law 2004), being a product of sociomaterial intra-action. There would seem two ways in which such a claim may be treated.

Take the personal documentation that staff members sometimes recorded on a paper towel or on a cardboard kidney dish as an example. On the one hand, it may be reasonable to argue that the surfaces that were used to record the personal documentation needed to have the property of being able to be written on and that this preexisted and was independent of any particular use of the surface as a site for the recording of personal documentation. If the available surfaces, a metal kidney dish, say, or a wet paper towel, could not be written on, or the staff member did not have a pen to hand, no amount of sociomaterial intra-action could enable them to record their personal documentation at that time. This is not to say that they might not have had other ways of keeping a record, such as asking a colleague to remind them later of what they would have written, but that the properties of a surface that make it possible to write on are not acquired only in the immediate intra-action of humans and technologies.

On the other hand, it could be argued that the instantiation of this property was only realized through the staff member’s recognition and exploitation of it. Unless and until a staff member picked up the cardboard kidney dish and wrote on it, this property existed only as a potential. Similarly, the socially effective properties of the CIS were dependent on the way in which it was deployed in particular practices (the reconfigured ward round, for example, senior clinicians drive to pursue evidence-based practice), or the capabilities of clinicians to treat patients were only realized through material means (and not just that associated with the CIS, but with the deployment of their bodies, the devices they used, the spaces in which they worked). A nurse’s ability to access the patient’s body to carry out some treatment may be affected by the clutter of devices around the bed, or their ability to write notes on a night shift by the difficulty of seeing the keyboard; a doctor’s choice of treatment for a patient may be modified in the light of data from the CIS. Indeed, in a high-tech medical setting, such as a CCU, the capability to keep a patient alive, if not always to restore them to health, is strongly influenced by the devices available. Without hemofiltration, kidney failure was often fatal. With hemofiltration machines available on the CCU, the boundary of care is enlarged to include the possibility of continuing treatment of patients, even after kidney failure. The “properties” of clinical care emerge in situated intra-action.

Taking this further, the argument might be that the reality of the CCU—that is, the activities that go on there, the medical system of which it forms a part, and even its physical existence are all products of particular, contingent sociomaterial intra-actions, albeit in many cases these take place outside the immediate context of the CCU itself. While this reality may be similar to those of other CCUs in other hospitals, the specific local interpenetration of the social and material means that each CCU varies in the activities it carries out, how it fits into the medical system, and its physical setting. Even with a common CIS, the different members of the national user group used it in different ways, with different functionalities and different attributes, reflecting such things as local IT infrastructure configurations, staff IT skills, and patient types. Nor were these configurations stable. Rather they continually evolved as social/material conditions changed: new staff joined the unit, a software upgrade was released, new guidance on clinical practice was issued. At a fine-grained level of analysis, therefore, the CIS is not a single, stable entity, but a “composite and shifting assemblage” (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 455).

## Performativity

It is not just that the data selected to represent the patient’s condition and the way in which these were represented in the record reflected a particular (Western, biomedical) model of human physiology, but that in representing the patient in this way, the CIS also served to reproduce this model and can be seen as creating the understanding that it seeks to account for. This would seem particularly evident where the data displayed in the CIS were not a direct measurement of a particular physiological phenomenon under investigation, but needed to be interpreted on the basis of a particular model of the phenomenon. Various electrocardiogram patterns, for example, are taken as evidence of specific types of behavior of the patient’s heart, from which clinical judgements could be made. The clinicians’ interpretation of the CIS data will also be shaped by their understanding of the physiological effects of their interventions. The increase in a patient’s blood pressure observed in the CIS, for example, would be taken to be a result of the noradrenaline that has just been administered. This is not to question the validity of these understandings, although there may be situations in which evolving knowledge leads them to be revised, but to draw attention to the way in which they contribute to the enactment of a particular set of clinical practices that serves to reinforce the assumptions on which they are based.

While this was also the case with paper records, the automation of data collection and the comprehensiveness of the record appeared to encourage greater reliance on it in clinical practice. Although concerns were raised by some staff that this meant that patients were becoming seen primarily as numbers and that decisions were increasingly being made on the numbers alone, this was generally seen to be outweighed by the richer picture of the patient’s condition that was seen as now being available.

This picture itself can be seen as performative, both in its constitution of the virtual patient that becomes the focus of clinical care and in the new modes of clinical practice that emerge in intra-action with it. Sitting ward rounds and remote prescribing, for example, contribute to a new understanding of the patient, reconfiguring the relationships between doctor and patient and between members of the multidisciplinary clinical team, not just through a reduction in face-to-face interaction, but also in the priority assigned to data compared to a more embodied assessment of the patient’s condition, and in the temporal and spatial disembedding of interaction.

Another performative influence associated with the CIS was seen to be its fragmentation of the patient’s body, splitting it into distinct organ systems, such as neurology, cardiovascular, ventilation, fluids and nutrition, each represented by its own screens. Although the CIS front screen followed the convention of the paper record in summarizing these different data in one page, this was not always detailed enough for patient care. When staff were working with the detailed screens, however, they found that they tended to focus on just the one aspect and had difficulty in developing an integrated picture across the different screens.

## Practices

The analysis has so far considered the effect of the CIS on clinical practices only in very general terms, as the activities that staff undertake in the care and treatment of the patient. Following Orlikowski and Scott, however, from a sociomaterial perspective, a richer notion of practices is required that conceives of them not simply as “what people actually do” (Whittington 2006, p. 616), but as routinized complexes of “bodily movements, of forms of interpreting, knowing how and wanting and of the usage of things” (Reckwitz 2002, p. 259). Each of these elements may be identified in the changes in practice associated with the introduction of the CIS.

Notwithstanding their quoting of the comments of Suchman regarding embodied labor and embodied knowing in everyday practice, Orlikowski and Scott do not pay particular attention to the role of bodies in sociomaterial entanglement. In the critical care setting, however, bodily movement was very much a central part of work. Nurses, in particular, spend a considerable proportion of their time, at least on daytime shifts, on the move: setting up and maintaining equipment, fetching drugs and supplies, taking samples and analyzing them, turning and cleaning the patient. Many of these movements are so habitual that they appear to be carried out without conscious deliberation; indeed ,the flow of work would be disrupted if too much attention was paid to it. Doctors, too, frequently moved around the CCU: attending patients, conducting procedures, making phone calls at the nursing station, writing up documentation in the doctors’ office.

Although many of these movements were not directly affected by the introduction of the CIS, except where, for example, a direct interface between the CIS and lab reporting systems meant that results no longer had to be fetched from the nursing station and entered into the record, there were some changes in work practices associated with the use of the CIS that altered how staff moved around the CCU. At the microlevel, the shift from a horizontal chart to a vertical computer screen changed the nurses’ movement around the bed and how they maintained surveillance of the patient while using the record. More substantial changes in embodied practices were evident in the configuration of the ward round and in doctors’ ability to access the record remotely and to conduct some tasks from their office without visiting the bedside.

The effects of CIS adoption on the handling of objects, how things are described, and the understanding of the world have already been referred to above, but the other element of Reckwitz’s account of practices that has not been discussed (and would also seem noticeably absent in the account of sociomateriality given by Orlikowski and Scott) is that of affect and motivation. This would seem particularly significant with respect to nursing practice, which, Gordon and Nelson (2006) argue, maintains a commitment to a “virtue script,” privileging service, altruism, emotional connection, and relationship over technical knowledge. This was evident in remarks regarding the “danger” that the CIS was encouraging a focus on numbers rather than the patient, and the sense of being overwhelmed by the quantity of data that the CIS provided, both of which were accompanied by strong emotions and affirmations of their inconsistency with “proper” nursing care. Although some doctors expressed similar reservations, if less forcefully, they also considered the richer data as enhancing care of their patients and some expressed an appreciation of the potential benefits of greater detachment. Sociomateriality, therefore, does not so much address new forms of practice as employ an extended conception of practice that draws attention to elements, such as emotions and bodies, that are arguably neglected in other accounts of practice.

## Putting Sociomateriality Together

Although the above analysis shows how the notions identified as significant to the sociomateriality concept can be used individually to provide insight on CCU work practices, it would seem important also to consider how they may be brought together. Rather than try to do this for all aspects of the CCU case, however, this integrative analysis will focus just on the virtual patient, which was identified as a significant new phenomenon associated with the introduction of the CIS.

While the virtual patient might be considered simply in terms of the expanded set of data to which the CIS provided access and which it integrated in a common database (compared to the multiple paper records), this would address only one aspect of the sociomaterial assemblage associated with this phenomenon. Thus, notwithstanding the apparent significance of the greater comprehensiveness and cohesion of the CIS data (compared to that provided by the paper record) in encouraging staff to give greater credibility to the virtual representation, it was not just the existence of the data alone that made the virtual patient a significant phenomenon in the CCU context. Many other influences were necessary to enable this representation to be effective. We might identify, for example, the practices that enabled the record to acquire its perceived comprehensiveness—the tasks involved in entering the data that were not automatically available to the CIS, such as drug administration, or the narratives that accounted for anomalous data, such as when a sensor was misplaced or malfunctioning. Or we might consider the discourses of evidence-based medicine or medical accountability that create a demand for auditable data, but that also shape clinical practices to produce data that conforms to their precepts.

A sociomaterial understanding of how the virtual patient was enacted in the CCU might also involve attention to tensions between data-centric and embodied views of care and the emotions and values invested in them. For the senior nurse overwhelmed by the volume of data she was expected to be in command of, the virtual patient was a threat to her selfperception as a competent and caring professional, while for the nurse who liked to hide behind the screen, the virtual patient provided a new depth of engagement. Similarly, while doctors might welcome the virtual patient being detached from a specific location, allowing them avoid the stress and distractions of visiting the bedside when carrying out certain tasks, the conduct of their work depended on the virtual representation being, at all times, inseparable from a specific body in a specific time and place. Nor do the data themselves have meaning except in their relation to expected values of what they are considered to represent, the narrative of the patient’s condition and the current understanding of the trajectory of their treatment, all of which are the product of other intra-actions associated with practices such as medical training, ward round discussion and doctors’ experience with other patients. With different groups of staff, with different experience and different preferences for data, with different time pressures to complete the ward round, with different conditions and procedures for viewing the CIS, with different levels of congestion on the hospital data network and different availability of laboratory results, the role played by the virtual patient may vary widely.

The shift from paper to electronic record is thus not just a change in materiality, but a change in an array of social and material relations. It opens new possibilities, but the realization of these is not a fixed outcome of either the technology or social relations. Rather it is the product of the contingent interplay of influences (that are themselves neither purely social or material) as these are enacted in situated practices, at the bedside, in the ward round, in the doctors’ office. Nor are these practices simply patterns of bodily and mental activity; they bring into play power relations, norms, and understandings (of experiences, of competencies, of medical knowledge) and evoke emotions (of fear, of interest, of relief).

The notions of materiality, inseparability, performativity, relationality, and practices may, therefore, all be brought to bear in understanding how the virtual patient was performed in the CCU. What is evident, both in this analysis and in that of the individual notions, however, is the extent of variation in how the notions are conceptualized. Thus, if sociomateriality is understood to be a combination of these notions, then they do not seem to coalesce into a single, unitary concept. Rather there would seem to be a range of approaches that may be considered, following Orlikowski and Scott, as coming under the “umbrella” or “banner” of sociomateriality that share a number of common notions, but adopt different, internally consistent, but potentially mutually exclusive, conceptualizations of them.

## Discussion

From the discussion in the sociomateriality literature, two particular variants may be identified that adopt strongly contrasting positions. The first of these, which we will call strong sociomateriality, draws predominantly on the work of authors such as Barad (2007) and Law (2005), who propose a fully relational ontology in which entities only exist in their relation to others. From this perspective, phenomena in the CCU would be seen as only coming into existence in sociomaterial intra-action. That is, for example, a patient’s blood would be considered to have no pressure, except as it is measured. This measurement, moreover, would be viewed as only existing in the context of particular metrological conventions (millimeters of mercury) as these are registered by specifically designed and manufactured pressure sensors. Furthermore, the presence of these sensors in the patient’s body would themselves be seen as the product of other sociomaterial intra-actions, typically involving a clinician inserting a cannula into one of the patient’s arteries. Even then, however, this is not the patient’s blood pressure, but a change in strain in the sensor that needs to be translated into a signal capable of transmission to a bed-side monitor and/or electronic record. Nor is this bit string itself the blood pressure, but rather a number that requires clinical, and perhaps also practical, interpretation. Further sociomaterial intra-actions between, amongst others, doctors, nurses, patient records, syringes, cannulae, and catheters in the immediate context of the CCU will be involved in responding to this number, all of which will need to have been brought into conjunction at the moment of action as a consequence of yet wider sociomaterial entanglements of the manufacture of pressure sensors, say, the training of doctors, or the staffing procedures on the CCU.

This argument has some similarities with Mol’s (2003) account of the multiple reality of atherosclerosis, in which she argues “ontology is not given in the order of things…instead, ontologies are brought into being, sustained or allowed to wither away in common, day-to-day sociomaterial practices” (p. 6). Patients, nurses, doctors, or technicians investigating a faulty sensor may each have very different ideas of what blood pressure is. The patient may not even be aware of their blood pressure, except if they experience symptoms of hyperor hypo-tension. For the nurse, it may be a value to be recorded and monitored to see that it falls within an accepted range. For the doctor, it may be viewed as an indicator contributing to the diagnosis of a particular disease or demonstrating the success or failure of some clinical intervention. For the technician, it may be an input that the sensor is supposed to translate reliably into a signal that is registered correctly by the recording device(s).

Unlike Mol’s atherosclerosis example, however, where there is a common label applied to multiple phenomena, blood pressure has a common phenomenon that is understood differently by different actors. In Mol’s case, atherosclerosis is a patient’s pain in walking, and the thickening of the intima in a section of arterial tissue on a microscope slide observed by a pathologist, and the shadow in the lumina of an artery visible in an angiogram, and the blood flow a technician detects with a Doppler probe in a patient’s ankle, and the occluded artery that a surgeon operates on. With blood pressure, in contrast, there is a common referent, the force exerted by circulating blood upon the walls of blood vessels. This may, or may not, be measured and may have a very different significance for different individuals in the CCU setting, but there is a force on the vessel walls created by the patient’s circulating blood.

With its extending chains of intra-action that bring phenomena into being, strong sociomateriality may also be seen to have considerable affinity with actor network theory. While, in principle, it is possible to trace these chains indefinitely, in practice, similar to Latourian notions of black boxing (Latour 1987), the further they extend from the immediate object of attention, the more they are taken for granted, and considered as fact. The reading in the CIS is usually treated unproblematically as a well-understood measure of the pressure of blood in the patient’s artery. Only if there is something that raises doubts about the reading do its immediate constituent intraactions come into question. And only in the case of a much more serious and persistent breakdown might the manufacturing process of pressure sensors or the training of doctors be subject to scrutiny. While it may be possible, therefore, to consider the sociomaterial entanglements entailed in particular events as being limitless, in most circumstances these limits remain both invisible and unquestioned.

In contrast to the focus in actor network theory (at least in its early manifestation in Latour 1987) on how networks become stabilized (such that they are taken as facts), however, strong sociomateriality offers a highly contingent conceptualization of phenomena. It is only, as Barad (2007, p. 333) puts it, through the “specific agential intra-actions that the boundaries and properties of the ‘components’ of phenomena become determinate and that particular material articulations of the world become meaningful.” Only when the CIS is used in a particular setting, for example, can we know what it is and what its effects are. Its properties remain indeterminate until they are enacted in practice.

The ontology of strong sociomateriality is thus strongly processual (Thompson 2011), viewing organizations as in a perpetual state of becoming (Tsoukas and Chia 2002). Although

Langley (2009) cites the computed tomography study of Barley (1986) as illustrating such an approach, strong sociomateriality arguably adopts an even stronger process view than Barley, who “parses the ceaseless flow of structuring into temporal phases” (p. 82), the sequential succession of which is initiated by technology. Rather, as Tsoukas and Chia (p. 567) argue, “change is inherent in human action, and organizations are sites of continuously evolving human action.” While at a macro-level, therefore, it may be possible to view the “implementation” of a technology, such as the CIS, as a distinct occasion triggering social dynamics, strong sociomateriality would seek to dissolve both the implicit separation of technology from the social and the episodic treatment of time. Thus the emerging patterns of change would be seen as the outcome of specific sociomaterial entanglements, not just between technology and organization but among an ecology of sociomaterial assemblages in a continuous flow of intraaction, from nurses struggling to use keyboards on the night shift to allergy alerts being registered (or not) by doctors. Nor would these assemblages be restricted to those immediately associated with the CIS; rather, the extended chains of intraaction might be traced through other tensions and alignments between the layout of the ward and the spaces for interaction, or between the power hierarchy in the hospital and the staffing of ward rounds.

From an Information Systems perspective this means that we can no longer assume a discrete object of attention that preexists its enactment in practice. Rather we need to consider contingent, “composite and shifting assemblages” (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 455) that coalesce only in their enactment. Nor can we assume that the phenomena of interest are located only in the immediate setting. While it may not be possible to trace all the extended chains of intra-action, we need to recognize the influence of these entanglements on the focal assemblage, rather than treat them as given.

While this suspension of assumptions about the form, attributes, and properties of IS may make it possible to observe phenomena afresh, it also poses significant challenges, especially given how far it is from mainstream positions in the IS field (Leonardi 2013). It would, therefore, seem important for the implications of strong sociomateriality to be explored empirically. This might involve investigating how the practice of monitoring a patient’s blood pressure varies over time, between CCUs, and between individuals, while retaining enough similarity to be identified as the same activity and how this relates to particular material configurations of which the CIS is a part. Such studies will need to be both highly focused on particular sites, but also much broader in their tracing of the web of articulations beyond the immediate hardware and software configuration that would typically be identified as the CIS. That is, the enactment of the CIS in the practice of blood pressure monitoring is viewed as the shifting balance of an array of entangled sociomaterial relations that includes medical devices, the physical setting of the CCU, staff and patients in the immediate setting, and also the spatially and temporally extended chains of entanglement with which these are connected.

As with Sandberg and Tsoukas (2011), the search is not for causal regularities, but for general templates, the specific enactment of which in particular contexts enables us to refine and extend our theorizing. Since the sociomaterial entanglements entailed in a particular setting may often be invisible and unconsidered, it would seem likely that the examination of temporary breakdowns may play an important role in enabling their significance to be explored. Instances of tension, for example over remote access to the CIS, or over treating the numbers rather than the patient, or between technical standards that prevent certain devices being interfaced with the CIS, may provide entry points for exploring how the CIS is involved in the performance of a particular practice of care.

Although this strong account of sociomateriality has so far largely been characterized in terms of its position on relationality, this is not independent of its stance on the other notions of materiality, inseparability, performativity, and practice. For Barad (2003), materiality does not correspond to physicality, but to the materialization of phenomena, while inseparability is explicitly ontological. Similarly, Barad’s posthumanist account of performativity focuses as much on discourse as it does on materiality. The virtual patient is just as much matter for Barad as a bed or a body and all of these are viewed as brought into being through sociomaterial intraaction. This is a process, moreover, in which we are not dealing with social and material relata<sup>4</sup> that preexist their relation, but phenomena, the intra-acting “components” of which are intrinsically inseparable. The performance of the virtual patient is not just about its materialization in practice, but involves issues of power and identity, as the tensions around it perhaps illustrate.

The influence of strong sociomateriality on Orlikowski’s account is evident in several of her claims, as a number of authors such as Faulkner and Runde (2011 2012), Leonardi (2010, 2012, 2013) and Mutch (2013) have noted. In particular, Mutch compares what he describes as strong sociomateriality (which he associates with the “agential realism” of Barad 2007) with critical realism (Sayer 2000), finding the former wanting in several respects and concluding that it represents a “wrong turning” for IS research. Although coming from a similar philosophical position as Mutch, Faulkner and Runde (2012) are more sympathetic to the arguments of sociomateriality and, like Leonardi (2013), their criticisms do not lead them to dismiss it altogether, but to suggest areas in which, they argue, its claims might benefit from some qualification. It may, therefore, be possible to identify what might be described as a weak account of sociomateriality that would still subscribe to some version of the five notions identified by Orlikowski and Scott (2008), while not endorsing all their claims.

For most of the notions, moreover, both strong and weak sociomateriality would recognize the same forms (as identified in Tables 2 through 6 above) although they might understand them rather differently. In terms of materiality, for example, both strong and weak sociomateriality would acknowledge the physicality of artefacts and contexts and the constitutive role of nonmaterial artefacts in the CCU context. From the viewpoint of strong sociomateriality, however, it would not be possible to distinguish between various forms of materiality, except as they were enacted in particular settings. Weak sociomateriality, in contrast, might argue that, while the particular enactment of materiality may vary depending on the setting, different materialities create different conditions of possibility for this enactment. While the significance of screen resolution in particular clinical practices may depend on the visual acuity of individual users, the lighting levels on the unit, or the task at hand, the resolution achievable with any particular screen will be limited by its physical design. Weak sociomateriality may also attribute greater significance to the particular form of materiality (e.g., physical or digital) as this will be considered to create different conditions of possibility: a physical object can only be in one place at one time, whereas a digital object can be in more than one location simultaneously.

In terms of relationality, however, the differences between weak and strong sociomateriality are more significant. Thus weak sociomaterialists would restrict relationality to human/ machine agency and reject strong sociomaterialist claims that the existence of entities is wholly relational. The existence of the moon, Faulkner and Runde (2012, p. 61) argue, is “not dependent on any agency of observation in which humans are implicated.” Contra Orlikowski (2007), there may be material without the social; the two can be separated. For weak sociomaterialists this also applies, albeit in a more attenuated form, in more mundane settings. For example, although recognizing that an ECMO machine is the product of extended sociomaterial entanglements in its design, manufacture, and purchase, weak sociomateriality would consider its existence on the CCU as independent of any immediate sociomaterial intra-action. If not in use, it will continue to exist, materially, in a store room without any human involvement.

Leonardi (2013) goes further in arguing that the social and the material exist independently of each other and it is only in their conjunction in human activities that they become sociomaterial. In support of this position, he states that

actors in the world…do not understand the world as sociomaterial, they can relatively easily point to a hammer or a piece of software and say “this is material” but they would likely have a hard time fathoming that a hammer was in any way social (p. 66).

While they may not talk in terms of sociomaterial phenomena, however, it is by no means clear that actors in the CCU necessarily make such clear distinctions (especially in the case of software), or at least see the distinction between the social and material as relevant to their practice. Rather, as Sandberg and Tsoukas argue, their basic form of being may be that of entwinement. That is, the nurse picking up a syringe to administer a drug to a patient does not parse the world into material needles and drug vials and social power relations and prescribing norms, but encounters a practice that involves all of these. Viewing the world in terms of the material and the social as independent entities, as Leonardi proposes, is not necessarily closer to the point-of-view of social actors than seeing them as entangled.

For weak sociomateriality, the properties of entities are not acquired only through their enactment in sociomaterial intraaction. Faulkner and Runde (2011), for example, identify non-rivalry in use, infinite expansibility, recombinability, and nondegradability as intrinsic properties of digital artefacts that exist irrespective of whether they are significant to practices involving a digital artefact in any particular situation. Similarly, Leonardi (2012) argues that material artefacts have properties that do not change from one place or time to another (indeed, this is what he refers to as “materiality”), although he accepts that these are not determining of their use in a particular setting.

In the CCU context, a weak version of sociomateriality might argue that while any particular instance of measuring of a patient’s blood oxygen levels will involve extended chains of sociomaterial intra-action that are brought into conjunction at the moment of measurement and that might lead to one particular value being measured rather than another, this does not mean that it is not a property of the patient’s blood that it carries oxygen. From a weak sociomaterialist perspective, moreover, acknowledgment of the contingent nature of the specific measurement would be qualified by the observation that any consequent variation is only rarely significant in practice: a clinician ignoring the contingency could carry out their work without any problem in most circumstances.

A further, and arguably more substantive, difference between strong and weak positions, may relate to the assumptions of the two positions on the stability of sociomaterial entanglements. For strong sociomaterialists, as was noted, phenomena are materialized only in the instance of practice. The patient’s blood pressure only exists when it is measured. Faulkner and Runde (2012) and Leonardi (2012), in contrast, propose that phenomena tend to persist (to become institutionalized, as Leonardi and Barley (2010) put it), arguing that while the pattern of sociomaterial entanglement may, in principle, be open to revision at any instant, it typically remains unchanged, and often even unnoticed. This is not to say that it may not be a useful contribution to question this stability, but that it is not unreasonable to make assumptions about the persistence of entities and their properties in many circumstances. It may be the case, for example, that the action of pressure sensors, the transmission of signals from the pressure sensor to some recording device, and the doctor’s interpretation of that record against their understanding of symptoms of hypotension are all contingent and could, and sometimes do, play out in different ways. In everyday clinical practice, however, a significant drop in the measured pressure of a patient’s blood will generally be consistently associated with an intervention to try to restore it, for example by the injection of noradrenaline, and the effectiveness of this intervention will be sufficiently reliable to not be questioned. Should this relationship not hold, the response is likely to be a search for a breakdown in the system (a misplaced sensor, a faulty connection with the CIS, a misread record), rather than to abandon the assumptions of the relationship. At times, of course, these assumptions may be faulty and new understandings of the relationship may emerge, but this possibility does not mean that we need consider the phenomena to be “unprecedented” (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 466).

For a weak sociomaterialist, the exclusive focus of strong sociomaterialists on the immediate, situated instance of action is also seen as evidence of their neglect of the broader social context within which these actions take place and of their temporal extent (Leonardi 2013). As Mutch (2013, p. 36) argues, following Archer (1995), “all agencies takes place in conditions that predate action, forming logics that condition and shape what is possible.” Indeed, for Mutch, this is one of the main reasons for his rejection of the approach. Sociomateriality has no place, he argues, for any concept of social structure that could precede, and endure beyond, the here and now.

In the CCU, for example, the power hierarchy that influences who participates in the ward round, who takes charge of the keyboard or mouse and thereby controls what data are viewed on the CIS, or whose views are solicited in discussions is not created anew every day, nor is it unique to the particular CCU. Indeed it is a remarkably persistent and pervasive feature of medical practice around the world.

Mutch’s argument that the notions constituting sociomateriality are incompatible with the preexistence of social structure, however, does not necessarily mean that sociomateriality cannot accommodate the relative persistence of social formations. Sociomaterial practices may show both macro-level stability and micro-level variation. Thus, notwithstanding the well-recognized ubiquity and rigidity of medical hierarchy, as the CCU case illustrates, it was not manifested consistently in the organization of the ward round. Rather this varied depending on such factors as the attitude of senior doctors, the relative experience of senior and junior doctors with the CIS, the number of people participating, and the ward layout. The focus of sociomateriality on immediate practice need not preclude wider logics (indeed, the extended chains of intraaction may be seen as addressing these, at least in part), but views them as templates, the specific enactment of which can be used to refine our understanding of the general pattern (Sandberg and Tsoukas 2011). Exploring the variety of ways in which ward rounds in different settings and at different times change with the shift from paper to electronic records offers insight on the range of influences on ward round practices that might be overlooked by viewing them simply as instances of the operation of medical hierarchy.

It may be that sociomaterial scholars do not always pursue such wider analysis, just as Mutch argues that they also often fail to be specific about the technology or “just which combinations of the social and the material are performing subjects in particular situations and how” (p. 32) As Scott and Orlikowski (2013) argue, though, this would seem a deficiency of the application of sociomateriality rather than a necessary weakness of the approach. It does suggest, however, that research identifying itself as pursuing a sociomateriality research agenda needs to engage more fully with all of the notions associated with the concept than would presently seem to be the case.

In criticizing sociomateriality for its failure to account for exclusively social structures that may be inscribed into, and hence maintain independent existence in, exclusively material technologies with inherent properties that may, in turn, have “impacts” on organizations and society, Mutch may be considered as misunderstanding its central premise. Such concepts, Scott and Orlikowski argue, have no place in sociomateriality and their absence is a sign not of the approach’s weakness, but of its strength. Rather, they propose, sociomateriality’s presumptions of entanglement and indeterminacy “offers conceptual and analytical traction for making sense of the world and its possibilities in new ways” (p. 79).

That Mutch rejects sociomateriality for not being completely consistent with the assumptions of critical realism, therefore, does not mean that a weak variant of sociomateriality is not possible. As Faulkner and Runde (2011, 2012) and Leonardi (2013) suggest, none of the notions of sociomateriality are wholly inconsistent with critical realism, although some would be conceived rather differently (and generally more restrictively) than by sociomateriality’s strong proponents. These differences are summarized in Table 7.

Thus Barad (2007, p. 151) argues “matter refers to phenomena in their ongoing materialization,” while for Leonardi (2012, p. 29) materiality refers to “ways that [an entity’s] physical and/or digital materials are arranged into particular forms that endure across differences in place and time.” In contrast to the claim of Orlikowski (2007, p. 1437) that “there is no social that is not also material, and no material that is not also social,” Leonardi (2011) adopts the metaphor of imbrication, in which entities with distinct, yet mutually implicating properties (the tegula and imbrex of ancient Greek and Roman roof tiles) are interlocked.

Performativity for Leonardi (2012) relates to the things that nonhuman entities can do that users cannot fully control, while for Barad (2007, p. 152) it refers to “the world’s iterative intra-activity” and has important political and discursive dimensions. Against the strong argument, that properties and existence are materialized only in relations, weak sociomaterialists argue that there are brute material objects that exist independently of our awareness of them, and that have properties that transcend the particularities of their immediate context of use. From the strong perspective, practices might be viewed as “a routinized way in which bodies are moved, objects are handled, subjects are treated, things are described and the world is understood” (Reckwitz 2002, p. 250), whereas for a weak sociomaterialist they are more narrowly conceived as social “activities and processes” (Faulkner and Runde 2012, p. 58), separate from the objects that are implicated in them.

In highlighting these differences it might seem that there is a fundamental and irreconcilable division between the strong and weak variants of sociomateriality and that the concept has little utility if it is capable of supporting such inconsistent positions. As has been argued, however, weak sociomateriality is about qualifying the scope and definitiveness of strong claims, rather than rejecting the notions outright. Is it a necessary condition of sociomateriality, that all material is social, or can it accommodate the independent existence of some material entities? Conversely, does the independent existence of the moon, say, make any difference to our understanding of sociomateriality in the sorts of setting we are likely to study? As most, if not all, organizational and IS research takes place in settings that are, necessarily, both social and material, and which are remote from the weak sociomateriality limit conditions, such as brute materiality or unmediated material properties, their differences may not be consequential in practice. It may be possible to agree that IS phenomena involve an entanglement of the social and material that is enacted in situated practice, while adopting quite different ontological assumptions.

Even if it is argued from a weak sociomaterialist standpoint, for example, that the patient’s blood is under pressure whether or not this is measured, it is largely only through its measurement (involving the sorts of sociomaterial entanglements discussed above) that it becomes significant in clinical practice (the caveat “largely” reflects the possibility that a patient’s blood vessel might spontaneously rupture, in which case whether or not it was under pressure might become highly significant to clinical practice as attempts are made to stop the bleed, although even here it may be argued that the rupture will have been the product of other chains of sociomaterial intra-action). From both weak and strong perspectives, therefore, the understanding of the sociomaterial entanglements entailed in the enactment of a patient’s blood pressure may not, in practice, be so very different.

The issue here, as with that of stability discussed earlier, is one of the scale and scope of analysis. At the level of immediate practice, both, despite their different starting assumptions, recognize the situated enactment of sociomaterial entanglement. For strong sociomateriality, however, these micro-processes are likely to be the primary focus, seeing them as fundamental to, and inadequately recognized in the larger-scale accounts that may be of more interest to weak sociomaterialists. Weak sociomaterialists, on the other hand, might view micro-level variation as noise in a relatively enduring macro-level pattern. As Leonardi and Barley argue in relation to similar differences between constructivism (especially in its enactment variant) and institutionalism, perhaps both are necessary to understand “how emergent and inherently indeterminate social phenomena are transformed into the seemingly objective and immutable facticities of organized life” (p. 41). The different orientation of the two may, therefore, be productive in encouraging both weak and strong sociomaterialists to broaden their focus.

Whether or not strong and weak sociomaterialities are in some sense mutually supportive, in concentrating on just these two variants of sociomateriality in some detail, it is not intended to suggest that they constitute a complete account of all possible ways in which sociomateriality is, or might be, construed, or that they represent the opposite ends of a single spectrum on which all other variants might be located. As Pierides and Woodman (2012) discuss, for example, the object-oriented ontology of Harman (2011) would seem broadly consistent with strong sociomateriality while rejecting strong relationality. Similarly, the literatures Orlikowski and Scott identify with sociomateriality differ in their interpretation of the notions. Suchman (2007) and Pickering (1995) offer a more asymmetrical account of entanglement than Barad (2007), for example, while Schatzki (2010) questions the conception of practice in Latour (2005). While the exploration of these variants would seem an important gap in the sociomateriality literature needing to be addressed, in general they indicate that, even taking sociomateriality as involving all five of the notions identified by Orlikowski and Scott, there are variety of ways in which they may be combined. Greater recognition of the complexity of the argument for sociomateriality and of the positions adopted by different variants within the approach would, therefore, seem necessary in advancing both development and criticism of sociomateriality.

Despite the burgeoning literature on sociomateriality in the organizational and IS literatures as shown in Table 1, it would seem that we are still some way from an agreed understanding of the scope and variety of approaches that might be considered as falling within its remit. Given the very different assumptions of the strong and weak variants, however, we may already recognize that it is unlikely to be possible to define its contribution in more than quite general terms, without being more specific about the particular variant we are considering. To go beyond sociomateriality as promoting greater attention to materiality, to the constitutive entanglement of the social and material, and to the way in which this is played out in particular, situated practices, would require that we focus on a particular variant: to explore the organizational realities that sociomaterial practices perform (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 467), for example, or to consider the underlying actual reality of which they are the empirical manifestation (Sayer 2000).

Described in general terms, however, sociomateriality, particularly in its weak variant, might not appear so different from other, more established research traditions and might, therefore, be considered redundant. In particular, there would seem to be considerable overlap between sociomateriality and what Sandberg and Tsoukas describe as the “framework of practical rationality” (p. 342). Both adopt a relational ontology that recognizes the entwinement of the social and material in the constitution and enactment of practices. Indeed, as was argued above, the account of practice theory provided by Reckwitz anticipates many sociomateriality themes and arguably addresses others, such as affect and embodiment, that it might be seen as neglecting. While these themes may be present in certain practice-based approaches (although by no means all, even among those cited by Orlikowski and Scott), it is notable that the specific discussion of materiality in the practice literature (e.g., Nicolini 2011; Nicolini et al. 2012) is relatively recent and explicitly connects it with the work of Orlikowski.

One contribution of sociomateriality may, therefore, be to highlight a latent aspect of practice theory and to encourage greater awareness of practice-based research approaches, especially those, such as activity theory (Engestrom and Middleton 1996), that give greater attention to materiality compared to others, such as communities of practice (Wenger 1998) or strategy as practice (Whittington 2006), in which the concept receives less attention. At the same time, in moving beyond the human-centeredness of much practice theory and in relating practice with the notions of inseparability and performativity, sociomateriality may be argued to extend these practice-based approaches, showing not just the entwinement of the social and material, but their mutual constitution, not just how practices are enacted, but how, in doing so, they serve to construct the phenomena they address. The challenge for scholars of sociomateriality is, therefore, to show what it adds to existing approaches. This paper has sought to provide some preliminary indications of what this might involve in the analysis of the practice of critical care.

## Conclusions

For Information Systems research, the initial appeal of sociomateriality has perhaps lain in its attempt to raise the profile of materiality (and, by extension, technology) in organizational research. While, to date, this remains the predominant focus of IS and organizational literature citing the concept, it has been suggested that this is an inadequate understanding of the argument made by Orlikowski (2007, 2010; Orlikowski and Scott 2008) in setting out her account of sociomateriality. In particular, Orlikowski identifies five, interrelated notions (materiality, inseparability, relationality, performativity, and practices) that are seen to be entailed in it, and these would seem only selectively recognized in the extant literature.

While it would seem important for research that claims to be employing the concept of sociomateriality to engage fully with these notions, this does not mean that they constitute a unitary perspective. Rather, as has been shown, a number of variants are possible that take different positions on these notions. Some proponents of sociomateriality, for example, see this relationality and performativity as going “all the way down.” From such a strong perspective, reality is not fixed, but a transient, situated becoming, in which distinctions between human and nonhuman do not exist a priori. Other, weak sociomaterialist, commentators, however, while accepting that objects may be “at least partly constituted” by sociomaterial entanglement (Faulkner and Runde 2012, p. 61), question whether this necessarily precludes the existence of both material and social phenomena independent of the observation of them, or requires that humans and nonhumans are considered equivalent.

At times, the differences between these variants can have an air of scholasticism. The existence, or otherwise, of undiscovered oil reserves, for example, would seem to have little bearing on practice in the CCU. As Leonardi (2013) argues, however, the differences between the agential realist (strong) and critical realist (weak) accounts of sociomateriality have

very practical consequences for questions scholars ask, the phenomena on which they focus their attention, and, ultimately, the insights and ideas they can generate to improve the way that organizations operate (p. 60).

Methodologically, he suggests, the former adopts a focus on the implications of practices (in which the social and the material are considered to be inseparably entangled) for organizing processes, while the latter views the social and the material as independent and possessed of distinct properties and focuses on the implications of their sociomaterial interlocking (or imbrication) for organizing.

In the CCU context, therefore, strong sociomateriality might direct attention to how the practice of, say, prescribing is performed; seeking, in doing so, to avoid any preconceptions about the properties or identities of the social and the material, but rather to elucidate the logic of the particular, situated practice. Prescribing activities would thus be seen as the result neither of social norms of good prescribing nor of rules inscribed into the CIS nor even of their interplay, but as a routinized type of behavior involving an array of elements that are not separable into distinct social and material realms. Such an approach, Sandberg and Tsoukas argue, can “enable practitioners to better understand, engage, and, above all, improve their own practice” (p. 354).

A weak sociomateriality perspective, in contrast, might focus more on how social and material agencies combine in shaping the prescribing process. The CIS would thus be viewed as having particular material properties, such as features of the software that make it easy to prescribe the standard order set, that preexist their interaction with the similarly preexisting social structures, manifested, for example, in power relations in the CCU that promote conformity to prescribing protocols. The search is, therefore, for mechanisms linking the social and material over time that can explain “how organizations and technologies come to be as they are” (Leonardi 2013, p. 74).

As has been illustrated, it is possible to present a sociomaterial account from either perspective, with the primary difference being in the scale and scope of the analysis. Thus the strong perspective tends to focus on the situated detail of recurrent local practice and on the temporally and spatially extended networks of sociomaterial intra-action that bring these into being. A more conventional, weak perspective, in contrast, focuses on the relative stability and similarity of practices enacted within a particular setting, some of the properties of which may be considered to be relatively enduring and independent. In neither case, however, could it be said that we have many exemplar studies that illustrate this breadth and specificity of analysis, nor do we have studies exploring the potential of other variants of sociomateriality. Only when we begin to do so will it be possible to get a clearer picture of its distinctive contribution.
