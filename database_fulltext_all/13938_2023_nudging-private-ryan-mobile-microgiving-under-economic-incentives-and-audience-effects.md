# NUDGING PRIVATE RYAN: MOBILE MICROGIVING UNDER ECONOMIC INCENTIVES AND AUDIENCE EFFECTS $^{1}$

Dongwon Lee
HKUST Business School, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology
Kowloon, HONG KONG {dongwon@ust.hk}

Anandasivam Gopal
Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University
SINGAPORE {anandasivam.gopal@ntu.edu.sg}

Dokyun Lee
Questrom School of Business, Boston University
Boston, MA, U.S.A. {dokyun@bu.edu}

Dongwook Shin
HKUST Business School, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology
Kowloon, HONG KONG {dwshin@ust.hk}

Technology-augmented choice-making impacts many facets of business. The use of economic incentives under the ubiquitous mobile ecosystem for prosocial behavior has been shown to be particularly effective. We build on the previous work on this topic and study how mobile-based economic incentives and environments influence charitable giving behavior. In contrast to traditional fund-raising, we consider the use of mobile devices to generate giving in small denominations, which we term microgiving. In collaboration with a US-based mobile app provider, we incorporated a functionality that allowed users to contribute their in-app reward points to charity. To encourage donations, we used economic incentives in the form of monetary subsidies, i.e., rebates or matching grants, as well as digital nudges in the form of push notifications. We studied the effects of these factors on giving behavior across two large-scale field experiments. Focusing on the different aspects of smartphones that could differentially impact charitable giving behavior—namely the intensely private and personal nature of smartphones—we examined how the visibility of donation decisions affects giving behavior by toggling audience effects. Our results show that the effectiveness of incentives is contingent upon the magnitude of the incentive as well as the extent to which individual decisions are visible to others. To situate our results in relation to the traditional medium of charitable giving, we propose an analytical model that internalizes the subsidy rates and the audience effect. This study provides initial empirical evidence and an analytical model to advance technology-augmented charitable giving that can provide insights to organizations and service providers.

Keywords: Smartphone, mobile, charitable giving, technology-augmented choice-making, digital nudging, rebate, matching, audience effects, field experiments

## Introduction

As of 2021, there were roughly 3.8 billion smartphones in use around the world—representing one smartphone for 62% of people alive today—and it is estimated that 81% of American adults own and operate a smartphone. $^{2}$ Arguably, no recent technology has spread so quickly and broadly. While it is possible to treat smartphones simply as an additional technological channel, current research suggests that this is far from the state of reality, thanks to five specific characteristics that contribute to the appeal and impact of smartphones: their haptic qualities, portability, their ability to combine financial transactions as well as communication and social activities with ease, their intensely personal nature, and the sense of privacy that they afford users. Indeed, due to these characteristics, the relationship between humans and smartphones transcends that of a simple device and has been shown to provide cognitive, emotional, and social benefits (Melumad et al., 2020). As more social and economic activities are conducted on smartphones, their attributes will continue to alter users' psychology, emotions, and preferences in ways that shape choices and behavioral outcomes (Clayton et al., 2015). Smartphones have thus not only opened up several new avenues for economic and social activities that have traditionally occurred offline (Ghose et al., 2013) but have the capacity to offer new opportunities in how these activities are conducted (Ransbotham et al., 2019) and how they affect users preferences and choices, relative to offline or PC-based contexts (Melumad & Meyer, 2020).

One arena in which smartphones have become increasingly salient is prosocial activities, where socially desirable outcomes may be promoted through the selective use of functionalities offered by smartphones. An example of such a functionality is SMS messaging. For example, research has shown that SMS messaging can be used to promote blood donation (Sun et al., 2019) and that the haptic properties of smartphones, in the form of vibrational alerts, can be used to motivate users to exercise more in lab settings (Hadi & Valenzuela, 2020). Building on such research, in this paper, we address the use of smartphones in the prosocial context of charitable giving involving relatively small amounts, which we term microgiving. $^{3}$ Specifically, we study how microgiving may be incentivized with appropriate functionalities offered by smartphones based on their intensely personal and private nature combined with the use of push notifications.

In our theoretical development, we integrate prior research studying the use of mobile devices and their benefits (Melumad et al., 2020) with research on charitable giving to consider how such giving initiatives may be effectively crafted. Prior work on charitable giving has largely focused on offline giving; however, there is enough evidence now to suggest that people's behavior, choices, and preferences change significantly within the mobile ecosystem (Perrin, 2017). In bringing these streams of literature together, we focus on two specific aspects of charitable giving. First, we highlight the use of incentives, through the medium of the smartphone. Second, we consider the impact of nudges, introduced using push notifications on a smartphone app. We then build on the argument that smartphones are intensely personal and private tools in considering how the absence of an audience may induce changes in individuals' charitable giving. Specifically, we examine how different economic incentives interact with the private and personal nature of smartphones and the presence of push notifications to influence giving behavior.

As mentioned above, research on charitable giving emphasizes the role of economic incentives offered to potential donors as a means of increasing donation rates and amounts (Anik et al., 2014; Benartzi et al., 2017; Eckel & Grossman, 2003; Sunstein, 2014). Such incentives represent monetary subsidies that reduce the perceived cost of donating (Eckel & Grossman, 2003). The two most common subsidies are rebates, i.e., rewards given to the contributing individual, and matching grants, which increase the value of the contribution (List, 2008). The literature suggests that matching grants may be more effective in the offline context (Epperson & Reif, 2019) because they emphasize collective action through cooperation (Eckel & Grossman, 2003) and endow donors with a feel-good factor, referred to as a “warm glow” (Bénabou & Tirole, 2006). In the presence of an audience, social pressures to donate can also make matching grants more effective (Filiz-Ozbay & Ozbay, 2014; Gee & Schreck, 2018). However, in the context of smartphones, which are an intensely personal and private medium (Andrews et al., 2016; Hatuka & Toch, 2016), such audience effects are reduced since donors’ decisions are rarely observed by others (Fullwood et al., 2017). We thus examine if matching grants are indeed more effective than rebates when moved to the smartphone context and offered through a specific app.

Beyond incentives, nudges can also enhance the salience of the choice to donate (Thaler & Sunstein, 2009). Nudges change the choices faced by individuals in ways that do not affect economic outcomes but lead to a more desirable or socially optimal choice (Benartzi et al., 2017). Distinct from incentives, nudges can be deployed to address particular factors that keep individuals from reaching suitable decisions by modifying the underlying “choice architecture” (Benartzi & Thaler, 2013). The various features available on smartphones, such as SMS messages, haptics, and visual prompts (Hadi & Valenzuela, 2020; Shen et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2019), can thus be deployed as nudges to attract the individual’s attention and enhance the salience of the choice being presented. The mobile environment thus allows for several ways in which nudges can be delivered, broadly termed digital nudging (Schneider et al., 2018), which combined with incentives, can enhance the efficacy of charitable initiatives.

We address these empirical questions here, effectively extending the literature on mobile devices and smartphones to the charitable giving context. First, we examine how the personal and private nature of smartphones impacts the effectiveness of offered subsidies by contrasting the effects of rebates and matching. Given the flexibility provided by mobile functionality in terms of the ability to adjust incentive parameters as needed, we also quantify the impact of changing the actual magnitude of offered incentives on giving behavior. In contrast, in offline charitable giving initiatives, such changes in parameters may require significant effort. $^{4}$ Second, we introduce push notifications on mobile devices as digital nudges and examine how they affect donation behavior. Third, we consider how altering the private nature of decisions on the mobile device by introducing an obvious audience may change giving behavior. Our work thus addresses the broad question: How can microgiving campaigns be effectively managed in a mobile environment through the appropriate deployment of economic incentives and nudges?

To address this question empirically, we collaborated with an application provider that had developed and introduced an Android mobile rewards app in the U.S. Users deploying the app allow it to display news articles and advertisements on the device's lock screen in exchange for in-app reward points that can either be used like cash at specific retailers or donated to specific charities through the app. To study the effects of incentives and nudging, we conducted two field experiments, developed and executed in collaboration with the app provider. The experiments used between-subjects full-factorial designs to examine (1) the probability that a consumer would choose to donate and (2) the amount of the donation as a function of the subsidies offered and mobile nudges. In each experiment, we varied the specific settings of the economic incentives and nudges to establish mechanisms and to better understand the effects of the treatments.

Our results provide important insights into how individual choices on smartphones differ from other settings where charitable giving has been studied. First, as a baseline, we found that any economic incentive is effective compared to no incentives. However, in keeping with the private nature of the smartphone (Melumad & Pham, 2020), the audience effects that typically make matching more effective in the offline context are absent when incentives are kept at lower levels (25% matching, 20% rebates). Thus, our findings indicate that rebates are actually more effective in eliciting donation behaviors. However, when donation behaviors were made visible by the reintroduction of audience effects (through postings on a social feed), rebates were no longer more effective than matching, supporting the argument that peer effects and social desirability contribute to the appeal of matching subsidies for individual donors. Observability thus brings the mobile context closer to the offline context, where matching is more effective. When subsidies are increased in magnitude (50% rebate, 100% matching), audience presence makes matching significantly more effective. Finally, corroborating recent work on digital nudging, we saw a direct and immediate effect of push notifications on both the decision to donate as well as the donation amount, suggesting that enhancing the salience of donation options can itself push positive prosocial outcomes among individuals.

In summary, our work provides support to the argument that smartphones cannot be viewed as simply another channel (Ghose, 2017); the relationship between users and smartphones and their use of smartphones to perform specific activities require further elucidation and empirical validation (Melumad et al., 2020). To this end, and building on our empirical results, we provide an analytical model to generate insight into the relatively new phenomenon of mobile microgiving, situated within the charitable giving literature. The proposed model distinguishes the efficacies of matching and rebate subsidies in private and less private settings, thereby providing practitioners with guidelines on how economic incentives (with and without an audience) and digital nudging may be effectively used to promote charitable giving.

From a managerial perspective, sustainable microgiving campaigns (in-app or through the mobile browser) are likely to become more mainstream over time (MacLaughlin et al., 2020), complementing larger, episodic large-scale fundraising campaigns. However, a better understanding of how to combine the economics of charitable giving (List, 2011) with the specific nature of smartphones (Melumad et al., 2020) is needed, which we provide here. Furthermore, as we show, different sets of incentives and visibility settings have differential effects on donor subpopulations. This knowledge will allow managers to effectively customize appropriate incentives for their user bases and deploy elements of mobile functionality (such as software agents, mobile payment systems, haptics, or in-app messages) as needed. Although mobile technology provides this flexibility, it requires a deeper understanding of the interactions between the user and device in specific settings—our work contributes to deepening this understanding in the literature.

## Literature Review

## Mobile Devices and Technology-Augmented Decision Making

Smartphones are increasingly being used in a multitude of economic and social activities, given their ubiquity in the world today. Earlier work studying the use of mobile devices has treated them largely as a complement to the desktop computer—i.e., an alternative channel through which information can be shared (Ghose et al., 2013). However, in recent years, this relatively passive role assigned to mobile devices has changed, especially following the introduction of smartphones (Perrin, 2017). Scholars have argued that smartphones have evolved into more than just an alternative device; rather, they have become a source of emotional and social support for users by virtue of their deep connection with users (Fullwood et al., 2017). Modern smartphones come with a set of properties that make them particularly indispensable to users. These include their intensely personal nature, their portability, the ability to conduct financial transactions with integrity and ease, their ability to provide a highly personal space, and their capacity to provide pleasure and engagement through haptics (Melumad & Pham, 2020; Xu et al., 2021). This emotional link can lead to addictive behaviors (Ward et al., 2017) and to the use of smartphones as a pacifying device (Melumad & Pham, 2020). A central thesis here is that smartphones are not simply a new channel; rather, they can become “an integral part of the consumer decision-making process itself, intelligently guiding, constricting, and in some cases, supplanting consumer preferences” (Melumad et al., 2020, p. 90).

This reasoning suggests that the study of how smartphones are used in various decision-making contexts needs to be reconceptualized—and indeed empirically reestablished—instead of expecting existing relationships to carry over. Even when compared to the PC, the smartphones features mentioned above, such as their use of haptics and their personal nature, can lead to significantly different kinds of use and engagement (Hadi & Valenzuela, 2020; Lee et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2021). As a result, recent work has focused on how online behavior may manifest differently when transferred to smartphones, invoking the specific features of smartphones discussed above. For instance, Ransbotham et al. (2019) showed that the process of creating user-generated content (such as restaurant reviews), primarily through text, can itself change as a result of using mobile devices—reviews written on mobile devices tend to be more emotional than those written on a PC, arguably because of the small screen size, keyboard, and the need to focus on affect. Similarly, the use of touchscreens, a specific mobile device feature, has been shown to change the manner in which lab participants choose hotel rooms, relative to a PC-based interface (Brasel & Gips, 2015). Surprisingly, smartphones can influence behavior even when they are not in use, simply by being near the user, attesting to the close bond between the user and the device (Ward et al. 2017). Thus, studying the use of smartphones in a particular choice context requires understanding the interplay between the specific features of the device as well as the personal nature of the relationship between the device and the user (Melumad and Pham 2020). In the next section, we discuss how smartphones have been applied to prosocial behavior in the literature before delving into the specific context of charitable giving.

## Mobile Devices and Prosocial Behavior

Prosocial behaviors are behaviors that benefit or help society as a whole without any obvious economic benefit to the agent (Penner et al., 2005). Examples of prosocial behavior include activities such as blood donation, volunteering for various socially relevant activities, and charitable giving (Meier, 2007). Prosocial behavior is generally considered socially optimal, but incentivizing or inducing such behaviors in individuals remains challenging (Andreoni & Payne, 2013; Benartzi et al., 2017). However, research has shown that prosocial behaviors can be induced through the use of specific functionalities present in mobile devices (Ghose et al., 2013).

One convenient functionality that has been used to promote prosocial behavior is the short message service (SMS) feature. Andrews et al. (2014) revealed the influence of SMS messaging on a campaign that asked consumers to purchase movie tickets to help students pay for tuition in China. Dubé et al. (2017) used SMS messaging to sell discounted movie tickets bundled with donations to determine the effect of offering different discount/donation levels. Konrath et al. (2015) and Rifat et al. (2017) also found that SMS messaging increased prosocial behaviors, and Sun et al. (2016) showed that in the context of blood donation, SMS messaging helped promote voluntary contributions in response to blood shortage of blood shortages and led to more willing volunteers, compared to standard email messaging. Similar uses of SMS messaging have also been documented by Sun et al. (2019) and Linos et al. (2021). A representative sample of this literature is summarized in Table A1 in Appendix A.

Beyond these papers, there is relatively little published work explicitly studying the use of mobile devices as a medium for reaching potential donors in the specific context of charitable giving. Charitable giving continues to be significant—in the U.S. alone, roughly 2% of the GDP is given to charitable organizations (Reiley & Samek, 2019). A rich literature has addressed how solicitations and charitable campaigns can be designed to match the needs of charitable organizations with the willingness of donors to contribute, mostly in offline settings based on field experiments (Harrison & List, 2004; Jasper & Samek, 2014). However, this literature has not fully addressed how such campaigns should be conducted in the mobile ecosystem. The assumption that the results from offline studies on charitable giving will seamlessly extend to the mobile context is likely flawed and there is a clear need to further study how charitable giving in mobile ecosystems can be managed in a holistic manner.

In doing so, three specific aspects need to be highlighted. First, the use of economic incentives to increase the attractiveness of charitable giving becomes particularly relevant, since mobile devices can be used to target users directly, relative to traditional offline solicitations or even PC-based initiatives, which may be shared (Dubé et al. 2017). The flexibility and customization provided by the mobile ecosystem allow for these incentives to be provided in a seamless manner. Second, and importantly, the effect of economic incentives may vary from the offline context due to the intensely private and personal nature of smartphones, where the visibility of user behavior is reduced. Furthermore, most mobile devices are linked to social media platforms where visibility and privacy can be appropriately manipulated, unlike offline settings where these are more difficult to achieve. Finally, mobile devices offer additional features that can attract attention and enhance the salience of charitable giving, such as SMS messaging and mobile alerts (Melumad et al., 2020). We address each aspect below.

## Economic Incentives for Charitable Giving

Providing economic incentives for potential donors remains one of the most effective methods to enhance any charitable giving initiative (Jasper & Samek, 2014). These incentives typically involve monetary subsidies, which lower the perceived or actual price of giving, thus increasing the propensity to donate and/or the amount of donated amount (Andreoni & Payne, 2013). Monetary subsidies can be provided in two forms—matching grants and rebates (Anik et al., 2014; Davis, 2006; Davis et al., 2005; Dubé et al., 2017; Eckel & Grossman, 2003, 2008, 2017; Karlan & List, 2007; List, 2008; Vesterlund, 2017). In the first case, potential donors are informed of the presence of a matching grant—the matching subsidy (M) reduces the cost of contributing \$1 to charity to $P = 1/(1 + M)$ . By reducing the cost of giving, matching encourages more price-sensitive donors to give. Karlan and List (2007) showed that matching increases donation participation and donation amounts, based on mail solicitations from a nonprofit. However, larger match ratios do not necessarily lead to higher donations. Karlan et al. (2011) found that larger match amounts (i.e., \$20 match for \$100, instead of \$2 match for \$10) have a negative effect on donation behavior, showing that beyond the match per se, the amount of funding sought can also influence the efficacy of the subsidy. More work addressing the value of matching grants can be found in Meier (2007), Rondeau and List (2008), Huck and Rasul (2011), Null (2011), and Huck et al. (2015). Epperson and Reif (2019) provide an extensive review of the literature on matching subsidies in charitable giving.

Alternatively, potential donors may also be offered a rebate (Davis, 2006; Davis et al., 2005; Eckel & Grossman, 2003, 2008, 2017), in which donors are offered some form of reward or compensation in return for the donation. Rebates can come in the form of gifts, such as gift cards, t-shirts, and mugs for contributing to specific causes (Chao, 2017). Effectively, rebates reduce the perceived cost of the donation, thereby incentivizing actual giving. In online settings, rebates can also take the form of discounts or coupons for other services or products that can be offered as a reward for donating (Dubé et al., 2017). Thus, a rebate (R) offered to the consumer reduces the “price” of a \$1 contribution to P = 1 - R.

Rebates and matching grants represent forms of incentives, relative to conditions where no such incentives are offered. Matching and rebates can be designed to provide the same expected value to the donor seeking to donate a fixed amount to charity. Intuitively, a rational donor should be indifferent between rebates and matching if the expected values of the two forms of subsidies are equal when $M = R/(1 - R)$ . However, the two subsidies do not produce equivalent results (Davis, 2006; Davis et al., 2005; Diederich et al., 2021; Duflo et al., 2006; Eckel & Grossman, 2003, 2008, 2017; Epperson & Reif, 2019; Saez, 2009). In offline settings, matching has been shown to generate greater participation and donations than equivalent rebates; however, these effects are contingent on other factors such as isolation effects (Davis, 2006) and the availability of unit donation schemes, rather than giving amounts per se (Diederich et al., 2021). Although some ambiguity on the relative efficacy of matching versus rebates remains, the cumulative weight of the current evidence, as described in Table A2 in Appendix A, suggests that matching subsidies are, on the margin, more effective than rebates. $^{5}$

This literature suggests several potential mechanisms to explain why matching grants are more effective. One potential mechanism is based on the framing effect (Eckel & Grossman, 2003); matching grants induce a cooperation frame by focusing attention on the recipient's payoff, in contrast to rebates which induce a rewards frame, where the donor's individual payoff is highlighted. Matching works because it makes the donation into a cooperative act between the donor(s) and the recipient where others are viewed as doing their appropriate and reasonable share (Eckel & Grossman, 2003). Matching conveys a feel-good factor, leading to a “warm glow” when donors choose to donate along with others to maximize the total donations for the receiving organization or individual (Bénabou & Tirole, 2006). In contrast, rebates tend to induce feelings of “greed” since the donor is “rewarded” for giving. From a social desirability viewpoint, matching grants deliver a greater proportion of the donation to the eventual recipient, thus making the process more effective than rebates (Chao, 2017).

Whether matching remains more effective than rebates when charitable giving moves to the ubiquitous and convenient mobile channel remains untested. The mechanisms arguing for the efficacy of matching versus rebates have only been studied in offline settings, where donors can be observed and the associated feelings of altruism and “warm-glow” can thus be more effectively generated. The impacts of visibility and social desirability are particularly relevant here, even if relatively muted in anonymous offline settings (Andreoni & Bernheim, 2009). These dynamics are unlikely to exist in the mobile environment, where users operate in an individualistic rather than collectivist mode. The private and personal nature of smartphones may change how incentives influence people (Melumad & Pham, 2020), which we discuss next.

## Mobile Devices as a "Private Realm": Audience Effects

It is generally acknowledged that mobile devices today represent a form of intensely private and personal technology (Andrews et al., 2016; Hatuka & Toch, 2016). They serve as immersive technologies, allowing individuals complete control over their space (Sommer, 2009). As a computing medium, they are distinct from personal computers in that they are often worn on the body, are highly individualized, and are often regarded as extensions of the self (Campbell & Park, 2008). They are associated with the user's identity, social network, and online avatars, and serve as a repository of everyday life events (Park & Kaye, 2019). Increasingly, they contain information pertaining to users' financial transactions, health data, romantic interactions, mobility data, and social and professional engagements (de Montjoye et al., 2018). Effectively, smartphone environments are a technology-enabled “private realm” (Bull, 2005); users feel at home while connected to their device. Scholars use terms like “cocooning” to refer to the privacy of the mobile environment (Kobayashi et al., 2015); even crowded locations like public transportation can feel like private “microplaces.”

This private and personal nature of the mobile device implies that in the context of charitable giving, the influence of social desirability on user behavior may not apply in equal measure (Melumad & Meyer, 2020; Melumad & Pham, 2020). Unlike offline or even PC settings (where devices may be shared), smartphones represent an exceptionally private space where decisions made by users are free of social or peer influence. $^{6}$ It is well known that people act differently when observed by others, compared to when they act in isolation or without their actions being visible (Zajonc, 1965), leading to audience effects (Andreoni & Bernheim, 2009). When actions are observable, even imperfectly, individuals tend to choose what is socially or morally more appropriate for multiple reasons (Ariely et al., 2009; Ellingsen & Johannesson, 2008; Harbaugh, 1998). First, reputational concerns induce individuals to comply with social norms such as altruism and sharing. Interestingly, even presenting images of watchful eyes to people making a donation decision has been shown to increase prosocial behavior (Ekström, 2012; Haley & Fessler, 2005). Second, in the presence of an audience, individuals are typically averse to standing out in positive or negative ways. Therefore, they mimic socially appropriate behavior because it draws less attention (Jones & Linardi, 2014). Finally, when individual decisions are made public or are visible to others, individuals will tend to seek social approval by acting more prosocially (Alevy et al., 2014; Karlan & McConnell, 2014). Thus, in the public sphere, individuals show greater consistency with socially responsible and altruistic norms than in the private domain (Bénabou & Tirole, 2006; Ellingsen & Johannesson, 2008).

Applying this logic to the use of subsidies on smartphones, we argue that matching grants may not be as effective in generating donations as rebates, since the extremely private nature of user interactions on smartphones deemphasizes the roles of social desirability and cooperative framing. In private settings, social norms are less influential and incentives that rely on cooperative framing or norms to be altruistic may thus be less effective. The mobile context significantly reduces any audience effect; therefore, rebates are likely to be more effective. By contrast, when actions are observable or publicly reported, incentives that rely on social norms and cooperation are typically more influential (Ariely et al., 2009; Ekström, 2012; Haley & Fessler, 2005). Thus, when some visibility is reintroduced in terms of making donation behavior public, matching grants would be expected to regain their effectiveness, since the reintroduction of an audience induces users to behave in a more socially responsible manner. As a baseline, the use of any incentive in the smartphone context would be expected to lead to better outcomes than when no incentives are offered.

## Evoking Salience Through Digital Nudging

Since mobile users operate in a private realm, it is necessary to draw their attention to the possibility of charitable donations through their phone as well as any monetary incentives. In the offline context, there are potential neutral ways to present such choices to the potential donor, such as through advertisements. In the mobile context, features present in the devices themselves may be used to draw attention to the presence of incentives or, indeed, the option to donate at all. Features such as SMS messaging, in-app alerts, reminders, and push notifications can thus be used to enhance the salience of specific actions (Purohit & Holzer, 2019). Collectively, these features represent digital nudges (Meske & Amojo, 2020; Schneider et al., 2018).

Nudges, as defined by Thaler and Sunstein (2009, p. 6), are strategies to change “people’s behavior in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentives. To count as a mere nudge, [an] intervention must be easy and cheap to avoid. Nudges are not mandates.” They provide stronger behavioral results, relative to the cost of implementation, than traditional economic incentives (Benartzi et al., 2017). Digital nudging can thus be defined as “a subtle form of using design, information, and interaction elements to guide user behavior in digital environments, without restricting the individual’s freedom of choice” (Meske & Potthoff, 2017, p. 2589). Nudges have been studied in recent IS work. For example, Huang et al. (2018) showed how digital nudges were influential in getting users to share content, simply by invoking the social angle and highlighting the value to friends. Similarly, Wang et al. (2018) showed that harmful online disclosures could be reduced by simply reminding users about their audience, providing support for the notion that digital nudges are useful in safeguarding privacy (Acquisti et al., 2017).

The literature argues that digital nudges should be designed in a way that keeps the overall ethical guidelines as well as the technological options in mind (Mirsch et al., 2017). In the case of charitable giving through smartphones, users may not naturally gravitate toward the option to donate to a charity since this is rarely the core functionality. It is necessary to draw the user's attention to the option to donate by making this option visually salient in a way that is native to the mobile context. Therefore, we consider the use of digital nudges—namely, mobile push notifications (Schneider et al., 2018). Push notifications enable service providers to reach customers regardless of location and time, drawing their attention to specific events or information (Grewal et al., 2016). They act as a simple yet effective modality to draw user attention to relevant information.

Push notifications serve as an appropriate digital nudge since they are relatively costless, can be easily ignored, and do not affect the specific options on the phone in any way (Weinmann et al., 2016). In our context, using push notifications is a relatively low-pressure communication modality, compared to door-to-door fundraising, phone calls, and charity banquets (DellaVigna et al., 2012; Sun et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2016). Compared to SMS messaging, push notifications are less intrusive since they are only sent to consumers who have already downloaded the app. They are common enough in mobile environments to not cause any significant change in user behavior in aggregate (Lembcke et al., 2019). By highlighting the option to donate, push notifications act as a classic digital nudge, directing the consumer to the app as a precursor to the actual decision to donate. We expect that receiving a push notification, all else being equal, may lead to the increased likelihood of giving in the mobile context. We now describe the field experiments used to test these expectations.

## Field Experiments

We used two large-scale field experiments to study the effects of economic incentives and nudges through a collaboration with a mobile application provider in the United States. The two experiments were conducted in April 2016 and from October to December 2018, respectively, on a mobile app developed by the provider. The firm operates an Android-based mobile rewards application which, when deployed, displays advertisements and news on the mobile lock screen. $^{7}$ Users who view the advertisements are awarded in-app reward points that can subsequently be cashed in with retailers that belong to the app provider's ecosystem. Alternatively, reward points can also be donated to a charity associated with the service provider. Prior to the first experiment (April 2016), the option to donate to charities did not exist on the app; this option was added as part of our collaboration with the firm. We describe the two field experiments below.

## Field Experiment 1

## Design and Model-Free Evidence—Experiment 1

The first field experiment was preceded by a development effort through which the option to donate to a charity was added to the app. The experiment focused on two interventions discussed above—the role of monetary subsidies and the use of push notifications, representing a digital nudge. Users were given the option to donate their mobile reward points to a charitable organization that we selected—a nationwide nondenominational charitable organization for hunger relief and food rescue that did not pertain to any political group or ideology (hereafter referred to as “the charity”). The charity was fixed over the course of the experiment and was the only choice of a charity provided to users through the app. We used a full factorial $2 \times 2$ between-subjects research design, with each user assigned to one of four possible cells for the two treatments (economic incentive—rebate/matching, push notification). $^{8}$ In addition, we retained a control group that received no treatments. At the time of the first experiment, the app provider had identified 52,324 registered consumers who had downloaded the app and started to accumulate redeemable rewards, with a minimum of 2000 rewards points that could be used for donations. $^{9}$ These users were randomly assigned to one of five groups. The first was a control group, while the remaining four groups were assigned based on a $2 \times 2$ factorial design, as shown in Experiment 1 of Table 1.

Groups T3 and T4 received push notifications regarding their monetary incentive and temporal distance manipulation. For the monetary incentive treatments, we used the following approach. As discussed above, a rebate subsidy of rate R is functionally equivalent to a matching subsidy of rate $M = R/(1-R)$ (Eckel & Grossman, 2003). Thus, a matching subsidy of 25% would be equivalent to a 20% rebate subsidy, with a fixed monetary amount to be donated to charity. We set the treatment parameters for matching grants and rebates using these equivalent options for the experiment. To maintain consistency across the experiment, we tailored the push notifications received by each user (in cases where the user was randomly assigned to this treatment) to suit their specific incentive parameters. For example, users in group T3 received a push notification reading: “Time to be a hero. An anonymous superhero has offered a grant to encourage your donation so you can receive a 25% match of your donation.” $^{10}$ Table 2 describes push notification message and donation page descriptions across treatment groups and a control group. The field experiment was conducted for a period of 30 days in April 2016. If users were assigned to a push notification treatment, one push notification was sent on the second day of the experiment. $^{11}$

We first tested the integrity of the randomization. We ensured the validity of the randomization process for Experiment 1 in Table 3, which shows no detectable variation across the various experimental groups in terms of available balance, months of use, and gender, indicating no significant differences across the pretreatment groups (we discuss the randomization checks for Experiment 2 later). The ANOVA across groups was also insignificant at conventional levels of significance. We also checked the randomization through pairwise comparisons of means using Tukey's HSD (Table C1 in Appendix C) and found no significant difference among groups; the well-balanced sample shows that the randomization was effective.

Table 4 provides preliminary model-free results for Experiment 1, summarizing key outcomes across treatment groups in aggregate, but without control variables. We focused on two key outcomes, consistent with prior work in charitable giving (Sudhir et al., 2016)—i.e., the decision to donate (Yes/No) and the donation amount (averaged over the full month). Figure 1 presents the average donation rates across the control and treatment groups. On average, the donation rate in our sample during the experimental period was 0.95%. Comparing the control group to treatment groups (T1 to T4) shows sizable treatment effects. While the donation rate was 0.39% in the control group, donation rates increased to between 0.73% (T1) and 1.53% (T4) in the treatment groups. The most striking increase was in group T4, which combined push notifications and a rebate subsidy; here, donation rates were 3.9 times higher than those of the control group. ## Results and Discussion—Experiment 1

We used different regression specifications to estimate treatment effects across the dependent variables in our analysis. We used a logistic regression specification for the donation decision (0/1—observed donation outcome) as described in Equation (1), and a Tobit specification for donation amounts (in dollars), presented in Equation (2). $^{12}$ Note that charitable giving represents a context in which donation amounts of zero represent a corner solution, as discussed by Wooldridge (2010), i.e. there is no censoring per se since individuals can choose to donate a zero amount to the charity. We calculated the marginal effects of the treatments for a corner solution. While the preliminary statistics shown in Table 4 provide evidence on the impact of treatments, we formally tested for these effects in both experiments (see Tables D1 and D2 in Appendix D) by comparing each treatment group to the control group in order to establish a base effect of each individual treatment. In this experiment, all treatment groups (T1 through T4) performed significantly better on donation decisions and amounts than the control group.

Table 5 shows the results pertaining to the treatment effects on the associated dependent variables. The independent variables, introduced as dummy variables, relate to the presence of push notifications, monetary incentives, and the monetary subsidy type (rebate / matching). The variables are defined as follows: push = 1 if a user received a push notification, 0 otherwise, rebate = 1 if a rebate was used, 0 if matching. All empirical analyses controlled for the user's rewards balance, months of app use, and gender to account for user heterogeneity. In comparing the effect of any form of subsidy to the absence of any subsidies, we used the following: incentive = 1 if a user received any form of monetary subsidy, 0 if not.

As a baseline, Columns 1 and 3 for Experiment 1 in Table 5 describe the impact of monetary incentives and push notifications overall on the decision to donate and the donation amount, respectively. These estimations compared the overall monetary incentive effect between the treatment groups and a control group. We found that sending a push message positively influenced the donation decision ( $\beta = 0.513, p < 0.01$ ) and also increased the donation amount ( $\beta = 2.184, p < 0.01$ ). Also, the presence of an economic incentive had a significant impact on both the odds of a donation ( $\beta = 0.747, p < 0.01$ ) and the donation amount ( $\beta = 3.005, p < 0.01$ ). On average, push notifications and monetary incentives increased the donation amount by 2.05 and 2.82 cents across all users respectively, based on the marginal effects from the Tobit model. Across 52,324 users, each treatment independently led to a marginal benefit of roughly 1,073 and 1,476 respectively.

In estimating the effects of specific types of monetary subsidies on donation outcomes, we excluded the control group for analyses presented in Columns 2 and 4 of Table 5. Since the objective here was to compare across incentives, the control group was not necessary, leading to a sample of 41,986 subjects. The results again show that push notifications had a sizable impact on user behavior. Interestingly, we found that users had a higher propensity to make a donation in the 20% rebate treatment relative to the 25% matching subsidy, contrary to previous work in charitable giving (Davis et al., 2005; Eckel & Grossman, 2003, 2008, 2017). The results in Column 2 of Table 5 reveal that users offered the rebate show an increase in the odds of making a donation of roughly 27.0% compared to those in the equivalent matching group ( $\beta = 0.239, p < 0.05$ ). We see similar results with respect to donation amounts as well ( $\beta = 0.980, p < 0.05$ ).

We summarize the specific insights gained from Experiment 1 thus far. First, we see results that run contrary to the prior evidence regarding the efficacy of matching versus rebates. It is possible that increasing the value of the incentives used here (25% matching, 20% rebates) could provide results that are more consistent with prior work in offline settings (Eckel & Grossman, 2003). Second, prior work suggests that charitable giving is clearly susceptible to “audience effects,” i.e., individuals respond differently when their charitable behavior is observed by others versus privately conducted (Andreoni & Bernheim, 2009; DellaVigna et al., 2012). Since the mobile context is intensely private in nature (Ghose, 2017; Melumad & Pham, 2020), matching could be made more effective if individual giving behavior is made visible in some form. Third, in this experiment, we used only one push notification over a four-week period. What if the digital nudge were applied more frequently? These questions represent either concerns about generalizability or the need to establish boundary conditions in the microgiving context. Therefore, we conducted a follow-up second field experiment with changes to the treatments that were intended to probe for mechanisms as well as establish boundary conditions for the results observed here.

## Field Experiment 2

## Design and Model-Free Evidence—Experiment 2

The second field experiment was conducted from October through December 2018 on the same mobile platform, with the same requirements for identifying potential subjects on the platform as Experiment 1. The charitable giving option that had been added to the app as part of Experiment 1 in April 2016 was subsequently removed in June 2016. Hence, we were able to restart the option to donate in October 2018 with minimal influence from Experiment 1, given the interim period of two years. In this experiment, we retained many of the same specific treatments but made modifications in order to test the stability of the earlier results and also probe the specific mechanisms pertaining to incentives and audience effects. We therefore made the following changes. First, we extended the experimental period from 30 days to 77 days (October 11 through December 28) to capture two consecutive billing and payout cycles. Second, we increased the magnitude of the incentives as follows: 50% rebate or 100% matching, both of which were equivalent but provided significantly larger rewards than are typical. Third, instead of one push notification, we provided a push notification to the treated subsample every two weeks, leading to five nudges in all.

Finally, in order to test for the possible effects of visible behavior and audience effects, we added an additional treatment—a social feed treatment. The app allowed the user’s actual donation behavior to be made public on the platform’s social feed wall, which was only visible to other users in the same treatment group. The social feed message presents users’ identities and their donation amounts. $^{13}$ In the donation page description and the associated push notification, users in the social feed treatment groups were informed: “Your donation information will be shared with other users in the feed.” We contend that this treatment should make the rebate less attractive and the matching grant more attractive, given that the presence of an audience will likely induce prosocial behavior and make altruistic behavior more likely (Andreoni & Bernheim, 2009; Karlan & McConnell, 2014). Similarly, this increased visibility should make the rebate incentive on the margin less attractive to the user, relative to the matching incentive. In either case, we would expect the presence of any incentive to outperform the absence of incentives, consistent with Experiment 1.

We made two additional changes in Experiment 2 that are worth noting. In Experiment 1, we did not include an option for a push notification independent of economic incentives, i.e., all push notifications were linked to the presence of an incentive. This made it impossible to estimate the effect of the push notification independently. In Experiment 2, we included push notifications with the option to donate independently of economic incentives.

Thus, control groups with no incentives received the message: “1 in 6 children struggles with hunger in the US. You can help. Please donate your rewards to the charity.” Those in a matching subsidy group received the message: “1 in 6 children struggles with hunger in the US. You can help. Please donate your rewards to the charity, and we will match your donation by 100%.” Those in the rebate group received the message: “1 in 6 children struggles with hunger in the US. You can help. Please donate your rewards to the charity, and receive 50% of your contribution back from us in rewards as a gift.” The specific push messages and donation page descriptions across the treatment and control groups are presented in Table 2. $^{14}$

## Results and Discussion—Experiment 2

We used regression analyses similar to Experiment 1 to estimate the treatment effects for this experiment. As expected, all treatment groups performed significantly better in terms of the decision to donate than Control Group C1. $^{16}$ Interestingly, we saw no difference in outcomes associated with the four control groups C1-C4. $^{17}$ As mentioned above, Group T5, which incorporated push notifications, a social feed, and 100% matching yielded the highest performance—roughly 6.62 times higher odds of a donation decision relative to Control Group C1.

In comparing Experiments 1 and 2, recall that we made two significant changes in the assignment of incentives. First, we increased the magnitude of the incentives. Second, we added the social feed treatment, to make giving decisions visible. One possible limitation of this approach is that direct comparisons across the two experiments were difficult since there were two confounding changes that were made. Ideally, we would have run three experiments in order to accommodate the two changes made in the experimental conditions and facilitate direct comparison. However, from a pragmatic and financial perspective, this was not considered feasible. $^{18}$ Therefore, we chose to run one experiment with the two changes but separated the treatment groups to facilitate comparisons across the two experiments. To understand the effect of the magnitude of the economic incentive, we extracted the appropriate treatment groups from Experiment 2, which mapped cleanly to those in Experiment 1. Subsequently, to understand the role of visibility, we considered all treatment groups within Experiment 2, since the social feed was only implemented for Experiment 2. This strategy allowed us to study the effects of changing incentives and the audience effects separately, as long as the randomization of the treatments within each experiment was valid. We recognize that this is less than ideal, but under the circumstances, we believe that these piecewise contrasts are informative and theoretically reliable.

## A Generalizable Theoretical Model of Microgiving

The domain of our experiments was the general population of mobile app users who were invited to donate their rewards. Why would the average user choose to donate when faced with the incentive of a matching grant or a rebate? More importantly, why would the average effect of these incentives change when the subsidy rate is increased significantly as we find? Additionally, how would the presence of an audience change donation behavior?

We addressed these questions by considering the utility of charitable giving. When people choose to donate to charity, there may be factors influencing their decisions other than altruism, such as social pressure, guilt, sympathy, or a desire for a “warm glow” (Andreoni & Bernheim, 2009; Andreoni & Payne, 2013; Bénabou & Tirole, 2006; DellaVigna et al., 2012; Sudhir et al., 2016). These alternative factors can induce effects that interact with altruism to affect donor behavior. A general model called “impure altruism” has been suggested, wherein the altruistic and egoistic motivations acting on the individual are simultaneously considered (Andreoni, 1988, 1989, 1990). In the matching subsidy condition, for instance, an individual may experience a diminished “warm glow” effect since less money is donated by the individual per se, given a fixed amount provided to charity. Similarly, in the rebate subsidy condition, the extent to which an individual experiences a sense of joy or accomplishment for helping others may be reduced when they receive a rebate as a reward for giving (Bénabou & Tirole, 2006). Without an understanding of these varying psychological effects, the propensity for the overall effect of matching and rebates to switch, as observed in our experiments, cannot be properly addressed. The population of mobile users that we targeted in our experiments represents this general body of individuals who might display such impurely altruistic tendencies.

In the literature on impure altruism, $^{22}$ the utility function of an individual comprises two components: a private component $U(b,d)$ and a public component $V(D)$ , where $b$ is a budget, $d$ is the donation amount, and $D$ is the total amount of donation from the population (including this individual). Specifically, $U(b,d)$ reflects the joy of giving to charity, while $V(D)$ represents the perceived value from the public good to which one's action contributes. In our context of microgiving, the individual donation amount contributes little to the public good (that is, $V(D) \approx V(D - d)$ ). Hence, in the remaining part of the section, we focus on the private component $U(b,d)$ to study the individual's propensity for matching and rebate subsidies. This utility model can be made more complex by adding monetary incentives to induce donor behavior suitably, as we do in our experiments.

## Utility Model with Monetary Incentives

In information integration theory (Anderson, 1981), the relationship between the monetary (or physical) value and the associated subjective value is called the valuation function. In the context of charitable giving, we adopt a power function to describe the subjective value of giving behavior on the part of an individual; see, e.g., Birnbaum (1974) and Wedell (1996) for some convincing evidence that valuation is conceived as a power function. Specifically, if one contributes a portion $x \in [0, 1]$ per unit amount of donation, while the other portion 1 - x is subsidized through economic incentives, then the individual experiences a sense of satisfaction—or “warm glow”—which is expressed in our model as:

$$
g (x) = c x ^ {\alpha}.\tag{3}
$$

The parameter c > 0 scales the subjective value on a monetary scale. Consistent with many contexts (Kahneman and Tversky 2013), we assume that $\alpha < 1$ to reflect the decreasing marginal value of donation.

When one receives a portion $y \in [0,1]$ as a reward for giving a unit amount to charity as a donation, the individual experiences the feeling of guilt or greed, which is expressed as:

$$
r (y) = - c y ^ {\beta}.\tag{4}
$$

For fixed y < 1, the individual feels less guilty as the parameter $\beta$ increases from zero to infinity. In our context of mobile-based charitable giving, the parameter $\beta$ represents the degree of confidentiality in the mobile ecosystem, which may depend on the visibility or salience of donors' actions, such as the number of people who will hear about it via a social feed and the length of time during which the record will be kept. When $\beta$ is high (i.e., close to $\infty$ ), the individual does not experience any disutility from a rebate, which reflects the intensely private nature of mobile microgiving. When $\beta$ is low (i.e., close to zero), the guilty feeling caused by a rebate is intensified, which reflects the audience effect: if donation activities are observed by others on the platform, donors would be more concerned about the consequences of actions that might spoil their social image.

Remark 1: In our model, the audience effect is asymmetric between prosocial and selfish activities: while the audience effect amplifies the level of discomfort associated with the act of receiving a rebate (i.e., lowers $\beta$ ), donation is deemed a socially desirable act such that donors' warm glow is not particularly augmented when such a prosocial activity is observed by other users (i.e., $\alpha$ is constant). This asymmetric cognitive effect was first proposed in prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), and has since been observed in a broad array of contexts, such as financial stock markets (Barberis et al., 2006) and politics (Weyland, 2004). Furthermore, although it is possible that the parameter c is different in the presence of audience effects, we assume that c is fixed; this assumption has no bearing on our qualitative conclusions but simplifies analysis.

Consider an individual with a budget b, who donates a portion x per unit amount of donation and receives a portion y as a reward. For a unit amount of donation to charity, the net subjective value is written as $g(x) + r(y)$ . We assume that the utility is linear in the donation amount d; that is, the subjective value of contributing d to charity is identical to that of making a unit contribution d times. The linearity assumption has no significant bearing on our results but simplifies analysis and exposition; see Remark 3 for details on this assumption. With a slight abuse of notation, the utility function can be written as:

$$
U (b, d, x, y) = b + d (g (x) - x) + d (r (y) + y).\tag{5}
$$

## Matching vs. Rebate Subsidies in Microgiving

Consider the matching and rebate subsidies whose net donation amount is identical, as discussed in the experimental set-up above; that is, $M = R/(1 - R)$ . The actual payment of matching and rebate per unit amount of donation in such situations are summarized in Table 8.

If an individual chooses not to donate, the utility is simply $U^{0} = b$ . In the matching subsidy case, the individual donates $x = 1/(1 + M)$ with the mobile platform providing a matching subsidy of $M/(1 + M)$ per unit amount of donation. Therefore, the utility for the individual under the matching subsidy treatment is:

$$
\begin{array}{c} U ^ {M} = U \left(b, d, \frac {1}{1 + M}, 0\right) \\ = b \\ + d \left(c \left(\frac {1}{1 + M}\right) ^ {\alpha} - \frac {1}{1 + M}\right). \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

Under the rebate subsidy condition, the individual pays x = 1 and receives back $y = M/(1 + M)$ per unit amount of donation. In this case, the utility for the individual is:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} U ^ {R} = U \left(b, d, 1, \frac {M}{1 + M}\right) & \\ = b + d (c - 1) & \\ + d \left(- c \left(\frac {M}{1 + M}\right) ^ {\beta} + \frac {M}{1 + M}\right). \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

Individuals thus have different tendencies, i.e., willingness to donate, for different subsidy types. If $(\alpha,\beta)=(1,1)$ , the subjective value functions scale linearly with the monetary values of donation and subsidies, and it is not difficult to confirm that the individual's preference between matching and rebate is independent of the subsidy rate M; that is, $U^{M}=U^{R}$ . This indicates that the switching preference between matching and rebate, which is identified in our field experiments, does not originate from the different monetary values but from emotional values captured by the nonlinearities in the subjective value functions.

We consider two distinct cases based on the visibility of donation actions: “low visibility” with $\beta > 1$ and “high visibility” with $\beta \leq 1$ . Note that the two cases are suggestive of our first and second field experiments without and with social feed, respectively. In order to generalize our empirical findings from the two field experiments based on specific subsidy rates (M) and the visibility ( $\beta$ ), we now analyze the structural properties of the preference between matching and rebate over continuous ranges of M and $\beta$ .

Proposition 1: Fix $\alpha \in (0,1)$ .

(a) If the visibility is low ( $\beta > 1$ ), the preference between matching and rebate subsidies depends on M; specifically, there exist positive thresholds $\overline{M}_{1} \leq \overline{M}_{2}$ such that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max (U ^ {M}, U ^ {R}, U ^ {0}) \\ = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} U ^ {0} & \text {for M\in[0,\overline {{M}} _{1})} \\ U ^ {R} & \text {for M\in[ \overline {{M}} _{1} , \overline {{M}} _{2})} \\ U ^ {M} & \text {for M\in[ \overline {{M}} _{2} ,\infty).} \end{array} \right. \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

(b) If the visibility is high ( $\beta \leq 1$ ), the preference between matching and rebate subsidies is independent of M; specifically, there exists a positive threshold $\widetilde{M}$ such that

$$
m a x (U ^ {M}, U ^ {R}, U ^ {0}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} U ^ {0} & f o r M \in [ 0, \widetilde {M}) \\ U ^ {M} & f o r M \in [ \widetilde {M}, \infty). \end{array} \right.\tag{9}
$$

See Appendix B for proof of Proposition 1.

In both cases, individuals are more inclined to donate (either with matching or rebate subsidy) if there is a sufficient subsidy $M \geq \overline{M}_{1}$ or $M \geq \widetilde{M}$ ; in particular, it can be confirmed that $\overline{M}_{1} = \widetilde{M} = 0$ if and only if $c \geq 1$ . In the case of low visibility, rebate subsidies are preferred to matching subsidies for a certain range of the subsidy rate, whereas in the case of high visibility, rebate subsidies give strictly lower utility than matching grants for any subsidy rate (see Figure 3 for an illustration). Specifically, in the former case, the private nature of the mobile setting alleviates the feeling of guilt when users receive rebates, thus making rebates more attractive. However, in the latter case, when individual donation behaviors are visible to others and therefore less private, augmenting the feeling of guilt associated with rebate subsidies eventually makes rebates less preferable to matching.

Further, note that in the absence of the social feed, individuals may switch their behavior when offered subsidy schemes with different matching/rebate rates (Proposition 1a), as suggested by our empirical findings. While a rebate is preferred when the subsidy rate is low (Experiment 1), this preference is switched to matching when the subsidy rate is higher (Experiment 2). However, such switching behavior is difficult to be observed in a less private setting (Proposition 1b), which explains our empirical results from Experiment 2; the preference for matching over rebate is intensified in the presence of a social feed. These structural insights reflect our arguments that the efficacy of economic incentives may be fundamentally different in mobile and traditional (offline) contexts, providing a normative guideline for practitioners on which incentives to deploy given the underlying circumstances.

Remark 2: In related work in economics, papers typically study utility models that depend on the donation amount d, which is endogenously determined to maximize the utility—see, e.g., Bénabou and Tirole (2006) and the references therein. Our work shares an important common theme with this prior research, insofar as it highlights the nonlinear effect of monetary subsidies on the utility. On the other hand, our modeling framework differs significantly from the antecedent literature along two dimensions: we fix the donation amount d, which is suggestive of the microgiving context that we study empirically, where the range of possible donation amounts is extremely narrow, and we capture the psychological effects of matching and rebate subsidies by decomposing the donation amount d into actual payment x and reward y. This modeling framework helps make the comparison between the two types of subsidies more transparent.

Remark 3: The utility in our model depends explicitly on the subjective value $r(y)$ when receiving a rebate y as a reward for charitable giving. In contrast, Turk et al. (2007) compared matching and rebate subsidies in the context of the income tax system in the absence of such subjective valuation for rebates. The latter is equivalent to setting $\beta = \infty$ in our model; in this case, it is easily shown that the rebate is preferred to matching (i.e., $U^{R} > U^{M}$ ) for any value of M, since

$$
\varDelta U = U ^ {M} - U ^ {R} = d c \left(\left(\frac {1}{1 + M}\right) ^ {\alpha} - 1\right) <   0.\tag{10}
$$

This is consistent with the results in Case 2 (pp. 63-64) of Turk et al. (2007). Essentially, Proposition 1 can be considered an extension of the latter to the case with finite values of $\beta$ .

Remark 4: The utility function in our model is linear in donation amount d, as commonly used in economic theory. To justify this assumption in the microgiving context, we note that (a) the utility function can be closely approximated by a linear function of d if the range of donation amount is sufficiently narrow and (b) narrow donation ranges are often enforced in microgiving contexts. Alternatively, our analyses can be extended to cases where the utility is a nonlinear function of d. This would unnecessarily add a significant layer of technical detail; thus, in the interest of transparency, we focus on the simpler class of utility functions.

## Discussion and Conclusion

The smartphone remains one of the most transformational technologies developed in recent decades in terms of how it has affected the way individuals think, interact, and make choices (Ghose, 2017; Melumad & Pham, 2020). Thanks to the unique capabilities incorporated by modern smartphones, their impact reaches far beyond traditional for-profit activities in retail and finance into societally relevant domains such as charitable giving, education, weight loss, food addiction, and volunteering. Each of these domains benefits from various aspects of the mobile ecosystem, including the features of the device as well as the relationship between the device and the focal user. As more economic and prosocial activities shift to the smartphone, and as individuals spend more time embedded within the private and personal environment enabled by these devices, the extent to which smartphones influence choices and decision-making needs to be reevaluated (Clayton et al., 2015). Although it may appear to be seamless on the surface, the process of transitioning activities to the smartphone can result in many nuances that are only now being explored (Dubé et al., 2017; Melumad et al., 2020; Ransbotham et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2019).

In particular, how users of smartphones participate in prosocial activities and the manner in which such activities should be managed remains a topic of great interest to researchers and practitioners. Our work examines this very intersection—the use of smartphones in charitable giving through a mobile app, termed microgiving. In addressing this context, we utilize the literature on charitable giving but embed our theorizing within recent work addressing the transformational impact of smartphones on changing user preferences, behavior, and decisions. Using two large-scale field experiments conducted in collaboration with an app provider, as well as an analytical model, we explored the theoretical and managerial questions of interest in terms of the impact of smartphones in the context of charitable giving.

From a theoretical perspective, our work addresses the question of how charitable giving initiatives, which have been largely studied in offline and lab settings, should be conducted using smartphones. The literature provides significant guidance on how economic incentives can be deployed to induce giving behavior (List, 2011). Indeed, as a baseline, we observe that providing any incentive leads to more giving behavior, compared to providing no incentives. However, we show that the ubiquity and personal and private nature of smartphones can significantly invert existing relationships. In particular, we show that the efficacy of matching incentives observed in the offline context does not dominate relative to rebates, especially at low incentive levels. As incentives increase in magnitude, our findings indicate that there are no significant differences between the two forms of incentives, which conflicts with findings in offline contexts (Eckel & Grossman, 2008). Furthermore, we reveal that when users are required to make decisions in the presence of an audience, the efficacy of matching incentives is enhanced. Indeed, when social desirability and peer effects are reintroduced through social media or functionalities like leaderboards, user behavior is closer to what is observed in the offline context, where matching incentives have been shown to be more effective in general (Eckel and Grossman 2008). Finally, we demonstrate that digital nudges, analogous to nudges used in the offline context (Benartzi et al., 2017), are necessary to invoke user attention to the option to donate on the smartphone. In summary, our work provides evidence for how the combination of smartphones, push notifications and incentives can interact to enhance the performance of prosocial charitable giving campaigns (List, 2011; Melumad et al., 2020).

## Managerial Implications

As charitable giving initiatives move into the mainstream mobile ecosystem (MacLaughlin et al., 2020), our work can inform managers involved in mobile-based charitable giving about the importance of enhanced visibility and providing economic incentives. Concerning incentives, we caution managers that while they are necessary, it is often worth providing proportionally larger incentives. When incentives are large (100% match, 50% rebate), mobile users who are somewhat altruistic but also appreciate rewards are more likely to donate. Furthermore, managers should actively consider social media and the technological features of mobile devices that can provide added visibility, and thus social desirability, when matching grants are used since these are more likely to invoke altruistic behavior through the audience effect. Our results suggest that combining matching incentives with social visibility may be especially effective in social media donation settings, as commonly seen on Facebook. In general, managers need to recognize that the smartphone is not simply an extension of the PC and should appropriately design interventions keeping the characteristics of the smartphone-user dynamic in mind (Ghose, 2017; Melumad & Pham, 2020).

Finally, we show that simple digital nudges through the modality of push notifications, when used intermittently and strategically, can pay dividends by attracting the attention of the mobile user at a very low cost. Increasing the frequency of these nudges does not appear to be useful. Push notifications satisfy all the requirements of effective digital nudges—they are cheap to generate, can be easily ignored, do not change the “choice architecture” in any way, and are effective at increasing salience (Lembcke et al., 2019; Schneider et al., 2018). When combined with economic incentives, digital nudges are highly effective in generating prosocial outcomes. Indeed, our field experiments generated \$1,952 in total donations to the charitable organization in the first experiment and \$1,300 in the second experiment. While charitable giving might be constrained by people’s reluctance to donate, inattention to donation elicitation, and donation effort, the mobile channel can effectively remove the barriers of inattention and effort. $^{23}$ Our work can thus be viewed as a “proof of concept,” providing managers with clear insight in terms of the possibilities of microgiving or prosocial campaigns in their organizations.

A common criticism of randomized field experiments remains the issue of generalization and the lack of external validity (Card et al., 2011; Deaton & Cartwright, 2018). In our context, we acknowledge that while the notion of charitable microgiving through a mobile app is relatively unique, the population of mobile consumers who download apps, accrue in-app points on these apps, and use them in retail outlets is fairly large. There are over 3.5 billion smartphone users across the world, and a reasonable proportion of these users represent the addressable market for the context of our study. However, generalizing from our field experiments to this population would require the development of theory, situated within the charitable giving literature. Thus, we also develop a simple theoretical model of microgiving based on impure altruism that incorporates the altruistic and egoistic motivations of individuals (Andreoni, 1988, 1989, 1990). Our model distinguishes the efficacies of matching and rebate subsidies in private and less private mobile settings, thus providing some generalized guidelines regarding the choice of economic incentives. The model we provide is a good starting point for theoretical work addressing charitable giving on smartphones.

## Limitations

Our work here is also subject to limitations. As is true of field experiments associated with for-profit firms, we were constrained by the app provider in terms of the treatments that were possible in our experiments. For instance, we were not able to use social media sites like Facebook or Twitter as a way to introduce audience effects beyond the app due to customer privacy issues. Similarly, we were not able to access individual consumer data beyond gender, age, and months of use due to confidentiality requirements. In terms of mechanisms, while we are able to infer these by virtue of the two experiments, we were unable to collect primary data in the form of customer surveys to better understand their motivations. Analogously, more nuanced effects, such as the isolation effects (Davis, 2006) that have been studied in the context of charitable giving primarily in the lab, could be studied by focusing attention on specific factors in isolation. In our context, for instance, one could argue that donor attention could be focused entirely on the maximum amount that is given to the charity rather than the amount that is returned to the donor in the form of a rebate. The presence of such strategies to isolate specific elements of the stimuli, consistent with Davis (2006), could lead to a reversal of the results we obtained on matching grants versus rebates. However, empirically, implementing such a specific (isolated) treatment was not possible in our setting where the donation amounts were relatively fixed and the form of the economic incentives varied in form while remaining equivalent in magnitude (by design). We acknowledge that such fine-grained mechanisms may represent potential confounds in our results and we rely on future research to provide further insights into these dynamics. $^{24}$

Further, we acknowledge that our analysis is limited to users who downloaded this specific app and were actively using this app during the experimental period; it is therefore prone to some selection effects, which are true of all field experiments conducted in collaboration with specific firms. In Experiment 1, the randomization was conducted prior to the identification of active users, which could potentially result in inadequate random assignment to treatment groups—our randomization checks indicated that this was not the case, but this remains a limitation.

A potential limitation of our work arises from the choice of application, and the resulting sample of users used for our experiments. Since the app is based on accumulating points that may then be used for retail, it could be argued that this sample of users had a greater propensity to be influenced by incentives, compared to the general population, and that any results obtained from this particular sample are thus likely to be biased due to selection. This is certainly possible since the users in our sample chose to download and install the app. However, we note that while users selected into installing an app that gives them points for viewing ads, philanthropy is not part of the core functionality of the app. Thus, whether users respond to incentives in order to donate their points to charity remains arguably independent of this selection effect. In other words, it is still possible for us to study the intensive margin: Given that users have downloaded and installed the app, what is their response to the presence of varying incentives to donate?

Beyond these limitations, we also note some minor inconsistencies in the phrasing of treatments across the two experiments, which may have affected comparisons across experiments. While this inconsistency is problematic, we took care to not explicitly compare coefficients across the two experiments but only the directionality of the coefficients in order to minimize any potential bias caused by inconsistent phrasing across the experiments. Finally, we note that while the elapsed time between the two experiments may be viewed as a limitation, we believe this helped us by decoupling the two experimental samples—effectively, there was little overlap in subjects across the two experiments, allowing us to estimate the treatment effects in each experiment with relatively little bias.

## Future Research

Our work provides several avenues for further research in the context of mobile-based charitable giving. First, we note that our field experiments were based on in-app rewards points that could only be used to make purchases from a select set of retailers associated with the app. Understanding the impact of push notifications and subsidies when real cash is donated to charity would represent a useful extension of the work presented here. Similarly, while we only considered a stand-alone microgiving initiative, organizations may find it useful to complement large-scale fundraising initiatives with similar smaller-scale microgiving campaigns in order to target multiple sets of potential donors. Such complementary initiatives could be conducted across online, mobile, and offline contexts. In the context of economic incentives, we studied the relative efficacy of the two most common forms—rebates and matching—in a setting where the donation amount was exogenously given by the organizer. In more general settings, where individuals can determine how much to donate within their budget, more complex economic incentives could be created and used (Jasper & Samek, 2014).

Further, our work could be extended to include many other features available on smartphones. For instance, the role of haptics and specialized software agents in the context of prosocial behavior has yet to be examined. Similarly, research demonstrating a deeper link between mobile devices and social media could help make charitable giving initiatives more effective. Indeed, many apps provide the option of publicizing donor behavior on Facebook or Twitter to attract the attention of donors' social contacts. Alternatively, personalization agents could be used to target incentives, promotions, or appropriate visibility means for individuals to maximize the efficacy of prosocial initiatives (Lee et al., 2020). These approaches could more easily be applied in the flexible yet ubiquitous mobile environment, compared to more rigid PC or offline contexts. Organizations like the Red Cross may find it advantageous to offer incentives to specific consumers to donate, based on their behavior on the mobile channel, through the appropriate deployment of nudges and modifiers. Research could help to identify what specific forms such campaigns might take. These remain open avenues of interest that could be resolved through more experimental and theoretical work aimed at explicating the use of smartphones at the intersection of economics and IS research. Our work here will hopefully help kick-start more work in this interesting and societally relevant sector.

## Proof of Proposition 1

We first prove Proposition 1b. Observe first that $U^{M} - U^{R} = dc\left(\left(\frac{1}{M + 1}\right)^{\alpha} + \left(\frac{M}{M + 1}\right)^{\beta} - 1\right) > 0$ for any $M \geq 0$ , with equality being hold if and only if $M = 0$ . Hence $U^{R} \leq U^{M}$ for any $M \geq 0$ and it is sufficient to compare $U^{M}$ and $U^{0}$ . To this end, observe that:

$$
\frac {\partial U ^ {M}}{\partial M} = \frac {1}{(M + 1) ^ {2}} \bigg (1 - c \alpha \left(\frac {1}{M + 1}\right) ^ {\alpha - 1} \bigg).
$$

Since $\alpha < 1$ , there exists some constant $m \in (0, \infty)$ such that $U^{M}$ is increasing for $M \in (0, m)$ and decreasing for $M \in (m, \infty)$ ; that is, $U^{M}$ has a unique maximum at $M = m$ . Further, using the first order condition it can be seen that the maximizer $m$ satisfies $\frac{1}{m+1} = (c\alpha)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}$ so that we obtain that:

$$
U ^ {M} | _ {M = m} = b + d (c \alpha) ^ {\frac {1}{1 - \alpha}} \left(\frac {1}{\alpha} - 1\right) > b = U ^ {0},
$$

after some straightforward algebra. Further, it is not difficult to check that $U^{M} \downarrow U^{0}$ as $M \uparrow \infty$ . Combining these observations, we deduce the following: (i) if $U^{M} \geq U^{0}$ for $M = 0$ , then the desired result in (8) holds by letting $\widetilde{M} = 0$ and (ii) if $U^{M} < U^{0}$ for $M = 0$ , then there exists $\widetilde{M} \in (0, \infty)$ such that $U^{M} \leq U^{0}$ for $M \in (0, \widetilde{M}]$ and $U^{M} > U^{0}$ for $M \in (\widetilde{M}, \infty)$ . This completes the proof of part (b).

For Proposition 1a, we first show that $\Delta U = U^{M} - U^{R}$ is negative for $M < \overline{M}_2$ and positive for $M > \overline{M}_2$ for some constant $\overline{M}_2$ . Recalling the expressions for $U^{M}$ and $U^{R}$ , and letting $z = \frac{1}{1 + M}$ , it is equivalent to show that the function $f(z) \equiv (z^{\alpha} + (1 - z)^{\beta} - 1)$ is positive for $z > \bar{z}$ and negative for $z < \bar{z}$ for some $\bar{z} \in (0,1)$ . To this end, observe that $f'(z) = \alpha z^{\alpha - 1} - \beta (1 - z)^{\beta - 1}$ and we now show the following: (1) $f'(z)$ is strictly convex; (2) $f'(z) \uparrow \infty$ as $z \downarrow 0$ and $f'(z) \uparrow \alpha > 0$ as $z \uparrow 1$ ; and (3) the minimum of $f'(z)$ over $z \in (0,1)$ is negative. The statement (1) follows from the fact that $\alpha z^{\alpha - 1}$ is strictly convex and $\beta (1 - z)^{\beta - 1}$ is strictly concave. The statement (2) follows from the fact that $z^{\alpha - 1} \uparrow \infty$ as $z \downarrow 0$ because $\alpha < 1$ . To show (3), observe that there exists at most one $z^*$ such that $f''(z^*) = 0$ because $f'(z)$ is strictly convex. Further, $z^* \in (0,1)$ satisfies:

$$
\alpha (\alpha - 1) (z ^ {*}) ^ {\alpha - 2} + \beta (\beta - 1) (1 - z ^ {*}) ^ {\beta - 2} = 0.
$$

Since the left-hand side of the latter approaches $-\infty$ as $z^{*} \downarrow 0$ and approaches some $c \in (0, \infty]$ as $z^{*} \uparrow 1$ , one can deduce that $z^{*} \in (0,1)$ exists. Further, it is straightforward to check that:

$$
f ^ {\prime} (z ^ {*}) = \beta (1 - z ^ {*}) ^ {\beta - 2} \left(\frac {\beta - 1}{\alpha - 1} z ^ {*} - (1 - z ^ {*})\right) <   0,
$$

where the inequality follows from the fact that $\alpha < 1, \beta > 1$ , and $z \in (0,1)$ . Combining the observations (1)-(3), we deduce that there exists two points $z_1, z_2 \in (0,1)$ with $z_1 \leq z_2$ such that $f'(z_1) = f'(z_2) = 0$ and $z_1$ and $z_2$ are the local maximum and minimum of the function $f$ , respectively. Observing $f(0) = f(1) = 0$ , we conclude that there exists $\bar{z} \in (z_1, z_2)$ such that $f(z) > 0$ for $z > \bar{z}$ and $f(z) < 0$ for $z < \bar{z}$ . Finally, recall from the proof for Proposition 1b that $U^M > U^0$ for a sufficiently large $M$ . Hence, $\overline{M}_1 \equiv \min(\inf\{M > 0: \max(U^M, U^R) > U^0\}, \overline{M}_2)$ is well defined and the desired results in Proposition 1a follows. The proof of the proposition is complete.

## Appendix F

## Robustness Tests for the Potential Violation of SUTVA in Experiment 2

A key assumption of field experiments is that there is no significant interference among users, commonly referred to as the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) (Rubin, 1980). In Experiment 2, there is the possibility of interference between users within the social feed treatment group—i.e., given that users in the social feed treatment group were able to observe other users' donations in the social feed—it is possible that the observed donation decisions were not driven by the audience but the exposure to other users' decisions to donate that were visible through the social feed functionality. To alleviate this potential interference concern between users in the social feed treatment group, we provide a series of additional tests to examine the presence, if any, of interference, as described above.

Specifically, we provide a series of robustness tests to show that (1) there are no systematic differences in terms of the time intervals of donation behavior between the social feed and non-social feed groups, (2) there is no significant impact of the number of donation-related social feed messages on daily donation decisions, (3) the social feed treatment effects of the results remain robust with randomization inference under the maintained assumption of interference between users, (4) there is no impact of noticeable interference attributable to covariates. We elaborate on these tests in detail below.

## Donation Time Interval and General Redemption Time Interval across Social Feed vs Non-Social Feed Groups

Beyond the time intervals between donation decisions, note that all users on the app get to see the social feed featuring general redemption behavior and other posts by the app service provider. However, users in the social feed group are exposed not only to these general messages but also to donation behavior by other users in the same social feed treatment group. If there was interference between users belonging to the social feed treatment group, we would find a significant difference in time intervals between general redemptions across the social feed and non-social feed groups. During the experimental period, the average time interval between general redemption behavior in the social feed group was 5.30 minutes, while in the non-social feed group, it was 5.37 minutes. The result of a difference of means t-test shows no systematic differences in time intervals of general redemption across feed and non-social feed groups (t-statistics = -0.817, p-value = 0.414).

On average, social feed group users are exposed to 272 social feed messages a day, which consist of 270 regular redemption feeds and two donation feeds. This indicates that a user can observe a donation feed in every 135 regular redemption feeds in 11 hours, and one additional message pertaining to someone else's donation decisions. We argue that the effect of the social group treatment is due to the audience effect on the focal user (since their decision will be made visible to others) rather than the interference that occurs from an average of one donation message on the social feed per day. This interference in the setting of Experiment 2 may be relatively small compared to audience effects.

## Impact of Number of Donation Feeds on Daily Donation Decisions

In order to identify potential interference, we also considered the number of donation decisions per day between social feed and non-social feed groups. While a user in the social feed group could see on average of two donation messages in a day, it is possible that the number of such donation messages may have had an impact on donation decisions, by virtue of peer or bystander effects (Wiesenthal et al., 1983). We constructed a daily panel across the social feed and non-social feed groups and examined the effect of the number of donations per day on the number of donations that occurred on the next day. Since donations in the social feed condition were visible, while those in the non-social feed groups were not, the presence of interference should have indicated some effect of visible donation messages on donations on the next day. As a baseline, we found that there were 2.15 donations per day in the social feed group and 2.32 donations in the non-social feed group, with no significant difference across these groups (t-statistic = 0.417, p-value = 0.678). In order to see if donations had an effect on next-day donations across the two groups, we ran a regression where the dependent variable is the number of donations on day t, while the independent variable of interest was donations on day t-1. To differentiate between the social feed and non-social feed groups, we added an interaction term between Number of Donations $_{t-1}$ and Feed. The results of this analysis are shown in Table F1 and show that the number of donation feeds had no impact on the next-day donation decision ( $\beta = 0.045$ , not significant, $\beta = 0.017$ , not significant, respectively). This suggests that any donation decision made within the social feed condition was likely not emerging from interference from peer effects or bystander effects, made visible through the social feed but from the intrinsic audience effect induced by the treatment.

## Randomization Inference of Social Feed Effects under Interference between Users

We evaluated the extent to which the estimated results in the main paper were prone to interference by virtue of randomization inference tests (Rosenbaum, 2007). Recall that our main results in Table 7 in the paper may have been prone to interference since the results obtained for the social feed condition may have incorporated interference from peer effects. As per Rosenbaum (2007), randomization inference is used to perform hypothesis testing for the presence of interference between users. This nonparametric test takes all possible pairs consisting of one social feed group user and one non-social feed group user, and counts the number of times that the treated user had a greater outcome than the untreated user. The exact p-value represents the fraction of placebo assignment distribution-free statistics that are larger than the actual statistics. The null distribution of this statistic is known a priori and does not depend on any aspect of the data-generating distribution of the outcomes, including the potential interference between users. Randomization inference tests the outcome against a null distribution in which there exist absolutely no treatment effects (Rubin, 1980). The sharp null hypothesis of no treatment effect implies that the observed outcome is equal to all potential outcomes arising from the treatment. Rosenbaum (2007) uses randomization inference testing to estimate treatment effects in the presence of interference. This allows for valid hypothesis testing even in situations where conventional standard errors are invalid due to potential dependencies and interference among users (Bowers et al., 2013; Rosenbaum, 2007; Young, 2019).

We therefore ran 1,000 iterations of this randomization process to get an exact p-value for all of the social feed-related models shown in Table 7. Each exact p-value is based on an independent draw of 1,000 iterations, as shown in Table F2. In Column 1, we show the estimated treatment effect from the eight models estimated in Table 7. In the second column, we show the exact p-value for that particular treatment effect from Column 1, as well as the standard error of the p-value from the 1,000 iterations. The exact p-value is based on the assumption of interference within the social feed group, and therefore provides a check on the hypothesis testing that may be conducted from the original coefficients and standard errors shown in Column 1. For example, in Row 1, the coefficient (standard error) for the social feed variable is -0.040 (0.117) with a p-value of 0.730. As shown in Column 2, the nonparametric exact p-value for this coefficient, with the potential presence of interference, is 0.729. This suggests that the presence of unspecified interference (if any) does not affect the inferences that can be drawn from the original analysis.

As shown in Table F2, all of the exact p-values appear to be largely consistent with the results shown in Table 7. For the interaction variables, the interaction between the social feed treatment and economic incentives is not statistically significant in Models (2) and (6) while the interaction between the social feed treatment and rebate shows consistent negative and significant results in Models (4) and (8). Thus, these post hoc results suggest that the presence of nonspecific interference may not be affecting the results or the inferences we can draw from them.

## Interference from Covariates

One particular form of interference emerges from covariate variables, which may help capture the manner in which peer effects manifest within the treatment group. For example, it is possible that peer effects within the social feed group show up through the covariate of gender—women are more likely to donate when they see another woman choose to donate through the social feed function. Thus, interference can be studied by virtue of creating face-to-face social interaction links or geographic proximity, and using these to conduct interference analysis (Sobel, 2006). In this context, Hudgens and Halloran (2008) and Sinclair et al. (2012) suggest multilevel experiment designs to identify SUTVA violations, since such a multilevel experiment can be based on how users are connected within a treatment group. However, in our context, we do not have access to geographical or social data for the users in our analysis, thereby making it difficult to conduct these multilevel analyses. This remains a limitation. However, we can measure proximity among users based on the observed characteristics we capture—balance, months of use, and gender.

Essentially, we recreated proximity between these users based on their covariates and created matched samples across the social feed and non-social feed treatment groups. Upon matching individuals from the social feed and non-social feed groups using a 1:1 matching algorithm, and studying their outcomes, we tested to see if these results were different from the original social feed and non-social feed samples. If interference did exist, based on proximity along gender, balance or duration on the app, then the results from the 1:1 matched sample should have systematically varied from the results reported in Table 7 in the paper.

In order to conduct the matching analysis, we first randomly sampled 5000 users from the social feed treatment group and used propensity score matching to perform a 1:1 match with users from the non-social feed groups. We repeated this process 10,000 times. For each of these samples, we estimated the direct effect of the social feed variable on donation decision. After 10,000 iterations of sample construction and estimation, we found that the social feed treatment had no impact on donation outcome, mean $\beta = 0.163$ with mean p-value of 0.497, which is consistent with the direct impact of feed treatment in Column 4 in Table 7 ( $\beta = 0.158$ ). This suggests that even limiting the sample to those who are likely to experience peer effects by virtue of gender, tenure on the app, or balance, does not result in any deviation from the results in Table 7. We repeated this analysis for all the direct effects shown in Table 7, as well as the interaction effects, and obtained fully consistent results. While we cannot rule out the effects of interference, we believe these results show that the effect of interference in our context may be small.
