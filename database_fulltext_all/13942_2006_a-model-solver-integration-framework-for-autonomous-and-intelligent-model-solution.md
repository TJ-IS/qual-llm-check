# A model-solver integration framework for autonomous and intelligent model solution

Keun-Woo Lee <sup>a,\*</sup>, Soon-Young Huh <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> IBM Business Consulting Services, ASEM TOWER, 159-1 Samsung-dong, Gangnam-gu, Seoul 135-798, Republic of Korea <sup>b</sup> Graduate School of Management, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, 207-43 Chongyang-ni, Dongdaemoon-gu, Seoul 130-722, Republic of Korea

Received 29 October 2003; received in revised form 8 August 2005; accepted 14 August 2005 Available online 12 September 2005

## Abstract

Complex mathematical models are being increasingly adopted for corporate decision-making, and lay users are becoming more involved through institutional decision-making processes. Because of the technical complexity and variability of mathematical models, difficulties arise in supporting complicated model solution processes and in maintaining new models with existing solvers (i.e., problem-solving algorithms). This paper proposes an intelligent model-solver integration framework that facilitates an intuitive and user-friendly model solution process and evolutionary model maintenance. Specifically, for an intuitive model solution, the framework gives a model management system the ability to suggest autonomously compatible solvers of a model without direct user intervention. In addition, it solves the model by matching intelligently model parameters with solver parameters without any serious conflicts. Thus, the framework improves the productivity of institutional model solving tasks by relieving the user from the risk of erroneous application of a solver to syntactically and semantically incompatible models, and by reducing the burden given by the considerable learning process of model and solver semantics. <sup>D</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Model management systems; Model-solver integration; Decision support systems

## 1. Introduction

As business environments change rapidly and become more competitive, precise and flexible analysis also becomes more important. Thus mathematical models have been increasingly adopted to support solutions on decision problems. A decision support system (DSS) is an information system that can be used to support solutions on decision problems faced by an organization [43,46]. In a DSS, for user-friendliness and intuitive solution, a decision problem is formulated as a model—a structured form composed of a set of declarative modeling language statements [20,25,36] that are easy to understand and execute. Specifically, a model management system (MMS) is dedicated for managing the entire life cycle of models as one of the three component modules of a DSS together with a database management system (DBMS) and a dialogue management system [43].

In the field of model management research, there have been a wide variety of studies ranging from a focus on the modeling languages for effective modeling of management science/operations research (MS/OR)

problems [10,17,21], and the model representation scheme for easier creation, retrieval, and execution of models [20,25,42,47], to the DSS component integration framework for the reuse of models with different data sets for different problems [5,13,37,41]. However, few studies have focused on the precise and flexible decision-making support provided by the reuse of solvers [15,25], i.e., problem-solving algorithms against diverse models and data sets. The reuse of solvers brings in two kinds of model-solver integration perspectives. While a solver can be applied to and reused in multiple models that have similar problem structures, a single model may have multiple problem-solving requirements and thus, may need multiple solvers. Such a flexible integration framework can make the decision support process more user-friendly and streamlined, and can result in a more simplified system architecture that leads to easier DSS implementation and maintenance.

To attain these benefits from the flexible modelsolver integration, the user should be knowledgeable in terms of what a model and its component statements mean and which solvers are applicable to the model for certain purposes. Once a suitable solver is chosen, the user should be able to solve the model flexibly by considering the individual model component statements and solver parameter values to meet the problem-solving requirements [25]. In practice, however, because the semantic understanding of a model or a solver is not a trivial task [45], lay users, sometimes even knowledgeable professional users, often have difficulty in choosing the suitable solvers from the organizational solver library and applying them appropriately to a given model [2,14]. Understanding such burdensome model and solver semantics makes users more hesitant and often poses obstacles in utilizing models in solving decision problems even if the overall usefulness of the models is accepted. Thus, the MMS as a dedicated tool should be able to demonstrate the following three capabilities to make the overall model solution process easy and productive.

First, for a specific model under consideration, the MMS should be sufficiently user-friendly to suggest autonomously a set of solvers that is both syntactically and semantically compatible with the model. This autonomous solver suggestion capability will enable the lay user who might not have enough expertise to identify compatible solvers, to select appropriate solvers easily and will prevent the user from misusing incompatible solvers. Second, when a particular solver is chosen for the model, adequate parameter matching is needed for model solution between the model and the solver in such a way that input values in the model are inserted as the corresponding input parameter values of the solver. In addition, output values in the solver are transferred as the corresponding output values of the model. Thus, the MMS should be able to perform parameter matching between the model and the solver intelligently, and it should result to a model solution even if the user has little knowledge of the meaning of parameter values of a solver and cannot perform exact matching between the model and solver parameters. Third, the MMS should be adaptive to any organizational needs for changes in the models and solvers. This requires a flexible architecture to accommodate those changes that are caused by the dynamic evolution of the business environment as well as the advancement of the problem-solving theories. More precisely, the MMS should be able to incorporate new models and solvers so that they are immediately functional with the existing models and solvers without requiring any drastic modification of the MMS. This adaptability is becoming imperative for the MMS as more organizations have constructed the DSS distributed across their internal/ external networks [23,24,26,36], and changes in models and solvers occur more frequently.

Recognizing such requirements of the MMS, this research focuses on the development of a model-solver integration framework that facilitates the autonomous solver suggestion and intelligent model solution capabilities while adapting to the changes in models and solvers. In developing the framework, first, we propose systematic and unified representation schemes for models and solvers by using the generic model concept [25] as a conceptual framework. Because the generic model concept is paradigm neutral, the representation schemes can accommodate both models and solvers in a variety of modeling practices and problem domains, and thus the proposed model-solver integration framework can be easily extended into any MMS in a variety of problem domains. Second, on the basis of the model and solver representation schemes, we propose a multi-agent approach as the physical system architecture of the framework. The agents can assist users with solving models by suggesting solvers and matching the two without direct user intervention and by proactively interacting with the users [1,4]. Furthermore, the multi-agent approaches on agent collaboration are effective in providing a flexible system platform for integrating the models and the solvers while arranging diverse forms of interactions and resolving possible conflicts between the models and the solvers [16,29,30].

We consider a financial MMS as the application of the framework. It is an institutional instrument in financial establishments, and it helps a user (e.g., a trader)

evaluate a financial product using various pricing algorithms in an intuitive and timely manner. The financial MMS is specifically considered as an appropriate example because it needs autonomous suggestion of solvers and adequate parameter matching between the model and the solver. This is particularly important in the presence of complicated and disparate stipulations between financial products and pricing algorithms caused by their wide variety of approaches to the product evaluation theories [27,28] and the need for extensive financial expertise to comprehend the semantics of the products and the algorithms [28,31,40]. If the financial MMS can relieve the user from a complicated problem-solving procedure, it can streamline the investment decision-making processes and enhance the preciseness of each user’s financial valuation.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the existing studies on model-solver integration. Section 3 describes the systematic representations of models, solvers, and their interactions with an option pricing example of a financial MMS. Specifically, modeling conflict issues that make it difficult to define a standardized interfacing scheme for the integration of models and solvers are explained. Section 4 presents the physical system architecture for the proposed framework using the object-oriented paradigm, and explains system procedures used for the autonomous solver suggestion and intelligent model solution. A prototype system is also presented to illustrate the proposed framework. Section 5 discusses the contributions and limitations of the proposed framework followed by directions of future research. The Final section summarizes this paper.

## 2. Literature on model-solver integration

In terms of the model-solver integration, most previous studies have adopted one of two approaches for the interaction between models and solvers [22]: (1) loose integration using intermediary files or database tables for data exchange, and (2) tight integration using hard-coded operations or subroutines performing data exchange and solver invocation.

First, in the loose integration approach, parameter data are exchanged through an intermediary text file in a standardized form such as a mathematical programming system (MPS) format file [43], which generally matches well with solvers [17–19,21]. In the MMS, a model is translated into a text file with the standardized format, and the solver obtains its input parameter values by reading the file. This loose integration approach has been adopted and implemented in many studies and software packages focusing on optimization models because these optimization models can be represented as a set of pre-defined algebraic formula blocks, typically consisting of decision variables, constraints, and objective functions. Based on the block structure of optimization models, the intermediary text file and its generation from a model can be standardized and implemented in a straightforward way, and solvers can interpret and execute the blocks in an algebraic form without the need to understand their semantics. Thus, this approach can fully automate the model-solver interfaces by constructing a program that reads the models and generates the intermediary text file [15,20]. Moreover, because the loosely integrated models and solvers are independent of each other, the addition of new models/solvers, and modification of existing ones, can be seamlessly conducted in the MMS. However, these advantages are limited and only relevant to optimization models that have such a typical pattern of model structure. For non-optimization models that do not have such a typical model structure, this loose integration approach might not be applicable because the intermediary text file cannot be standardized and has to be customized for every model.

Second, in the tight integration approach, special operations or subroutines for model-solver interfacing are developed and hard-coded into the MMS [25,34]. Tight integration tends to execute computing much faster than loose integration because hard-coded interfacing operations mostly use memory processing and very rarely access persistent storage (e.g., file). Moreover, because the interfacing operations can be customized for individual models and solvers according to their structural and semantic characteristics, this approach does not restrict the model structures and can be applied to a wide variety of problem domains and modeling paradigms. Consequently, such closely-coupled modelsolver interactions could lead to more precise and reliable results. However, because the operations are hardcoded into the MMS, this tight integration approach has a disadvantage in terms of system maintenance, that is, whenever a new model or a new solver is added into the MMS or an existing one is modified, the entire system should also be revised to reflect such changes in the hard-coded interfacing operations.

In this sense, despite the considerable benefits attainable from flexible integration of models and solvers, the two previous approaches were still unsatisfactory in providing a practical and plausible model-solver integration framework, partly due to the maintenance problem of the tight integration approach, and partly due to the intermediary file-based loose integration approach.

These limitations have also caused users of the MMS to perceive the solver suggestion and model solution capabilities as impractical and to put a low priority among desirable MMS features [45]. Overcoming these limitations while accommodating advantages of the previous approaches, this paper proposes a combined modelsolver integration framework that can provide sufficiently practical utilities of the solver suggestion and model solution capabilities to the MMS users.

Specifically, the proposed framework is developed based on the loose integration approach but allows customization of interfacing operations as in the tight integration approach. By customizing interfacing operations according to individual model structures, the framework enables interoperability for models and solvers developed on different modeling paradigms with different model structures, which makes the framework applicable to a wide variety of problem domains. This paradigm and domain independence of the framework was previously impossible in the intermediary file-based loose integration approach because the interfacing operations adhere to the optimization model structure through the rigid intermediary text file. For intermediating models and solvers, we define an information registry as a dictionary for managing the required interfacing parameters and for providing instructions regarding customized interacting operations. For effecting solver suggestion and model solution, model and solver agents are employed. They refer to the information registry with autonomy and with a communication capability. This approach that separates the interfacing instructions (in the registry) from the executing applications (the two agents) reduces the maintenance problem in the interface customization because we can easily change the model-solver interfaces only by updating the information registry with no need to revise the entire system. In addition, we use objectoriented concepts for designing the information registry and the agents to be flexible enough to support various forms of parameter exchanges and interacting operations on the basis of inheritance and polymorphism [6,34]. Thus, the framework aims to ensure the independence of models and solvers, and aims to adapt flexibly to diverse model structures while taking advantage of the generality and reliability found in the tight integration approach.

## 3. Generic representations of models and solvers

This section describes systematic and unified representations of models, solvers, and their interactions with particular focus on the modeling conflicts among the models. First, we explain the core concepts of the representations, and define the three modeling conflicts:

semantic, naming, and structural conflicts. An option pricing example of the financial MMS is also provided to illustrate representations and conflicts.

## 3.1. Core concepts of the model and solver representations

We adopt the generic model concept [25] as a conceptual framework for representing models, solvers, and their interactions, and extend it to be more flexible in selecting and applying solvers to a model. Specifically, a solver, which was previously embedded in the models represented using the original generic model concept, is now separated from the models and introduced as an independent construct. Hard-coding a solver into the models is not desirable because it mixes solver implementation with model implementation and makes the model harder to understand and maintain. Moreover, it is almost impossible to add new solvers or modify existing ones unless the model is modified.

In our representations, a model, characterized by an object type (model type: ModelType), represents the semantic issues of the decision problems, and thus is responsible for maintaining and updating all the problem statements. The model type has a name, a set of ports, and primitive manipulating operations. A port, characterized by a port type (PortType), has a one-toone mapping relationship to a problem statement of a model. The ports, as a whole, constitute the external interface of a model, through which the model interacts with its outside environment (i.e., human modelers, solvers and databases). With respect to the interaction role, the ports of a model are classified into two types: input ports (inports) admit data and output ports (outports) produce problem-solving results in connection with their environment. The attributes of the port type are intended to directly capture the meaning and structure of a single problem statement of the model. Each port includes a unique name, a set of attributes, and operations to describe the information pertaining to data values and the algebraic expression of the statement.

A solver, characterized by a solver type (SolverType), focuses on the actual problem-solving computation, and is responsible for generating the computation results of the decision problem modeled. The solver type has a name, a set of ports for interfacing with model ports, primitive manipulating operations, and a calculation operation implementing the solving algorithm. The ports of the solver type are also classified into inports and outports; the inports accept input parameters of the calculation operation while the outports hold the output parameters after the calculation operation is executed. In the presence of a model, the inports of the solver correspond to the inports of the model and the outports of the solver correspond to the outports of the model (Fig. 1). That is, after a compatible solver is selected for model solution, the model delegates its outports to the solver by assigning its inport values to the corresponding inports of the solver. The solver then produces the outport values using its calculation operation and returns them to the outports of the model. As such, the model solution process requires individual ports of the model to correspond to the solver ports in a one-to-one relationship. We call such a relationship a port mapping.

Once such port mappings between individual models and their compatible solvers are pre-defined, the MMS can maintain dependence relationships between models and solvers and thus can facilitate the suggestion of solvers for a given model and the model solution by matching the model’s ports with the corresponding ports of its compatible solvers. In this capacity, however, from a solver’s perspective, the models might have semantic and/or syntactic conflicts with one another in their ports [3,8,15,38,44], and such conflicts require port mappings to be customized for each model. Typically, the following three types of modeling conflicts are observed among the models:

<sup>!</sup> Semantic conflicts imply that, depending on the problem semantics of the individual models, the port mappings vary and thus a solver needs to look up different ports in each model to get its inport values. Consider two optimization models that share a linear programming solver: a manufacturing cost optimization model and a salesperson shortest-path search model. In solving the models, different ports of the models are to be assigned to the inports of the solver depending on which model is applied: production costs for the cost optimization model and distances between sales locations for the shortestpath model. In brief, the port mappings between semantics-bearing model ports and semantics-free solver ports are to be pre-arranged for errorless execution.

<sup>!</sup> Naming conflicts arise when models use synonyms to describe the same ports, or homonyms for different ports. For example, in the manufacturing cost optimization model, the port for the production plant can be named <sup>b</sup>plant,<sup>Q</sup> but the same port may be named <sup>b</sup>factory<sup>Q</sup> in other models. In such a case, the <sup>b</sup>plant<sup>Q</sup> and the <sup>b</sup>factory<sup>Q</sup> are homonyms. On the other hand, <sup>b</sup>quantities<sup>Q</sup> may mean production quantities in a model, while it may mean raw material quantities in other models, where the two <sup>b</sup>quantities<sup>Q</sup> become synonyms. As seen in semantic conflict, the port mapping between synonym- and homonym-bearing model ports and solver ports must be pre-arranged for correct execution.

<sup>!</sup> Structural conflicts arise when the same ports in individual models are represented in different data formats. For example, production costs can be represented as a list (e.g., a set of costs for different production quantities) in a model but in other models, they might be represented with a scalar value (e.g., the average cost).

Regarding these three modeling conflicts, the MMS should specify not only the adequate port mappings between individual models and their compatible solvers (for resolving semantic and naming conflicts) but also suitable data format conversion methods in each port mapping (for resolving structural conflicts). In this paper, such port mappings and data format conversion methods are called interfacing rules. If the interfacing rules were to be properly customized for individual models and solvers, it would enable the MMS to maintain the dependence and port-matching relationships accommodating the semantic and syntactic heterogeneity of the model ports while resolving the three modeling conflicts. Therefore, the MMS can be equipped with the autonomous solver suggestion and intelligent model solution capabilities.

## 3.2. An option pricing example

In the model management literature, many studies on model-solver integration have focused on the optimization models, which can be represented as a set of predefined and typical formula blocks. Because of the semantic-free block structure of optimization models, the above-mentioned three modeling conflicts rarely occur among the optimization models and thus the interactions between the models and their solvers are relatively simple to implement. However, because this paper aims to develop a generic model-solver integration framework applicable even for non-optimization models that do not have such a standardized block structure, we will verify our proposed framework from a non-optimization perspective.

Consider the financial MMS that supports a user’s evaluation of financial products by calculating their various numerical analysis factors, such as the net present value (NPV) [27]. In the financial MMS, for user-friendly and intuitive calculations, each financial product is formulated as a model composed of a set of declarative modeling language statements capturing the properties of the product and the analysis factors to be calculated. Additionally, a set of pricing algorithms is also provided as solvers for these financial product models. To exemplify such product models in the financial MMS, we consider two kinds of financial products: a stock option and a currency option. The stock option allows the holder to buy or sell a certain stock (designated as its underlying product) at some prearranged price at a specified time in the future, and the currency option allows buying or selling a foreign currency with similar pre-arranged conditions. Fig. 2 shows models for the two options using the structured modeling language (SML) [21] that represents a model as a set of ports (i.e., a set of genera). Each port captures a single problem statement of the model and semantically related ports may be aggregated into higher level conceptual units called modules.

In Fig. 2(a), the stock option model named STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION is built with three modules, OPTION<sup>\_</sup>DATA, STOCK<sup>\_</sup>DATA, and VALUATION<sup>\_</sup> RESULTS. OPTION<sup>\_</sup>DATA aggregates ports for the properties relevant to the option; STOCK<sup>\_</sup>DATA aggregates ports for the properties relevant to the underlying stock of the option; VALUATION<sup>\_</sup>RESULTS aggregates ports for the numerical analysis factors relevant to the option’s evaluation. Because we consider only the NPV among those analysis factors, VALUA-TION<sup>\_</sup>RESULTS has only one port, NPV. Meanwhile, the currency option model named CURRENCY<sup>\_</sup>OP-TION also consists of three modules, OPTION<sup>\_</sup>DATA, CURRENCY<sup>\_</sup>DATA, and VALUATION<sup>\_</sup>RESULTS (Fig. 2(b)). CURRENCY<sup>\_</sup>DATA aggregates ports for the properties relevant to the underlying foreign currency of the currency option. In both models, the port NPV is chosen as an outport because the purpose of the models is to calculate their NPVs. The rest of the ports are chosen as inports because they are instantiated with data sets supplied by the human modeler or external data sources.

A well-known pricing algorithm called the Black– Scholes method [27,28] is provided as a solver for the two models. This method requires the following three inports for calculating NPV: an exercise price, a maturity date, and a dividend rate. First, the exercise price is the pre-arranged price at which the holder of an option can buy or sell the underlying product. The exercise price is also called the strike price. Second, the maturity date is the date at which or by which the underlying product can be bought or sold. Usually, it is represented as a specific date or as a time interval between the present date and the maturity date. The Black–Scholes method solver uses the latter representation. Third, the dividend rate is the rate per annum at which a continuous dividend is paid by the underlying stock of a stock option. However, in a real situation, because the dividend of a stock is paid discretely and irregularly, the Black–Scholes method solver needs proper conversion from the discretely paid dividend amounts into the continuously paying rate. In the case of a currency option, the dividend rate is analogous to the interest rate of the underlying currency because the currency pays interests as a stock pays dividends. Because the interest rate itself is a continuously paying rate, such a conversion for the stock option’s dividends is not required for the currency option’s interest rate.

Thus, according to the model and solver representation scheme shown in Fig. 1, the option pricing example, based on the two models STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION and CUR-RENCY<sup>\_</sup>OPTION, and the Black–Scholes method solver, can be illustrated as in Fig. 3. On the right side of Fig. 3, BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES designates the Black–Scholes method solver and has three inports named EXERCI-SE<sup>\_</sup>PRICE, MATURITY, and DIVIDEND<sup>\_</sup>RATE and an outport named NPV. On the left side, STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OP-TION and CURRENCY<sup>\_</sup>OPTION designate the two option models, showing only ports that map with the corresponding ports of BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES. Detailed port descriptions are omitted to focus more on the interactions between the two models and the solver.

The three modeling conflicts, semantic, naming, and structural conflicts described in the previous section occur in the option pricing example. First, a semantic conflict occurs because the ports of STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION and CURRENCY<sup>\_</sup>OPTION that correspond to the port DIVIDEND<sup>\_</sup>RATE of BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES are different from each other. In the case of STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION, the port DIVIDEND should be mapped with DIVI-DEND<sup>\_</sup>RATE of BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES whereas in the case of CURRENCY<sup>\_</sup>OPTION, the port INTERE-ST<sup>\_</sup>RATE should also be mapped with DIVIDEN-D<sup>\_</sup>RATE of BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES. Second, a naming conflict occurs because the port EXERCISE<sup>\_</sup>PRICE of STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION and the port STRIKE<sup>\_</sup>PRICE of CURRENCY<sup>\_</sup>OPTION are synonyms. Both ports need to be mapped with the port EXERCISE<sup>\_</sup>PRICE of BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES. Third, a structural conflict occurs because the port DIVIDEND of STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION represented as a matrix (i.e., a list of the two values: dividend paying dates and dividend amounts) should be converted to a single number following the data format of the port DIVIDEND<sup>\_</sup>RATE of BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES (i.e., a number as a rate). However, the port INTERE-ST<sup>\_</sup>RATE of CURRENCY<sup>\_</sup>OPTION represented as a number can be delivered to DIVIDEND<sup>\_</sup>RATE of BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES directly.

In the following sections, we explain how the model-solver integration framework proposed in this paper resolves these three modeling conflicts by managing the interfacing rules for port mappings and data format conversion between the two models (STOCK<sup>\_</sup> OPTION and CURRENCY<sup>\_</sup>OPTION) and the BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES solver. Based on the interfacing rules, we also present how the framework facilitates the autonomous solver suggestion and intelligent model solution capabilities. In explaining the functionality and mechanisms of the framework, a simplified version of core construct classes is presented on the basis of object-oriented design concepts.

## 4. Model-solver integration framework

The model-solver integration framework has two primary tasks: management of the interfacing rules and execution of the solver suggestion and model solution. To support these two tasks, a two-layered approach is provided. On the lower layer, the port mapping dictionary exists as an information registry for managing the interfacing rules for the customized port mappings and data format conversion methods between models and solvers. On the upper layer, the model and solver agents perform the autonomous solver suggestion and intelligent model solution. In conducting the tasks, the agents specifically look in the port mapping dictionary and resolve the semantic, naming, and structural conflicts. Fig. 4 shows the conceptual architecture of the model-solver integration framework using the two-layered approach. The port mapping dictionary and the two agents are explained in the following sections with the definitions of model and solver classes.

## 4.1. Model class

A model class is an abstract data structure realizing the model type in Section 3. Because the model type has a set of ports as its external interface for interacting with its outside environment (i.e., human modelers, solvers, and databases), the model class aggregates the port class, implementing the port type. Fig. 5(a) shows an object-oriented schema for the model class represented in the Unified Modeling Language (UML) [6].

In Fig. 5(a), the model class and the port class are named Model and Port, respectively. Model has two one-to-many aggregations, inport and outport, respectively indicating its inports and outports. To handle a wide range of data formats effectively, Port can transform into several sub-classes including Numberport, StringPort, DatePort, ListPort, and MatrixPort. In determining the data format of a port, two factors are specifically considered: the data domain (e.g., number, string, and date) and the data cardinality (e.g., scalar value, list, and matrix). For the data domain, StringPort, NumberPort, and DatePort represent a string, a number, and a date value in the attribute Value, respectively. For the data cardinality, ListPort and MatrixPort capture a list or a matrix of values by containing multiple Port objects through the element aggregation relationship with the class Port. Based on the class diagram, Fig. 5(b) presents an object data example for the model STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION of Fig. 3 using the object diagram of UML. In Fig. 5(b), m1 is a model object instantiated from the class Model and stands for STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION. The STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION model, m1, has three inport objects representing exercise price (p1), maturity date (p2), and dividend (p3), respectively instantiated from NumberPort, DatePort, and MatrixPort. Note that the dividend (p3) matrix consists of a set of past dividend payments composed of the payment date (p5) and dividend amount (p6). The model also has an outport object representing net present value (p4), instantiated from NumberPort.

Thus, a user could write these commands to understand which data values should be supplied for a model to solve.

## 4.2. Solver class

## 4.3. Port mapping dictionary

Once the model and solver classes are defined, it is necessary to facilitate the autonomous solver suggestion and intelligent model solution while resolving the three modeling conflicts. Thus, the MMS should manage the interfacing rules for adequate port mappings and the data format conversion methods between individual models and their compatible solvers. The port mapping dictionary, as a dedicated tool for interfacing rule management, maintains interfacing rules in a tabular form where each row represents an individual interfacing rule that specifies the port pairs to be mapped and their data format conversion method. Typically, every row of the dictionary has these six attributes (i.e., columns): model, solver, model port, solver port, port type, and conversion script. The attributes model and solver indicate the model and solver names to which the interfacing rule is applied; the attributes model port and solver port indicate the corresponding port names of the model and solver to be linked; the attribute port type determines whether it is for an inport or an outport; finally, the attribute conversion script describes the detailed instructions of the data format conversion.

Specifically, conversion script is expressed as an algebraic formula built on two kinds of operands–port references and environmental variables. A port reference means a port value and is denoted by a port name enclosed by ampersands (&) at both ends (e.g., and EXERCISE<sup>\_</sup>PRICE and ); an environmental variable refers to a pre-defined system variable and is denoted by a capital string (e.g., TODAY for the current date). These operands can be recursively composed by a set of operators. The operators include domain casting operators for converting the data domain of a port (e.g., TO<sup>\_</sup>NUM for conversion to integer and TO<sup>\_</sup>STR for conversion to string), aggregation operators for converting the data cardinality of a port (e.g., SUM for summation of a list and AVG for average of a list), and other mathematical operators for various numerical calculations (e.g., LOG for logarithm and SQRT for square root). Because enumeration of all the possible operands and operators for conversion script is beyond the scope of this paper, we focus on its fundamental requirements and practical uses in the subsequent part.

Table 1 lists a conceptual structure of the port mapping dictionary with the option pricing example of Fig. 3 where two models (STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION and CURREN-CY<sup>\_</sup>OPTION) and one solver BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES are under consideration. Most of the interfacing rules are self-explanatory except conversion script in the third row. The conversion script between the DIVIDEND port of STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION model and the DIVIDEND<sup>\_</sup> RATE port of the BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES solver is written as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \& \text {DIVIDEND\_RATE} & = \operatorname{LOG} (1 + \operatorname{SUM} \\ & \times (\& \text {DIVIDEND} & [ * ] [ 2 ]) \\ & / \& \text {STOCK\_PRICE} &). \end{array}
$$

The above formula converts the DIVIDEND matrix composed of the two columns—dividend paying dates and dividend amounts—into a continuously paying rate that the port DIVIDEND<sup>\_</sup>RATE of BLACK<sup>\_</sup> SCHOLES requires. In the conversion, the formula uses two pairs of brackets for indexing elements of the DIVIDEND matrix (the first pair for the row index and the second for the column index), and thus the expression SUM( and DIVIDEND and[\*] [2]) adds up all the second column values of the matrix, i.e., summation of the dividend amounts. We will provide a more detailed explanation of the formal specifications and design of the script in another study [33].

Fig. 8(a) shows an object-oriented schema for the port mapping dictionary class named PortMappingDictionary. In Fig. 8(a), to capture the interfacing rules listed in the dictionary, PortMappingDictionary aggregates the interfacing rule class (InterfacingRule) through a one-to-many aggregation relationship (rules). Additionally, for the MMS to manipulate the interfacing rules, PortMappingDictionary provides three operations, AddRule (), RemoveRule (), and ParseScript (). AddRule () and RemoveRule () are for registration and deletion of the interfacing rules; ParseScript () is for interpretation and execution of conversion script. Fig. 8(b) presents example commands showing how the MMS can use the port mapping dictionary in conducting the autonomous solver suggestion and intelligent model solution. The first command shows how to retrieve the compatible solver names of the model STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OP-TION from the port mapping dictionary. Because the compatible solvers must have interfacing rules with the model in the dictionary, this command finds the solvers by extracting the attribute solver from the interfacing rules that have the model name <sup>b</sup>STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION<sup>Q</sup> in the attribute model. Thus, using this command, the MMS can identify and suggest the compatible solvers autonomously by referring to the port mapping dictionary without any direct user intervention. The second command retrieves the interfacing rules for the inports between the STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION model and the BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES solver from the port mapping dictionary. Using the interfacing rules obtained by this command, the MMS can match the model ports with the solver ports adequately and convert the data formats of model ports if they are different from those of the corresponding solver ports. The third command converts the data format of the port DIVIDEND according to the conversion script script1 obtained from the port mapping dictionary. The converted port (sp) returned by this command, will be further linked to the corresponding port of the solver (i.e., DIVIDEND<sup>\_</sup>RATE of BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES).

## 4.4. Model and solver agents

On the basis of the classes Model, Solver, and PortMappingDictionary, two software agents–model agent and solver agent–are defined at a higher level to perform the autonomous solver suggestion and intelligent model solution. Referring to the port mapping dictionary, these two agents cooperate for exchanging port values between models and solvers while resolving the three modeling conflicts. We provide the responsibilities of the two agents and explain their cooperating procedures in conducting the solver suggestion and model solution.

The model agent, which interacts with the user views directly, assists a user’s model-solving activities by providing two types of information to the user view: compatible solver names of the model to be solved and the results of the model solution. First, to provide the compatible solver names, the model agent looks them up in the port mapping dictionary using such commands as shown in Fig. 8(b). These solver names are then displayed in the user view, and the user selects an appropriate one among the solver names depending on the user’s problem-solving requirements. Second, once the user selects a solver and makes a request for model solution, the model agent sends a model-solving request message to the solver agent. This message consists of the solver name to be used and the inport values required for the solver to execute its calculation operation. In creating the message, the model agent consults the port mapping dictionary to understand how to generate the inport values of the solver by referring to the interfacing rules between the model and the solver. Afterwards, when the solver agent returns the results of its model solution, the model agent provides them to the user through the user view.

In this context, the solver agent performs the model solution and returns its results to the model agent in response to the model-solving request message from the model agent. When the solver agent receives the model-solving request message, it retrieves from the solver library the solver specified in the message, fills in the inports of the solver with the inport values contained in the message, and executes the calculation operation of the solver. This solver execution process can be done by such commands as shown in Fig. 7(b). Once the calculation of the solver is finished, the solver agent sends a modelsolving result message to the model agent. In sending the message, as the model agent does to create the model-solving request message, the solver agent also consults the port mapping dictionary to understand the interfacing rules for the outport mappings between the model and the solver.

Fig. 9 shows detailed steps of the solver suggestion and model solution processes performed by the model and solver agents using the sequence diagram of UML. First, the solver suggestion proceeds with the following four steps. When a user starts solving a model, the user view (1) retrieves the model from the model-base and (2) sends a query about the compatible solvers of the model to the model agent. On receiving the query, the model agent (3) looks in the port mapping dictionary to find the names of the compatible solvers and (4) sends the names to the user view. From among the solver names displayed on the user view, the user would select an appropriate one according to the user’s problem-solving requirements. Second, the model solution is performed using the following steps. When the user selects a solver to be used, the user view (5) sends a request for the model solution with the selected solver name to the model agent. Then, the model agent (6) queries the interfacing rules between the model and the solver from the port mapping dictionary. According to the interfacing rules, the model agent (7) creates the model-solving request message consisting of the solver name and the inport values for the solver, and (8) sends this message to the solver agent. Upon receiving the model-solving request message, the solver agent (9) retrieves the solver specified in the message from the solver library, (10) sets the inport values of the solver, and (11) executes the calculation operation of the solver. When (12) the calculation is finished, the solver agent (13) gets the calculation results from the outports of the solver and (14) queries the interfacing rules for the outport mappings between the model and the solver from the port mapping dictionary. Referring to the interfacing rules, the solver agent (15) creates the model-solving result message and (16) sends it to the model agent. Then, the model agent (17) provides the model-solving results to the user through the user view.

The model and solver agents perform the solver suggestion and model solution autonomously by communicating with each other and referring to the interfacing rules maintained in the port mapping dictionary (Fig. 9). In addition, because the interfacing rules provide the port mappings and data format conversion methods between the individual models and their compatible solvers, the agents can intelligently exchange port values between the two while resolving the three modeling conflicts. By virtue of this autonomous and intelligent characteristic of the two agents, the user needs only to select an appropriate solver from among the suggested ones on the user view. This simplicity and user-friendliness of the model-solving activities improve the productivity and usefulness of the MMS by allowing even non-professional lay users to use with ease the diverse sets of models and solvers provided by the MMS.

## 4.5. Prototype system for investment decision-making

We describe a prototype implementation of the proposed model-solver integration framework. From the implementation, we show how the proposed framework-based MMS can support a user’s problem-solving activities and discuss the advantages gained from the framework.

Our proposed prototype, the Flexible Integration of Models and Solvers (FIMS) system was implemented using the Java programming language. Core constructs of the system—model, solver, model agent, solver agent, and port mapping dictionary—are implemented as Java objects according to their corresponding object schemas as explained in Section 4. The interfacing rules defining port mappings and data format conversion between models and solvers are stored in a relational database system, MySQL. These rules are managed by the port mapping dictionary object. Fig. 10 shows some sample screens of the prototype system for pricing financial products like the option pricing example in Fig. 3. The screen in Fig. 10(a) calculates the NPVs (solutions) of a user’s option products (decision problems) using the models and solvers for the options. For example, the product <sup>d</sup>IBM Call Mar 90<sup>T</sup>, an option on IBM stock (Product Name column) is modeled as a

STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION model (Model column), and the BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES solver is assigned (Solver column) as the solver for calculating the NPV. The screen in Fig. 10(b) shows detailed modeling information for the product <sup>d</sup>IBM Call Mar 90<sup>T</sup>. By assigning data values of <sup>d</sup>IBM Call Mar 90<sup>T</sup> to the corresponding ports of the STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION model, it defines the specific model instance [25] for the particular product. In the prototype system, although the product data should be manually entered into the system for simplicity of implementation, a real MMS uses database systems to store such product data and assigns them to the model ports systematically [41].

In solving these product models, first, the system suggests applicable solvers for the models so that the user can select appropriate solvers easily. The screen in Fig. 10(a) suggests four solvers—BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES, BINOMIAL, FINITE<sup>\_</sup>DIFF, and MONTE<sup>\_</sup>CARLO— for the STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION model (autonomous solver suggestion). The user selects what he or she wants (e.g., BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES) from among the suggested solvers, then the system determines the solution according to the interfacing rules (e.g., CalcNPV<sup>1</sup>) between the model and the selected solver (intelligent model solution). Through these processes, the user can get the solution only by selecting the appropriate solver from among the system-suggested ones, without worrying about the technical details behind the scenes such as the complex structures and relationships between the model and solvers. These simple and intuitive userinterfaces, like the screen in Fig. 10(a) greatly improve the user-friendliness of the MMS with significant convenience and thus facilitate better MMS utility in solving decision problems.

In addition to the user-friendliness, another advantage of the FIMS system found from the prototype implementation is that the system can uniformly consider every model and solver as a set of ports, and thus the system can manipulate all the models and solvers by dealing equally with their ports regardless of their modeling paradigms. This structural uniformity of the port structure of the models and solvers makes the MMS architecture more simplistic and streamlined and thus it leads to easier MMS development and maintenance in a wide range of problem domains. For example, consider the implementation of the abovementioned model-solving request message that the model agent sends to the solver agent. The message contains input parameter values required by the solver for calculating the solution. The model agent generates these parameter values from the model statements in such a way that they conform to the data formats of the solver parameters. Although the model statements and solver parameters may have any arbitrary data formats, the model agent can generate the input parameter values by equally considering every model statement and solver parameter as a port.

An example of the model-solving request message between the STOCK<sup>\_</sup>OPTION model and the BLACK<sup>\_</sup>SCHOLES solver is as follows:

```xml
<fims:message type="model-solving request" modelName="STOCK_OPTION" solverName="BLACK_SCHOLES">
    <fims:imports>
    <fims:port name="EXERCISE_PRICE" cardinality="Scalar" format="Number">
    <value data="95"/>
    </fims:port>
    <fims:port name="MATURITY" cardinality="Scalar" format="Number">
    <value data="0.22"/>
    </fims:port>
    <fims:port name="DIVIDEND_RATE" cardinality="Scalar" format="Number">
    <value data="0.03"/>
    </fims:port>
    </fims:imports>
    <fims:exports>
    <fims:port name="NPV" cardinality="Scalar" format="Number">
    <value data="" />
    </fims:port>
    </fims:exports>
</fims:message>
```

In the message, the model agent sends four inport values (<sup>b</sup>fims:inports<sup>N</sup>) required by the solver, and demands an outport value (<sup>b</sup>fims:outports<sup>N</sup>) as the solution from the solver’s calculation. The inport and outport values are specified by the three attributes— name, cardinality, and format—for representing any data formats that a port can have. These three attributes are evenly applied to all the model and solver ports. To generate the inport values by transforming the model inports, the model agent refers to the interfacing rules provided by the port mapping dictionary. These interfacing–rule interpretation and port-value transformation of the model agent can also be applied to all the models and solvers because of their uniform port structure. Thus, even when a new model or solver is added, only if the interfacing rules for the new model or solver are defined in the port mapping dictionary, can the model agent write a proper model-solving request message for the new one as it does for the existing ones.

## 5. Contributions and limitations

We now report the contributions and limitations of the proposed model-solver integration framework. Initially, among the contributions, the following three are worthy of attention.

First, while we have developed the model-solver integration framework in the context of financial decision support, its base methodology itself is generically applicable to a wide variety of problem domains where model-based DSS can help. To achieve such generality, we have defined core constructs of the framework including model, solver, port mapping dictionary, model agent, and solver agent without any restrictions on the modeling paradigms. Specifically, every model statement or solver parameter is uniformly captured as a port regardless of its data format, and thus the model-solver interactions are considered as data exchanges between the model and solver ports. Based on this standardized model-solver interfacing scheme, the port mapping dictionary, model agent, and solver agent consolidate all the primitive solver suggestion mechanisms and model solution mechanisms generically applicable to diverse sets of models and solvers. Providing these constructs as base building blocks, the framework can work as an architectural foundation for the development of an MMS, and make it easy to build an MMS equipped with the solver suggestion and model solution capabilities served by the constructs.

Second, because the interfacing rules between models and solvers are not hard-coded into the MMS and are managed in the port mapping dictionary, the framework can easily adapt to the changes in the models and solvers. When a new model or solver is added to the MMS or an existing one is modified, the framework can additively accommodate it and make it functional with other existing ones by just updating the related interfacing rules in the port mapping dictionary without any re-implementation or re-compilation of the entire system. This adaptability of the framework is quite beneficial in reducing the maintenance cost of the MMS because the models and solvers are continually changing due to the dynamic evolution of the business environment and the advancement of problem-solving theories.

Third, the autonomous, intelligent, and collaborative characteristics of the model and solver agents streamline a user’s model-solving activities and ensure userfriendliness. By autonomously cooperating with each other and intelligently understanding the interfacing rules provided by the port mapping dictionary, the model and solver agents perform the autonomous solver suggestion and intelligent model solution processes while hiding the complicated interactions between the models and solvers from the user. Thus, without having precise knowledge of model-solving procedures, users can experiment and solve a model with multiple problem-solving requirements using diverse sets of solvers. Moreover, in a fully distributed model management environments, the model and solver agents can have much larger roles in identifying and coordinating the models and solvers distributed across internal and/or external networks. For example, regardless of the locations on the network, system platforms, and modeling paradigms, the agents can discover and execute compatible model-solver pairs applicable to the user’s decision problem. We are now studying such an extended model-solver integration framework in fully distributed environments [32] by enlarging the roles of the model and solver agents and adopting Web Services technology [7].

Though these contributions are significant, we admit the following three limitations of the proposed framework. Future research directions are presented to supplement the limitations.

First, whereas the framework helps a user’s to select a solver by suggesting a set of compatible solvers of the targeted model, it is still limited in that eventually, the user has to decide which solver to use from among the suggested ones. Such solver selection requires a user to understand the semantics of the suggested solvers and to compare them considering the user’s specific purpose. This requirement may limit the use of this framework to experienced end users and analysts. To overcome this limitation, the creator of a solver can provide human-readable documents about the solver, describing its objective, base assumptions, and the meaning of its calculation results. Referring to those documents, even less sophisticated users without sufficient knowledge of solvers will be able to select appropriate solvers for their model-solving requirements. Further, we will be able to fully automate this solver selection process in the future, as the emerging technologies on XML-based [9] machine-readable metadata management (e.g., Resource Description Framework (RDF) [35]) are being developed rapidly and the documents on solvers can be created in a machine-readable form.

Second, the model-solver interfacing rules should be enumerated for every compatible model-solver pair in the port mapping dictionary. Also, these rules are manually registered and managed by modeling experts (e.g., creators of models and solvers) who have the responsibility of configuring compatibilities between models and solvers. Such manual rule management for every model-solver connectedness could cause an overwhelming maintenance problem as the number of models and solvers grows large, even with the independence of models and solvers facilitated by the port mapping dictionary. To reduce the burden created by interfacingrule management, we are contemplating the development of model taxonomy classifying models into groups (i.e., model types [25] or model classes [8]) in such a way that every model in a group can share solvers with another [32]. By assigning solvers to the individual model groups, we can represent which models a solver can be applied to. Given such taxonomy, the interfacing rules will be defined only for the model groups not for individual models, and thus the number of rules modeling experts should manage can be significantly reduced.

Third, in defining the model-solver interfacing rules, modeling experts resolve the structural conflict between model and solver ports by writing a conversion script transforming data formats between the ports. In this paper, however, we have focused on the fundamental requirements and practical uses of the conversion script to emphasize the possibility of modeling conflicts in integrating models and solvers, as well as to illustrate desirable system architecture for the conflict resolution (for a more detailed discussion, see Refs. [3,8]). In practice, the management of conversion scripts could be more considerably more complicated and thus it could swamp the maintainability of the system. What makes the maintenance of conversion scripts most difficult is that transforming the data format between ports is not always statically defined but may change frequently because of changes in external environments or shifts in the user’s problem-solving requirements. For example, think of the currency conversions one might encounter when instantiating a global finance model. The currency rate fluctuates all the time and the user might want to use different rates (e.g., the current rate or today’s official rate) even for the same model. To support such dynamically changing conversion needs, we are now elaborating specifications of the conversion script to be more flexible and adaptable to such changing needs [33]. Specifically, variables that reference external data sources (e.g., a database table of currency rates in the above example) are defined as another operand of the conversion script. Using these referencing variables, the conversion script will state only the access paths to the external data and not the data itself, and thus the script does not need to be rewritten even when the external data (e.g., currency rates) change. In addition, we are developing graphical user interfaces through which the user (including less sophisticated users) can edit the scripts easily to reflect the user’s problem-solving requirements.

## 6. Conclusion

An intuitive and user-friendly model solution process is an important functional requirement for the MMS. This is because the semantics of the models and solvers are usually too complicated for most lay users to identify their compatibilities or to understand their parameter-matching patterns. Recognizing this requirement, this paper proposes a model-solver integration framework that enables the MMS to suggest autonomously the compatible solvers of a model without direct user intervention and to solve the model by matching intelligently its parameters with those of the solvers without any serious modeling conflicts (i.e., the semantic, naming, and structural conflicts). In constructing the framework, this paper represents models and solvers as building blocks with input and output ports, and defines a standardized canonical model-solver interfacing scheme that subsumes the core interactions between models and solvers through port mapping and data format conversion methods. Port mapping and conversion methods are managed as interfacing rules in the port mapping dictionary. Referring to the interfacing rules, the model and the solver agents perform the solver suggestion and model solution autonomously by communicating with each other. The uniqueness of our contribution is that we provide a paradigm-neutral and domain-independent framework facilitating the autonomous solver suggestion and intelligent model solution by developing the constructs (i.e., the model, solver, port mapping dictionary, model agent, and solver agent) without any restrictions on the problem domains and modeling paradigms.
