# An empirical investigation into factors affecting patient cancellations and no-shows at outpatient clinics

John B. Norris <sup>a,1</sup>, Chetan Kumar <sup>b,</sup>⁎, Suresh Chand <sup>a</sup>, Herbert Moskowitz <sup>a</sup>, Steve A. Shade <sup>a</sup>, Deanna R. Willis <sup>c</sup>

## a b s t r a c t

Medical facilities competing in the US Healthcare system must consider the likelihood of patient attendance when scheduling appointments. This paper analyzes a robust, panel style registration data set from 9 outpatient facilities consisting of 5 years of patients’ attendance outcomes. The three outcomes, arrivals, cancellations prior to the scheduled appointment and failure to arrive (no-shows), distinguish this paper from prior empirical research that typically treats patient arrivals as a dichotomous outcome by grouping cancellations and no-shows together or excluding cancellations. Distinguishing cancellations from no-shows reveal different effects from patient age and appointment slot day and time. Findings focus on the variables having the greatest impact on attendance and conclude with the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in predicting individual appointment outcomes and the observation that a rather small number of patients represent a disproportionately large percentage of no-shows. Four factors that have the greatest association with patient nonattendance are lead time (call appointment interval), <sup>fi</sup>nancial payer (typically insurance provider), patient age, and the patient's prior attendance history. Lead time has the greatest impact and is the most addressable, whereas a patient's age, insurance provider and, to some extent, patient behavior cannot be altered. Results reveal quite a paradox that scheduling systems designed to help ensure full utilization on a future date also contribute to underutilization by increasing the chance that patients will not show.

## 1. Introduction

Containing rising healthcare costs in the United States is becoming an increasingly important issue. The health industry alone is expected to be about \$3 trillion in 2011 [46]. The Congressional Budget Of<sup>fi</sup>ce (CBO) expects total spending on health care to increase from 16% of gross domestic product (GDP) in 2007 to 25% in 2025 and 37% in 2050 [13]. Medical costs are expected to increase by about 9% in 2011 alone [46]. Clearly these projected cost increases are unsustainable and there has to be concerted efforts to contain them. Reducing administrative costs and making the system more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient is a key area for curtailing health care costs [25,26]. Studies on patient compliance including attendance may help in mitigating healthcare costs by reducing inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies.

Failure by patients to attend scheduled medical appointments can increase medical care costs [55]. In addition it may affect treatment effectiveness [22]. Weinger et al. [55] list the following negative effects of short notice cancellations: they cannot be easily replaced leading to lost revenues with no reduction of labor or facilities costs, they reduce number of appointments available to all patients, and they may undermine clinician-patient relationship. These factors are possibly true in varying degrees to all patient cancellations. Therefore research on patience attendance contributes to strategies for reducing healthcare costs.

Medical literature on patient compliance includes barriers patients encounter in seeking medical care [36]. Topics such as appointment keeping have appeared for the past half century [45], yet the problem persists. This paper adds to the patient attendance literature by providing thorough analysis of an extensive and timely data set of patient attendance. Our study differs from others in that we collect and model three discrete outcomes: patients who arrive, patients who call to cancel prior to their appointment, and patients who fail to do either (no-shows).

Existing literature typically handles cancellations in one of three ways: cancellations grouped with no-shows as missed appointments, cancellations are explicitly excluded or there is no mention of cancellations. Patients may cancel for reasons such as scheduling con<sup>fl</sup>icts, symptom resolution, visiting another provider or other issues, but at least demonstrate responsibility and communicates to the clinic that there was no confusion in the appointment being scheduled. Ideally the appointment is cancelled with suf<sup>fi</sup>cient time to provide the spot to another patient, thus recovering the capacity at the clinic. Our data set provides the opportunity to explore the mathematical relationship that a number of independent variables have on cancellations and no-shows including lead time (call appointment interval), patient age, insurance provider, weather, time of day, and a patient's prior history of keeping appointments.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss healthcare literature related to patient attendance, followed by a description of methodology of analysis. Next we present the results and revised model. Finally we conclude with discussion and areas for future research.

## 2. Related literature

The patient attendance literature explores a number of reasons patients may miss appointments. Deyo and Inui [16] provide an exhaustive table of potential “determinants” and to date is still one of the most comprehensive literature reviews on this topic. Bean and Talaga [4] provide additional reasons. Emotional issues may also account for missed appointments [29], particularly in patients with psychological or behavioral problems [21,43]. Call appointment interval, or lead time, is a commonly cited factor in patient attendance and it creates a barrier to care that could result in poorer medical outcomes [3,16,33]. Benjamin-Bauman et al. [5] conducted experiments <sup>fi</sup>nding higher attendance rates between lead times less than 7 days than lead times more than 2 weeks. Bean and Talaga [4] found lead time for appointment the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of patient attendance.

Lead time is not a universal consideration in attendance models, sometimes due to the unavailability of when an appointment was scheduled [47]. In a study of psychiatric outpatient visits, Centorrino et al. [8] did not <sup>fi</sup>nd lead time signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. George and Rubin [20] discuss the United Kingdom's goal of improving patient satisfaction and patient care by scheduling 90% of patients within 24 h for a primary care physician and 48 h for a general practitioner. Almog et al. [2] suggest patients may shop around for an earlier appointment and may not cancel other appointments. This could result in clinics competing as multiple appointments could be made for the same procedure.

Prior appointment keeping behavior is frequently mentioned as a determinant of patient attendance. Dove and Schneider [17] found prior no-shows as the greatest predictor of future no-shows. Bean and Talaga [3] reported an increased likelihood to miss appointments among past appointment skippers. George and Rubin [20] found mixed results from looking at prior attendance. Patient attendance history is quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed in a variety of ways which may explain mixed results; rate of failed attendance, count of appointments missed or kept, or with an indicator variable for patients missing a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed number of appointments within a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed time frame. Shonick and Klein [51] used a proportion of appointments made but did not mention cancellations. Goldman et al. [21] calculated the percentage of appointments kept in the last year, excluding cancellations. Indicator variables for patients missing a certain number of appointments were used by Neal et al. [42] and Qu et al. [47]. Goldman et al. [21] found no effect on attendance rate from tenure with the clinic or tenure with the physician.

There are con<sup>fl</sup>icting results among demographic and socio economic factors, possibly confounded with method of payment. Goldman et al. (1982) did not <sup>fi</sup>nd an effect from method of payment on attendance rates. George and Rubin [20] found patients with state sponsored insurance, self funding or less coverage more likely to miss appointments. Lowes [33] suggested Medicaid patients may miss appointments due to transportation dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, a covariate dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to obtain. Lower education and lower socio-demographic status has been associated with lower attendance of appointments [3,16]. Age is another common factor, with younger patients having lower attendance rates [3,16]. Goldman et al. [21] found older patients more likely to keep appointments, but the effect disappeared for patients older than 80.

Race or ethnicity of the patient is commonly investigated. Deyo and Inui [16] noted an unclear relationship between race and attendance. Goldman et al. [21] found higher attendance rates among white patients. Bean and Talaga [3] found that race did not have much impact. George and Rubin [20] found mixed results in race as a predictor for nonattendance. Gender is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in many of the studies [16,21]. Bean and Talaga [3] did <sup>fi</sup>nd studies where men had higher rates of appointments kept, but later [4] found men had lower attendance with lead time above 7 days. Marital status was mostly non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant [3,16]. Goldman et al. [21] found a higher rate of attendance keepers among sufferers of cardiovascular, endocrine, respiratory and hematological medical problems. George and Rubin [20] found chronic illness sufferers more likely to show up.

The review of nonattendance models by George and Rubin [20] found lower attendance on Monday than on Friday. Bean and Talaga [3] report lack of consistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings for day of the week and time of the appointment. Dove and Schneider [17] correlated longer driving distances with higher no-show rates. Bad weather, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally precipitation, correlates with lower attendance [37]. Differences exist among the literature regarding the inclusion of cancellations with no-shows. Some literature bundles the cancellations and no-shows calling them missed appointments. Some studies remove cancellations from analysis [8], while others do not mention cancellations [51]. Concern over exclusion of cancellations was noted by Deyo and Inui [16], and Bean and Talaga [3]. A patient may try to cancel, but be unable to get through to the clinic [20,42]. Appendix 1 summarizes results from past studies.

Many methods for dealing with missed appointments exist: reminders and orientation letters, [2,3,6,20,34], exit interview education [23], email reminders [31], text messages [18,53] and reducing the call-appointment interval [5]. One solution, same day appointment scheduling, referred to as “advanced access,” is often credited to Murray [7,38–41,48]. Same day scheduling reduces waste from no-shows, cancellations, con<sup>fi</sup>rming appointments and rescheduling, but may require adding short term capacity to work down the backlog of appointments. One challenge of same day scheduling is ensuring continuity of care, which has been linked to more preventative care visits [19], fewer emergency room visits [10,11] and higher quality care [12] among pediatrics.

Other yield management techniques, such as overbooking are prescribed in [30,32]; however, Sharp and Hamilton [50] caution that overbooking may increase waiting times for patients showing up. Predicting individual appointment arrivals have been described as “extremely inaccurate” and “futile” [16]. Shonick and Klein [51] suggest focusing on patients prone to no-shows. Miller et al. [36] propose future research for “identifying persons at greatest risk for noncompliance.” One practitioner [24] put habitual no-show offenders on probationary status. Knox et al. [28] recommend establishment of a pre-operative assessment clinic to reduce elective surgical case cancellations. The restaurant industry may advocate charging for no-shows [14,35], an unlikely solution in healthcare where an insurance carrier requires service for payment. Some high tech approaches to improve resource utilization in healthcare have been proposed in speci<sup>fi</sup>c settings. Walczak et al. [54] develop a decision support tool to predict resource utilization of costly hospital resources. Day et al. [15] propose a dynamic allocation of cardiac testing time slots using real time scheduling decisions. Tarakci et al. [52] incorporate buy-in from patients in show-up rate depending on level of technology in a specialty hospital offering telemedicine. No-shows have been shown to affect the waiting time in the clinic, as presented in simulation models [9].

The most common method of statistical analysis of patient attendance is multivariate logistic regression [1,27] with a binary dependent variable (for example: [8,21,23,42,47]). Bean and Talaga [4] used CHAID (Chi-squared Automatic Interaction Detector, [56]) to construct decision trees while Shonick and Klein [51] used various chi-square tests.

Many of these prior studies use a binomial dependent variable to examine patient attendance as either arrivals (show) or no-show. Cancellations were typically discarded, bundled with no-show, or not mentioned at all. In our multinomial logistic regression study we go beyond by considering three dependent variables: shows, no-shows, and cancellations. Cancellations are important for service operations because a clinic could devise a strategy to recover the capacity. For example, if a clinic typically receives 2–3 cancellations daily 2–3 h in advance, they could inform someone who needs to get in today to call back or be put on a call back list. On the other hand no-shows are typically lost in available capacity utilization, assuming the clinic does not have a line of walk-ins waiting for an open appointment. Therefore, we may contribute to strategies for improving clinic capacity utilization by using our multinomial methodology for examining patient attendance rates including cancellations. This study expands on Norris et al. [44] by considering a comprehensive set of factors for patient attendance including lead time, prior attendance history, scheduling effects, weather, age, and form of payment.

## 3. Methodology

Institutional Review Board approval for the use of human subjects was obtained at both Indiana University Purdue University at Indianapolis (IUPUI) and Purdue University. Data was extracted at IUPUI, de-identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and then supplied to Purdue University researchers. IU Medical Group-Primary care is the faculty practice for Indiana University School of Medicine. It is one of the largest primary care groups in the state of Indiana and it has over 20 clinical venues in central Indiana staffed by over 110 faculty physicians. These clinical outpatient practices have two subtypes, inner city underserved community health centers and suburban private-practice type clinics, the latter representing the 9 clinics in this study. This data set includes 88,345 anonymous patients and their 858,579 scheduled appointments, after removing 46,050 pending or clinic rescheduled appointments and 507 invalid appointment dates from the 905,136 scheduled appointments. No appointments were scheduled for Sundays and very few on Saturdays, thus weekends were excluded. The registration data set contains 5 years of data (10/1/2001 to 9/29/2006).

Predictive models focused on 3 years of appointments immediately preceding the introduction of an automated telephone reminder system, of which results are not available. The 18 months prior to the analysis are used to develop variables to track a patient's history of prior no-shows and cancelations. The dependent variable, appointment outcome, has three discrete outcomes: (1) arrival, (2) cancellation, and (3) no-show representing 70.7%, 19.4% and 9.9%, respectively. Independent variables include: lead time (call appointment interval), prior attendance history, payer (insurance carrier), patient age, weather (average temperature, snow, rain), and schedule (day of week, time of day). Factors unavailable to us include marital status, gender, race, and ethnicity. Patient's purpose of visit was unavailable due to concerns that patients may describe unreliable information on the phone. Physician specialty was excluded since the 132 physicians are predominantly primary care, mixed across pediatrics and adult.

Charting each independent variable against appointment outcome helped visualize the data. Predictive modeling with multinomial logistic regression utilized the CATMOD procedure in SAS 9.1 [49].

Coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for no-shows and cancellations provide the increased tendency (positive sign) or decreased tendency (negative sign) of a factor's correlation with the respective outcome and can be used to compute an odds ratio for easier interpretation.

Decision trees from the SPLIT procedure in SAS's Enterprise Miner [49] use CHAID (Chi-Squared Automatic Interaction Detection, Kass, 1980) to determine optimal split or cut points based on chi-square values to best aggregate the data. The independent variable with the largest chi-square value is used as the <sup>fi</sup>rst break or node. Decision trees have fewer observations than the predictive models due to partitioning the data into training and validation sets. Models for the 3 years described excluded a patient's <sup>fi</sup>rst scheduled visit, to allow for tracking patient history, under 2 speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation 1 allows for multiple visits per patient, potentially creating bias since individual patients may unfairly weight the results (results in Tables 1 and 2). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation 2 includes only the last visit per patient, removing any bias due to multiple visits per patient but, reducing the data size (Tables 3 and 4). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation 1 (multiple visits per patient) and Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation 2 (last visit per patient) were modeled across all days and all locations (“All”), by day (5 weekdays), by location (9 clinics), thus 15 models by speci<sup>fi</sup>cation or a total of 30 multinomial logistic regression models.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Lead time

Lead time plotted by <sup>fi</sup>nancial payer for arrivals (Fig. 1), cancellations (Fig. 2) and no-shows (Fig. 3) suggests shorter lead times correlate with higher attendance rates. George and Rubin [20] hypothesized a linear relationship. However a transformation appears necessary to capture the relationship for our models. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned lead time as the number of days between and including day scheduled and day of appointment, where same day equals 1 day, next day equals 2 days, etc. We hypothesize that lead time has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on patient attendance rates [4,5].

The log of lead time created a more linear trend (Fig. 4), but with high variability at high lead times. Coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for no-show were slightly higher than for cancellation in every model suggesting a larger impact from lead time on no-shows than cancellations. All coef<sup>fi</sup>cients were statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 99% con<sup>fi</sup>dence level and relatively stable. Coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for no-show ranged from 0.42 to 0.69 for all visits (Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation 1) and 0.68 to 1.01 for last visit (Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation 2), while coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for cancellations ranged from 0.36 to 0.46 for all visits (Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation 1) and 0.48 to 0.85 for last visit (Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation 2). This con<sup>fi</sup>rms our hypothesis that lead time is indeed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor for patient attendance.

## 4.2. Prior attendance history

Initial models included count and rate for each type of outcome. We later concluded the percent missed and percent cancelled yielded the best results. We hypothesize that patient attendance history is an important predictor of attendance rates [3,17]. The variable for the count of prior no-shows (NumAptNS) was statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for all but the two smallest location models. The variable for the count of prior cancelled appointments (NumAptCA) has consistent and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for predicting future no-shows across almost all models. The variable counting the number of prior visits arrived (NumApt), a proxy for tenure as a patient, indicates a decreased chance of cancellations and even greater reduction in no-shows with more prior arrived visits. A pair of novel variables created to analyze missing subsequent appointments is: PREVNOSHOW=1 if a patient no-showed at the previous appointment, 0 otherwise, PREVCANCEL=1 if a patient cancelled at the previous appointment, 0 otherwise. ## 4.4. Weather

Charts of appointment outcomes plotted by temperature (degrees Fahrenheit) show relatively stable rates for temperatures above the low 20's, but are highly variable below the low 20's (Fig. 6), possibly confounding with snowfall. Weather data, consisting of temperature, rain and snow, was acquired from the Indianapolis, IN, airport. We hypothesize that weather may not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect patient attendance rates given lack of extreme weather variations in our data set time span. The rainfall variable (WTR) appeared to be mostly insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. The snowfall variable (SNW) is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in all 15 Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation 1 models and largely mixed for Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation 2. Snow is associated with increased chance of no-shows, but even greater cancellations. Snow by day of the week suggests people are more likely to cancel on Monday or Friday and more likely to not show up during the middle of the week. The daily temperature variable (AVGTEMP) provided a very slight effect signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in only the largest models. These results support our hypothesis. Weather effects were mostly limited to snowfall, an occasional concern in Indianapolis.

## 4.6. Form of payment

The data set consists of seven funding sources or payers (insurance types) with an additional 10% unknown. We hypothesize form of payment to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor affecting patient attendance [20,33]. Seven indicator variables were created to compare payers relative to unknown, with transplant coverage excluded from analysis due to few observations. Patients with private insurance (“provider with largest market share” and “the remaining top 5 insurers”) represent 51% of the data and had the least likelihood of missing appointments. Medicare, which provides coverage to senior citizens (17% of data), was the next most reliable group. Contracted and commercial insurance (2% of data) were slightly better than average at making appointments. Medicaid, which provides coverage to indigent or welfare recipients (13% of data), had a much higher rate of noshows than the population. Self pay patients (8%) had similar noshow rates to Medicaid recipients but with higher cancellation rates. These results support our hypothesis that form of payment does impact patient attendance.

## 4.6.1. Decision trees

Given a data set of this size, many variables are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant; we create decision trees with SAS Enterprise Miner to provide insights as to the relative importance of our independent variables. Fig. 8 provides a representative example of what many of the decision trees produced. Across virtually all models, the <sup>fi</sup>rst node of the tree was log of lead time (call appointment interval). As lead time increases there is a clear trend in decreased arrivals, increased cancellations and increased no-shows. Another revelation was that with shorter lead times other factors were mostly irrelevant, that is few or no branches were created to further explain the data. At longer lead times the models always branch to further segment

## 4.6.2. Revised model

Based on the logistic models and decision trees, we narrowed the focus of investigation to just 4 factors to run a set of predictive models with training and validation partitions to determine the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation results for the validation set. The four dimensions most associated with missed appointments were lead time, patient appointment history, funding source (insurance carrier), and age. Lead time is modeled as the log of the lead time. Patient appointment history is comprised of two variables; no-show rate and cancel rate, each made up of a weighted average of up to <sup>fi</sup>ve prior appointments, weighted in monotonically decreasing weights, 30%, 25%, 20%, 15%, 10%, with higher rates going to more recent appointments. Funding source is modeled as an indicator variable for each of the major groups of insurance. Age is modeled with a series of four groups (young children, children, adult and senior), designated by three indicator variables. Each age group has a trend variable.

There were 4 models speci<sup>fi</sup>ed to be run with training and validation with the variables listed in Table 5. For all ages, both multinomial logistic regression and binary logistic regression (omitting cancellations) were run with the variables listed above and 2 way interaction variables. Additionally, the same multinomial and binary logistic regression were run for only adult patients (22–75), to provide a reduced model without all the age variables, except the trend line for the adult age group.

For all four models, Table 6 reports the odds ratios for the signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant main effects for the time period of May 1, 2003 to April 30, 2006. Results are consistent with models discussed earlier. Lead time had the highest odds ratios, which were stable across all 4 <sup>fi</sup>nal models. ## 5. Discussion and conclusion

In this study we conduct an extensive empirical analysis of a robust data set on patient attendance from Indiana University Purdue University at Indianapolis (IUPUI) medical group. Studies on patient compliance including attendance may help in curtailing healthcare costs by reducing inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies. Mitigating rising healthcare costs in the United States is an important issue. At the current rate of expansion, total spending on health care is expected to increase from 16% of GDP in 2007 to 25% in 2025 [13,46]. A key area for curtailing health care costs can be to reduce administrative costs and to make the system more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient [25,26]. Failure by patients to attend scheduled medical appointments can increase medical care costs and may affect treatment effectiveness [22,55]. Therefore research on patience attendance contributes to strategies for reducing healthcare costs.

This study adds to the patient attendance literature with a novel approach on examining patient attendance rates. We analyze panel style registration data set from 9 outpatient facilities consisting of 5 years of patients’ attendance outcomes. Our paper differs from others in that we collect and model three discrete outcomes for the dependent variable patient attendance: arrivals, cancellations and no-shows. This multinomial approach is in contrast to prior studies where cancellations were typically discarded, bundled with no-show, or not mentioned at all. Studying cancellations are important for service operations because clinics could potentially use the information for rescheduling appointments. This may help in developing strategies for improving clinic capacity utilization. In contrast no-shows typically result in an immediate loss of available capacity.

We conclude the following results from our multinomial logistic regression. The four most predictive factors for patient attendance are shown to be lead time, a patient's prior attendance history, patient's age, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial payer (typically insurance provider). The largest contributing factor to patient nonattendance was lead time, or call appointment interval. The shorter the wait for the appointment, the more likely the patient is to make the appointment and the less other factors matter. Lead time was modeled through a log transformation to correlate days between scheduling an appointment and visiting the clinic. Patient history was accounted for with a separate rate for cancellations and no-shows using the last <sup>fi</sup>ve appointments with weighting favoring the more recent visits. There is a strong likelihood for patients prone to no-shows in the past to continue such behavior, as is the case for cancellations although with a weaker relationship. A minority of patients accounts for a disproportionate amount of no-shows. Less than 7% of the patients account for nearly 43% of no-show appointments.

The conclusion of this paper that the strongest factor affecting patient attendance, lead time to appointment, poses quite a paradox, because it is also the factor that clinics have control over. It would appear that well intentioned efforts of <sup>fi</sup>lling all appointment slots in near future actually results in a disservice: higher rates of noshow, the further out those appointments are scheduled. Unfortunately, the data did not provide purpose of the visit, which could be used to investigate how the effects of lead time vary by type of visit. Lead time is likely a sign of an imbalance between supply and demand. Clinics may need to open up additional slots or increase staf<sup>fi</sup>ng to keep up with the demand. For clinics dealing with such issues, literature on same day scheduling may be helpful. Future research should be conducted to see what lead time thresholds should be enforced so as to reduce patient no-shows.

There are a number of additional, interesting avenues for improving clinic capacity utilization and for further research. Predicting speci<sup>fi</sup>c appointment outcomes are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Therefore, a more appropriate approach for further study may be to focus on individuals who have missed appointments in the past. Patients with private insurance and Medicare are more likely to make appointments, whereas patients who are self pay, uninsured, or who have Medicaid are less likely to show. As a result, focusing on the latter groups may be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant area for improvement. No-shows decrease noticeably for adults aged 22–75, while children and senior citizens have less consistent correlations. An interesting follow up study could be to examine casual factors for the difference in attendance rates among children and seniors. This may yield strategies to improve patient compliance in these groups. Day of the week and time of the day effects reveal that as the week and day progress there is a decreased likelihood to no-show and an increased likelihood to cancel, possibly due to greater access in cancelling during business hours and weekdays. A solution for this may be that clinics establish a quick and easy way for patients to cancel appointments, such as 24 h 7 day a week voicemail or no-wait phone number. This should reduce communication delay and improve a clinics chance to <sup>fi</sup>ll that spot. A future research area could be to empirically examine attendance rates before and after introduction of such a system.

This study aims to contribute to patient attendance literature by adopting the novel approach of examining three discrete outcomes for patient attendance. The results demonstrate there are many avenues for improving clinic capacity utilization by identifying important factors related to attendance rates. This may contribute to curtailing rising healthcare costs by reducing inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies.

Suresh Chand is a Professor of Operations Management and Louis A. Weil, Jr., Chair of Management at Purdue's Krannert School of Management. He conducts research in many different areas including supply chain management and healthcare operations management. He has published close to four dozen papers in leading management journals including Management Science, Operations Research, Production and Operations Management, and Manufacturing and Service Operations Management. He has served as associate or senior editor of several leading management journals. He teaches the Operations core course to both regular and executive MBA students. Herbert Moskowitz was a Lewis B. Cullman Distinguished Professor of Manufacturing Management at the Krannert School of Management at Purdue University and is now retired. His area of specialization was management science and quantitative methods with emphasis on manufacturing and technology, total quality management, quality improvement tools, and judgment and decision making. He has been at Purdue since 1970 and has had visiting appointments at the University of Mannheim, West Germany, the University of British Columbia, the London Business School, and the Wharton School. He holds a BS degree in mechanical engineering, an MBA and a PhD in management from UCLA. Dr. Moskowitz is the co-author of 5 texts and has published about 170 refereed articles in the areas of decision making, optimization, management science, and quality control in academic journals. He was an Associate Editor of Operations Research, Decision Sciences, the Journal of Behavioral Decision Making, the Journal of Interdisciplinary Modeling and Simulation and a special associate editor of Management Science. He is a fellow of the Decision Sciences Institute, has served as a vicepresident, secretary, and member of the executive board, and had been nominated for president-elect of this organization.

Steve Shade is the Managing Director of Purdue's Institute for Competitive Manufacturing.
