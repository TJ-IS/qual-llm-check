# Reflections on Information Systems Journal’s thematic composition

James Love & Rudy Hirschheim

E. J. Ourso College of Business, Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, LA 70803, USA, email: rudy@lsu.edu

Abstract. This article analyses the scholarly output of Information Systems Journal (ISJ) in relation to its seven peer journals in the Association for Information Systems Senior Scholars’ Basket of Eight journals (SSB8) since ISJ’s inception in 1991. To do so, cluster analyses are generated using metadata (i.e. titles, keywords and abstracts) from the articles published. The analysis results revea commonalties and some distinguishing differences between ISJ and its peer journals. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings illuminate that ISJ has published articles in the area of information systems development at a much higher rate than its counterparts. The analyses also illustrate that ISJ has embraced broader philosophical and methodological underpinnings than other SSB8 journals.

Keywords: IS history, IS research themes, IS research, quantitative literature review, latent semantic analysis, cluster analysis

## INTRODUCTION

In the 25th year of Information Systems Journal (ISJ), it is apropos to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the historical tradition formed by the articles that collectively embody the journal’s gestalt. Editors-in-chief have offered prior re<sup>fl</sup>ections on the 10th and 17th anniversary of the journal’s publication (Avison et al., 2001: Avison et al., 2008). More recently. Avison & Fitzgerald reflected over /SJ's <sup>fi</sup>rst 25 years as the editorial leadership transitioned to Davidson, Powell and Trauth (Avison & Fitzgerald, 2012).

We are wise to take pause periodically in order to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on our collective work efforts as researchers. Indeed. it is healthy to take stock of our actual performance in relation to our intended and stated goals. Recently, within the information systems (IS) research community, an appreciation for the historical tradition of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has gained momentum (cf. Hirschheim et al., 2012; Bryant et al., 2013; Porra et al., 2014; Hirschheim & Klein, 2012). In the case of a journal, such as ISJ, we can evaluate its legacy by analysing its output in relation to its peer journals. In doing so, ISJ’s commonalities and distinctive qualities illustrate the nature of its the matic composition as a top-tier IS publication outlet.

This article takes on the task of analysing ISJ’s publication output in relation to the other seven journals that comprise the Association for Information Systems (AIS) Senior Scholars Basket of Eight journals (SSB8). To this end, a cluster analysis of articles published since ISJ’s inaugural year in 1991 is performed to tease out the categories that represent the underlying structure of that broader corpus of published articles. This same process is also applied to the other seven peer SSB8 journals collectively. Subsequent to performing the cluster analyses, we interpret their <sup>fi</sup>ndings to glean insights about the thematic nature of mainstream IS research published since the inception of ISJ.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. A review of the literature presents previous efforts focused on structuring the IS research output and reviewing the history of the IS domain. Then, the research methodology applied in the present study’s cluster anal yses is detailed. Findings of the cluster analyses are reported and discussed with particular attention focused on ISJ in comparison with its seven peer SSB8 journals. Lastly, the article notes limitations of the study and suggests future directions for the <sup>fi</sup>eld in light of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

Because the IS discipline emerged from its reference disciplines, researchers have attempted to conceptualize the makeup of the discipline. This review traces previous research publications that characterize IS academic literature situating the present study within that prior research activity. In doing so, the early works to create ‘IS research frameworks’ are discussed. This is followed by a review of subsequent works that categorize the extant IS research literature. Then, the emerging area of IS history research is reviewed.

The initial attempts by IS researchers to identify the IS discipline’s conceptual composition can be traced to contributions known as the ‘IS research frameworks’ (cf. Gorry & Scott-Morton, 1971; Chervany et al., 1972; Lucas, 1973; Mason & Mitroff, 1973; Mock, 1973; Dickson, 1981; Ives et al., 1980). Ives et al. (1980) subsequently reviewed <sup>fi</sup>ve previously published management information systems (MIS) research frameworks and created a comprehensive concep tual framework that was validated by mapping doctoral dissertations to it. These research frameworks were motivated to classify past and present research activities as well as to serve as a launching point for generating future research hypotheses (Ives et al., 1980). Furthermore, the frameworks were not only intended to assist researchers in pursuing a balanced research programme but also to help researchers communicate their work to MIS and non-MIS colleagues (Nolan & Wetherbe, 1980). This collection of studies serves as an early effort to organize IS research literature into groups. In the decades since, IS researchers have continued this research stream of categorizing IS research efforts.

Since the early IS research frameworks were published, works have continued the quest of capturing the essence of the IS discipline. These scholarly reviews target a variety of aspects of the previously performed research. For example, reviews have focused on research charac teristics such as the methodological, theoretical and philosophical approaches as well as the thematic categorization of the research content.

Van Horn (1973) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four research methods used in MIS: case studies; <sup>fi</sup>eld studies; <sup>fi</sup>eld tests; and laboratory studies. Chen & Hirschheim (2004) found positivism represented the dominant paradigm being used in 81% of empirical articles. Furthermore, surveys and case studies were the leading IS research designs utilized in the years sampled (1991 to 2001). Barkhi & Sheetz (2001) reviewed theories used in 237 articles <sup>fi</sup>nding that about 50% cited a theory. Only 30 theories were cited in multiple articles illustrating the theoretical diversity in IS. Vessey et al. (2005) put forth that IS research adopted three general approaches (descrip tive, evaluative and formulative) and 18 distinct research methods.

Information systems research endeavors specializing in thematic categorization efforts often take on a narrowed focus for the targeted phenomena. For example, researchers limited their scope to review IS research efforts in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c geographic region such as Australia (Gable, 2008), Canada (Serenko et al., 2008; Grant & Koop, 1995), Europe (Avgerou et al., 1999) and Scandinavia (Iivari & Lyytinen, 1999). Some reviews restricted their focus to where research was published (i.e. individual journals or conferences). Examples include journalspeci<sup>fi</sup>c studies published about ISJ (Avison et al., 2008), Information & Management (Palvia et al., 2007), Information Systems Research (Swanson & Ramiller, 1993) and European Journal of Information Systems (Dwivedi & Kuljis, 2008). Gosain et al. (1997) and Lee et al. (1999) compared topics in academic journals with the practitioner literature. Other publications compared journal output such as EJIS and MISQ (Mustafee, 2011; Cordoba et al., 2012). Similar work was performed with conferences including Becker et al. (2004) that assessed changes in mini-tracks at Americas Conference on Information Systems over the preceding 10-year period.

Reviews categorizing IS research were motivated to serve many purposes. Barki et al. (1988. 1993) established and revised a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme for MISQ. Culnan (1986, 1987) derived informal clusters of research to identify hidden intellectual communities within IS. Iivari et al. (2004) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed knowledge areas within IS to promote the establishment of an IS Body of Knowledge. Iivari et al. (2004) are distinct in that its overarching goal was to organize an IS framework relevant to practice rather than the IS research community. Vessey et al. (2005) sought to unify the <sup>fi</sup>elds of Computer Science and Software Engineering with IS. In doing so, the Vessey et al. (2005) review went beyond classifying the topic (i.e. research theme); it also presented a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation for dimensions such as research approach, method, unit of analysis and reference discipline. No other proposed classi<sup>fi</sup>cation system offered was as comprehensive in terms of dimensions covered. With the exception of Nevo et al. (2009), who created themes and IT artefacts, all other reviews presented a framework with a single dimension designed to classify research themes.

Recently, an IS history research stream has gained strength. Indeed, our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of themes that have emerged over the course of time in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld is consonant with this growing interest in the history of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. While the <sup>fi</sup>eld has seen its share of debates on fundamental areas such as ‘the identity crisis’ (Backhouse et al., 1991; Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Agarwal & Lucas, 2005; King & Lyytinen, 2006), ‘what constitutes the core of the <sup>fi</sup>eld’ (CAIS, 2003; Weber, 2006; Lyytinen & King, 2004; Lyytinen & King, 2006; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003), ‘is the <sup>fi</sup>eld relevant’ (Markus, 1997; Lyytinen, 1999; CAIS, 2001; Applegate & King, 1999; Benbasat & Zmud, 1999), and such like, more recently the focus has shifted to identifying the history of the <sup>fi</sup>eld. The recent article ‘A Glorious and Not-so-short History of the Information Systems Field’ (Hirschheim & Klein, 2012) appeared to usher in a wave of interest in the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s history. For example, the Journal of the Association for Information Systems had a special issue devoted to the topic (Hirschheim et al., 2012). Similarly, the Journal of Information Technology also had a special issue on the topic (Bryant et al., 2013). In 2012, the Association for Informa tion Systems instituted an AIS Historian whose job it was to attempt to document the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s rich history (Zhang, 2014; Zhang, 2015). Our analysis of research themes appearing in the major IS journals is another example of how the <sup>fi</sup>eld is taking an interest in its past activities. We hope this paper helps contribute to the growing body of work focusing on the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s past undertakings.

In summary, multiple streams of the IS literature exist aiming to structure the IS discipline into meaningful categories and facilitate the understanding of previous IS research efforts. However, nearly all of this research on IS’s thematic composition have been in publication for over a decade creating the need for a fresh, updated re<sup>fl</sup>ection on the IS discipline’s thematic con<sup>fi</sup>guration. The currently published studies reviewing IS literature have typically only analysed research conducted prior to 2002. With the exception of Nevo et al. (2009), who reviewed the corpus of Information Systems Research and Management Information Systems Quarterly through 2006, the most recent review including the SSB8 journals appears in Larsen et al. (2008) that includes 65 journals from 1990 to 2002. Our study adds a renewed look into IS literature themes by including articles from all SSB8 journals particularly in relation to ISJ’s publication output.

The textual data analysis in this study adopts a latent categorization methodology. This research method teases out the representative categories from a large volume of textual data that constitutes the overall corpus of published documents. ## Data selection

On 6 December 2011, the AIS senior scholars revised the ‘basket’ of eight journals (SSB8 are considered the ‘top journals in our <sup>fi</sup>eld’ (AIS, 2014). The impetus for creating the basket was to ensure ‘more consistency and meaningfulness to tenure and promotion cases’ (AIS, 2014). Since these eight journals are generally accepted as the top mainstream IS journals according to leading IS scholars, this study collected data from the articles of all SSB8 journals from the beginning of ISJ’s publication in 1991 through 2013, which was the most recently completed year when the research analysis was performed.

Metadata from each journal’s offering of articles were collected through querying EBSCOHost Business Source Complete or from the journal’s website directly. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c metadata collected for each article includes the article’s title, keywords and abstract. Addition ally, article publication data such as the year, volume and issue were gathered in order to assess changes over time. In total, 4,745 articles comprised the corpus of documents collected. Table 2 provides a summary of the SSB8 journals’ publication counts from 1991 to 2013.

This corpus excluded results such as ‘Errata’, ‘About the Authors’ and ‘Introductions’ to issues that merely summarized the research articles in the respective issue. All available data were collected for the articles included in the corpus. Some articles do not have keywords or abstracts associated with them. In these instances, the available textual data were collected to represent the article in the analysis.

The result of data selection process was combined into a <sup>fi</sup>le that consists of one article’s data per row. The textual data containing the article title, article keywords and article abstract were concatenated to allow for further processing to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the dataset for analysis. The original dataset was composed of 4,745 rows of articles (d) having a total of 455,638 terms (t).

## Data preparation

The original dataset underwent a series of steps to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the raw textual data into useable terms. First, words of little value in determining clusters were eliminated from the dataset. These words, commonly referred to as stop words, include many parts of speech such as articles, prepositions and pronouns. Additionally, decisions were made to exclude other stop words that offered little help in discriminating journal articles into clusters of themes. For example, the term ‘research’ appeared 3,410 times in the dataset, yet it was not valuable in discriminating whethe a document should be assigned to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c cluster over others because articles are ‘research publications. Other examples include terms such as ‘article’, ‘special issue’ and ‘study’ that were frequently occurring words, but offered no value in determining the thematic nature of its respective research article.

Furthermore, words with multiple pre<sup>fi</sup>xes and suf<sup>fi</sup>xes were stemmed in order to consolidate the respective terms into a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed concept regardless of tense or whether plural or singular form occurred in the text. For example, the term ‘organization’ appeared 647 times in its singular noun form and 1,087 times as ‘organizations’ in the plural form. Stemming this term allowed fo these variations to combine into a single term. The term also frequently goes by the alternative spelling of ‘organisation’. The creation of synonyms allowed these variations to be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a single concept as well. A strength of the text analytic software is its sensitivity to the contextual usage of the word with respect to the part of speech. For instance, the verb ‘organize’ coalesced into a separate concept from the aforementioned term of ‘organization’.

The text analysis software also parsed whether a single-worded term was a part of a larger group of words such as ‘enterprise resource planning’ or ‘open source software’ creating noun group terms when appropriate. These two noun groups are often referred to by their acronyms, ERP and OSS, respectively. To accommodate for these variations, synonyms were created to link the acronyms to their respective spelled-out word terms.

The remaining terms representing each document were then converted to a term-document matrix (A). This matrix consisted of 4,745 rows (d) representing the documents (i.e. one journal article per row) and 7,619 columns representing the unique terms (t) that appear in the corpus of documents. Each cell within the matrix (A) contained the frequency of occurrence for the term (t) in the respective document (d).

## Artefact weighting

Because the metadata of the journal articles such as the titles, keywords and abstracts vary in length, weighting of terms was applied to correct for any over-representation of articles with lengthy metadata. Several weighting techniques may be applied to account for the varying size of the artefacts. They primarily focus on the three aspects of the corpus. The <sup>fi</sup>rst aspect, global effects (g), measures the importance of the term throughout all of the documents. Second, the local effects (l) describe the importance of the term within its respective document. Third, normalization (n) of the documents adjusts the documents length to become equal.

The two most common technigues for weighting are term-frequency inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) and entropy (Evangelopoulos et al., 2012). The recommended weighting is the TF-IDF technique when larger groups of terms are present in a complex semantic space (Evangelopoulos et al., 2012). TF-IDF determines the local weight $( I _ { i j } )$ , as the term’s frequency $( t f _ { i j } )$ . The global weight (g) is calculated as the inverse document frequency

(idj = log(nDocs/nDocs ), where nDocs is the number of documents in the corpus, and nDocs is the number of documents with term i. TF-IDF normalizes the length of all documents to 1 (Larsen & Monarchi, 2004). The result is a transformation of the original frequencies in each cel of matrix (A) to weighted ones as follows:

$$
a _ {i j} = t f _ {i j} * \log (n D o c s / n D o c s _ {i})
$$

For this study, inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) was selected as the weighting scheme data analysis guided by recommendations in Larsen & Monarchi (2004) and Evangelopoulos et al. (2012). Additionally, the choice was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the desire to remain consistent with prior LSA studies in IS research literature such as Larsen et al. (2008) and Sidorova et al. (2008) that both opted for the TF-IDF transformation approach.

## Numeric transformation

Because the matrix (A) is often large and sparse, a matrix operation known as singular value decomposition (SVD) was derived to reduce the size of the matrix. This separated the matrix (A) into three matrices: (U), (S) and (V), where (U) and (V) were orthogonal matrices and (S) was a diagonal matrix of (A)’s singular values in decreasing order. The matrix (U) contained (A)’s vectors that represented the rows of $( \mathsf { A } )$ , and matrix (V) contained the vectors forming the columns of (A). Then, the smaller approximation matrix $( \mathsf { A } _ { \mathsf { k } } )$ that retained 100 SVD dimensions was created for further statistical analysis. The decision to retain 100 dimensions is arbitrary; however, it is consistent with prior studies of this type (Evangelopoulos et al., 2012). At this point, the transformation of the original textual data into a numerical data was complete.

## Cluster analysis

Several quantitative analysis options have been used in literature to interpret the LSA results such as cosine similarities, classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, clustering and factor analysis This investigation utilizes the cluster analysis technique because the natural, data-driven groupings of published articles are the phenomenon of interest. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c method of measuring similarity is the expectation-maximization clustering algorithm. This method determines a maximum likelihood estimation for assigning the units of analysis (i.e. journal articles) to the clusters.

The appropriate number of categories to create was decided upon as well. Proper dimensionality selection remains an unresolved methodological issue in research literature (Evangelopoulos et al., 2012), yet a number of suggested approaches are available to arrive at the appropriate number of clusters. Scree plots were created to observe eigenvalues plotted against principal components for both groups. The determination was made to restrict the cluster analysis to 15 resultant clusters. Lastly, the clusters were generated and interpreted to elici meaningful, representative names for the categories.

For instance, the more focused Mobile Adoption cluster in ISJ group clearly <sup>fi</sup>ts within the broader Adoption/Innovation/Diffusion cluster in the SSB8 group.

Other weaker between-group similarities are noted when the matches are not as stark. The descriptors of one group’s cluster might play a smaller role in the counterpart group’s cluster resulting in differing cluster names. This is the situation for the cluster Virtual Teams in ISJ group and the Social/Virtual Networks cluster in the SSB8 peers group. Because the ‘virtual’ descriptor was less prominent in the SSB8 peer group, it did not warrant its own cluster name.

Perhaps more intriguing are the areas that ISJ differs from its SSB8 peers. A deviation between ISJ and its SSB8 peers in terms of research approaches is clearly pronounced in the two cluster analyses. The willingness of ISJ to take on non-functional research approaches is evident in this analysis. In the ISJ cluster analysis, one can see the interpretivist leanings of the journal as revealed through the Philosophical/Research Approach and Ethics/Critical Research clusters. The research Philosophical/Research Approach descriptors include terms commonly in the interpretivist’s lexicon such as phenomenon, concept, perspective and case study. In contrast, the SSB8 peers cluster analysis reveals the more functionalist approach as af<sup>fi</sup>rmed by the Model Testing cluster. This cluster contains descriptors such as constructs, models, tests and measurements. No evidence of the critical research approach appears in any of the 15 SSB8 peers’ cluster descriptors.

Another <sup>fi</sup>nding that distinguishes ISJ from the SSB8 peer group relates to the higher prominence of the IS development themes published in ISJ. As previously mentioned, three IS development clusters in the ISJ group (i.e. the Requirements/Speci<sup>fi</sup>cations Process, Development Methodology and IS Design/Development clusters) are counterparts to the Development clus ter in the SSB8 peer group. This collectively represents 25% of total articles ISJ has published compared with the 9% publication rate in the SSB8 peer journals.

## DISCUSSION

In Powell’s editorial that begins this 25th volume of ISJ, he re<sup>fl</sup>ects on the nature of the publications over the past quarter-century (Powell, 2015). In doing so, he mentions some priorities that have guided the journal to this point and, presumably, will continue to direct the journal moving forward. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study’s quantitative literature review bring further evidence to support many of these claims. A number of the themes emerging from the ISJ cluster analysis clearly resonate with comments in Powell’s editorial. Here, we compare several <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our analysis with Powell’s editorial comments, and we offer some additional interpretations tha we gleaned from the results of this study.

Information Systems Journal’s SSB8 peer journals have demonstrated an inclination to expand their thematic scope beyond the traditional con<sup>fi</sup>nes considered to be mainstream IS. The ISJ editorial board may indeed join in on this journey. As Powell states, ISJ welcomes research submissions about information systems development and management as well as research on emerging topics. ISJ’s traditional acceptance of IS development and management research is corroborated by this cluster analysis. In particular, these two research traditions are visible through the three IS development-related themes of Requirement/Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation Process, Development Methodology and IS Design/Development along with the IS Management theme. Powell’s reference that the majority of ISJ authors reside within business and management schools or in information systems departments coincides with the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant subset of research studying the IS management theme. It is reasonable that authors in these departments have often oriented their research toward management-focused topics. Although the overall thematic evolution of the IS discipline’s mainstream journals is trending towards more thematic areas, the IS management and IS development areas remain traditional pillars of mainstream IS research. The balancing act of maintaining these established thematic areas while encouraging newly emerging themes will shape the journal’s thematic composition in years to come.

Powell mentions that ‘ISJ was established to be a publication outlet for qualitative interpretive and later critical research’ (Powell, 2015). Here again, our study’s analysis reiterates this generally accepted declaration through empirical evidence. The resulting Philosophical/Research Approach cluster is consistent with descriptors re<sup>fl</sup>ecting this fact. Furthermore, an origina founding goal of the ISJ was to ‘publish work that pushed out the boundaries of acceptable methodology for conducting information systems research’ (Powell, 2015). ISJ’s adherence to this principle is highlighted in the cluster analysis via the Ethics/Critical Research cluster.

The efforts of ISJ to reach out to under-represented regions are captured in this cluster anal ysis as well. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a cluster of articles formed around the theme of China. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is the direct result of ISJ’s overt willingness to encourage information system research conducted in China in addition to promoting China as a research setting. The 2008 special issue on information systems in China is a noteworthy example of this outreach.

Lastly, Powell asserts that ISJ ‘has improved the gender balance of authors’ (Powell, 2015). Because the purpose of this review is to analyse the thematic structure of the IS discipline’s publication output, the data do not inform us with <sup>fi</sup>ndings such as average author counts o gender balance. However, the thematic inclusion of a Woman/Gender cluster in ISJ separates the journal’s efforts from the SSB8 peer journals in this regard. This is in part resultant of ISJ’s 2012 special issue on women and IT. The SSB8 peer journals’ analysis is void of any cluster descriptors of this kind. The presence of a Woman/Gender cluster in the ISJ corpus, albeit a small one, can be viewed as evidence of a stride in the direction towards improving gender balance.

In addition to the analysis that we interpret as aligning with Powell’s previous editorial comments, we offer some further insights that we take away from the analyses’ results. For exam ple, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) research that often receives much attention is not visible in the ISJ cluster. We suspect that this <sup>fi</sup>nding is a result of the functionalist nature that much of the Adoption/Innovation/Diffusion research follows. Perhaps for the same reason, IT Value research does not appear important to ISJ, although it does to the SSB8 peers.

We also notice that multiple IS applications turned up in the SSB8 cluster analysis such as E-Commerce, Decision Support/Group Support and Knowledge Management, yet the only IS application area in the ISJ cluster is Enterprise Resource Planning. Instead, the ISJ analysis generated more clusters around user-oriented themes such as Virtual Teams, Online Community, China, Public Sector and Woman/Gender. While the SSB8 cluster of Social/Virtual Networks is analogous with the ISJ clusters of Virtual Teams and Online Community, the SSB8 peer publications’ clusters do not focus on themes such as China, the Public Sector and Woman/Gender to any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant extent.

## LIMITATIONS

Some limitations to these <sup>fi</sup>ndings should be taken into account. First, methodological choices exist throughout the analysis that can potentially affect the resultant clustering results. An obvious example is the determination of the number of clusters that are generated. We opted for 15 clusters as the appropriate size for this research study. Had the number of predetermined clusters been a different size, the resultant cluster names would not have necessarily re<sup>fl</sup>ected the same themes as were found in this study.

Another methodological decision that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impacts cluster results is the use of stop words. Judgement calls were made by the authors based on their knowledge of the IS domain to remove irrelevant terms that were not valuable for discriminating articles’ similarity. While we reached consensus on the words to eliminate, the process is admittedly value-laden to some degree. One example is that we disregarded the term ‘not’ in our analysis. Had we opted to retain the term’s 2,811 occurrences found in 1,699 articles, it would have impacted the calculations and subsequent formation of clusters. In the two aforementioned cases, we diligently evaluated the alternative options and ultimately arrived at decisions that we believed revealed the purest re<sup>fl</sup>ection of journals’ thematic structure.

Additionally, the interpretive process of noting the two groups’ similarities is subjectively shaped by the authors’ informed view of the IS domain. The respective pairs of matched groups are denoted as either ‘strong’ or ‘moderate’ matches is an attempt to add nuance to the interpretative process. Furthermore, an exhaustive listing of cluster descriptive terms is available in the appendix to shed additional light on the terms contributing to the resultant analysis and interpretation.

## CONCLUSION

The majority of top-tier IS journals, such as ISJ, has now chronicled scholarly research activity for 25 years or more. We, indeed, have a sizable history that can be evaluated and re<sup>fl</sup>ected upon together as a research community. We hope this study triggers similar efforts in other journals re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on the nature and evolution of the IS scholarship. In doing so, these reviews not only help us collectively identify the composition of the field but also point out under represented areas that deserve greater attention in future IS scholarship.

In this article, we looked at the thematic composition of ISJ since its inception. The data an alytic technique of latent semantic analysis condensed the large textual dataset into meaningfu thematic clusters for two populations. One population accounted for all ISJ articles published and another contained all of ISJ’s SSB8 peer journal articles published in the corresponding time span. Then, 15 clusters were derived from each population, and clusters were subsequently analysed in terms of content and proportionality.

The more insightful results from the cluster analysis perhaps resided in the differences be tween ISJ and its SSB8 peer journals. The interpretive methodological underpinnings often characteristic of ISJ research output are apparent in the cluster analysis of ISJ articles. Further more, ISJ’s inclusion of critical research methods surfaces as well. These methodological pref erences are in stark contrast to the functionalist underpinnings emergent from the SSB8 peer journals’ cluster analysis.

A second distinctive attribute of ISJ that stands out in this cluster analysis is the journal’s more prominent publication of IS development articles. The IS development research area has been a long-standing pillar of the IS research domain, and it accounts for one-quarter of the articles published in ISJ. This proportion of IS development research output in ISJ far outpaces the remaining <sup>fi</sup>eld of SSB8 journals where it is collectively at 9%. By re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on insights such as these, we gain a better understanding of what makes ISJ a distinctive IS research outlet.

Strengthening our knowledge of these historical traditions better serves us moving forward as an academic community. True growth of an academic discipline occurs when it achieves the self-awareness to evolve when the status quo is deemed inadequate to satisfy the scholarly community’s needs. This was the case 25 years ago when ISJ set out to publish high-quality IS research that did not always adhere to the dominant functionalist paradigm. As a result, ISJ has enriched the IS research legacy in areas that would have perhaps otherwise remained unchartered.

Moving forward, we believe it is of vital importance for the IS research community to continue to push its boundaries, whether thematically or paradigmatically, in order for our academic endeavors to achieve greater relevance and impact. Yet, we must simultaneously balance these efforts with the traditions that constitute the IS discipline. This balancing act serves as the challenge going forth for ISJ and for the broader IS academic community. How the community collectively responds will dictate the legacy that is written about ISJ and the IS domain in the next 25-year time period.
