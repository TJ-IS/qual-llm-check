# ‘Orchestrating’ sustainable crowdsourcing: A characterisation of solver brokerages

Joseph Feller <sup>a</sup>, Patrick Finnegan <sup>b</sup>, Jeremy Hayes <sup>a,</sup>⇑, Philip O’Reilly <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Accounting, Finance and Information Systems, University College Cork, Ireland

<sup>b</sup> Australian School of Business, University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Available online 21 July 2012

Keywords: Open innovation Crowdsourcing Interorganisational systems Knowledge mobility Appropriability Stability

## a b s t r a c t

Exemplars of open innovation have revealed that intellectual property (IP) need not only be sourced through existing hierarchical or market relationships. Rather IP can be acquired from individuals and firms with whom an organization has no prior relationship. In such cases, an intermediary, operating as an innovation exchange or brokerage, frequently facilitates the development and acquisition of IP. This paper examines one type of innovation intermediary, the ‘Solver Brokerage,’ which enables innovation exchanges between organizations and unknown external firms and individuals (i.e. a crowdsourcing process). While the commercial success of Solver Brokerages indicates the potency of arguments concerning the potential of crowdsourcing, little is known about the operation of such brokerages or the crowdsourcing processes that they enable. This paper examines extant research on innovation networks, crowdsourcing, and electronic marketplaces to identify three processes (knowledge mobility, appropriability and stability) that we argue are necessary to ‘orchestrate’ crowdsourcing. Using a field study of four Solver Brokerages, an innovation seeking organization, as well as 15 innovation providers (i.e. members of the ‘crowd’), the paper illustrates the ways in which the three orchestration processes are enhanced in Solver Brokerages. It reveals that while knowledge mobility and appropriability processes can be enhanced by activities under the control of the Solver Brokerage, stability is largely determined by innovation seeking organizations and the innovation providers. The paper concludes that broker-provided value-added ‘orchestration’ services need to enable knowledge mobility and appropriability, and to ensure that ‘unsuccessful’ innovation seekers and providers appropriate sufficient value to participate again.

## 1. Introduction

The beneficial effects of inter-organizational cooperative relationships have been advocated for decades (Kaufman, 1966; Van de Ven, 1976; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Henderson, 1990; Legner and Schemm, 2008). However, in the 45 years since organizations were first urged to explore inter-organizational data sharing (cf. Kaufman, 1966), inter-organizational systems (IOSs) research has been dominated by concerns associated with enabling product/service transactions (e.g. Malone et al., 1987; Rosenthal et al., 1993; Golden and Powell, 2004; Legner and Schemm, 2008); although a rich vein of research on Computer-Supported Collaborative Work (CSCW) in the context of inter-organizational knowledge sharing and virtual teams also exists (e.g. Majchrzak et al., 2000; Majchrzak and Rice, 2000). As a result, IOS research has been dominated by the economic theories of Coase (1937) and Williamson (1975, 1981, 1991, 1999). Thus, researchers have predominately conceptualized IOS within the context of a hierarchy or market (although hybrid governance has also been studied). Malone et al. (1987) predicted an increased use of electronic markets over electronic hierarchies. However, rather than the emergence of the type of electronic markets proposed by Malone et al. (1987), researchers (e.g. Bakos, 1991; Lee and Clark, 1996; Soh et al., 2006) have instead revealed the growth of third-party market makers. Such intermediaries are not only evident in industry markets for physical products (cf. Kambil and Van Heck, 1998), but also, more recently, in markets for intellectual property (IP) and innovation capacity (cf. Feller et al., 2009). The growth in IP markets is due to an increase in the number of firms using external parties to source innovation (cf. Linder et al., 2003); leading to more sophisticated, and potentially riskier, forms of inter-organizational information and knowledge sharing (Cui et al., 2009; Almirall and Casadesus-Masanell, 2010). This practice, referred to as open innovation (cf. Chesbrough, 2003), challenges IOS researchers to extend their thinking beyond the traditional logic of the industrial era (cf. Teece, 2010) characterized by the hierarchical exchange of goods and services between buyers and sellers.

This paper aims to address the paucity of research on IP exchanges by exploring how one type of intermediary, a ‘Solver Brokerage’ (c.f. Feller et al., 2009), enables organizations to acquire IP from individuals and other organizations with whom they have no prior relationship. Specifically, the objective of the research is to understand how three innovation ‘orchestration processes (knowledge mobility, appropriability, and stability) are enhanced in Solver Brokerages. The paper begins by discussing the role of inter-organizational networks in open innovation strategies, and identifies the emergence and importance of Solver Brokerages as innovation intermediaries. Acknowledging the lack of research on Solver Brokerages, it uses extant research on innovation networks, crowdsourcing, and traditional marketplace intermediaries to theorize the ‘orchestration processes necessary for mediating crowdsourcing. A field study is presented revealing the ways in which Solver Brokerages can enhance knowledge mobility and appropriability, and uncovers the importance of other participants (innovation seeking organizations and the individuals that participate in the innovation communities) in ensuring stability. The paper concludes that broker-provided value-added ‘orchestration’ services need to enable knowledge mobility and appropriability, and help ensure that ‘unsuccessful’ innovation seekers and providers appropriate sufficient value to participate again.

## 2. Open innovation and inter-organizational networks

Open innovation is the use of ‘‘purposive inflows and outflows of knowledge to accelerate internal innovation, and expand the markets for external use of innovation, respectively. . . This approach places external ideas and external paths to market on the same level of importance as that reserved for internal ideas and paths to market in the earlier era’’ (Chesbrough, 2008, p. 1). In open innovation, firms supplement, or even supplant, internal Research and Development (R&D) efforts by leveraging a variety of sources for knowledge ‘inflows’ including suppliers, partners, customers, competitors, academic researchers, etc. (Chesbrough, 2003).

Research on open innovation processes in commercial settings such as consumer electronics (Blau, 2007: Cui et al., 2009). pharmaceuticals (Lane and Probert, 2007), and automobiles and computer hardware (Gwynne, 2007), as well as in public administration (Feller et al., 2011), illustrate that organizations seeking to acquire innovation and/or innovation capacity will look to establish cooperative relationships with external parties; supporting claims regarding the critical role played by inter-organizational networks in open innovation (cf. Vanhaverbeke and Cloodt, 2006).

Nevertheless, as shown in Fig. 1, the exchange of knowledge with external entities already known to an organization is arguably only the first stage in the evolution of open innovation. Such exchange relies on existing inter-organizational networks and, as a result, limits the range and diversity of knowledge available. More advanced forms of open innovation necessitate the development of new collaboration models and new business models, in order to be able to benefit from innovation capacity in, and knowledge flows between, external entities with which the organization does not have a pre-existing relationship (Pereira, 2007).

The new collaboration models referred to in Fig. 1 are evident through the use of Web-based communities to access knowledge and innovation capacity not available through traditional hierarchical and market relationships (cf. Chesbrough, 2003; Surowieki, 2004). Of these, the Open Source Software (OSS) phenomenon (cf. Fitzgerald, 2006) has had most prominence. However, there is also significant interest by businesses in leveraging the knowledge of unknown individuals and groups to improve innovation processes by acquiring a solution (e.g. a new design) or the knowledge and skills to develop that design (e.g. specialist skills) (Von Hippel, 2001, 2006; Howe, 2008; Feller et al., 2009).

Many organizations seeking to acquire innovation resources (knowledge, skills and solutions) favor traditional inter-firm licensing agreements. However, both the dispersed nature of knowledge and the difficulties associated with accessing innovation and knowledge from individuals and organizations with which a firm does not have an existing business relationship are likely to give rise to the use of intermediaries (Pereira, 2007; Feller et al., 2009). Companies such as Proctor and Gamble and Threadless.com have employed dedicated Web-presences to source innovation and knowledge from those with which the company does not have an existing relationship. Other organizations have turned to third-party open innovation intermediaries to source existing IP (e.g. Yet2), or alternatively to firms like InnoCentive, NineSigma and YourEncore, which mediate access to innovation providers (see Fig. 2) (Chesbrough, 2003; Feller et al., 2009).

The acquisition of innovation capability (i.e. knowledge and skills) through direct or mediated means is a form of crowdsourcing. Crowdsourcing is ‘‘the act of a company or institution taking a function once performed by employees and outsourcing it to an undefined (and generally large) network of people in the form of an open call’’ (Howe, 2008). Crowdsourcing is seen as having great potential as a means for implementing open innovation strategies (Ahonen et al., 2007), and is central to the design of innovation intermediaries (cf. Tapscott and Williams, 2006; Whitla, 2009). Such intermediaries aggregate both demand for capabilities (firms seeking innovators capable of meeting specific challenges) and supply (a large and diverse population of innovators), and have been labeled ‘Solver Brokerages’ (Feller et al., 2009). However, while the commercial success of such brokerages (e.g. InnoCentive, NineSigma and YourEncore) indicates the potency of arguments concerning the importance of crowds (e.g. Surowieki, 2004; Howe, 2008), little is known about the operation of such brokerages (cf. Lichtenthaler and Ernst, 2008) or the process of effectively stimulating a community of innovators (Ebner et al., 2009).

We argue that a richer understanding of the crowdsourcing processes mediated by Solver Brokerages can be achieved b taking into account the distinct nature of the transactions that they support. Specifically, such brokerages do not focus on commodity-for-cash exchanges, such as those found in many markets. Rather, companies like InnoCentive, NineSigma and YourEncore broker collaborative innovation exchanges. Viewed from this perspective, Solver Brokerages are conceptually similar to ‘Innovation Networks’ (cf. Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006) in that they both seek to enable innovation by leveraging dispersed knowledge/skills/innovation capacity (cf. Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006; Feller et al., 2009). However, unlike Innovation Networks, where IP is jointly developed and owned by network participants (cf. Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006), Solver Brokerages leverage crowdsourcing to develop IP for one client organization (cf. Feller et al., 2009). In exploring how such crowdsourcing is ‘orchestrated’ (i.e. enabled), the next section uses an analysis of extant research on Innovation Networks, crowdsourcing, and traditional marketplace intermediaries.

## 3. ‘Orchestrating’ crowdsourcing

At its core, innovation is about knowledge and emerges as a result of combining different knowledge sets (Nonaka et al., 2003; Tidd et al., 2005). While such knowledge is frequently to be found outside the organization (Chesbrough, 2003; De Wit et al., 2007), organizations struggle to determine how to effectively engage with external innovation communities (Di Gangi and Wasko, 2009). Therefore, we posit that the emergence of Solver Brokerages, as crowdsourcing mediators, may be explained by the work of Hayek (1945) on the emergence of intermediaries in the market system. Hayek argued that one of the main issues for parties conducting economic activity is access to market knowledge, as such knowledge does not exist in a concentrated or integrated form but as ‘‘dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all separate individuals possess’’ (Hayek, 1945, p. 77). Hayek argued that one of the key considerations for firms was the process for obtaining and aggregating such knowledge (e.g. potential suppliers, potential buyers, price, availability, etc.), and that this was a process that could be undertaken (as a value-added service) by third party merchants (intermediaries). This consideration of market knowledge is not reflected in the majority of IOS research, which instead views product characteristics asset specificity and structural embeddedness (cf. Granovetter, 1992; Jones et al., 1997) as the key determinants of the appropriateness of autonomous or coordinated inter-firm adaptation that characterize electronic markets and hierarchies.

Building on the work of Hayek (1945) as well as more recent work on IP intermediaries by Tapscott and Williams (2006), Feller et al. (2009), and Whitla (2009), we conceptualize Solver Brokerages as intermediaries that facilitate innovation exchanges between organizations and crowds. Solver Brokerages thus seek to match innovation problem owners (referred to in this paper as ‘‘innovation seekers’’) with potential innovators (referred to in this paper as ‘‘innovation providers’’). In doing so, Solver Brokerages aggregate dispersed knowledge, and mitigate risks and uncertainties for market participants. Our conceptualization of Solver Brokerages is thus in line with the function of indirect markets (cf. Hayek, 1945; Clemons and Weber, 1990; Lee and Clark, 1996) and with brokerages in innovation and technological development contexts (Howells 2006), particularly in terms of diffusion/technology transfer and innovation management (Watkins and Horley, 1986; Hargadon and Sutton, 1997; Howells, 2006). Solver Brokerages thus facilitate the exchange of innovation skills and knowledge that can lead to the production of IP. However, as we have noted, Solver Brokerages support transactions that are different from commodity-for-cash exchanges found in many markets. They are thus closer in character to innovation exchanges (collaborations) found in Innovation Networks (cf. Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006).

Innovation Networks can be conceived of as ‘‘loosely coupled systems of autonomous firms [that require a hub firm] to orchestrate network activities to ensure the creation and extraction of value, without the benefit of hierarchical authority" (Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006, p. 656). The purpose of an Innovation Network matches that of Solver Brokerages, as both aim to foster innovation by leveraging dispersed knowledge/expertise (cf. Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006; Feller et al., 2009). Both Innovation Networks and Solver Brokerages are organizational forms within which firms can safely seek external knowledge, collaborate on solutions to problems, and capture the value associated with such solutions. There are, however, key differences between the two structures. Most notable is that Innovation Networks are steered by a hub firm that is an active participant in the network. In contrast, a Solver Brokerage is a third-party market maker that facilitates the innovation process. Additionally, while the development of IP within Innovation Networks is undertaken by the firms that ultimately share ownership of it (Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006), in the Solver Brokerage, the development of IP is undertaken by external parties (the crowd) and is acquired by a single firm (Feller et al., 2009). Due to the dispersion of knowledge amongst crowdsourcing participants, Solver Brokerages have a mediating role (cf. Latour, 2005) in ‘orchestrating’ the innovation process. As mediators, the activities of Solver Brokerages are shaped by the nature of the resources aggregated on the supply-side; namely the potential capabilities to be leveraged through the process of crowdsourcing (Howe, 2008; c.f. Shirky, 2008; Sunstein, 2006; Surowieki, 2004).

Extending the comparison between Innovation Networks and Solver Brokerages, as well as leveraging extant work on knowledge in groups and crowdsourcing (e.g. Surowieki, 2004; Tapscott and Williams, 2006; Whitla, 2009; Howe, 2008; Shirky, 2008; Sunstein, 2006), we further characterize Solver Brokerages as:

Value-added service providers that enable firms to leverage crowdsourcing techniques by providing access to an appropriate Virtual Innovation Community (VIC) i.e. the group of people that can, individually or collectively, provide or generate the desired innovation solution.

In addition to providing access to an appropriate VIC, Solver Brokerages need to enable knowledge exchange between innovation seekers and providers (Feller et al., 2009). Very little is known about knowledge exchanges in crowdsourcing environments, although early discourse on crowdsourcing (Howe, 2008) emphasizes the importance of problem articulation so that potential solvers can solve it. In the context of Innovation Networks, Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) refer to ‘‘the ease with which knowledge is shared, acquired, and deployed’’ as knowledge mobility. Enhancing knowledge mobility is a key function of the hub firm in an Innovation Network. The hub firm relies on three processes to enhance knowledge mobility. These are knowledge absorption (i.e, increasing the absorptive capacity of the network), network identification (i.e, creating a common identity amongst participants in order to increase knowledge sharing), and inter-organizational socialization (i.e. creating formal and informal linkages amongst participants) (Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006).

Rewarding participants for participating in innovation communities is another key aspect of crowdsourcing. Researchers (Von Krogh, 1998; Von Hippel, 2001; Brabham, 2009) believe that rewards can be provided in a variety of forms including opportunities to make money, develop creative skills, and elevate participant reputation. Other rewards include creating reciprocal relationships and community building. While such arguments are concerned with community participants, it is also clear that innovation seekers (i.e. firms) must be able to derive value from crowdsourcing. Similarly, Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) use the work of Teece (1986) to argue that hub firms in an Innovation Network must engage in activities to maximize members’ ‘‘ability to capture the profits generated by an innovation’’ (p. 660, quoting Teece, 1986, p. 610); a process that they refer to as innovation appropriability. Within Innovation Networks, Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) propose that hub firms enable innovation appropriability by building trust amongst participants, ensuring procedural justice (i.e. ensuring a fair process) and facilitating joint ownership of assets (i.e. IP) developed by the network.

It is evident that crowds can be effective problem solvers and innovation generators (Surowieki, 2004; Sunstein, 2006) Nevertheless, ensuring the on-going viability of crowdsourcing is a non-trivial task that is central to future value creation and extraction. Managing the growth of the network and the resultant complexity is of concern to firms seeking to extract value from open innovation processes (Almirall and Casadesus-Masanell, 2010). Likewise, sustaining the participation of an appropriate community of innovation providers is essential for the success of crowdsourcing (Howe, 2008). In the context of Innovation Networks, Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) argue that hub firms need to balance the agility offered by multiple innovation partners with the sustainability of the innovation ecosystem as a whole to ensure dynamic stability (i.e. non-negative growth over time). They propose that the hub firm can enable such stability by enhancing its own reputation as a market leader, fostering long-term cooperation (referred to as lengthening ‘the shadow of the future’), and building multiple relationships between firms (i.e. multiplexity).

To conclude, we argue that because Solver Brokerages, like Innovation Networks, seek to foster innovation by leveraging dispersed knowledge/expertise, the innovation orchestration processes proposed by Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) are likely to also be central to the task of orchestrating crowdsourcing, However, the differences between Innovation Networks and Solver Brokerages in terms of the nature of the participants, the relationships between participants, and the ownership of developed IP, mean that the manner in which these orchestration processes are supported is likely to be different for Innovation Networks and Solver Brokerages. In particular, strategies involving the creation of a common identity and the establishment of formal and informal relationships between participants (to enhance knowledge mobility), joint ownership of assets (to enhance appropriability), or the building of long-term and multiple relationships (to enhance stability) are not necessarily feasible in crowdsourcing environments. Building on the argument made above as to the necessity to better understand Solver Brokerages and drawing on the work on Innovation Networks, we formally present the objective of this research, namely to understand how knowledge mobility, innovation appropriability and dynamic stability (hereafter simply mobility, appropriability and stability) are enhanced within the crowdsourcing arrangements ‘orchestrated’ by Solver Brokerages

## 4. Research approach

The research objective stated above has been operationalized in the form of three research questions.

1. How is mobility enhanced in Solver Brokerages?

2. How is appropriability enhanced in Solver Brokerages?

3. How is stability enhanced in Solver Brokerages?

The study was categorized as exploratory due to the scarcity of empirical work in the area, and the focus on discovery rather than validation of theory. In line with the recommendations of Lee and Baskerville (2003), we began by examining extant theories of innovation networks, crowdsourcing and marketplace intermediaries to frame our conceptual understanding. In designing the empirical study, we took direction from Marshall and Rossman (1989) who propose that either a case study or field study method can be used in exploratory research. We decided that a field study would be most appropriate for this study as it would facilitate the collection of data from a larger number of organizations, and would form the basis for more focused research at a later stage; an approach consistent with the thinking of Galliers (1992). The field study method consists of narrowly focused cross-sectional qualitative case studies (McGrath, 1979) that measure dependant variables without any attempt to control independent variables (Buckley et al., 1976).

The study focused on four Solver Brokerages: InnoCentive, YourEncore, NineSigma, and InnoCrowding. InnoCentive, YourEncore and NineSigma were selected based on their maturity and because they have all been identified as leading examples of open innovation marketplaces (cf. Motzek, 2007; Huston and Sakkab, 2006). Additionally, we included InnoCrowding, which is a relatively new brokerage and provides a useful counterpoint to the more established brokerages.

Data was gathered between August 2008 and December 2010 using a mixed methods approach. An initial phase of data gathering incorporated an analysis of (1) documents (e.g. white papers) and interviews published by the brokerages (2) the brokerages’ web-based systems for exchanging IP (where applicable), and (3) secondary content (e.g. news articles) related to the brokerages. ## 5. Analysis

This section presents the findings of the study and is structured around the three research questions described previously. Our analysis (see summary in Table 2) reveals that knowledge mobility is enhanced through aggregating a critical and diverse mass of innovation providers, making innovation providers and innovation problems discoverable, and supporting the process of problem articulation. Appropriability is enhanced through mechanisms for filtering proposed solutions, mechanisms for managing the transfer of IP, and diverse rewards for successful innovation providers and innovation seekers. Finally, the stability of the Solver Brokerage is enhanced by the existence of rewards for all innovation providers and seekers (whether successful or not) and trust by participants in the fairness and efficacy of the brokerage system/process.

## 5.1. Enhancing knowledge mobility

Within Solver Brokerages, knowledge mobility (the ease of transferring knowledge from innovation providers to innovation seekers) is enhanced by (i) the aggregation of a diverse population of innovation providers, (ii) the provision of mechanisms that make innovation expertise and innovation problems discoverable, and (iii) the provision of support for problem articulation. Aggregation supports mobility by increasing the amount of knowledge that can potentially be brought to bear on any given problem. Discoverability supports mobility by increasing the likelihood that the most relevant/useful knowledge is applied to any given problem. Finally, problem articulation supports mobility by ensuring that innovation providers have the information needed to effectively work on a problem, while the confidentiality of the innovation seeking firm is protected. ## 5.1.1. Aggregation

Different forms of aggregation were observed within the brokerages. Aggregation activities were found to focus on providing innovation seekers with access to a large, anonymous population (e.g. InnoCentive), to a specific community (e.g. the community of retirees at YourEncore), or to communities of internal employees (e.g. NineSigma and InnoCrowding). All of the brokerages identified the need for problems to be presented to a Virtual Innovation Community (VIC) of innovation providers having relevant knowledge, as well as diverse skills and perspectives. The aggregated knowledge and capabilities of such a population form the basis for sharing, acquiring and deploying innovation. Two operational models for aggregating the VIC were observed.

As evident from the study, the first approach to creating a VIC is to maximize the number of participants, relying on large groups of self-selecting participants to collectively provide a diverse set of relevant skills and perspectives. This strategy is evident in the operating model of both established and emerging brokerages. For example InnoCentive makes all innovation challenges visible via the public web. Likewise, reaching the largest possible solution community was seen to be strategically important by InnoCrowding, whose Executive VP argued that ‘‘we haven’t even touched the tip of the iceberg of the amount of people that are available on the planet. . . .If you start to ‘close the gate’ you’re going to have a problem on your hands.’’ In this model, the aggregation of large communities that contain diverse skills affects mobility by increasing the potential number and diversity of solutions proposed; thus increasing the volume of knowledge that can potentially be shared.

An alternative approach to creating the appropriate VIC is to target a smaller, more specific group of innovation providers by pre-defining a set of desirable skills and backgrounds. For example, YourEncore focuses on aggregating the capability of retired experts, whose qualifications and experience have been authenticated by the brokerage. The importance of having a pre-validated community was highlighted by the VP at YourEncore, who noted ‘‘we offer our clients a secure environment, and this group of highly vetted experts have a lot of knowledge’’. Such strategies seek to increase knowledge mobility by ensuring that all innovation providers have a background deemed relevant to the problem space. This approach also ensures that the VIC represents a diverse set of perspectives and mitigates concerns that firms seeking solutions might have about engaging in a fully ‘open’ process.

Regardless of the model employed, there was widespread evidence that diversity of perspectives and backgrounds within the crowd was critical to mobilizing knowledge. The Executive VP at InnoCrowding stated, ‘‘breakthrough innovation often comes from a person that has a different point of view, or is from an unrelated discipline. This type of innovator may or may not be a scientist, and [thus] makes an open minded recommendation.’’ The VP of YourEncore also described the value created when different disciplinary viewpoints are brought to bear on a problem. For example, in an instance where a firm sought a solution for a bacterial build-up on some processing equipment, ‘‘they posted this question out to the innovation community and they got 20 or 30 responses back that were pretty good ideas, and whittled that down to 5 that they thought were good solutions. One of them was a chemical process. Another was a heat process. One guy suggested totally redesigning the process so you don’t have a build-up in the first place’’.

## 5.1.2. Discoverability

The analysis revealed that the existence of a knowledgeable and diverse VIC does not, of itself, ensure that this expertise will be brought to bear on the most relevant innovation problems. Thus, brokerages use a variety of mechanisms to ensure that both innovators and innovation problems are discoverable. Our analysis revealed three main modes of discovery. These are (i) innovation provider-led discovery (self-selection), (ii) innovation seeker-led discovery (search), and (iii) broker-led discovery (matchmaking).

In the innovation provider-led discovery mode, problems are self-selected by potential solution providers. The self-selection is facilitated by a variety of technical and information management mechanisms; including the categorization of problems based on disciplinary nature, problem type (e.g. reduction to practice vs. ideation) and other criteria, and the provision of search and communication mechanisms for identifying potential problems of interest (e.g. keyword email alerts). One brokerage (InnoCentive) also facilitates problem discovery based on the identity of the seeking organization by enabling innovation seekers to create branded pavilions showcasing a portfolio of innovation challenges.

In innovation seeker-led discovery the brokerage works with the innovation seeker to identify the characteristics/skills required in a group of innovation providers. To facilitate innovation seeker-led discovery, all of the brokerages provide mechanisms for innovators to build an identity (profile) within the brokerage. They also provide mechanisms for creating collaborative groupings, to varying degrees, amongst innovation providers. For example, YourEncore formally organizes their innovation community into sub-groups and documents the knowledge, experience and skills of the community as accurately as possible (experts may be members of more than one sub-group). For any specific problem, the innovation seekers, together with the brokerage’s team, utilize the brokerage database to choose from a list of individuals and sub-groups as they construct their appropriate target audience for the problem. Similarly, NineSigma provide a service whereby they assemble panels of experts in a specific area, at the request of clients.

Finally, matchmaking involves the brokerages putting innovation seekers in contact with particular innovation providers. In addition to leveraging the existing VIC, this entails searching for potential innovation providers that are not current brokerage participants. In this scenario, NineSigma have a process for finding innovation providers outside their VIC that involves identifying possible partners through extensive searches of published articles, patents etc.

## 5.1.3. Problem articulation

Our analysis revealed that once a high quality VIC has been established and both potential expertise and innovation problems are discoverable, mobility is further enhanced by the clarity and quality of problem descriptions. Mobility is thus dependent upon the ability of innovation seeking firms to effectively articulate and communicate questions that are (i) of a suitable scope and nature, (ii) well structured, (iii) relevant, and (iv) solvable. As revealed by several informants, decomposing problems into unambiguous and independent modular parts maximizes the percentage of the VIC who can potentially contribute solutions, thus enhancing mobility.

The brokerages studied all utilize similar processes in order to ensure that questions are relevant and solvable. Management in all the brokerages revealed that the proper construction of a problem posting is essential to generate meaningfu responses. For example, in InnoCentive, innovation seekers are asked during the problem drafting stage, whether they believe that a solution, which meets the criteria of the problem, (i) could be evaluated within the necessary time-frame and (ii) would represent a result that they could further develop and derive value from. InnoCentive’s Innovation Development Manager notes, ‘‘asking these questions up front ensures that we are delivering relevant solutions’’.

Additionally, brokerages and innovation seekers analyze potential problems to determine the most appropriate broadcast method. The broadcast methods that the brokerages utilize differ in terms of complexity, ranging from simple open-ended questions posed to the VIC (often without financial rewards), to more specifically detailed and complex projects. YourEncore’s Vice-President revealed ‘‘the wording of these requests, the amount of information provided, and what is asked for in the response vary significantly between these different types of postings’’. The brokerages work with the clients (innovation seeking organizations) through dedicated account teams in order to determine the exact nature of the solution that the client is looking for. Therefore, the brokerages play a key role in ensuring the relevancy of the question posted to the VIC, as their intervention is key to helping solution seekers scope and communicate the problem. Indeed, there is a belief among management in the Brokerages that not all problems are solvable. Consequently, NineSigma’s European CEO notes, ‘‘we always ask them [the innovation seekers] to come up with perhaps twice the amount of project ideas’’.

Mobility is also enhanced by decomposing problems into unambiguous and independent modular parts in order to protect the IP/confidentiality of the innovation seeking organization while communicating the problem to the VIC. Problem decomposition serves to make questions ‘safer’, as it mitigates the IP risk associated with posing problems in a ‘public’ manner. For many organizations, there may be a risk of exposing sensitive information to competitors (e.g. R&D for a new product/service). There is thus a need to decompose the problem into a challenge (or a number of challenges) that conceal(s) the overall nature of the innovation being sought, and the purpose that it will be used for. The VP of InnoCrowding explained such decomposition as being akin to a glass sculpture; ‘‘imagine that you have a piece of art made out of glass. . . Now you take it and break it into small pieces. . . It’s like that in open innovation. . . Each piece of glass becomes a small task in our challenge. You won’t see something that says let’s cure cancer. You will see a lot of smaller tasks instead that are related to the overall problem. . . Reconstituting the artwork from the pieces is the final goal that [the Brokerage] wants to achieve.’

Brokerages utilize many mechanisms to enable a seeking organization to control their IP when articulating problems/ challenges. For example, YourEncore provides a sophisticated compliance tool as part of the broadcast search method in order to generate ‘safer postings’. This compliance mechanism ensures that the challenge is reviewed by appropriate staff in the innovation seeking organization, regardless of the broadcast method utilized (e.g. ‘Simple Question’, Request for Expertise, or Request for Ideas). Similar mechanisms are also used by other brokerages to place approval in the hands of the client. According to InnoCentive’s Innovation Development Manager ‘‘only the Seeker’s lawyers can determine safety’’.

In order to ensure that a problem is solvable and (thus enhance mobility). brokerages also help clients structure their problems to ensure that a wide variety of innovators can engage with the problem without necessitating the integration of multiple disciplines/technical domains. InnoCentive's Innovation Development Manager cites the example of the devel: opment of a challenge relating to a problem in the genomics space. Through conversations between InnoCentive and the client organization. it was determined that the most likely solution would likely come from innovation providers with backgrounds in electrical engineering. Therefore, the problem was formulated in a way that a person with an electrical engi neering background, but without any knowledge of genomics or biology, had a reasonable chance of finding a suitable solution. Even in such cases, there may be a need for the innovation seeking organization to both answer questions posed by potential innovation providers and to guide them through the solution process. One innovation provider noted that ‘‘very rarely have I been able to just grab [a challenge] right off the bat and just run withit. . . Usually I need to ask at least one question.’’

Finally, the brokerages work with their innovation seeking organizations in order to identify (i) what they believe to be the characteristics of ‘winning’ innovation providers and, in some cases and (ii) who the potential innovation providers within the VIC are likely to be. Once the problem is recognized as being relevant, safe and solvable, a decision is taken in relation to the most appropriate broadcast method for communicating with the VIC (or the most relevant subset of the VIC).

## 5.2. Enhancing appropriability

The analysis revealed that once mobility has been achieved a diverse array of innovation providers can engage with a portfolio of well-constructed problems; leading to a variety of possible solutions. The question then becomes one of appropriability (capturing value from the crowdsourcing process). The study revealed that, within Solver Brokerages, appropriability is enhanced through (i) the provision of mechanisms for filtering proposed solutions, (ii) support for managing the transfer of IP, and (iii) rewards for successful innovation providers and innovation seekers. Filtering mechanisms enhance appropriability by enabling innovation-seeking organizations to effectively evaluate a large number of potential solutions. Support for IP transfer enhances appropriability by ensuring that chosen solutions are exploitable by the seeking firm. Finally, diverse rewards are necessary to ensure that both innovation providers and innovation seekers derive value from engag ing in the crowdsourcing process. ## 5.2.1. Filtering

The study revealed that in order to capture value by exploiting the solution to an innovation problem, innovation seekers must first efficiently and effectively evaluate the body of potential solutions. To facilitate this, Solver Brokerages were observed to implement a variety of processes and tools designed to help firms filter the proposed solutions (See Table 5).

Full-service provision of filtering, in which the brokerage, rather than the innovation seeker, carries out the evaluation of potential solutions, is less common than other types of filtering. However, there are cases where it is deemed necessary by the innovation seeking organization. The Innovation Development Manager at InnoCentive explained that, ‘‘some less technical seekers (such as not-for-profit organizations) need additional help to determine the technical evaluation of the submissions Often we utilize a panel of experts to perform the evaluation. . . We filter solutions into three classes (high quality, low quality and automatically reject).’’ This service is also used in situations where the client needs assistance in evaluating the solutions proposed for complex problems.

Innovation seeking firms that conduct their own evaluations may decide that they want all proposed solutions to be provided to them. However, they sometimes request that the brokerage to do some pre-filtering on their behalf. One common form of filtering is based on the domain of the solution (discipline or similar characteristics). The Executive VP of InnoCrowding commented that there are occasions where ‘‘the company has predetermined some steps and says that a, b, c, must be in place before the solution will be evaluated, like a chemical compound that you don’t want or other thresholds that you have’’.

## 5.2.2. IP transfer support

The analysis reveals that once innovation seekers are able to effectively evaluate potential solutions, and choose one for acquisition, brokerages provide a number of services focused on supporting the exchange of IP for payment. These include IP due diligence services to ensure that IP is unfettered, escrow services to protect both parties, and electronic transaction support.

IP transfer support at YourEncore starts with all members of the expert community signing IP transfer agreements in order to participate in the VIC. Brokerages first assist in the IP transfer process through enabling innovation seekers to determine if a solution (or something similar) already exists. Confidentiality is very important in supporting IP transfer. NineSigma, for example, provide an IP filtering service in order to anonymize the innovation seeker, and to avoid IP contamination. The CEO (Europe) said that clients often ask, ‘‘would you please stay in the frame because we’d like to use you as an IP buffer. . . let’s keep the thing confidential as long as humanly possible’’. In instances where the seeking firm requires legal assistance with the transfer of IP, the brokerages are also able to assist.

Nevertheless, the level of involvement by the brokerage in providing IP transfer support is dependent on (i) the complexity of the request, (ii) the domain or discipline involved, and (iii) the requirements of the innovation seeker. For example, InnoCrowding offers two levels of challenge, ‘Public-ContestPost’ and ‘Private-ContestPost’. With Public-ContestPosts, requests for collaboration or proposals do not feature IP transfers. Therefore, this form of service requires minimal intervention by the Brokerage.

## 5.2.3. Rewards for success

Finally, appropriability is enhanced by ensuring that both the innovation provider and seeker capture value from the successful (i.e. winning) solution. On a direct financial level, the value appropriated by innovation providers and innovation seekers consists of payment for the innovation (provider) and ownership of the innovation (seeker). However, our analysis revealed that a complex collection of indirect financial and non-financial benefits are also enjoyed by successful innovation providers and innovation seekers, and that these diverse rewards are important to participants’ perception of the brokerages level of appropriability. These indirect and non-financial benefits are also important to the stability of the brokerage, as they are potentially enjoyed by all participants, while direct, monetary profit emerging from exchanges within the brokerage is limited to (i) providers of winning solutions and (ii) the firm who exploits the solution. This is discussed later in the paper under Rewards for Participation

Rewards for successful innovation providers include direct financial gain (payment for solution), and well as indirect financial gain through enhanced professional reputation and future business opportunities (e.g. employment and/or consulting). The primary non-financial benefits identified in the study were (i) learning and skill building and (ii) personal satisfaction and development. Learning benefits include gaining knowledge about new problem spaces and disciplines, gaining knowledge and experience working with new materials and processes, gaining new (and refining existing) problem solving skills and developing expertise in utilizing the brokerage and similar systems. Personal benefits were described as being a sense of enjoyment/fun derived from the creative problem solving process and/or the thrill of competition, opportunities to make a contribution to societal/humane causes and intellectual validation (the desire among innovation providers to know that their ideas were of practical relevance to industry/society). The Innovation Development Manager at InnoCentive described the members of the VIC as ‘‘very creative. They wish to. . . challenge themselves, and they are curious to learn more.’’

Similarly, the Vice President of YourEncore commented ‘‘we found that what’s driving most of our members or experts is the desire to stay connected, to stay involved, keep [their] skills current, and not to be playing golf or sitting on the front porch on a rocking chair. . . Sure, they are looking for money and supplemental income, but it’s the other kinds of intangibles that are the biggest drivers.’’ It is noteworthy that in some instances the non-financial rewards are viewed as being equally as, or some times more important than the financial rewards. For example, many innovators explained that they decide to work on innovation challenges in order to participate in something that ‘‘makes a difference’’. This is borne out by the large numbers that choose to work on problems for ‘not-for-profit’ organizations where the financial rewards are typically smaller, but the challenge may be geared towards a greater societal good. One innovator commented ‘‘it’s a. . . way of. . . using my talents and my insights to try to help other people/companies’’.

The direct financial reward for successful innovation seekers is the right to exploit the chosen solution. However, indirect financial rewards are also evident. New business development opportunities may also arise if the firm gains a reputation for using radical (and, critically, successful) innovation processes. Additionally, successful innovation seekers enjoy two kinds of organizational learning. First, they usually develop an improved understanding of both the problem and solution space; derived from the process of problem articulation, the nature of the chosen solution, the process behind the chosen solution, and the nature of the other proposed (unsuccessful) solutions. Second, the innovation-seeking firm gains a better understanding of the brokerage’s process specifically, and more generally, of the process of working with unknown individuals firms for innovation purposes. While some organizations prefer to remain anonymous when posting challenges, other benefit from publicizing their involvement. According to the Innovation Development Manager at InnoCentive such companies ‘‘wear it as a badge on the side of their jacket to proudly display that they are using open innovation’’.

## 5.3. Enhancing stability

Just as the success of a single problem-solving cycle is dependent on exchanging knowledge (mobility) and capturing value (appropriability), it is evident that the long-term success of a brokerage relies on the preservation and growth of both the VIC and the innovation seeking community. Our study reveals that the stability (capacity for non-negative growth over time) of Solver Brokerages is enhanced by (i) the ability for all participants (whether successful or not) to capture value from participation in the brokerage and (ii) the trust of all participants in the fairness and efficiency of the brokerage. Successindependent rewards enhance stability by ensuring that participants who are unsuccessful in a particular problem-solving cycle (which is the majority of innovation providers) also capture value from the process. Likewise, brokerage stability can be damaged over time by a lack of trust in either the efficacy or fairness of the processes enabled by the brokerage (as participants choose alternative innovation strategies). ## 5.3.2. Trust in the system

Finally, the stability of a Solver Brokerage is also affected by the extent to which participants trust the system. This trust takes several forms.

From the point of view of the innovation provider, our study showed that there is the need to:

\- Trust that the brokerage will protect their IP from exploitation without compensation.

\- Trust that the ‘‘contest’’ or other format used for the selection of submitted ideas is unbiased.

\- Trust that their submissions will be duly considered and evaluated.

Thus, brokerages acknowledge the need to make the innovation process as transparent as possible (for example, through feedback on submissions and public documentation of the evaluation criteria/process) and also to supply IP escrow services as discussed previously (see IP Transfer Support).

Additionally, the study revealed that innovation providers must trust that the rewards for participating and winning (be they financial or otherwise) are commensurate with the level of effort required. As one innovation provider stated, ‘‘there can be very differing ways of rewarding people, from a ‘pat on the back’ and ‘way to go’ to ‘we’ll put you on a website’ to substantial financial remuneration’’. The innovation provider also indicated that there could be a disparity between the value of a chal lenge’s prize and the potential profits generated for the innovation-seeking firm through commercialization of the innovation. He believed that there should be alternative processes in place in order that all parties can fully benefit from the potential value of the innovation e.g. a percentage of future profits derived from its exploitation. Such perceived disparities on the part of the innovation providers constitute a threat to the stability of Solver Brokerages. The situation is acknowledged by the brokerages, which see the issue of the parity of rewards as critical to ongoing success (i.e. stability). For example, YourEncore’s Vice-President, in discussing the importance of the correct reward system revealed that ‘‘properly constructing the right reward package for a given question/request is one of the most difficult, and important, aspects of our model’’. In order to ensure that the best reward package for any given situation is provided, YourEncore, for example, seeks advice from internal consultants and Network Asset Specialists.

Likewise, innovation seeking organizations need to trust the brokerage, its systems and processes as being capable of protecting their identity, background IP, etc. There is also a need to trust the brokerage to act as an intermediary for the final IP transaction, particularly where due diligence/IP clearance services are involved. In situations where the brokerage provides ‘high touch’ filtering services, there is the additional need to trust the brokerage as an evaluator of potential solutions. Finally, and more generically, innovation seekers need to retain the conviction that: open innovation (in general), open innovation through crowdsourcing (more narrowly) and specifically crowdsourcing through the particular Solver Brokerage, represent an effective and efficient strategy for solving innovation problems.

## 6. Conclusions

This paper is a response to calls to better understand both the emerging phenomenon of Solver Brokerages (cf. Lichtenthaler and Ernst, 2008; Feller et al., 2009) and the virtual innovation communities (cf. Ebner et al., 2009) with which they support engagement. Solver Brokerages are of scientific and practical interest because (i) as intermediaries, Solver Brokerages enable organizations to access knowledge and innovation capacity not available to them through hierarchical and market relationships and (ii) as mediators, Solver Brokerages improve knowledge transfer and increase innovation capacity through facilitating the crowdsourcing process.

Based on the forgoing analysis, we present three propositions to explain how three innovation ‘orchestration’ processes (knowledge mobility, appropriability, and stability) are enhanced in Solver Brokerages in order to enable organizations to acquire IP from individuals and other organizations with which they have no prior relationship.

P1: Mobility is enhanced by (i) establishing a diverse knowledgeable VIC, (ii) making members of that VIC discoverable to innovation seekers and making innovation problems discoverable to the VIC, (iii) helping innovation seekers ask relevant, safe and solvable questions, and (iv) ensuring the VIC has access to the information needed to work on an innovation challenge.

P2: Appropriability is enhanced when (i) solution seeking firms are able to effectively evaluate potential solutions, (ii) exchanges of IP for payment are simplified and protected, and (iii) when both the innovation provider and innovation acquirer are rewarded for the exchange in diverse ways.

P3: The stability of a Solver Brokerage is dependent on the brokerage’s ability to (i) maintain/grow both the VIC and the client list of innovation seeking organizations and (ii) ensure that all participants trust in and benefit from the process, regardless of whether the particular problem-solving cycle was successful or not.

This research answers a call by Feller et al. (2010) who call for research focusing on organizational and electronic intermediaries to facilitate open innovation, specifically focused on understanding innovators and the innovation process. This research addresses both, thereby providing detailed insights for solver brokerages on how to successfully orchestrate open innovation. In relation to understanding innovators, it extends extant research through providing greater understanding of solver motivation for participation, and mechanisms for establishing and enhancing trust. In terms of the open innovation process, it adds to the extant literature by providing detailed insights on mechanisms for enhancing appropriability and knowledge mobility. Specifically, it provides new insights on the filtering process and IP transfer support enabled by Solver Brokerages. The paper makes several contributions to the extant literature on open and collaborative innovation in general, and in particular provides an increased understanding of the similarities and differences between the Solver Brokerage and related organizational structures (e.g. Innovation Networks).

Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) state that three processes can be used to enhance knowledge mobility in an Innovation Network: knowledge absorption, network identification and inter-organizational socialization. Our findings are consistent with certain aspects of their work, in two ways. First, we found that aggregation activities (both supply and demand side) were central to enhancing mobility in Solver Brokerages, which thus extends our understanding of the role of network identification in this new context. Likewise, our analysis reveals that socialization remains important, although this takes the form of discoverability, rather than deep relationship building, due to the ad hoc and transient nature of innovation interactions within Solver Brokerages. In contrast to extant work on Innovation Networks, we found that the solver brokerages studied did not support knowledge absorption through formal mechanisms/processes. This may be explained by the fact that the solver brokerages studied support the transfer of knowledge and are not concerned with how that knowledge is ultimately internally applied by innovation seekers.

Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) purport than hub firms enable innovation appropriability by building trust amongst participants, ensuring fair, procedural justice is in place and facilitating joint ownership of assets. Again, our study extends our current understanding of innovation exchanges in several ways, In the context of Solver Brokerages, our analysis reveals that procedural justice does not manifest in the form of behavioral contracts but rather through the mechanisms and processes designed to keep exchanges safe and efficient. Furthermore, appropriability within brokerages was revealed to rely on mechanisms and processes for filtering and selecting innovation solutions. In contrast to Innovation Networks, ownership issues are addressed through the combination of reward mechanisms incorporated into IP transfer mechanisms, rather than through joint. Finally, in contrast to extant work, our analysis revealed that trust was not deemed to be a factor that enabled innovation appropriability, but rather manifests as an important factor vis-à-vis stability over time.

Finally, Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) state that hub firms need to balance the agility offered by incorporating numerous partners into the innovation process with the sustainability of the innovation ecosystem. They argue that this is achievable through enhancement of the hub firm’s reputation as a market leader, the fostering of long-term cooperation, and the building multiple relationships between firms. Within Solver Brokerages, it was revealed that past experience (mutual and diverse rewards for both successful and unsuccessful participants, and trust in the fairness and efficacy of the system) were more important to the non-negative growth over time than other factors. However, in line with extant work, the reputation of the brokerages and the promise of future value capture through open innovation were also present. Finally, the unique crowdsourcing context of Solver Brokerages meant that the fostering of deep, long term cooperative relationships between parties is not necessarily required for stability, as is the case in Innovation Networks.

## 6.1. Future research: testing of propositions

The research process utilized in this study proved to be effective, yielding three broad propositions regarding the factors enabling mobility, appropriability and stability within the emerging structure of Solver Brokerages. Having done this, we now call for research to test these propositions. To facilitate future research efforts, Table 7 provides a sub-set of empirical indicators for the key constructs referenced in the propositions, e.g. mobility, discoverability, etc.

By substituting indicators from the table into the propositions, researchers can articulate testable and refutable hypothesis, using data gathered through a variety of methods, but particularly through the analysis of quantitative data. By illustration, we provide one sample hypothesis for each proposition, as follows:

P1 could be operationalized through H1: Innovation seekers will receive a wider range of potential solutions from innovators when Solver Brokerages provide support for decomposing innovation problems into modularized sub-tasks. P2 could be operationalized through H2: Innovation Seekers will experience a greater ability to capture value from participation in the Solver Brokerage when mechanisms are provided to filter potential solutions based on various criteria. P3 could be operationalized through H3: Innovation providers are more likely to repeatedly participate in challenges over time when they have experienced opportunities for learning in both successful and unsuccessful problem solving efforts in the past.

First, knowledge mobility may be the primary value-adding role of Solver Brokerages, but it is the least understood from both a theoretical and practical perspective. It is evident that Solver Brokerages are more than mere conduits for innovation exchanges by ‘matching’ innovation seekers and providers. Rather, Solver Brokerages offer value-added services that mobilize knowledge by helping organizations specify their innovation problems in a manner that will increase the possibility of it being solved by the VIC (which the brokerage assembles and filters). In this context, effective knowledge mobility involves either (i) identifying the innovation provider who can best meet the organization’s need (i.e. ‘wisdom in crowds’) or (ii) leveraging the collective intelligence of the solution community to meet the organization’s need (i.e. ‘wisdom of crowds’). Of these, our study has demonstrated that the first is better understood from a process and outcome perspective. However, the second may potentially be more valuable in the context of enhancing open innovation activities.

Second, stability represents the biggest challenge to the viability of Solver Brokerages in that continued participation (of both innovation seekers and a large pool of innovation providers) is vital to successful crowdsourcing. As the dominant knowledge mobility practices of Solver Brokerages focuses on leveraging the individual ‘wisdom in crowds’, each challenge normally results in just one ‘successful’ innovation provider. In addition, the nature of the ‘problems’ with which organizations approach Solver Brokerages means that the brokerages cannot guarantee success. As crowdsourcing requires the continued participation of ‘unsuccessful’ seekers and solvers, the real appropriability challenge for brokerages is ensuring that unsuccessful innovation seekers and providers can also derive value from participating.

Third, dissecting the appropriability challenge reveals that brokerages must ensure that (i) organizations that are new to the crowdsourcing process can derive value by learning about crowdsourcing, (ii) all organizations can derive value by learning about their ‘problem’ even if a solution is not found, and (iii) all innovators can learn or derive enjoyment from attempting to solve the ‘problem’. While further research is required, we believe that the challenge specification process is central to meeting the needs of these various participants. This is because the manner in which Solver Brokerages decompose the problem and specify the challenge helps to improve an organization’s understanding of their problem. In addition, the scope and complexity of the challenge that innovation providers undertake affects the degree to which they enjoy, learn or otherwise benefit from the experience.
