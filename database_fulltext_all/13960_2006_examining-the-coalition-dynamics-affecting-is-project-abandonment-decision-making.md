# Examining the coalition dynamics affecting IS project abandonment decision-making

Gary Pan <sup>a,1</sup>, Shan L. Pan <sup>b,T</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Accounting and Business Information Systems, Faculty of Economic and Commerce, The University of Melbourne, Parkville VIC 3010, Australia

<sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems, School of Computing, National University of Singapore, 3 Science Drive 2, Singapore 117543, Republic of Singapore

Available online 22 April 2005

## Abstract

Despite recent reports that suggest growing evidence of a higher rate of information systems project success, experts have warned against complacency over the risks associated with project failure. While IS project failure has been attracting constant attention in recent years, IS project abandonment—which is a subset of the larger organizational issues of IS failure—has been largely ignored. This study aims to provide a deeper understanding of the project abandonment phenomenon. We undertook a case study of an abandoned electronic procurement project to investigate the coalition dynamics affecting project abandonment decision-making—an area that is remotely addressed in the project abandonment literature. We found that antecedent conditions, level of coping with uncertainty, level of substitutability, and level of centrality are key issues in the coalition dynamics that influence project abandonment decisions. Research and practical implications of these findings were discussed, and we concluded with a summary of the contributions of this study and a note on its limitations. <sup>D</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information systems project abandonment; Coalition dynamics; Case study approach

## 1. Introduction

Recent reports from the United States (US) and the United Kingdom (UK) [46,43] suggest growing evidence of a higher rate of information systems (IS) project success. However, despite such advances in software project development, experts have warned against complacency over the risks associated with project failure, as troubled projects continue to absorb considerable amounts of resources from the organizations that undertake them [44]. For example, in 2002, US\$55 billion dollars were wasted in failed information technology (IT) projects in the US [46]. For that reason, project failure in the IS field may be considered a costly problem that deserves sustained attention.

IT projects fail for any number of reasons. One pattern of failure frequently observed is that projects tend to consume huge amounts of resources due to the escalation of commitment by decision makers [20] before the projects are eventually abandoned. The observation has led to an array of research on the escalation of commitment to IT projects in recent years [29,21], but in the process, the subject of IS project abandonment has been given little attention [11,33].

In the limited project abandonment literature that exists [11,12], many reasons have been put forward to explain IS project abandonment. Among those reasons, organizational issues have been identified as the main causes of project abandonment [33]. In particular, an interesting theme that has emerged is the failure to properly manage multiple project stakeholders who may hold varying or even conflicting views about a project [10,41]. It has also been noted that stakeholders may be closely banded in different coalitions<sup>2</sup> possessing varying levels of influence during project development [35], and when these coalitions are not appropriately managed<sup>3</sup>, the IS project fails.

Since organizations often undertake decisions in response to the interaction of multiple influences from the entire stakeholder set [39], this paper argues that project abandonment decision-making should also require an analysis of the complex interdependent relationships among the stakeholders existing in a project environment. Therefore, we undertake the case study of an abandoned electronic procurement (eprocurement) system project to investigate the coalition dynamics that affect project abandonment decision-making—an issue that has only been remotely addressed in the project abandonment literature. To facilitate data analysis and enhance the theoretical grounding of this research, we propose a tentative framework that combines concepts from the stakeholder literature [13] and the strategic contingency literature [16] to examine the coalition dynamics affecting project abandonment decision-making.

The paper is structured as follows: We will review the past literature on IS project failure and abandonment and on the importance of project stakeholders. We will then discuss the concepts of coalitions and their relative power during project development. Next, we will discuss our research methodology, the research context, and our data extracted from an abandoned e-procurement project. Following that, we will identify and discuss the key issues surrounding coalition dynamics that affect project abandonment decision-making. We conclude with a summary of the findings of this study and a note on its limitations.

## 2. An overview of IS project failure and abandonment

Research on IS project failure has evolved from the study of technical to organizational issues such as uncovering the organizational factors associated with failure [1], organizational structure [41], culture [14], contingency [36], power [7] and context [2].

An interesting trend in the IS failure literature suggests that one pattern of failure—the escalation of commitment to IT projects [20,29]—has attracted vast attention in recent years. The escalation phenomenon may be considered to be influenced by four categories of factors: project, psychological, social and organizational [20]. Decision makers may also decide to continue troubled projects as a result of inaccurate status feedback [21], an inability to receive negative project information, and the refusal to take corrective actions amid unambiguous negative feedback [21]. Most troubled projects tend to be abandoned or redirected after consuming huge amounts of resources and project abandonment has been viewed as a solution to reduce commitment to troubled IT projects.

IS project abandonment can be characterized as a subset of the larger organizational issue of IS project failure [11]. IS project abandonment is defined as <sup>b</sup>the anticipated or perceived failure of the project prior to its full implementation whereas IS project failure is the consequence of failing or dwindling expectations of the implemented system<sup>Q</sup> [11, p. 67]. IS project abandonment can be further divided into three categories, namely, total, substantial and partial abandonment [12].

IS project abandonment is considered both a recurring syndrome of failure that deserves more effective remedies [12], and an exit strategy used for the reduction of commitment to troubled IT projects [21]. Various reasons have been put forward to explain why projects are abandoned. Similar to IS project failure, the causes for IS project abandonment are largely due to organizational, political and humanrelated factors, rather than technical ones [12,33]. This is evident from the findings drawn from the well known Taurus case, which indicate that political influence could cause project failure since changing technologies threaten the interests and status of groups with the power to resist [7]. Furthermore, as IS become increasingly implicated in corporate decisions, building overly ambitious systems as a result of trying to meet demanding business goals could also lead to project abandonment [42]. Looking at another case, the Confirm reservation system project is a good example of project abandonment, showing a project plagued by missed deadlines, slippages and continuous re-planning owing to a combination of organizational factors [32]. All these debacles provide a valuable reminder that human-related issues such as the management of stakeholders during project development may prove crucial to ensuring project success. It is therefore necessary to have a clear understanding of who the stakeholders are, what their roles involve, and how they may affect the project development process [22].

## 2.1. Stakeholders in IS projects

The concept of the stakeholder is popularized through the seminal work of Freeman [13] on strategic management, where he defined a stakeholder to be <sup>b</sup>any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of the organization’s objectives<sup>Q</sup> (p. 46). While individual stakeholders have been the focus in the stakeholder literature, the notion of coalitions has also attracted growing attention from researchers [39]. Pouloudi and Whitley [35] argue against the isolated study of the one-to-one relationship, stating that it is an over-simplistic view of reality. Instead, they suggest that study should focus on a complex network of interactions, interests and power games. As <sup>b</sup>networks are stable but not static<sup>Q</sup> [8, p. 23], coalitions may alter the reactions of other stakeholders and generate frequent changes. Finally, to obtain a deeper understanding of the dynamics of coalitions, Pouloudi and Whitley [35] put forward that one should examine how each stakeholder is linked with (e.g., communicates, exchanges information with, influences or is influenced by) other stakeholders.

Even though the term <sup>d</sup>stakeholder<sup>T</sup> was not commonly used in the context of IS projects till the late 1980s [22,40], the concept of the stakeholder was already implicitly applied to the planning and development of IS projects since the 1970s [27]. To date, there have been several uses of the concept of the stakeholder in the IS literature, which includes studies on stakeholders’ interpretations of a technology change initiative [31], stakeholders’ dependence on context [47] and stakeholder identification techniques [35,23].

The IS project abandonment literature has also acknowledged the contributions of stakeholders in project abandonment [11]. Sauer [41] argues that the success of IS development projects is directly dependent on the proper management of their stakeholders. Interestingly, even though the contribution of individual stakeholders has been extensively discussed in the IS project abandonment literature [10], little of the literature has focused on examining coalition dynamics and how they could influence project abandonment decision-making. This important issue certainly appears overlooked and remains to be addressed.

## 2.3. A theoretical lens for examining how coalition dynamics affect project abandonment decision-making

In view of the importance of coalition dynamics and their influences on project abandonment decisionmaking, it is our intention to help address the insufficiency in the existing IS project abandonment literature through this paper. For insights that could serve our purpose, we look to the concepts in the IS project abandonment, stakeholder and strategic contingency literature. By combining these concepts, we propose a tentative framework for examining how coalition dynamics may affect project abandonment decision-making. The tentative framework is shown in Fig. 1 below.

The framework indicates how coalitions and their relative power can be identified and assessed during project development. The framework suggests that coalitions may influence decision makers to either abandon or continue a project, depending on their relative degrees of power. For example, if a particular coalition resists a system project and is relatively powerful, the decision maker may be influenced to abandon the project. In some situations, the decision maker(s) may be part of a coalition which may also be influenced by other coalition(s). This framework will be further illustrated when we discuss our case findings.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Research strategy

The experiences of previous IS project abandonment studies [11,33] show that it is difficult to obtain data on abandoned IS projects even with the guarantee of anonymity to the subjects. Therefore, we decided to conduct research in an organization where one of the authors was an employee. One advantage for having an <sup>d</sup>insider<sup>T</sup> is familiarity with the culture and informal structures of the case organization. Additionally, informants would find it harder to hide behind formulaic responses when asked to explain their behavior by an <sup>d</sup>insider<sup>T</sup> than if they were queried by an external researcher. This strategy is in accordance with Riemer’s [37] suggestion that rather than neglecting <sup>b</sup>first hand<sup>Q</sup> knowledge or expertise, researchers should turn familiar situations, timely events and special expertise into objects of study. We highlight here that even though the researcher was an <sup>d</sup>insider<sup>T</sup>, he was not involved in the project development process being studied, and had no influence at all over any project decision.

We selected this particular case for investigation because of its magnitude and complexity involving several groups of stakeholders. The complicated interrelationships between the coalitions offered valuable insights into why coalitions play crucial roles in influencing abandonment decision-making. The research employed an interpretative case study approach [47], which was useful for explaining what went on in the case organization during the period being examined. The focus was on a new e-procurement system project called E-PRO, in an organization named ElectroCo (a pseudonym). ElectroCo was a Japanese electronic subsidiary based in Singapore. E-PRO started in January 1999, and was abandoned in December 1999, with an estimated loss of around US\$200,000.

## 3.2. Data collection

Eight months were spent collecting data on the abandoned project at ElectroCo (January–August 2000). Primarily, 28 semi-structured interviews were conducted with 14 interviewees, with each interview lasting 1–1.5 h. Details of the dates of the interviews, the subjects’ job titles, the duration of the interviews, and whether a transcript was produced are shown in Appendix A. These interviews form the main source of our data, but secondary data such as reports, memos and meeting minutes were also gathered to supplement the information collected through interviews.

We established a set of topic guides to help us carry out our interviews. Excerpts of the interview topic guides are shown in Appendix A. Even though interview topic guides were prepared before each interview, the interviewees were allowed to express their views on other aspects they considered important. The material drawn from the literature on project abandonment, stakeholder and strategic contingency guided the design of the interview questions. All interviews were conducted on a one-to-one basis. Most interviews were taped-recorded and transcribed with the interviewees’ permission. We took notes on the few occasions where the interviewee refused permission for the use of a tape recorder. This was unsurprising, considering the extreme sensitivity of the subject [45].

## 3.3. Analysis of data

As a first step in the analysis, transcripts of interviews and meeting minutes were used to create a detailed history of E-PRO in the form of a table showing events that affected the trajectory of the development process and the actions that were taken in response to these events. Knowing the history was useful since it helped unravel the entanglement between phenomenon and context [49]. The next step of the analysis was to identify the coalitions of stakeholders in E-PRO and assess their relative power in affecting project abandonment decision-making. We used the theoretical framework (shown in Fig. 1) to help us identify the potential coalitions and understand their relative power in the project. Ratings for the variables affecting relative coalition power were derived from descriptions of the data obtained about each coalition. The field researcher gave values of high, medium or low to the variables for each coalition independently. The next step in the analysis involved comparing and contrasting the E-PRO case against various possible causes discussed in the project abandonment literature, and identifying which of these possible causes seemed to be present in the case. This also involved the identification of additional causes influenced by the coalition dynamics that were present in E-PRO but had not been widely discussed in the project abandonment literature.

To reduce the researcher bias, the project information and interview transcripts were shown to the coresearcher who was uninvolved in the fieldwork and therefore unfamiliar with the case to <sup>b</sup>bring a different and possibly more objective eye to the evidence<sup>Q</sup> [9, p. 538]. After reviewing the interview transcripts and other secondary documents, the co-researcher developed a separate list of issues that might be relevant to the coalition dynamics that had influenced the project abandonment decision in the case study. The field researcher and the co-researcher then merged their individual lists of issues into a shared list of potential issues. Where an issue was difficult to categorize, both researchers exercised their own judgment to assign the possible causes that appeared to best fit the information presented. The entire process was highly iterative, in recognition that such <sup>b</sup>triangulation<sup>Q</sup> [19] might allow for the validation of each data source and also enrich a researcher’s interpretation of the data [26].

## 4. The case of E-PRO

The study concerned the development of an eprocurement project called E-PRO in a large Japanese subsidiary, ElectroCo, whose purpose was to produce electronic components in Singapore. At the time of the study, the parent company was a Japanese consumerelectronics company whose products included audio and video equipment, television sets, and other electronics equipment. The project was proposed by ElectroCo’s procurement department in 1999. Prior to that, the bulk of procurement activities at the company involved the use of paper-based documents: purchase orders, delivery schedules and accounting statements were sent to suppliers by post.

## 4.1. Antecedent conditions

ElectroCo had adopted a concentrated purchasing strategy that relied on a few but good-quality suppliers in order to enjoy discounts on bulk purchases. It had a set of exceptionally stringent criteria for the selection of suppliers, with 120 items on its supplier assessment checklist and a review process that lasted at least 9 months. Even though ElectroCo considered these suppliers vital business partners who provided the material necessary for production, the suppliers did not value their relationships with ElectroCo reciprocally. As a representative of one of the suppliers described:

Our customers consisted of several major Japanese electronic manufacturers. If we were to rank them in terms of business turnovers, ElectroCo would never make it to our list of top 20 customers.

Before the Asian financial crisis of 1997, ElectroCo enjoyed sound relationships with its suppliers, and when the crisis struck, the suppliers voluntarily reduced the prices of their material considerably to help ElectroCo weather the financial storm. When the crisis was over, the relationship between ElectroCo and its suppliers soured over ElectroCo’s refusal for raw material prices to be restored to the pre-crisis level. One supplier explained his frustration:

We just wanted to make a little profit since many of our items were selling at break-even prices. It was unfair from our point of view: since we voluntarily reduced our prices to help them in 1997, how could they later refuse to allow us to adjust the prices to a more acceptable level?

The disparity between how ElectroCo valued its suppliers and vice-versa, and their disagreement over raw material pricing would appear later as an important issue in the development of E-PRO.

## 4.2. The development of E-PRO

In December 1998, the procurement manager at ElectroCo proposed setting up E-PRO to help his department improve its work productivity. E-PRO would be implemented between the procurement department of ElectroCo and its suppliers. Despite the high risk of adopting a new technological platform, the managing director was slightly concerned but still gave his strong support towards the project since it would mean an improvement in purchasing efficiency within the organization. The IS manager was wary of the new system’s magnitude and complexity, but was instructed by the managing director to assist in the development of the system. The decision to opt for the in-house development of the software over outsourcing was mainly cost-driven. Even though the development was to be undertaken by the IS department, the procurement manager was assigned the role of project leader. The IS manager explained why he was not given the project manager role:

The procurement manager worked in this company longer than I did, and he had also worked for this managing director for many years. The managing director obviously valued him more than me.

One of the IS analysts also commented on the IS manager’s position within the organization:

He [the IS manager] seemed to be overshadowed by the procurement manager in this company. In several projects, people valued the procurement manager’s opinion more than his. As a result, we sometimes felt undervalued.

Despite their manager’s optimism, the procurement executives were conservative and reacted negatively about the change initiative. They were worried about the automation of their daily tasks, which could potentially lead to job cutting. As for the suppliers, they were not informed about the project at that stage. The procurement manager did not identify and investigate the stakeholders’ perceptions of the project. In fact, he seemed rather obsessed with the frenzied rush by other organizations to adopt e-commerce solutions.

The project started in January 1999, with a budgeted amount of US\$200,000 and a target completion period of 1 year. When the project first started, it progressed very slowly due to some resistance from the target system users. One major problem faced by the IS analyst was the difficulty of obtaining information from the users regarding the purchasing procedures needed for prototype building. The IS analyst explained:

The users were uncooperative in providing us details of their purchasing procedures. They gave us conflicting information which, in the end, we had to spend a lot of time comparing with the purchasing manual.

When asked about the purchasing executives’ uncooperative behaviors, the procurement manager admitted that his staff had tried to delay the project. He explained:

I knew exactly what they were trying to do. The problem was quickly resolved when I transferred two of them out of the department and the rest were given warnings over their behaviors. Even if they had all quit, I was sure we could still carry on, with the suppliers delivering the raw material directly to the production departments.

After that, software development progressed smoothly and quickly. The next step was for the procurement manager to involve the suppliers in the new project. It was around June 1999 when the procurement manager made an announcement about the new project at a suppliers’ briefing session. In his speech, he showed little intention of eliciting the suppliers opinions over the implementation of the new system. In fact, he expected little opposition from the suppliers:

I could not see why the suppliers would oppose to such advanced technology which could also benefit them in terms of faster delivery of purchasing orders and payment.

The suppliers were, however, cautious about the news, and very few immediately committed to the new system. One of the suppliers explained why he was unsure about the new system:

Another of our customers had earlier requested that we take part in their e-procurement system and we did. We only found out later that there were lots of hidden costs such as transaction clearing cost, account management cost and sales commissions paid to the third-party service provider. What was worse was that even when we received no purchase orders for a particular month, we still had to pay these miscellaneous charges.

Another supplier explained why he refused to participate in ElectroCo’s E-PRO,

We were already making meager profits due to their refusal to allow us to raise our prices. We could not possibly afford to invest in additional equipment to support the new system.

Even though the suppliers seemed uncommitted to the new system, the procurement manager did not expect their concerns to alter the trajectory of the development process. In fact, he assumed that they would eventually relent to ElectroCo’s request for participation. Therefore, where ElectroCo was concerned, the project could proceed as planned.

Meanwhile, the suppliers were upset over not being informed of the project at the outset. Moreover, the pre-existing deadlock over the raw material price negotiation had already fueled rumors that several major suppliers might discontinue business with ElectroCo, hence contributing further confusion to the situation. Most suppliers also perceived E-PRO as redundant and were keen to stop the project. Several major suppliers called for supplier unity, since they learnt from the earlier price negotiations that dealing with ElectroCo individually would not yield any positive outcome. It transpired later that several suppliers met during an informal dinner to devise plans to counter the proposal. One of the suppliers confirmed the fact of the meeting:

We met over dinner and maintained frequent communication about the issue of E-PRO and how we could effectively influence ElectroCo to abandon the implementation of the system until the deadlock over the pricing negotiation was resolved. It was really pointless for us to continue business with ElectroCo at that price level, let alone investing more to participate in E-PRO.

In October 1999, the suppliers officially informed the procurement manager of their wish for the project to be abandoned. At that time, E-PRO was almost completed and had depleted most of its original budget. The procurement manager rejected their request instantly and demanded their full participation in the new system. The procurement manager was determined not to let the suppliers’ action affect his project. He commented:

We had the right to implement any system that we deemed beneficial to the company. If they wanted to continue doing business with us, they would have to comply with our terms.

The next two months were a period of uncertainty for some of the project team members and the suppliers. News of the suppliers’ opposition had unsettled the project team at ElectroCo. Despite the procurement manager’s optimism, the situation was not as favorable as it seemed. To push for their case, the suppliers informed the IS manager of their position on the new project. The IS manager was sympathetic but could offer little help. However, realizing the seriousness of the matter, the IS manager blew the whistle on the procurement manager, informing the managing director of the suppliers’ opposition to the new project. The IS manager explained why:

To be honest, I could not understand why the managing director had valued the procurement manager so highly over the years. This time, he might have made a mistake by making him the project manager of an IT project.

Throughout the development process, the managing director had not been involved with the project, and was therefore surprised by the suppliers’ opposition. In fact, he had been under the impression that the project development was progressing smoothly. He commented:

The procurement manager had assured me in several conversations that the project development was going very well. I was totally perplexed by the news that the suppliers did not support the new system.

The managing director immediately called a major meeting involving everyone in the project, including the suppliers. According to subsequent reports gathered by the researchers, the discussion at that meeting turned into a heated argument, with the procurement manager yelling:

They were trying to delay our project so as to pressure us over the issue of material price negotiation. I felt we definitely had to carry on with the project.

Even though the managing director was anxious to reach a consensus on the matter, the suppliers refused to compromise. In fact, several major suppliers threatened to stop delivering material immediately if the new system were to go ahead. The IS manager and his analysts refused to be drawn into this conflict and remained silent. The purchasing executives were afraid to go against the procurement manager and opted to support the new system. When interviewed, one of the executives explained their decision:

Two of our colleagues were transferred to another department after voicing their refusal to use the new system. So it was not difficult to understand why the rest of us decided to embrace the new system.

After carefully considering the views of all project stakeholders, the managing director decided to abandon the project. The situation ended with the suppliers <sup>b</sup>winning the confrontation<sup>Q</sup>, as one of the project members described it. In a subsequent interview, the managing director attributed the project cancellation to the procurement manager’s mishandling of the situation. In the managing director’s opinion, after knowing the suppliers’ displeasure over the raw material pricing deadlock, the procurement manager ought to have addressed their doubts and offered them concrete solutions. Even if his efforts had failed, he should have reported the matter to the top management without delay and not kept them in the dark. When asked why he had made the decision to abandon the project, the managing director admitted:

The suppliers seemed determined and united over project abandonment. The material pricing matter had to be resolved before the project could continue. It would be a disaster if they all turned against us. The whole production would have to shut down. However, we might have to start re-examining our procurement strategy to avoid such a scenario from happening again.

## 5. Case findings

Using the criteria and principles suggested in the literature, we identified four possible coalitions in E-PRO: the suppliers, the purchasing executives, the IS department, and the procurement manager–managing director coalition. Our case findings suggest that these coalitions had various levels of influence on the project abandonment decision-making over E-PRO. Ratings for the variables affecting coalition power were derived from the descriptions of the data obtained about each coalition. The variables used were: coping with uncertainty<sup>8</sup>, non-substitutability and centrality [16]. We interpreted the case findings carefully and gave values of high, medium or low to the variables for each coalition. Inter-subject reliability was increased by using the narratives from one subject to confirm or contradict others in a social triangulation [26]. But there was no attempt to privilege one account over another. The researchers judged that there was no overt attempt by subjects to systematically conceal details or to distort their stories.

## 6. Discussion of the coalition dynamics that affected project abandonment decision-making in E-PRO

Given that stakeholders often find themselves in a complex network of interactions, interests and power games [8], it is possible for various coalitions to try to prevent an organization from carrying out its IS project so as to fulfill their own interests. Therefore, this paper argues that understanding the coalition dynamics that are at play during project development becomes important to ensuring project development success. ## 6.1. Antecedent conditions

The antecedent conditions can be treated as the relationships between users and analysts before a project begins. They are essentially the outcomes of a whole history of prior projects, and they usually affect subsequent events. Newman and Robey [28] suggest that to ensure IS project success, it is important to obtain a desirable relationship between project stakeholders by overcoming previous histories of resistance, conflict or even indifference. Furthermore, the psychological literature on selective perception [18] also provides support for this notion by arguing that an antecedent condition is a psychological factor that can influence a manager’s decision frame for identifying, interpreting and acting on project information.

In E-PRO, the antecedent conditions between the procurement manager, the suppliers and the IS department were unfavorable. The suppliers were discontented over the procurement manager’s unwillingness to allow them to raise the prices of their material. At the same time, employees in the IS department were experiencing low self-esteem as they perceived their status in the organization overshadowed by the procurement manager.

It is unsurprising that such poor antecedent conditions could later bear major implications on the decision to abandon E-PRO. Even though the IS department had only medium power relative to other coalitions in the project development process (see Table 4), its manager made a significant contribution by blowing the whistle on the troubled project. This is a critical event in the case since it altered the trajectory of the development process and prevented further commitment of valuable resources to the troubled project [21]. His decision to blow the whistle could be attributed to the unfavorable relationship he had with the procurement manager–managing director coalition prior to the project. He was clearly disappointed at how the managing director thought more highly of the procurement manager than him: <sup>b</sup>I could not understand why the managing director had valued so highly the procurement manager over the years. This time, he might have made a mistake by making him the project manager of an IT project.<sup>Q</sup>

On the other hand, the unresolved issue of the raw material price negotiation prior to the project onset was the main reason why the suppliers had demanded E-PRO to be abandoned. Their case was made even stronger by the fact that they had relatively high power rating as compared to the other coalitions. The antecedent condition clearly played a crucial role in the project abandonment decision in E-PRO as the managing director later admitted: <sup>b</sup>The suppliers seemed determined and united over project abandonment. The material pricing matter had to be resolved before the project could continue.<sup>Q</sup>

## 6.2. Level of coping with uncertainty

Uncertainty may be referred as a lack of information about future events, so that alternative options and their results are unpredictable [17]. Crozier [6, p. 219] proposes that <sup>b</sup>if organizations allocate to their various subunits task areas that vary in uncertainty, then those subunits that cope most effectively with the most uncertainty should have most power within the organization<sup>Q</sup>. Hickson et al. [16] also suggest that it is important for organizations to have the ability to cope with uncertainties for adequate task performance as they often deal with environmentally derived uncertainties in the sources and composition of inputs, with uncertainties in the processing of throughputs, and again with environmental uncertainties in the disposal of outputs.

In E-PRO, the ability to cope with uncertainties varied among the coalitions. The data suggest that the suppliers and the IS department had the highest level of coping with uncertainties whereas the purchasing executives had the lowest. For example, the suppliers’ ability in coping with uncertainties could be interpreted from their swift actions in reducing their prices to help ElectroCo to weather the Asian financial crisis in 1997. The experience improved the coalition’s interconnectedness, which is commonly described as the characteristic of a network that measures the relative number of ties within the network that link actors together [39]. This is important as Oliver [30] suggests that the interconnectedness within coalitions may influence their degree of resistance to institutional pressures. The purchasing executives, however, were conservative, rigid and behaved negatively towards the change initiative.

It is interesting to note that coalitions’ various levels of coping with uncertainty had major implications on the organization’s decision to abandon E-PRO. For example, the suppliers showed tremendous agility and adaptability dealing with the new technological initiative, i.e. E-PRO as they reached unanimous consensus on terminating the new development project within a short period of time. Furthermore, they showed great courage as they were unafraid to bring the matter to the managing director despite facing immense pressure from the purchasing manager. Overall, the suppliers’ high level of coping with uncertainty helped them to achieve their objective— stop the project development. On the other hand, the purchasing executives were poor in coping with uncertainty as they were na<sup>R</sup>ve in believing that negative behaviors such as the lack of cooperation towards the IS executives and the head-on collision with the purchasing manager would influence the organization to stop the development project.

To conclude, our case findings support the argument of Hickson et al. [16] that the more a coalition copes with uncertainty, the greater its power within the organization. As in our case, it would be that the ability of the coalitions to constrain the organization’s initiatives also increases. In other words, the strength for coordinating pressure on the organization grows in accordance to the rise in the coalitions’ level of coping with uncertainty.

## 6.3. Level of substitutability

Pfeffer [34] suggests that power is reflected in the ability to overcome resistance in achieving a desired objective or result. Dispositional power addresses sources of power and discusses the basis of that which gives power to a social actor or group [5]. Hickson et al. [16] suggest that the level of substitutability, which is the extent and the ease with which activities can be performed by other coalitions, could be used to indicate the degree of the power of a coalition.

In the case of E-PRO, we rated the suppliers high on non-substitutability compared to the other coalitions (Table 4), based on the fact that ElectroCo had adopted a concentrated purchasing strategy which relied on a few but good-quality suppliers. Furthermore, the high rating was also supported by the disproportionate relationship between ElectroCo and its suppliers. While ElectroCo viewed the suppliers as vital business partners for providing the raw material necessary for its production, the suppliers did not value their relationships with ElectroCo reciprocally. This was due to their wide customer base which consisted of several Japanese electronic manufacturers whom they had considerable business dealings with. This disproportionate relationship naturally increased the suppliers’ bargaining power when dealing with ElectroCo [4], and this explains why the suppliers were so influential in the abandonment decision making in E-PRO.

## 6.4. Level of centrality

The centrality of a coalition’s activities is related to the immediacy and pervasiveness of the group’s workflows [4]. Immediacy refers to the speed with which a coalition’s workflow affects the output of the organization. Pervasiveness is the degree to which a coalition’s workflows are linked to the workflows of other coalitions. Rowley [39] argues that centrality is a coalition’s position within the context relative to others, and it refers to the power obtained through the coalition structure, as opposed to the power gained through individual attributes.

In E-PRO, the suppliers’ centrality was high relative to other coalitions, which directly contributed to their high degree of power. Particularly, immediacy was a major determinant as the managing director admitted in the end: <sup>b</sup>It would be a disaster if they all turned against us. The whole production would have to shut down.<sup>Q</sup> The impact of the loss of the suppliers would be far greater than the departure of any other coalitions. This is because without the raw material supply, production would have to stop and this could jeopardize ElectroCo’s main source of revenue. Our finding suggests that as a coalition’s relative centrality increases, its ability to resist other coalitions’ pressure also increases.

Overall, a highly dense coalition with a relatively high centrality is capable of forming a strong, unified force against the organization, just like the suppliers coalition in E-PRO did. Such confrontation might create an unstable and uncertain project environment that could pose a challenge to decision makers since the resource dependence theory suggests that <sup>b</sup>organizational decision makers have a strong preference for certainty, stability and predictability<sup>Q</sup> [30 p. 170]. Furthermore, when faced with such a densely connected coalition, the organization tends to become a compromiser, attempting to balance, pacify, and bargain with [30] its influential stakeholders. This also explains why ElectroCo eventually abandoned E-PRO in their attempt to pacify the suppliers.

## 7. Implications for research and practice

The findings from our analysis of the coalition dynamics affecting project abandonment decisionmaking carry important implications for both researchers and practitioners. For researchers, this study is significant in that it is one of the very first case studies to investigate the influence of coalition dynamics on project abandonment decision-making. While there are several previous studies involving stakeholder analysis in project failures, these studies tend to focus on the isolated study of one-to-one stakeholder relationship rather than the relationship between coalitions, which are considered a more realistic view of organizations [40]. The issues raised in this paper represent opportunities for integrative empirical research in the areas of stakeholder theory, strategic contingency theory and IS project management. The framework (Fig. 1) proposed in this study aims to highlight the impact of coalition dynamics in project abandonment decision-making. Further work is needed to operationalize the framework in a methodological framework for use in other IS contexts. The findings in this paper are applicable, and should be testable across different organizations and project settings.

Further investigation of coalition dynamics is required to examine the phenomenon in greater depth and explore other manifestations beyond the few possible issues that have been found to influence project abandonment decision-making in this study. Longitudinal field studies that involve multiple case studies are clearly called for so that the diversity of coalition dynamics may be accounted for. Finally, more research should also be conducted to identify other variables affecting coalition power as compared to the ones proposed by Hickson et al. [16].

For practitioners, our contribution consists of articulating a theoretical framework to make sense of the identification of coalitions and their relative power in the setting of project development. This paper illustrates the explanatory power of the framework by providing an empirical account of how coalition dynamics could contribute to a project abandonment decision. This study highlights the importance of understanding the interests of those promoting particular objectives of transformation and the interests of those opposing them [38]. Importantly, practitioners should assess the political feasibility of a project, and evaluate the possible coalitions before embarking on the project. They may draw on our proposed framework to determine the possible formation of coalitions and the sources of the coalitions’ relative power, so as to formulate responsive strategies to prevent any coalitions from getting overly powerful. Such measures, if put in place, should enhance more positive coalition relationships during project development. Furthermore, organizations often encapsulate knowledge into methodologies and their supporting tools such as the decision support systems, and overlook the importance of reviews and revisions. This is important as <sup>b</sup>for all the great accomplishments of IT, the process of application development continues to be plagued by ineffective practices<sup>Q</sup> [24, p. 100].

## 8. Conclusions and limitations

## 8.1. Conclusions

As there are very few empirical studies on IS project abandonment, this study represents a contribution to filling the knowledge gap in the area. It complements the existing literature by offering a new perspective to examining the abandonment phenomenon. In this paper, by drawing on a case study of the short-lived E-PRO, we have demonstrated the use of a theoretical framework to make sense of how coalition dynamics could influence project abandonment decision-making. Through interviews with relevant stakeholders and the use of secondary documents, we have gathered qualitative data to offer an understanding of the sources of coalitions relative power and the actions taken during project development which can change the trajectory of the development process. Finally, in the current climate of technology-driven organizational transformation, there is a strong desire to be aware of forces within the organization. Identifying who they are and examining the distribution of their relative power could help both project managers and decision makers during the IS development and implementation process.

## 8.2. Limitations

While the case study approach adopted here has several strengths, there are three limitations that should be noted. The first limitation concerns the generalizability of a single case study. Herriott and Firestone [15] suggest the preference of a multiple case-study design over a single case-study design for obtaining more compelling and robust data. However, in our view, project abandonment is a sensitive subject, and it may be difficult to obtain the opportunity for a multiple case-study design. Therefore, we posit that <sup>b</sup>one must follow a more opportunistic approach even if that means settling for a single case study<sup>Q</sup> [20, p. 447]. Besides, a single case is useful in theory building [9].

In our attempt to motivate the interviewees to speak openly about their experiences in the project, we tried to reduce their suspicion and uneasiness by explaining our research objectives and the significance of our study clearly, which was to help improve future project management performances within the company. In addition, we conducted all our interviews on a one-to-one basis and assured them that all interview conversations would be kept confidential, even from their colleagues.

The second limitation of this study is that to conduct a study of such sensitive nature, the researcher has to develop a strong rapport and trusted relationship with the interviewees. We acknowledge the limitation that the informal contacts the researcher had with the interviewees could affect how they viewed their own affairs and how they presented their data to the researcher. In our attempt to overcome this limitation, we explained our research objectives and the significance of our study clearly, which was to help improve future project management performances within the organization. In addition, we conducted our interviews on a one-to-one basis and assured the interviewees that all interview conversations would be kept confidential.

The third limitation involves the generalizability of the findings, given the somewhat unusual relationship between the company and its suppliers in this particular study and the use of strategic contingencies theory in a fluid and complex environment. In most organizations, the customer is usually more powerful than the supplier because of the availability of alternative sources of supply. The nature of the typical power relationship between the organization and its suppliers may differ only where there is a single or very limited source of supply, such as the case in this study. Therefore, generalizing the findings in this study to organizations with a different customer–supplier power relationship may not be feasible. Furthermore, the strategic contingencies theory base may be useful but a limited account of power in fluid, complex environments as it sees power as essentially structural rather than interactive and processual. Despite the limitations, we are convinced that this study remains relevant as project abandonment is a common and costly problem among IS development projects [11], and there can be no question about the importance of a deeper understanding of its nature and avoidance [24].
