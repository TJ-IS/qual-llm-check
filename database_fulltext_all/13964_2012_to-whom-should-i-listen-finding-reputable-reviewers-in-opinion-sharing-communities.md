# To whom should I listen? Finding reputable reviewers in opinion-sharing communities

Yi-Cheng Ku <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Chih-Ping Wei <sup>b</sup>, Han-Wei Hsiao <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Computer Science and Information Management, Providence University, Taichung, Taiwan, ROC

<sup>b</sup> Department of Information Management, National Taiwan University, Taipei, Taiwan, ROC

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information Management, National University of Kaohsiung, Kaohsiung, Taiwan, ROC

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 9 October 2010 Received in revised form 13 December 2011 Accepted 13 March 2012 Available online 23 March 2012

Keywords: Reputation estimation Review quality Opinion forum Web trust network Electronic word of mouth

## a b s t r a c t

Online opinion-sharing communities, which allow members to express personal opinions and preferences about speci<sup>fi</sup>c products, provide important channels for consumers to learn about product quality and support their purchase decision process. Firms can use these reviews to understand customers' responses to their products and improve their products accordingly. Furthermore, opinion-sharing communities provide an alternative, effective marketing channel to <sup>fi</sup>rms by offering electronic word of mouth (eWOM). However, due to the openness and anonymity of opinion-sharing communities, their members face a challenging issue, that is, whether to believe or disbelieve information provided by other members. This study attempts to discriminate members (i.e., reviewers) with a high reputation from those with a low reputation on the basis of members' web trust network and review behaviors in an opinion-sharing community. We collected sample data pertaining to four product categories from Epinions.com to test our research model. The results indicate that four variables (trust intensity, average trust intensity of trustors, degree of review focus in the target category, and average product rating in the target category) successfully discriminate reviewers into the two groups, and product type is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant control variable. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings not only help <sup>fi</sup>rms identify reputable reviewers for marketing campaign purposes but also enable the members of an opinion-sharing community to determine who reputable reviewers are and whose reviews they should trust.

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid proliferation of e-commerce and Web 2.0 innovations, the Web has become an excellent platform for gathering and sharing consumers’ personal views on, preferences for, and experiences with products [8,11,13,16,44]. Many third-party product review websites (e.g., Epinions.com,<sup>1</sup> rateitall.com, Cnet.com, Edutainingkids.com, ZDNET.com) have established opinion-sharing communities to facilitate the exchange of consumer reviews about a variety of products. It also has become a common practice for retailers (e.g., amazon.com, target.com, bestbuy.com, tripadvisor.com, Shopping.com, Walmart.com) and product manufacturers (e.g., Hewlett-Packard, Nike, Levi's) to create their own opinion-sharing communities, where customers can express their opinions about products they have purchased or in which they are interested.

Consumer reviews are essential to <sup>fi</sup>rms (both retailers and product manufacturers) in their efforts to understand the general responses of customers to their products and improve their products accordingly [44]. In addition, opinion-sharing communities provide an alternative, more effective marketing channel to <sup>fi</sup>rms, in the form of electronic word of mouth (eWOM), that does not require huge advertising investments [18,42]. Accordingly, prior studies indicate that consumer reviews can increase sales [5,6,13,15,27], and the presence of customer reviews on a company's website can improve customer perceptions of the website's usefulness and social presence [26], attract customer visits, increase the time spent on the site (i.e., stickiness), and create a sense of community among frequent shoppers [32].

From a customer perspective, consumer reviewers offer a key information source about product quality [6,10,19] and thus can facilitate purchase decision processes [32]. However, as the number of reviews in an opinion-sharing community increases, it becomes more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for a member to search or browse through the many reviews contributed by other members to obtain suf<sup>fi</sup>cient and useful information. The search process can be not only time consuming but also frustrating, because the quality of consumer reviews varies greatly. Some reviewers appear professional and devoted to the task, but others are subjective and biased. An even more serious concern arises from the openness and pseudo-anonymity of opinion-sharing communities, which confronts members with a challenging issue: should they believe or disbelieve the information provided by other members in the opinion-sharing communities [49]? Consumer reviews can be manipulated easily by interested parties [9]. For example, <sup>fi</sup>rms might anonymously post positive reviews of their own products to increase customers’ awareness and perceptions of those products.

One solution to this challenge is a reputation mechanism that reveals the reputation of reviewers who participate in an opinionsharing community [8,39]. With such a mechanism, members in the opinion-sharing community can attend to the reviews contributed by more reputable reviewers and avoid those written by less reputable reviewers. The <sup>fi</sup>rms also can promote new products to reputable reviewers, with the hope they will like the products and write favorable reviews. Many third-party product review websites (e.g., epinions.com, rateitall.com, ZDNET.com) and opinion-sharing communities hosted by retailers or manufacturers (e.g., Amazon.com, Walmart.com, Nike, Levi's) attempt a peer-evaluation-based approach (i.e., explicit reputation system) that enables members to evaluate and rate the reviews of other members. The site then can aggregate these evaluations (e.g., taking their average) as a measure of the reputation of a reviewer. While this practice appears feasible and involves straightforward computations for reputation estimation, whether to calculate reputation scores for members in the community or classify members into different reputation categories, this user-driven approach also suffers some limitations.

First, for comprehensive coverage, this approach requires at least one user evaluation on some reviews of each reviewer. If none of the reviews written by a reviewer ever gets evaluated by other members, his or her reputation cannot be derived. However, members may not bother to provide feedback (i.e., user evaluation) at all, thus creating a sparsity problem [39]. In addition, similar to other online behaviors, user evaluations tend to be skewed. That is, contributions by some reviewers receive hundreds of user evaluations, whereas others attract no user evaluations. Such sparsity and skewness can result in low coverage and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly limit the applicability of the user-driven approach to reputation estimation. Second, the userdriven approach is vulnerable to malicious and in<sup>fl</sup>ated evaluation behaviors [12,39]. For example, a member may intentionally give unfavorable evaluations to some members, regardless of the quality of their reviews. Or a member could attempt to in<sup>fl</sup>ate his or her own reputation dishonorably, for instance, by creating “fake” members and hiding behind these pseudonyms to create more positive user evaluations to his or her reviews. In both cases, the userdriven approach is ineffective for accurate reputation estimation of the members of an opinion-sharing community.

Motivated by the importance of reputation estimation and the limitations of the user-driven approach, this study develops a reputation estimation model to measure the reputation of a member in an opinion-sharing community. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our proposed reputation estimation model is based on factors derived from members’ web trust network and review behaviors in an opinion-sharing community. Because we do not rely on information pertaining to user evaluations, our reputation estimation model will not suffer from the limitations inherent to the user-driven approach (i.e., low coverage and vulnerability to malicious and in<sup>fl</sup>ated evaluation behaviors). To present this solution, we organize the remainder of our paper as follows: Section 2 reviews literature relevant to this study. We propose our research hypotheses in Section 3. In Section 4, we describe the research methodology and discuss important analysis results. Finally, we conclude with a summary and discussion of some future research directions in Section 5.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Concepts related to reputation

Reputation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the relative estimation or esteem in which a person or thing is held” [41] or “what is generally said or believed about a person's or thing's character or standing” [21]. In e-commerce environments, reputation refers to the shadow of the future, which supports each transaction made by buyers online. That is, it creates expectations among other users who search for sellers. Users tend to look back on what sellers have done and how they have done it [39,45,47]. Accordingly, we can infer that, in opinion-sharing environments, reputation creates expectations among other members who search for reviewers. The reputation of a reviewer can be determined by how other members evaluate the reviews written by this reviewer.

In an opinion-sharing community, two main concepts relate to the reputation of a member. First, the reputation of a member is associated with trustworthiness, because reputation provides a collective measure of trustworthiness (in the sense of reliability) from the perspective of all users in the pertinent environment [19]. A member in an opinion-sharing community is more reputable if his or her past contributions to the community are perceived by other members as positive and valuable. They then are willing to develop trust relationships with this focal member. That is, a reputable member in an opinion-sharing community is generally trustworthy. Accordingly, the trustworthiness of a member should be positively associated with his or her reputation.

Although the trustworthiness of a member is a subjective belief, collectively formed by all other members in an opinion-sharing community, it is likely re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the trust network that surrounds the focal member. Trust is “the subjective probability by which an individual, A, expects that another individual, B, performs a given action on which its welfare depends” [21]. Diverse motivations might prompt a member to “trust” someone [25], but the development of trust relationships in an opinion-sharing community is likely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced predominantly by the reviews and preferences of trusted members. For example, Epinions.com supports the “web of trust” (or web trust network) mechanism, which it de<sup>fi</sup>nes as “a network of reviewers whose reviews and ratings you have consistently found to be valuable,” suggesting that “If you were trying to decide whether or not to buy a specific product and the member in question had reviewed it, would you want that review to be among the first you saw? If so, you have a good reason to add that member to your Web of Trust.”<sup>2</sup> Thus, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant function of the web trust network is to help members distinguish valuable information sources from less valuable ones. In other words, it is likely that member A trusts member B because A considers that the reviews written by B are consistently valuable. As a result, there exists a strong and positive correlation between trust and preference similarity [48], because the preference of a member is likely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the reviews and preferences of the members that he or she trusts. When more members develop trust relationships with member B, he or she should be more trustworthy and, hence, more reputable.

The second important concept related to the reputation of a member in an opinion-sharing community is the member's expertise with regard to the issues the member comments on or the knowledge he or she shares in the community [3]. A member's opinions are more likely to be adopted by other members when they perceive those opinions as useful and the information source as credible and reputable [23,38]. Writing reviews is not only about writing but also about sharing knowledge with other members [7]. Therefore, members reading reviews judge, whether objectively or subjectively, the quality of the reviews and simultaneously assess the expertise level of the contributing members. Accordingly, readers form perceptions of the credibility of contributing members in the opinion-sharing community. If a contributor's expertise appears unacceptable to readers, he or she will seem less credible and, hence, less reputable. However, if the assessed expertise level appears adequate, the contributing member is deemed more reputable. Therefore, the expertise level of a contributing member should positively relate to his or her reputation in an opinion-sharing community. The expertise level of a member is likely manifested in his or her review behavior. For example, a member with a higher expertise level in a certain domain probably is a more active contributor to this domain. As a result, the number of reviews in the target domain by the focal member should be greater, and his or her reviews should concentrate more on the target domain than on other domains.

## 2.2. Reputation estimation

Few prior studies concentrate on reputation estimation, though a related area involves expert or in<sup>fl</sup>uential user identi<sup>fi</sup>cation [43]. Expert identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, typically within the context of an organization, involves <sup>fi</sup>nding experts in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c area or subject [30,46]. Several prior studies exploit the documents authored by employees in an organization to <sup>fi</sup>nd such experts. However, these studies mainly focus on expertise, rather than reputation, which is broader than expertise. In addition, existing expert identi<sup>fi</sup>cation research cannot apply directly to our study on reputation estimation, because the activities and behaviors commonly observed in an opinion-sharing community (e.g., review, rating, trust) are not available or dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to implement in knowledge-sharing practice in the organizational settings on which existing expert identi<sup>fi</sup>cation studies are based.

Some prior research has been devoted to assessing reviewers' quality. For example, Riggs and Wilensky [40] develop an algorithm for automatically determining the quality of reviewers, which can be incorporated into a metric of merit for the item under review. Their basic idea is that good reviewers are those whose ratings predict the ultimate consensus ratings of items, where the consensus rating of an item is the average of ratings given, weighted by the quality of the reviewers. Their study considers three additional factors to adjust for the quality of a reviewer: number of items reviewed, number of reviews of an item, and time of review.

Our study differs from their approach [40] in two respects. First, they focus on the quality of reviewers, which is different from reviewers’ reputation (the focus of our study). Second, the three factors that Riggs and Wilensky [40] consider pertain to review behavior only. As we discussed previously, the trustworthiness and expertise level of a member should positively relate to his or her reputation. Therefore, our study considers both variables pertaining to members' review behaviors in an opinion-sharing community and variables derived from their web trust network to predict our dependent variable — the reputation of reviewers in an opinion-sharing community.

## 3. Research hypotheses

We assume in this study that an opinion-sharing community allows members to (1) write reviews about a variety of products across different product categories, (2) provide product ratings on an ordinal or numeric scale for a variety of products across different product categories, and (3) assert members they trust (forming a web trust network). Information about these activities or behaviors becomes the basis for our reputation estimation. Our assumption about allowable activities in an opinion-sharing community is reasonable, rather than an exception, because many third-party product review websites (e.g., Epinions.com, FilmTrust<sup>3</sup>) allow such activities in their opinion-sharing communities. We draw on two dimensions to de<sup>fi</sup>ne factors pertaining to web trust network and review behavior to explore the independent variables.

## 3.1. Factors pertaining to web trust network

We <sup>fi</sup>rst de<sup>fi</sup>ne a trustor and a trustee in a trust relationship. If member A trusts member B (or B is trusted by A), A is the trustor and B is the trustee in this trust relationship. The trust intensity of a focal member therefore is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the number of members who trust that focal member (i.e., in-degree or centrality of the focal member). As we illustrate in Fig. 1, because members $u _ { 7 } , u _ { 8 } , u _ { 9 } ,$ and $u _ { 1 0 }$ trust the focal member, the trust intensity of the focal member is 4.

Social network research suggests that the structure of a social network positively relates to the reputation of members participating in the network. For example, Mehra et al. [31] investigate how a group leader's centrality in his or her external and internal social networks relates to the leader's personal reputation for leadership. On the basis of data from the sales division of a <sup>fi</sup>nancial services <sup>fi</sup>rm, their empirical results reveal that leaders’ centrality in their social networks is related positively to their reputation for leadership among different organizational constituencies. Because a web trust network is a special type of social network, we infer that members’ centrality (i.e., trust intensity) in the web trust network relates positively to their reputation. In other words, if the trust intensity of the focal member is high, he or she should be a more trustworthy reviewer. In this case, the reputation of the focal member also should be higher. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1. The trust intensity of a member relates positively to the reputation of the member.

The second factor related to web trust network is the average trust intensity of trustors of the focal member. This factor measures the average trustworthiness of all the trustors of the focal member (i.e., average trust intensity of members who trust the focal member). For example, as Fig. 1 illustrates, the set of trustors of the focal member consists of $\{ u _ { 7 } , u _ { 8 } , u _ { 9 } , u _ { 1 0 } \}$ . The trust intensity of member $u _ { 7 }$ is 2, because $u _ { 7 }$ is trusted by members $u _ { 1 }$ and $u _ { 2 } .$ . Likewise, the trust intensity of member $u _ { 8 }$ is 2 (members u and $u _ { 3 } )$ , that of member $u _ { 9 }$ is 1, and that of member $u _ { 1 0 }$ is 3. Thus, the average trust intensity of the trustors of the focal member is $( 2 + 2 + 1 + 3 ) / 4 = 2$ . Evidently, if the average trust intensity of the trustors of a member is greater, the focal member is trusted by more trustworthy members. Prior research empirically shows that if a member is trusted by more trustworthy members, the focal member tends to be more trustworthy [24] and thus should be more reputable. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2. The average trust intensity of the trustors of a member relates positively to the reputation of the member.

## 3.2. Factors related to review behavior

Prior research suggests that for assessing the quality of reviews contributed by members in an e-commerce site, their review behaviors offer substantial clues [20]. The quality of reviews implies the expertise level of the contributing members, which positively relates to their reputation. Therefore, the review behaviors of members in an opinion-sharing community likely are associated with their reputation. Accordingly, we also include factors pertaining to the review behaviors of members to estimate their reputation. A product review focuses on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c product category, and the effect of electronic word of mouth (eWOM) depends on product type [36]. Therefore, we consider review behavior to be product category-dependent. In other words, for a target product category, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the factors related to reviewers’ review behaviors in that product category. We posit that the number of reviews in the target category, degree of review focus in the target category, and average product rating in the target category each relates positively to reviewers’ reputation in the target product category.

The number of reviews in the target product category denotes the number of reviews written by the focal member in this category. More reviews of the products in this target category by a focal member imply that this member is a more active reviewer of the category. Prior research also indicates that the number of reviews contributed by a focal member positively correlates with the helpfulness of his or her reviews [28]. Thus, the likelihood that this focal member is a reputable reviewer of the target category should be higher, and we propose:

H3. The number of reviews in the target category by a member relates positively to the reputation of that member.

Because a member can write reviews on a variety of products across different product categories, the degree of review focus in the target category implies his or her interest and expertise. When a member has a greater expertise in the target category, he or she often writes more reviews pertaining to this category [20]. For the focal member, we therefore measure the degree of review focus in the target category as the number of his or her reviews in this category, divided by the total number of reviews written by him or her. We hence propose the following hypothesis:

H4. The degree of review focus in the target category by a member relates positively to the reputation of that member.

In addition to writing reviews, a member might provide product ratings on an ordinal or numeric scale for products in different product categories. According to Alba and Hutchinson [1], consumers with different expertise levels have different information-processing capabilities in inferring bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from product attribute information. As a result, professional reviewers emphasize the performance of a technical product from the perspective of technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, while general (less sophisticated) reviewers evaluate a technical product based on their personal usage situations [4]. When evaluating digital cameras for example, professional reviewers often give lower ratings than general reviewers do [4]. Because a more reputable reviewer in a particular target category generally has a greater level of expertise and knowledge in the target product category, the average product rating in the target category given by a more reputable member is expected to be lower than that granted by a less reputable member. We therefore propose:

H5. The average product rating in the target category given by a member relates negatively to the reputation of that member.

We further consider members' reputation product type-dependent, because product type is a moderating factor of the effect of review extremity and depth on the helpfulness of a review [32]. Mudambi and Schuff [32] also show that reviews of experience goods – de<sup>fi</sup>ned as goods for “which it is relatively difficult and costly to obtain information on product quality prior to interaction with the product” [32] – with extreme ratings are less helpful than reviews with moderate ratings. In addition, compared with its in<sup>fl</sup>uence for experience goods, review depth has a greater positive effect on the helpfulness of the review for search goods [32], where search goods are products with features and characteristics that are easy to evaluate prior to purchase [34]. Therefore, we employ product type (experience vs. search goods) as a control variable.

## 4. Methodology and analysis results

## 4.1. Data collection

Park et al. [37] <sup>fi</sup>nd that third-party-hosted word of mouth is an important source of information for online retail customers and can have a greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence than retailer-hosted WOM in particular contexts. Thus, an understanding of how to <sup>fi</sup>nd reputable reviewers in third-party-hosted opinion-sharing communities should be more important to retailers and consumers. In this vein, for our empirical data collection, we target a popular third-party product review website. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our data set contains reviewers collected from Epinions.com, which we use to test our <sup>fi</sup>ve hypotheses. Because our study focuses on reputation estimation in a product category, we must de<sup>fi</sup>ne the members of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c product category. In this study, we focus on four product categories (i.e., cars & motorsports, restaurants & gourmet, computers, and home & garden) and consider a member a reviewer of a given product category if he or she has submitted at least <sup>fi</sup>ve reviews in that category. As a result, we collect information about 1,044 members across the four product categories. Furthermore, on the basis of prior studies [14,32–34], we classify the <sup>fi</sup>rst two product categories (i.e. cars & motorsports and restaurants & gourmet) as experience goods and the remaining two product categories (i.e., computers and home & garden) as search goods.

We also need to determine the reputation score for each member in a product category. On the Epinions.com website, any member can express evaluations of the quality (i.e., helpfulness) of product reviews contributed by other members. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, user evaluations indicate the review is “Very Helpful,” “Helpful,” “Somewhat Helpful,” and “Not Helpful.” These user-evaluated helpfulness levels are similar to a Likert scale. Thus, it is appropriate to convert the helpfulness levels into interval-scaled scores, because it has become a common practice to assume that Likert-type levels constitute interval-level measurement [2]. Accordingly, we convert the four helpfulness levels into a numerical scale,<sup>4</sup> as follows: “Very Helpful” =3, “Helpful” =2, “Somewhat Helpful”=1, and “Not Helpful”=–2. We then take the macro-averaged helpfulness score provided by other members for the reviews written by the target member in the product category. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we calculate the average helpfulness score of each review in the target product category written by the focal member, then take the average across all reviews by that focal member in the same product category. The resultant average helpfulness score represents the reputation score of the focal member in the target product category.

We understand that some user evaluations in an opinion-sharing community may not be reliable (due to malicious and in<sup>fl</sup>ated evaluation behaviors). In addition, we acknowledge that treating ordinal scales as interval scales may be controversial [22]. To lessen these undesired effects, we choose not to use the average helpfulness score of each member in our data set as the dependent variable. Rather, we attempt to differentiate two groups of reviewers (i.e., high reputation and low reputation) as our dependent variable. To convert the average helpfulness score into groupings, we sort the average helpfulness scores of the 1044 members in our data set in the descending order and use a polar extremes approach [17] to classify reviewers into the high-reputation or low-reputation group. Thus, reviewers with a high reputation are those whose average helpfulness scores are above the 60th percentile (421 members in our data set), and those with a low reputation earn average helpfulness scores below the 40th percentile (416 members). The middle segment of 207 members is excluded intentionally, which also helps lessen the effects of possibly unreliable user evaluations and probable scale transformation errors on the quality of our data set. The resulting sample size of our data set is 837, including 421 high-reputation observations and 416 low-reputation observations. Furthermore, to obtain the split-sample validation, we divided the sample (n=837) randomly by SPSS statistic software into two subsamples. One subsample, the analysis sample (n=582), which approximates 70% of the original sample, is used to develop the logistic regression model. The second subsample, the holdout sample (n=255), is employed to test the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy [17]. Table 1 contains the statistical summary of the data set used in our analysis.

Prior research suggests that reciprocity likely affects the formation of trust relationships in an online community [29,35,48]. That is, if member A trusts B, it is likely that B will trust A subsequently. If establishing reciprocal trust relationships is a common behavior across all members in the opinion-sharing community from which we gathered our data set (i.e., Epinions.com), such trust-reciprocity could bias our data set and the test of our research model. To ease this concern, we examine the degree of trust-reciprocity between the members of the two reputation groups (i.e., high- and lowreputation, abbreviated HR and LR) in our data set. As Table 2 illustrates, among all initial trust relationships initiated by members in the high-reputation (HR) group to those in the same group (i.e., HR → HR), 65.38% receive reciprocal trust relationships. For initial trust relationships that indicate HR→ LR and LR →LR, the degree of trust-reciprocity is lower (i.e., 50%). For initial trust relationships pertaining to LR → HR, the degree of trust-reciprocity is even lower (i.e., only 16.13%). That is, members in the high-reputation group generally do not develop reciprocal trust relationships when they originate from members in the low-reputation group. Our analysis suggests members in different reputation groups exhibit different trustreciprocity behaviors. Because trust-reciprocity behavior is an intentional act, depending on the reputations of both members who initiate trust relationships and members who receive initial trust relationships, it likely does not represent a major concern in our data set.

## 4.2. Logistic regression analysis

Using a binary dependent variable, <sup>fi</sup>ve independent variables, and one control variable (coded as a dummy variable to measure the effect of product type), we adopt logistic regression to discriminate reviewers with a high reputation from those with a low reputation. Our choice of logistic regression, rather than linear or nonlinear multiple regression, is explained as follows. Logistic regression does not involve some key assumptions of linear regression or nonlinear multiple regression models. For example, it does not require a linear relationship between dependent and independent variables. In addition, independent variables need not be multivariate normal, and the homoscedasticity assumption is not required. Furthermore, some of our variables (e.g., trust intensity) do not follow the normal distribution, and our dependent variable was converted from the ordinal scale. ## 5. Conclusion

This study focuses on reputation estimations of reviewers in opinion-sharing communities based on factors derived from members' web trust network and their review behaviors. The sample data for this study re<sup>fl</sup>ect four product categories from an opinion-sharing community, Epinions.com. Our proposed reputation estimation model can supplement or complement the user-driven approach (i.e., explicit reputation system). For those opinion-sharing communities already implemented explicit reputation systems, our proposed reputation esti mation model can collaborate with the user-driven approach to achieve more effective reputation estimation with wider coverage. For example, an opinion-sharing community can employ its explicit reputation system to measure the reputation of members whose reviews have been extensively evaluated by other members and adopt our reputation estimation model for those members whose reviews never or seldom get evaluated by other members. Alternatively, for each member, the opinion-sharing community could cross-check the reputation obtained with the user-driven approach against that estimated by our reputation estimation model to identify members possibly attacked by malicious evaluation behaviors or engaged in in<sup>fl</sup>ated evaluation behaviors.

## 5.1. Managerial implications

The effective identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of reviewers with a high reputation in an opinion-sharing community can help members attend better to the reviews contributed by more reputable reviewers and avoid those written by less reputable reviewers. Hence, opinion-sharing communities hosted by third-party product review websites, retailers, or manufacturers can employ our proposed model to identify reviewers with a high reputation and then explicitly recognize their reputation (e.g., by highlighting them as “reputable reviewer”). Such reputation recognition mechanisms not only can help members in an opinionsharing community reduce their efforts to evaluate the reputations of other members but also motivate reputable reviewers to contribute more in the opinion-sharing community. It is worth noting that our proposed model and the aforementioned reputation recognition mechanism needs to be re-executed periodically, because members’ web trust network and their review behaviors in an opinion-sharing community evolves dynamically.

In addition, because the success of an online community depends on whether the community has suf<sup>fi</sup>cient number of reputable members, <sup>fi</sup>rms that host opinion-sharing communities should develop effective strategies to manage relationships with reviewers with a high reputation and provide incentives to retain them. Moreover, our proposed reputation estimation model can help <sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fi</sup>nd these reputable reviewers in opinion-sharing communities for marketing campaign purposes. For example, manufacturers and retailers might actively provide trial products to reviewers with a high reputation. When these reviewers have favorable evaluations of trial products, their reviews likely will in<sup>fl</sup>uence other members’ attitudes toward the products and thus increase sales. Meanwhile, <sup>fi</sup>rms should pay special attention to negative opinions from these reviewers and improve their products or services accordingly. Without taking appropriate actions in response to these opinions, negative eWoM will spread quickly through these reputable reviewers.

Finally, our results indicate that the effect of product type is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. As Table 3 illustrates, with search goods as the baseline, the logistic coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of experience goods is negative. This <sup>fi</sup>nding implies that the criteria for high-reputation reviewers for experience goods are higher than those for search goods. Thus, it is essential to classify products into experience and search goods, when an opinionsharing community adopts our proposed model to estimate its reviewers’ reputation.

## 5.2. Limitations and future research directions

Our study also has several limitations that warrant additional research attention. First, our empirical evaluation and analysis was based on a single data set collected from a popular third-party product review website (i.e., Epinions.com). For greater validity and robustness, additional studies should expand the current evaluation in both scope and scale. For example, additional research might evaluate our model using empirical data collected from other third-party product review websites or opinion-sharing communities established by retailers or product manufacturers. Second, this study did not analyze the content of reviews written by target members. Reviews with more details likely provide more helpful information to readers, in which case the corresponding reviewer may receive higher evaluations by other members in the community and thus be more reputable. Further research should include additional variables extracted from the content of reviews and empirically evaluate the effects of their inclusion on the effectiveness of reputation estimation.

Third, our proposed reputation estimation model is based on factors derived from members' web trust network and review behaviors in an opinion-sharing community and thus assumes that members in the opinion-sharing community can provide product ratings on an ordinal or numeric scale and assert other members they trust (to form a web trust network). While reasonable, these requirements may con<sup>fi</sup>ne the applicability of the proposed reputation estimation model somewhat. Further studies might extend our reputation estimation model to opinion-sharing communities in which these requirements are not fully satis<sup>fi</sup>ed (e.g., opinion-sharing communities without required trust mechanisms). Fourth, our study assumes that trust relationships are not associated with different strength levels and thus measures the trust intensity of a member according to the number of other members who trust that focal member. Although this assumption is reasonable, in that many existing opinion-sharing communities (e.g., Epinions.com) do not allow members to differentiate strength levels when asserting trust relationships with other members, additional research should extend our proposed reputation estimation model by considering trust relationships with varying strength levels.

Fifth, our study considers the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of product type by experience and search goods, and the results indicate that this product type is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant control variable. The impact of other classi<sup>fi</sup>cations (e.g., utilitarian vs. hedonic products, functionally complex vs. functionally non-complex products) on reputation estimation is another important issue for research. Finally, our study reveals four variables that relate signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to reviewers’ reputation. This result provides a basis for further studies to investigate related issues in similar contexts (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nding opinion leaders in a public policydiscussion forum).
