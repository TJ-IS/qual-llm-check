# Selling or Advertising: Strategies for Providing Digital Media Online

MING FAN, SUBODHA KUMAR, AND ANDREW B. WHINSTON

ABSTRACT: Media and network companies are increasingly providing digital media online. We develop a model to examine optimal strategies for media providers to utilize the online channel to distribute digital media. We examine a number of options for media providers. Our results suggest that media companies should sell programs online when content quality is relative high and online access cost is low. When online access cost is relative high, media providers could use the advertising strategy. Overall, companies are better off providing both pricing and advertising options to consumers. We derive the optimal price and advertising level, and analyze the factors that affect the price and advertising decisions. We find that as advertisement revenue rate increases, advertising level should be kept low. In addition, media companies should set online price and advertising level with consideration of the traditional channel in order to avoid channel cannibalization. We also analyze the advertising level in the traditional channel. Our results suggest that as digital video recorder technologies provide more convenience to consumers, media companies should increase, rather than decrease, revenues from advertising.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: advertising, digital media, distribution strategies, information goods pricing, online channel, pricing.

THE INTERNET AND THE WORLD WIDE WEB are becoming a viable channel to distribute digital media to consumers. At Apple’s iTunes online media store, consumers can select from over 400,000 digital songs and videos to download, starting at just 99 cents [23]. Broadcasting companies have also started posting TV programs to sell on their own Web sites or through the iTunes store. For example, CBS allows customers to buy shows such as Survivor and CSI for \$1.99 per episode. It is estimated that Apple sold more than 35 million videos online from October 2005 to June 2006 [25].

Although the popular business practice is to sell digital media online, some companies are experimenting with “free” online programs. ABC has unveiled a site where people with broadband connections can watch free episodes of Desperate Housewives, Lost, and other hit shows on their desktops. Users can pause, rewind, and fast-forward the shows; but they cannot skip the commercials of the programs. Advertisers, such as Procter & Gamble and Ford have quickly signed up with ABC to market their products through those programs. The initial results of the practice were very encouraging. In less than a month, ABC had 11 million streams and over 85 percent of those viewers remember the advertisements [21].

Some of the media companies are experimenting with both pricing and advertising options to customers. For example, CNN.com provides free video contents to its customers with commercial advertisements. In addition, it also offers commercial-free access to video content at CNN Pipeline, for which customers need to pay a fee.

There are a few reasons that media and network companies are quickly embracing the online channel. First, technology advancement, especially broadband and digital media streaming technology, has made it much easier for consumers to download and watch programs over the Internet. High-definition monitors can make the viewing experience quite pleasant. Second, selling media or showing advertisements online provides an additional revenue source for media companies. Media and network companies typically have high fixed costs in producing programs. Once programs are created, marginal distribution costs are relatively low. Thus, any additional channels that can help distribute their programs will be a big plus for the media companies. CBS Chief Executive Leslie Moonves admits that it is the company’s strategy to exploit content across as many platforms as possible [7].

More importantly, in our opinion, massive changes are taking place in the media industry. Digital technology and high-speed Internet are fundamentally changing the way media and broadcasting companies distribute their products. How and where consumers will get information and entertainment programs will continue to evolve in a big way [22]. Network and media companies can no longer think of themselves as just broadcasters of TV, radio, or other entertainment programs. Rather, they have to deliver content to their audiences through whatever channels that make sense for the customers [6].

So far, media providers have been experimenting with various advertising and pricing strategies as discussed earlier. However, it is unclear which strategy is optimal. There is a lack of rigorous research on firm strategies in providing online digital media and there are no clear best practices in the industry. In this research, we aim to study the following questions:

• Under what circumstances should media providers choose to use the pricing or advertising strategy in the online channel? When should they use both options?

• What are the optimal levels of online price and advertising? How do factors such as content quality, advertisement revenue rate, and ease of use affect the media provider’s pricing or advertising level?

## Prior Literature and Motivation

IN THIS PAPER, WE USE THE TERM DIGITAL MEDIA to refer to audio or video programs that exist in digital format and can be distributed through the Internet. In a broad sense, digital media belong to information goods, which are goods whose main value is derived from their digital content [18]. Information goods usually incur large fixed development costs and low production costs. Works of literature, music recordings, movies, and software products are typical examples of information goods [16, 18].

The topic of information goods is an important research area in information systems (IS). Prior studies have examined the selection of product quality of information goods [4]. Riggins [16] studied a separating equilibrium for a two-tier fee-based and sponsorship-based information Web site. Prasad et al. [14] examined a model with two customer types and studied a policy to charge a subscription price with advertisements for an Internet Web site. Lang and Vragov [10] examined a pricing scheme for distributing digital content over centralized versus decentralized networks. Kaya et al. [8] developed an economic and operational model to examine the conditions to distribute content by a monopolistic firm. Chellappa and Kumar [5] studied how “free” product-augmenting services affect pricing and customer retentions online.

An important parameter in this study is the content quality. Researchers from different disciplines, such as marketing, operations, and IS, have widely studied what constitutes quality. For example, Berry and Parasuraman [2] suggested that there are five general dimensions that influence customers’ assessment of service quality—reliability, tangibles, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy. Pitt et al. [13] used an empirical study to conclude that SERQUAL, an instrument based on the five dimensions mentioned above, is an appropriate instrument for measuring IS service quality. Reeves and Bednar [15] summarized different definitions of quality, and described the trade-offs in accepting one definition over the other. Another important stream of research deals with the assessment of data quality [12]. In this study, we use quality to represent the intrinsic value of the program content to customers [14].

This study is also related to the problem of business cannibalization [11, 16]. In a hypercompetitive market, it could be optimal for firms with a leading position to aggressively cannibalize their current advantages before competitors step in to steal the market [11]. When a firm has certain monopoly power, cannibalization problems could occur if high-type consumers are tempted to purchase the product aimed at low-type consumers. For example, in a two-tier Web site, high-type consumers may choose to access the free, lower-quality content instead of paying a fee for the higher-quality content [16]. In this study, cannibalization could happen to any media providers’ traditional TV or radio channel if consumers are being lured away by the online channel.

This study makes a contribution to prior research by focusing on the following areas: (1) We model consumer heterogeneity and assume that consumers are continuously distributed in a support range. (2) We study pricing and advertising strategies for distributing digital media. We model at the level of media programs, rather than for a general Web site. One of our research questions is whether the media provider should sell a given program or provide it for “free” with advertisements, or use both options. (3) We extend the baseline model and analyze how factors such as content quality, advertisement revenue rate, and ease of use affect price and advertising levels. Our research is particularly relevant to the media industry, which is undergoing a tremendous transformation, and provides important managerial implications on how to use the Internet channel to effectively distribute digital media to consumers.

## Model

IN THIS SECTION, WE PRESENT THE MAJOR ASSUMPTIONS of our analysis and describe the parameters and decision variables used in the model. We also derive consumer demand functions and set up firm optimization problems. The parameters and decision variables for the basic model are presented in Table 1. We explain these notations in the paper as well.

Consumers choose either the traditional channel (t) or the online channel (o) to access a program based on their utilities. First, the content quality, which we denote as T, is the value of a program to consumers. We assume that companies can reasonably estimate the content quality. If there are previous seasons to a series, it is easy to get quality estimates from previous ratings or number of downloads. For new programs, network companies often estimate the quality by showing the programs to focus groups before nationally broadcasting. Second, consistent with prior literature, we view advertisements as unwelcome intrusions to consumers, which contribute to consumer disutility [1, 20]. We discuss the implications of relaxing this assumption in the fifth section as well. We use $a _ { _ t }$ and $a _ { _ o }$ to represent advertising levels in traditional and online channels, respectively.

Further, there are various costs associated with accessing the programs in the traditional and online channels. In traditional channels such as TV and radio, consumers have to plan ahead in order to watch or listen to the programs and, thus, incur the opportunity cost of time [9]. We denote this cost of using traditional channels as $c _ { t } .$ On the other hand, in order to access online programs, consumers have to search, download, and buffer the programs. We use $c _ { _ o }$ to denote the cost of watching programs online. If companies decide to sell digital media, consumers have to incur additional cost for registering and for paying online media providers. We denote $c _ { p }$ as the additional cost of registering and using an online payment system in order to access the programs.

The online channel provides significant advantages in terms of anytime, anywhere access, while access to the traditional channel is limited in terms of time and place of access. Hence, in most of our analysis, we assume that the inconvenience cost of the traditional channel is larger than the costs in the online channel—that is, $c _ { _ t } - c _ { _ o } - c _ { _ p } >$ 0. However, the emerging digital video recorder (DVR) technology may help in providing anytime access in the traditional channel. Therefore, with the progress in DVR technology, it is possible that the inconvenience cost of the traditional channel also decreases. We will later discuss its implications by relaxing this assumption.

Consumers are heterogeneous and have different sensitivity to the above costs. We denote the cost sensitivity as $\theta ,$ which is randomly drawn from a uniform distribution with support on [0, 1]. This approach is standard in economics and marketing literature in order to derive the demand functions [17, 24].

We use $\boldsymbol { u } _ { { } _ { t } }$ to denote consumers’ utility for accessing media in the traditional channel, which is given by

$$
u _ {t} = T - a _ {t} - \theta c _ {t}.
$$

We use $u _ { o } ^ { p }$ to denote consumers’ utility to purchase the media online. Given that the media provider decides to sell digital media online, consumers’ utility function $u _ { o } ^ { p }$ is

$$
u _ {o} ^ {p} = T - p - \theta (c _ {o} + c _ {p}),
$$

where $p$ is the price for the online program.

We use $u _ { \phantom { } _ { o } } ^ { a }$ to denote consumers’ utility to watch the media with advertisement online and the function is

$$
u _ {o} ^ {a} = T - a _ {o} - \theta c _ {o}.
$$

We first derive demand functions from the utility model for the pricing scenario. This approach follows the Hotelling model and consumer differentiation model based on consumer utility functions [24].

From the utility functions, we know that a consumer is indifferent between the two channels if $u _ { t } = u _ { o } ^ { p }$ . Thus, $\theta ^ { * } = ( p - a _ { t } ) / ( c _ { t } - c _ { o } - c _ { p } )$ is the indifference point for consumers. For consumers to choose the traditional channel, the utility functions have to satisfy $u _ { t } \geq u _ { o } ^ { p } .$ , or $\boldsymbol \theta \leq \boldsymbol \theta ^ { * }$ . Thus, the demand for the traditional channel under the pricing case is

$$
D _ {t} ^ {p} = \theta^ {*} = \frac {p - a _ {t}}{c _ {t} - c _ {o} - c _ {p}}.\tag{1}
$$

For consumers who choose the online channel, the utility function has to be positive—that is, $u _ { o } ^ { p } \geq 0 _ { : }$ , or $\theta \leq ( T - p ) / ( c _ { _ o } + c _ { _ p } )$ . In addition, their utility functions have to satisfy $u _ { o } ^ { p } \geq u _ { t } ,$ , or $\boldsymbol { \theta } \geq \boldsymbol { \theta } ^ { * }$ . We can derive the demand function in the online channel for the pricing case as

$$
D _ {o} ^ {p} = \frac {T}{c _ {o} + c _ {p}} + \frac {a _ {t}}{c _ {t} - c _ {o} - c _ {p}} - p \left(\frac {1}{c _ {o} + c _ {p}} + \frac {1}{c _ {t} - c _ {o} - c _ {p}}\right).\tag{2}
$$

In order to have demands in the online channel, we need the condition that $( T - P ) / ( c _ { o } + c _ { v } ) - \theta ^ { * } > 0 ;$ otherwise, the online demand will be zero. Substituting the expression of ${ \boldsymbol { \theta } } ^ { * }$ , we get the following condition for the media provider if it decides to sell the program online:

$$
T > \frac {p c _ {t} - a _ {t} (c _ {o} + c _ {p})}{c _ {t} - c _ {o} - c _ {p}}.\tag{3}
$$

Under the advertising scenario, a consumer is indifferent between the two channels if $u _ { t } = u _ { o } ^ { a }$ , and the indifference point is $\boldsymbol { \Theta } ^ { * * } = ( a _ { o } - a _ { t } ) / ( c _ { t } - c _ { o } )$ . Also, the utility functions have to satisfy $u _ { t } \geq u _ { o } ^ { a } ;$ , or $\theta \leq \theta ^ { \ast \ast }$ , in order for consumers to choose the traditional channel. We derive the demand for the traditional channel under the advertising case as

$$
D _ {t} ^ {a} = \theta^ {* *} = \frac {a _ {o} - a _ {t}}{c _ {t} - c _ {o}}.\tag{4}
$$

For consumers to choose the online channel under advertising, the utility functions have to satisfy $u _ { \ o o } ^ { a } \geq u _ { \ o _ { t } }$ , or $\boldsymbol { \theta } \geq \boldsymbol { \theta } ^ { \ast \ast }$ <sup>\*</sup>. Hence, the demand function for the online channel in the advertising case is

$$
D _ {o} ^ {a} = \frac {T}{c _ {o}} + \frac {a _ {t}}{c _ {t} - c _ {o}} - a _ {o} \left(\frac {1}{c _ {o}} + \frac {1}{c _ {t} - c _ {o}}\right).\tag{5}
$$

The condition to have demand for the online channel is $T ( 1 - a _ { o } ) / c _ { o } - \Theta ^ { * * } > 0$ . Thus, we can derive the following condition, which has to be satisfied if the media provider offers the program online with advertisement:

$$
c _ {o} <   \frac {c _ {t} (T - a _ {o})}{T - a _ {t}}.\tag{6}
$$

We now set up the problem for a media provider. Under the pricing scenario, the media provider’s profit function is

$$
\pi^ {p} = D _ {t} ^ {p} a _ {t} \beta_ {t} + D _ {o} ^ {p} p,\tag{7}
$$

where $D _ { t } ^ { p } a _ { t } \beta _ { t }$ is the profit from the traditional channel and $D _ { o } ^ { p } p$ is the profit from the online channel. The media provider’s objective is to maximize the total profit π<sup>p</sup>.

Under the advertising case, the media provider’s profit function is

$$
\pi^ {a} = D _ {t} ^ {a} a _ {t} \beta_ {t} + D _ {o} ^ {a} a _ {o} \beta_ {o},\tag{8}
$$

where $D _ { t } ^ { a } a \mathbb { \beta } _ { t }$ is the profit from the traditional channel and $D _ { o } ^ { a } a _ { o } \beta _ { o }$ is the profit from advertisement in the online channel. The objective is to maximize the total profit $\pi ^ { a }$ .

## Pricing Versus Advertising

IN THIS SECTION, WE SOLVE THE MEDIA PROVIDER’S pricing and advertising problems optimally. We then derive the condition under which the media provider will choose either pricing or advertising strategy. Further, we analyze the pricing and advertising policies.

We first examine optimal solutions for the pricing and advertising models. To obtain the optimal price for the online program, we take the first-order condition of the profit function (7) and get

$$
p ^ {*} = \frac {T \left(c _ {t} - c _ {o} - c _ {p}\right) + a _ {t} \left(1 + \beta_ {t}\right) \left(c _ {o} + c _ {p}\right)}{2 c _ {t}}\tag{9}
$$

and the following result:

Proposition 1: If the media provider sells the program online, the program quality has to satisfy $T > I ,$ where T\_ is defined as

$$
\underline {{T}} = \frac {a _ {t} (\beta_ {t} - 1) (c _ {o} + c _ {p})}{(c _ {t} - c _ {o} - c _ {p})}\tag{10}
$$

and the optimal price p<sup>\*</sup> is given by Equation (9).

Proof: See the Appendix.

P1 outlines the necessary condition for the media provider to sell the program online and provides the solution for the optimal price. When the content quality is too low and the condition given in P1 is not satisfied, consumers are not willing to take the trouble to purchase the program online.

For the advertisement case, we derive the optimal advertising level by taking the first-order condition of the media provider’s problem (8):

$$
a _ {o} ^ {*} = \frac {a _ {t} (\beta_ {t} + \beta_ {o}) c _ {o} + \beta_ {o} (c _ {t} - c _ {o}) T}{2 \beta_ {o} c _ {t}}.\tag{11}
$$

Proposition 2: If the media provider offers the program with advertising in the online channel, the condition $c _ { o } < \tilde { c } _ { o }$ has to be satisfied, where $\tilde { c } _ { o }$ is defined as

$$
\tilde {c} _ {o} = \frac {\beta_ {o} c _ {t} T}{a _ {t} (\beta_ {t} - \beta_ {o}) + \beta_ {o} T}\tag{12}
$$

and the optimal advertising level $a _ { _ o } ^ { * }$ is given by Equation (11).

Proof: See the Appendix.

P2 provides the necessary condition for the media provider to offer the program online with advertising. It also specifies the optimal advertising level. This proposition simply states that the condition for the advertising model is that the online access cost should be less than a threshold level $\tilde { c } _ { o }$ . When the inconvenience cost is too high, the online channel does not provide enough benefits to consumers and will not draw enough demand. We can see that the threshold level $\tilde { c } _ { o }$ increases in $c _ { _ t }$ because $\partial \tilde { c } _ { o } / \partial c _ { t } >$ 0. The higher the inconvenience cost in the traditional channel, the higher the threshold level for the online channel. We also find that $\tilde { c } _ { _ { o } }$ increases in $T$ because $\partial \tilde { c } _ { { } _ { o } } / \partial T > 0$ The higher the content quality, the higher the tolerance level for the inconvenience cost in the online channel.

P1 and P2 state optimal price and advertising levels, respectively, given that the media provider has decided to use the pricing or the advertising model. However, the question is which model the media provider should select—pricing or advertising. To answer this question, we need to compare the profits for the two scenarios.

Substituting optimal price $p ^ { * }$ into the profit function (7) for the pricing model, we can obtain the optimal profit value $\pi ^ { p ^ { * } }$ . Similarly, substituting optimal advertising level $\boldsymbol { a } _ { _ o } ^ { * }$ into the advertising model (8), we get the optimal profit value $\pi ^ { a ^ { * } }$ . The expressions for $\pi ^ { p ^ { * } }$ and $\pi ^ { a ^ { * } }$ are given in the proof of P3 in the Appendix. By comparing these profits, we define another term ∆π, which is the difference between the profits of pricing and advertising options. Its expression is provided in the Appendix. Now we present the conditions for the media provider to choose either the pricing or the advertising model in P3.

Proposition 3: (a) When $T > T$ and $\Delta \pi > 0 ,$ the media provider should choose to sell the program in the online channel and the optimal price is given by Equation (9). (b) When $c _ { o } < \tilde { c } _ { o }$ and $\Delta \pi < 0 ,$ , the media provider should offer the program in the online channel with advertisement and the optimal advertising level is given by Equation (11). (c) When $T \leq T$ and $c _ { o } \geq \tilde { c } _ { o } ,$ the media provider should offer the program only in the traditional channel.

## Proof: See the Appendix.

P3 implies that we should choose the option that provides the higher profit, subject to feasibility constraints. Figure 1 illustrates the profit difference graphically. We can see that when the content quality (T) increases, the pricing option becomes more profitable. When the online inconvenience cost $( c _ { o } )$ increases, ∆π decreases and the advertising option is more profitable. Thus, we can see that when the content quality is high, the media provider is better off selling the program online. When the online access cost is high, the media provider should provide the program using the advertising strategy as consumers are unlikely to take extra trouble to use online payment to purchase programs.

Now we analyze the optimal pricing and examine the factors that affect the pricing strategy.

Proposition 4: Given that the media provider offers the program online with the pricing-only option, the optimal price increases in (a) content quality and (b) the advertising level of the traditional channel.

## Proof: See the Appendix.

P4 suggests that the optimal price goes up as content quality increases. This result is intuitive. In practice, we often observe that media providers use a single price for different online media programs regardless of their qualities or popularities. Our results suggest that media providers can increase their revenues with differentiated prices for different programs.

P4 also suggests that the media provider should price online programs by considering the advertising level in the traditional channel. If the price is too low, then customers will flee the traditional channel. Companies must walk a fine line to balance the demands on both channels and should choose the price level in order to maximize overall profit.

We next analyze the case when the media provider chooses the advertising option in the online channel.

Proposition 5: Given that the media provider offers the program online with the advertising-only option, the optimal online advertising level decreases in the advertisement revenue rate of the online channel.

## Proof: See the Appendix.

P5 provides an important guideline for media companies in deciding advertisements online. An implication of this finding is that the media provider is better off offering a lower amount of advertisements if the revenue from advertising per person is high enough. This will further encourage people to use the advertisement-based online channel. With advancement in technologies that allow companies to collect customer preference data, media companies have great opportunities to design advertisements that target a particular customer segment. Targeted advertising can certainly increase the advertisement revenue rate. As a result, media providers should provide shorter and more focused advertisements in offering digital media. To consumers, this is less intrusive and it helps to generate robust demand in the online channel.

Proposition 6: Given that the media provider offers the program online with the advertising-only option, the optimal online advertising level increases in (a) content quality and (b) the advertising level of the traditional channel.

## Proof: See the Appendix.

The first part of P6 simply states that with a higher content quality, the media provider should increase the advertising level. In addition, it suggests that in making decisions on the advertising level in the online channel, companies should use the advertising level and inconvenience cost in the traditional channel as a reference. Because the two channels are related, the decision on the online advertising level is affected by factors in the traditional channel.

P6 also provides interesting implications on the effects of the technologies that allow users to skip advertisements. Although it is much more difficult for users to skip commercials while watching programs through the Web [21], a study by the Nielsen Company finds that people with DVRs skip some, but not all, commercials [19]. Thus, the use of DVRs, in effect, decreases the advertisement level in the traditional channel. As a result, the optimal online advertising level has to decrease, according to P6. Therefore, it is important for media companies to provide targeted and short advertisements to the online audience.

We have so far assumed that the inconvenience cost of the traditional channel is higher than that of the online channel—that is, $c _ { { } _ { t } } - c _ { { } _ { o } } - c _ { { } _ { p } } > 0$ . When this assumption is relaxed, such as $c _ { { } _ { t } } < c _ { { } _ { o } } + c _ { { } _ { p } } ,$ , the demand function for the pricing model will change. Following the same analysis procedure, we can find the optimal pricing and advertising levels. Specifically, we derive the following properties when $c _ { { t } } < c _ { { o } } + c _ { { p } }$ . First, the online price should be kept low in order to have demand for the pricing model. We can derive this condition directly from the demand function in the traditional channel for the pricing model, which is $( a _ { _ t } - p ) / ( c _ { _ o } + c _ { _ p } - c _ { _ t } )$ . Second, the profit of the online pricing option strictly decreases in $( c _ { o } + c _ { p } )$ , suggesting that as the costs of the online channel increase, the pricing model becomes less attractive. The profit of the online pricing option also increases in $c _ { t }$ . Thus, with the innovation of DVR technology, the inconvenience cost of traditional would decrease, making the traditional channel more competitive and the online pricing model less profitable. Consumers are less likely to pay for content online. Therefore, as technology in the traditional channel improves, media companies have to reduce prices for online programs.

## Simultaneous Pricing and Advertising Options

WE EXAMINED THE CASE THAT THE MEDIA PROVIDER EITHER sells the content or provides the content for “free” with advertisement. However, it may be optimal for the media provider to offer both pricing and advertising options to the customers, and let them self-select which option to use.<sup>1</sup> Here we set up the problem for the media provider when the firm offers both pricing and advertising options simultaneously. The profit function for the media provider is as follows:

$$
\pi = D _ {t} a _ {t} \beta_ {t} + D _ {o p} p + D _ {o a} a _ {o} \beta_ {o},\tag{13}

We derive the demand functions through consumers’ utility functions following a similar approach as in the third section. Specifically, for consumers to choose the online advertising option, their utility functions have to satisfy $u _ { o } ^ { a } \geq u _ { o } ^ { p }$ and $u _ { { } _ { o } } ^ { { a } } \geq u _ { { } _ { t } }$ . For consumers to use the online pricing option, their utility functions have to satisfy $u _ { o } ^ { p } \geq$ $u _ { o } ^ { a }$ and $u _ { o } ^ { p } \geq u _ { t }$ . $$
D _ {t} = (p - a _ {t}) / (c _ {t} - c _ {o} - c _ {p})
$$

$$

## Discussion

MEDIA AND NETWORK COMPANIES ARE TAKING advantage of the Internet to offer programs online. There is no doubt that technologies are fundamentally transforming the media industry. We observed different business strategies in providing digital media. This research analyzes the trade-offs between pricing and advertising strategies. We find that when content quality is high and online access cost is low, companies could sell commercial-free programs online. On the other hand, when online access cost is relatively high, media providers should offer free programs with advertisement. Our results provide important managerial implications for media companies. Often, a media provider prefers one strategy over the other. The rationale behind it is not all that clear. For example, CBS, News Corporation, and music studios, along with Apple’s iTune store, are selling digital programs. Disney and its subsidiary ABC are experimenting with the advertising model. Our analysis suggests that media providers should adopt both strategies in most situations and let consumers self-select from the options. For example, network companies can sell hit programs at the iTunes store and offer those programs with advertisements on their Web sites.

We also examine the factors that affect the pricing and advertising level. Our findings suggest that media providers should charge different prices, rather than a single price, for their programs. High-quality programs should be charged a higher fee. For the advertising model, as online advertisement revenue rates increase, companies should show fewer advertisements to customers.

In addition, price and advertising level in the online channel should be set with consideration of the traditional channel. Most media providers are operating in dual channels, which are competing against each other for customers. As technologies get better and easier to use, there are risks of cannibalizing media providers’ traditional channels. If consumers are quick to adopt the online channel, revenues of the traditional TV or radio channels will be hurt, which may affect their overall profit. Hence, companies need to realize that any operational policy in the online channel will affect the performance of the traditional channel. Therefore, it is important to optimize the overall profits of the company instead of a single channel and consider factors in both channels to make operational decisions.

We also study the level of advertising in the traditional channel. We find the advertising level should be higher when the inconvenience cost is lower. DVR technologies provide consumers more convenience and also allow them to skip advertisements. This practice is not optimal for the media industry. Our results suggest that if a technology provides consumers with more convenience, companies should increase, rather than decrease, revenues from advertising.

We note that our results are based on the assumption that consumers are evenly distributed in a market. Therefore, multiple differentiated channels usually outperform a single-channel strategy. However, when a company is operating in a market with customers highly concentrated in one segment, cannibalization of the old channel could be beneficial.

## Limitations and Future Research

THERE ARE SEVERAL LIMITATIONS OF THIS RESEARCH. First, we focus on the innovations in the online distribution of digital media and analyze how this affects media providers strategies. Innovations are also taking place in the traditional channel; for example, the DVR and coming convergence of computers and televisions. We did not analyze the latter situation in detail. Future research could analyze the dynamic competition between the two channels.

Second, we follow the traditional approach in modeling consumer choice between traditional and online channels. We assume consumers are heterogeneous in their sensitivity to the cost of accessing the programs while the effect of advertisement is homogeneous. It is possible that consumers’ sensitivity to advertisement is also heterogeneous. Incorporating this point may lead to a more complex demand function.

Third, there are other ways that media providers could distribute online digital media. For example, media companies could use subscription models. Future studies could analyze a subscription model with possible advertisement.

Further, in this paper, when we discuss advertising level, we do not distinguish between long advertisements and several short advertisements. Future studies could analyze the trade-offs of various advertisement options.

This study examines strategies for media companies to distribute contents in multiple channels. We observe that major media companies, such as ABC, NBC, CBS, and BBC, have already started providing their programs online. In order to maximize their overall profit from both traditional and online channels, they need to optimize their decisions very judiciously for the online channel, which is a new channel for many of these companies. In this regard, we presented the results for media companies that are either contemplating to provide or already providing their programs online.

In contrast to the prior literature, we consider the problem at the level of each program provided by the company. Based on the conditions presented in this paper, the company may decide to provide some of the programs in the online channel, and use pricing or advertising strategies.

Many companies are experimenting with innovative ways of delivering programs to customers. This is a promising area for future research. As mentioned earlier, the focus of this study is to examine the innovations in the online channel. Future research could also examine innovations of DVR and related technologies in the traditional channel, and investigate how those technologies could affect business strategies. Another direction for future research is to consider personalized advertisement for different customers based on their profiles and usage behavior. However, such analysis would require extensive information about consumers, which could be difficult to gather. Media providers may also decide to offer a bundle of more than one program in the online channel. For such problems, the composition of bundles and the price or advertising level for those bundles may again depend on consumer behavior.
