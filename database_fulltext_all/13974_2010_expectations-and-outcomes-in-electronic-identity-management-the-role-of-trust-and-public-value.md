# Expectations and outcomes in electronic identity management: the role of trust and public value

Philip Seltsikas<sup>1</sup> and Robert M. O’Keefe<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Discipline of Business Information Systems, University of Sydney, Australia; <sup>2</sup>School of Management, University of Surrey, U.K.

Correspondence: Philip Seltsikas, Economics and Business Building (H69), Discipline of Business Information Systems, University of Sydney, NSW 2006. Australia. Tel: þ 61 2 9351 7580; Fax: þ 61 2 9351 7294; E-mail: philip.seltsikas@sydney.edu.au

## Abstract

This paper is concerned with the issues facing stakeholders attempting to develop, implement and maintain electronic identity management (IdM) systems in government. It argues that perceptions of electronic IdM vary, and thus a single conceptualisation of the concept is difficult. Using data collected from stakeholders in 17 European countries, Canada, New Zealand and the U.S.A. from 2003 through 2007, a qualitative analysis identifies 10 dominant stakeholder areas of concern which we term ‘themes’. These are then mapped onto a framework of public value due to Grimsley and Meehan which relates services to outcomes and the development of trust. We conclude by questioning the conceptualisation of trust within the context of IdM, and suggesting that considering trust as the mitigation of relational risk is more appropriate. We also consider the usefulness of public value as a notion for informing IdM and highlight the lack of national differences in our diverse data set. European Journal of Information Systems (2010) 19, 93–103. doi:10.1057/ejis.2009.51; published online 2 February 2010

Keywords: identity management; e-ID; e-Government; digital identity; public service provision

## Introduction

This paper focuses on a specific aspect of e-Government information systems (IS) development – the management of the digital identities of citizens, businesses and governments themselves – by governments. The area of electronic identity (e-ID) has received relatively little attention in academic literature of a non-technical nature.

As governments have increasingly embraced electronic means of transacting with their citizens, businesses, and even internally between departments and agencies (Gasco, 2003; Thomas & Streib, 2003; Gil-Garcı´a & Pardo, 2005), the need for understanding and facilitating secure electronic identification of persons, natural or legal) is manifest. When traditional government transactions are ‘moved’ to the e-Government setting (Dearstyne, 2001; Layne & Lee, 2001), the remoteness of the users who are normally ‘transacting’ using the Internet or other electronic means produces a strong requirement for governments to ensure that the person (natural or legal) transacting online is indeed who they say they are and entitled to the government benefits and services they are accessing, receiving or consuming.

When considering the adoption of e-Government services, the significant costs of e-Government implementation (INPUT, 2009) do not appear to be matched with the expected or planned take-up/adoption in public services. Data concerning the costs and use of such systems is extremely difficult to obtain as it is not likely to be in the interest of governments to reveal sufficient cost detail to support critic’s suspicions that massive spending has not yet yielded expected benefits (e.g., see Whitley & Hosein, 2008). As with most IS projects, calculating the true total cost of ownership (Zarnekow et al., 2004) can lead to highly contentious debate; this seems to be particularly true for government IS.

However, with less than expected use of e-Government services, governments continue to invest in programmes and projects to facilitate and foster the adoption of their online services. When we consider e-Government in general, there are few studies reported in the academic literature that focus on the IS associated with e-Government security (Lenk, 2002; Gil-Garcı´a & Pardo, 2005), and particularly systems for managing digital identity – which is the focus of this research. This research specifically focuses on understanding the following. How is electronic identity management (IdM) conceptualised in practice? What outcomes are expected from electronic IdM? What are the stakeholder concerns relating to Information Technology (IT) management in the context of electronic IdM?

The research has been conducted in two main phases over a period of 5 years. The first phase was conducted during 2003–2006, with the second phase conducted during 2007 and focusing on implementation. To frame the research and present the results, this paper uses a framework drawn from the work of Grimsley and Meehan (2007), who employ the concept of public value due to Moore (1995).

In the next section, we provide further background to electronic IdM from both an academic and practical perspective. We review literature and models of potential use to our research, explaining and presenting the reason for using the public value framework. Following details on our methods, we then consider the conceptualisation of electronic IdM, and the various qualitatively distinctive themes that our research uncovered. The paper concludes with a discussion of findings.

## Background to the research

The management of e-ID (or IdM) has been high on the agenda of most governments for the last 5–10 years. According to IT vendors and analysts, the ‘market’ for secure IdM solutions seems to be very large indeed; the Radicati Group (2005), for example, suggested that the IdM market will grow to over \$2462 million by the end of 2009 and Cser et al. (2008) have forecasted that the IdM market will grow from nearly \$2.6 billion in 2006 to more than \$12.3 billion in 2014.

Notably, there has been a considerable effort to ‘solve’ the IdM problem for e-Government in the U.S.A. In 2004, the Federal U.S. Government started a Pan-U.S. presidential project to research, develop and implement an IS architecture for managing electronic identities to support 26 core e-Government applications (e-Authentication,

2006). Holden & Millett (2005) note the requirement for ‘electronic authentication’ in e-Government. They highlight the fact that electronic IdM (or e-ID) is not simply a technological solution, but a ‘balance between access, security, authentication, and privacy’ (ibid, p. 367).

In Europe, there have been initiatives similar to those in the U.S.A. aimed at designing a secure IS architecture for cross-border IdM at a pan-European level. However, in the European context this is considerably complicated by the legal principle of Member State ‘subsidiarity’ which is a fundamental tenet of European Union Law. (Subsidiar ity generally means that a ‘central authority’, such as the European Commission, should have a subsidiary function and perform only tasks which cannot be performed effectively at a more immediate or local level.) The result is that each European Member State has normally developed their e-Government IS in an independent manner. Otjacques et al. (2008) illustrate the resulting diversity in European approaches to managing identities by providing a comparison of individual IdM approaches across 18 of the 25 EU member states, focussing particularly on the use of Single Identification Numbers (SINs). This diversity in the management of identities in Europe (and even within European Member States) has resulted in considerable fragmentation of e-ID development efforts which has led to multiple ‘identity silos’. This is where identity data or ‘credentials’ (name, password, address etc.) are stored in a multitude of places for the same people or companies, resulting in multiple SINs for a single natural or legal person.

Some governments, such as the Austrian government, have gone a long way toward solving the fragmentation of identity data and processes at a national level (Makolm, 2004; ACS, 2006; Makolm, 2006; Otjacques et al., 2008) through the implementation of sophisticated integrating IS. However, in the context of ‘cross-border government applications such as those envisaged by Europe's ministers (OIEU. 2000: IDABC. 2006). the interoperability of Europe’s disparate government applications is a major challenge for European governments.

Research on e-ID is mostly found in the more technical computing journals that deal with the underlying security technologies such as ‘cryptography’ (see, e.g., Miyata et al., 2006). Context and use are rarely considered in depth and published research directly related to the topic of this paper is sparse. Gil-Garcı´a et al. (2007) look at issues surrounding management and benefits expectations in e-Government projects where disparate stakeholders are required to collaborate to exchange information. This relates to the context of e-ID as identity information is often ‘managed’ across disparate (and often normally unrelated) entities. Pavlou et al. (2007) deal with the problem of uncertainty in online relationships. This is an area which IdM tries to mitigate through both technical and non-technical means. Pavlou et al. argue that online relationships between buyers and sellers (presumably unknown communicating entities) can be better understood if they are viewed as ‘agency relationships’ and that ‘perceived uncertainty is a barrier to online transactions’ (p. 131). Hui et al. (2007) explore aspects relating to user’s attitudes with respect to ‘privacy in using an online website and imparting personal data. Their work empirically demonstrates that online users care about how their personal data (including identity data) is ‘managed’ online.

The provision of appropriate models or frameworks to better understand IdM and/or e-ID is equally limited. Carter & Be´langer (2005) have produced a ‘model of e-Government adoption’ that showed ‘trustworthiness’ to be an important factor to citizen uptake of government services. In Carter & Be´langer’s work trustworthiness can be influenced by a ‘privacy statement’ on the online service website. Moon & Norris (2005) examine a wide range of factors that may impact upon the implementation of e-Government initiatives and find that ‘managerial orientation’ and ‘government capacity’ can have an effect. Their work indicates that studies concerning the implementation of e-Government need to consider multiple possible factors, many of which are ‘institutional and environmental’; factors which may not be directly related to technology. Straub & Welke (1998) explore the issue of IS security and propose a risk management analysis approach for the protection of a corporation’s information assets. IdM policies and systems are similarly concerned with risk management in the specific domain of authorisation, authentication and identification.

Within the broader area of e-Government, models and frameworks have frequently been brought into research to explain concepts and relationships. Grimsley & Meehan (2007), in their research regarding the design and evaluation of e-Government systems, present a simple framework that relates services and service outcomes to trust (see Figure 1). In this framework, services used by clients can engender trust in e-Government. Beneficial outcomes from these services can also create trust, in both the clients using the services and the broader community of citizens.

The framework is presented as capturing the elements of ‘public value’. This is a concept drawn from Moore (1995), in which value goes beyond rigid notions of efficiency and consumption; citizens are concerned with, and expect, fairness and equity in government dealings with them. Moore suggests that managers in public institutions should aim to create public value, rather than to unquestioningly implement political decisions. They can do this through two different activities: deploying ‘the money and authority entrusted to them to produce things of value to particular clients and beneficiaries’ and ‘establishing and operating an institution that meets citizens’ desires for properly ordered and productive public institutions’.

Grimsley and Meehan note that this framework is ‘very general, applicable in a number of contexts’ but limited due to the ill-definition of its elements. They go on to develop a stronger framework that contains satisfaction as well as trust – hence making it a better measurement instrument within the process of evaluation. Within the context of IdM and e-ID, services can be viewed as e-Government systems that employ (or could employ) e-ID as a means of authenticating citizens, business or government departments. Outcomes are experienced by citizens (individually or collectively) and businesses, but in our use of the model also the main stakeholders in and around IdM. Trust, as viewed by Grimsley and Meehan, is viewed largely in terms of the legitimacy it bestows on government. We chose to be initially liberal in our interpretation of trust since our stakeholders consistently mentioned trust and associated aspects of trusting without landing upon a single or simple definition.

Our purpose is not to in any sense confirm or better this very flexible e-Government framework, or even to provide an instantiation of it within the context of IS or IdM, but simply to use it to help us explain our data and findings. Having done this, however, we do question the concept of trust as used in the framework, and point to discrepancies between the framework and the original concepts developed by Moore (1995). This allows us to suggest a way forward in developing a better potential model of IdM. We also consider if there are any national differences across our data that could lead to differing conceptualisations of IdM.

## Methodology

A critical methodological point to note with the research reported in this paper is that the data was collected directly from the setting and allowed the respondents to delineate what was important to them. The researchers did not predefine what was important by developing a priori theoretical constructs or models, and brought the framework to bear only as part of the analysis. Essentially this research has been conducted using post-positive methodological assumptions (ontological (Berger & Luckman, 1967; Walsham, 1995), epistemological, and axiological (Lincoln & Guba, 1985) as the authors believe that these are more appropriate to the aims and setting under investigation (ibid.; Gummesson, 1988).

The research in this paper was conducted using multiple data collection techniques; participant observation (Spradley, 1980), documentary evidence (Scott, 1990), and unstructured interviews (King. 1994). With little known about IdM or e-ID in the non-technical IS literature, this research has been necessarily ‘exploratory’. In accordance with the methodological assumptions adopted, it was not appropriate for us to develop an interview schedule or protocol (as this would constrain the data collection). Instead, we used simple opening statements to get the unstructured interviews started. In this respect, we explained to respondents that the area of interest is in hearing about what their experience had been with IdM and e-ID and how their interest in this domain had come about in their government or business. The data has been anonymised due to the fact that many government respondents have indicated that the subject matter is politically sensitive for them and is frequently subject to confidentiality controls of their respective government organisations.

We have engaged with 17 of Europe’s 25 governments, multiple Directorates of the European Commission, and with administrative authorities of the Canadian, New Zealand and U.S. governments. The data has been collected using the techniques mentioned above and then coded with the aid of qualitative data analysis software (NVIVO) so that dominant themes can be identified (Glaser & Strauss, 1968). The ‘respondents’ were also the main sources of documentary evidence. Many of the respondents were consulted in the context of a major research and development project that aimed to establish an IS architecture for the secure management of IdM in e-Government. This R&D initiative emphasised the secure cross-border interoperability of national identity credentials (of citizens, businesses and governments) for supporting and enabling online government applications. The authors collected participant observation data in the context of this R&D programme through direct liaison with key government information management and government information security stakeholders internationally. The research and development programme began in 2004 and concluded during 2006.

At the end of 2007, the authors conducted further research and consulted key government stakeholders in the U.S.A., Europe and New Zealand. These respondents were specifically selected by the researchers as they had made significant progress in actually implementing IdM technologies with live e-Government systems.

The major respondents in this research have been senior government officials responsible for e-Government development and/or strategy. They included government policymakers, ‘application owners’ and ‘data and infrastructure owners’. Sample job titles of the respondents are ‘Minister of Information Society’, ‘Director of Information Security’, ‘Director of Economy and Finance’; ‘e-Envoy’, and ‘ICT director’. A smaller set of data from respondents at the U.S.A. Federal government’s General Services Administration (GSA) and the Canadian government’s Department of Public Works and Government Services has also informed the first phase of this research. The data from 270 respondents is included in the analysis that follows.

## Perceptions of electronic IdM

Definitions of IdM abound. A fairly typical definition, taken from an online set of IT definitions and explanations (SearchUnifiedCommunications.com, 2009), appears thus:

Identity management y deals with identifying individuals in a system (such as a country, a network, or an enterprise) and controlling their access to resources within that system by associating user rights and restrictions with the established identity.

The notion of ‘access’ and ‘restrictions’ is common across many definitions, essentially extending a security view of access to identity. Many definitions resort to the use of the words ‘secure’ or ‘security’, thus resulting in definitions that are perhaps tautological (e.g., one vendor has described IdM as ‘secure access for users with enterprise identities’).

But how is electronic IdM actually conceptualised in practice? Information technologies are frequently labelled and employed without any accepted definition of the particular label. Our respondents appear aware of this. A not unusual comment was:

I’m always confused by what people mean by identity management, because everybody I talk to means something else.

There is also a firm understanding that vendors have viewed IdM as a market into which rebranded software tools can be sold:

I think that, quite honestly, the software industry in large part is responsible for some of the confusion around identity management, because, every-time we get a buzz word, every marketer for every software company in the world, instantly rebrands all their products as – whatever.

What is missing in the data is any view that IdM can in any sense be a sole solution to identifying citizens or other entities of interest. In line with criticisms of the U.K. identity card project (Whitley & Hosein, 2008), where technological determinants have suggested that identity cards can provide for absolute and certain identification of citizens, our respondents are aware of the shortcomings. IdM is often viewed as a set of technologies and processes to manage the risks associated with identity:

I think the mythology out there is that somehow we can prevent identity theft, it’s much like preventing the next winter, we can’t, but we can prepare for it.

What are your business needs and then how do you determine what kind of risk is associated with the application that you’re going to put on-line, and then how do you then map a technology to be able to enable that?

In fact, there are considerable and frequent suggestions in our data that not only is a single identification system not achievable or desirable, but that even if it was, full interoperability of government services is not a desirable end. Government agencies can be of the view that citizens do not want all government information relating to them to be bundled into, say, a single Portal.

In the private sector y one of the things that we strive for is that single view of the customer, and I would argue that y as a citizen, that’s totally unacceptable y there are functions within State government that are silos for a reason, and the reason that they’re silos is that nobody wants to log into their y homepage and see, you’re behind in your child support, and oh by the way, you got a parking ticket in Chicago y citizens expect us, as a security and privacy method to keep some of those things silo-ed.

In analysing our data we find that while there is no common or single definition of IdM, there is however a common set of outcomes that predominantly government stakeholders have from their IdM development efforts. We summarise them in the following section.

## Outcomes and expectations from electronic IdM

What outcomes are expected from electronic IdM? As explained above, as part of our research we coded our non-documentary data, that is, drawn from interviews and participant observation. We identified a total of 10 major themes across all the data, which are presented in Table 1 in order of prominence.

We have mapped these themes onto the Grimsley & Meehan (2007) framework as shown in Figure 2. Under this framework, the provision of e-ID-enabled services results in beneficial outcomes (expected or actual) and an increase (or otherwise) in the trusting relationship between the service provider and users.

## Trust

Much as the development of trust between citizens and government is key to the public value model, the issue of ‘trust’ was dominant in our data. Trust, in many forms, appears to be central to e-Government because of the special responsibility that governments have towards their citizens as custodians of personal data. The interest in trust extends in both directions of e-Government transactions as citizens appear to be rather sensitive about the electronic data held about them by governments (Tillman, 2003), and governments need a level of assurance that the remote user is legitimate. Superficially, at least, trust appears to be both an important moderator and outcome within its own right.

Among our respondents, the security of identity data and how it is transmitted and managed is strongly linked to the issue of trust. For example,

How could we have ever imagined that we could outsource parts of government to India as we do today? We could never have persuaded the bureaucrats to let us do this without the well established identity management systems that we have in place today. They provide the trust and confidence that our onlookers demand y and give our customers a fair amount of security in their transactions.

Relating this back to the literature, there is a considerable body of work that identifies trust as a critical element in the use and development of e-Business services on the Internet (e.g., Bart et al., 2005; Wang & Emurian, 2005), but only a few authors have also identified this as a factor in e-Government (see, e.g., Lowe, 2003). Yet, our respondents have carried around concerns over trust in e-Government for sometime:

y when managed properly, given our agreed rules, an identity federation eliminates the trust problems that have held us back in the past.

National security is perhaps a very macro instantiation of trust, where citizens trust the government to secure their safety and property. Yet, it was considered by only a few respondents to be an important factor in IdM, and its occurrence in our data is perhaps lower than expected. The general sentiment was that security threats such as terrorism could not be avoided with technological identity solutions – even those involving biometrics to identify persons at countries’ borders (such as with the U.K. and Dubai airports iris recognition systems). This relates back to the conceptualisation of IdM as a means of risk mitigation, rather than absolute identification, again suggesting that trust is better operationalised within the context of IdM as a corollary of risk management by citizens and other clients.

When discussing this theme, government respondents emphasised the main potential gains to be economic and mostly dismissed ‘national security’ as a reason for their interest in IdM technology. However, there was a recognition that political events can drive security concerns which then impact IdM:

After 9/11 we ended up with a huge number of unrelated identity initiatives, all trying to improve security.

Businesses and government respondents expressed the need for e-ID solutions so that they can record specific audit information about who (i.e., specific civil servants) accesses what data and when. The need for the nonrepudiated storage of access credentials for audit purposes was strongly expressed by some respondents. In some government jurisdictions, for example Belgium, this was said to be a legislative requirement. Several governments expressed the view that the ‘common access policies’ of passwords and user IDs which is typical today, do not provide sufficient accountability as these credentials can be easily compromised. There was considerable emphasis placed by some respondents on how to achieve a system of auditing in a cross-border context with pan-European e-Government applications that are envisaged by the European Commission. Several government respondents said that cross-border auditing was necessary, but were not sure how this could be achieved. Some respondents considered that a solution to this could be created using a combination of e-ID technologies and policies (i.e., together with the requisite legislation) agreed at a European level, and that this could have a positive impact on public value:

We handle really sensitive data, and if miss-used, could have a very bad impact on any citizen concerned, so you see, it is quite important for us [to] be able to track who has accessed what and when our laws also make us do this in any public service application. In the paper world, this does not happen; we fax, make phone calls and data leaks out all over the place but in [the] on-line world, we have a real opportunity to make it work.

## Outcomes

There are many potential outcomes from e-ID implementation. Perhaps national security should be conceptualised as an outcome rather than a variation of trust, and outcomes such as fairer or more effective services would be in keeping with the original tenants of public value. Thus, identifying what is a tangible outcome vs less tangible outcomes such as trusting citizens and improved national security is not obvious (a theme we will return to).

Our data, however, strongly presents outcomes that are efficiency based – which could be expected given the respondents. The strongest theme in the data relating to outcomes is online fraud and the possibility for IdM to reduce its incidence. Respondents often stressed that the technologies and architectures they were designing or deploying were supposed to ensure that the online person (legal or natural) is indeed who they say they are. Connected with this theme was the concern expressed that the online authority they choose to transact with is indeed the official and competent institution for the purpose of the transaction (this was sometimes also referred to as the risk of phishing). This is particularly pertinent in areas where citizens are expected to impart detailed personal information – such as for visa applications. Some government respondents believe that there are few effective ways to avoid phishing problems and many recommended e-ID solutions based on smartcards. These require users to have smart card readers normally attached to their computers, but are perceived by others as a major barrier to the take-up of government services.

Online fraud is a theme that is also dominant in the discourse of the popular media. Thus, it is difficult to ascertain whether the dominance of this theme in the data is fuelled by media coverage on identity fraud. Nonetheless, respondents strongly expressed a view that secure IdM solutions can dramatically reduce the possibility for fraudulent activity online. Respondents frequently mentioned the apparent ease with which online fraud and crime can be committed. The majority of respondents felt that strong e-ID solutions were needed to tackle identity theft and allow for safer online transactions for government:

You see how quickly the Internet grew, and now we kind of need to transform all of our services to take advantage of the technology. What hasn’t grown as fast are the safeguards that we need because that was never the point of the Internet in the first place. In my department we are of the view that IdM technology is critical in securing our applications and combating the problems of fraudulent activity, but of-course it’s only one of the things that can help us.

Despite the lack of data from governments concerning the true costs of e-Government, both governments and business respondents cited cost as a major desirable outcome influencing the need for interoperable and secure identity services. In the government domain, the maintenance of disparate identity silos and the high costs of user registration (for online services) provide compelling reasons to manage IdM more efficiently. Government respondents frequently cited ‘cost reduction’ as a major factor, but were often unable (or unwilling) to be more specific. Some administrations who were more confident about the state of their national efforts to manage identity were more willing to express orders of magnitude costs. For single e-Government application areas, some estimates given by respondents for cost savings through implementing e-ID solutions, ran into tens of millions of euro per annum:

y this stuff [IdM] forces us to do Business Process Reengineering and we are saving millions.

As discussed earlier, the adoption of e-Government services by businesses and citizens has been much slower than expected by governments. In providing a ‘secure’ online environment, government and software industry respondents in this study expressed a belief that user’s (citizens and businesses) reluctance to transact online will be reduced. However, the respondents who cited this as a driver for implementing IdM solutions were not specific with regards to the magnitude of this positive potential outcome, because of the difficulty in actually measuring such an effect. One respondent said

Identity Management gives us what we needed to provide our customers with a safe and secure way to do whatever they want with the government in the same convenient way that they are used to visiting their banks and shops online 24/7.

Respondents expressed the view that online authentication that is quick, simple and secure will provide citizens with a convenience in transacting with government that is not normally expected. This is an example of an intangible outcome, one that is an intermediate step to tangible outcomes.

Some governments have already gone a long way to providing ‘single sign on’ that provides authenticated access to several government services from a single entry point of validation (e.g., in the U.K., New Zealand and the U.S.A.). In some government schemes, however, this has not worked to the convenience of the citizens or businesses as there is still a requirement to register separately with each government application in order to add it to the single point of entry (e.g., as with the U.K. Government Gateway, 2008). In the U.S.A., the GSA’s e-Authentication programme found that transaction convenience providing 24/7 access to government services was one of the most dominant drivers in their context for implementing e-ID technologies. One government respondent said

Make this as simple as shopping and banking has become on-line and people will use it. Create complicated registration procedures, erm like, you know, some of our past systems, and no-one will use it, we know that.

## IT management

What are the stakeholder concerns relating to IT management? Given our background and engagement with many respondents that are responsible for IT/IS implementations, issues specifically relating to IT management naturally emerged. We present these here for completeness and reader interest, but see these as underlying the implementation of e-ID and not obvious items with our framework of Figure 2.

Governments and their software suppliers seem to be most interested in implementing e-ID solutions through Commercial Off-the-Shelf (COTS) products. This has been a challenge for governments because IdM standards that are supported by COTS products have been emerging over the last few years. Until recently vendors’ products were not interoperable despite saying so ‘on the box’. In 2004, the U.S. government established an interoperability lab to test the interoperability of vendors’ IdM COTS products. This placed pressure on the U.S. vendors to make their products interoperable. The use of COTS for IS is well documented in academic literature for industrial application (see, e.g., Light, 2005) and now emerges as a primary theme driving the use of secure ID in government. Governments assert that they are able to use COTS (which drives down their costs of deployment of IS) where products can support secure connections and the handling of secure identity data as part of a managed architecture (normally an Identity Federation). One respondent said

We got all the vendors in a room and told them to make their products work together it was the only way we could prepare the ground for our agencies to procure this stuff in a way that made sense for all of us.

Secure IdM solutions that are based on a ‘federation model’ (Shim et al., 2005) were regarded by respondents as a way to preserve their legacy systems for as long as possible. Some of the larger governments that participated in this study have significant legacy systems that have been transformed with front-end, web-enabled, modules to provide some online capabilities. Federated secure IdM solutions that appear to be most popular at present do help preserve legacy systems to an extent. Some government legacy systems are considered by respondents to be extremely costly, and complex to replace. There were also several references to wanting to preserve the ‘power-bases’ that existing (legacy) system ownership gave particular government departments. Therefore, solutions that preserved as many of these as possible were seen as easier to adopt.

In this regard, one respondent said

By reusing existing data stores we don’t get the enormous problems of having to justify why we are capturing personal data – because we’re not we leave things as they are and whack this on-top we have to make absolutely sure our agreements are up to scratch.

Several respondents stated that one of the possible benefits of implementing secure e-ID solutions is increased process integration within and especially between government applications. If online users can be authenticated with a high degree of confidence, then it was felt by several respondents that this could allow users to access a much wider range of services that could be offered in an integrated manner. The concept of ‘trusted federations’ of users was referred to frequently as the mechanism through which this could be (and is being) achieved. Federated identity solutions which can be created using COTS products, are considered central to this approach, and the major benefit for users is convenience that is delivered through process integration that can result. One respondent stated,

This identity management project that we are seen to be running here in my group, is really a Business Process Re-Engineering project.

## Another said,

By identifying users at a single trusted entry point in our information architecture, we can give them seamless access to lots of different application services, in one swoop. You see, previously we would have to register users separately for each service, and each service owner would need to administer that access control. Now we can pull all our services together in a tremendously more efficient and cost-effective architecture.

## Discussion

In this section, we critically reflect upon our presentation of the expectations and outcomes for IdM and e-ID. We consider the theoretical implications of our work, specifically the role of trust, the usefulness of the Grimsley and Meehan framework and the concept of public value. We also comment on the lack of national differences we observed in our global collection of data.

## Concepts of trust and employing a framework

Trust is, currently, a very popular concept across much management related research, and a commonly used construct in quantitative research. It typically appears as a moderator, especially in studies related to the consumer (e.g., see Vlachos et al. (2009), on trust as a mediator in perceptions of Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR), and Pappas and Flaherty (2008), on the influence of trust on social exchange with consumers). As stated earlier, we did not impose any restriction on trust as a concept, simply taking the data at face value. We now reconsider this.

We would suggest that notions of consumer trust, as pursued in the above referenced work, are inadequate when considering IdM. Carter and Be´langer (2005) is one example where trust in e-Government is considered from a consumer standpoint. Grimsley and Meehan, as mentioned above, see trust as ensuring legitimacy in government – this again focuses on the user as a consumer of services. Trust in IdM, however, operates in both directions: governments want to be able to authenticate citizens, and citizens want to be reassured that their data are safe with government. This dichotomy of trust in IdM needs to be captured. Moreover, whereas some of our data might suggest that stakeholders see trust as a moderator, Grimsley and Meehan view it as an outcome in its own right. So is ‘trusting’ an outcome, a moderator or simply an overused term that really only captures a range of related concerns and outcomes?

Ibbott & O’Keefe (2004), who draw upon Gallivan & Depledge (2003), suggest that trust in the context of electronic relationships mitigates relational risk, that is, trust between parties reduces the perceived risk of undesired outcomes. Theoretically, relational risk is a variation of economic trust – the risk that a partner might fail to meet its commitments (Das & Teng (2001), provide a good starting point to the relevant theory). Ibbott & O’Keefe (2004) also note that when trust is defined in terms of relational risk it can be seen as an alternative to control mechanisms, that is, partners trust the actions of each other as an alternative to specifying detailed protocols for other’s behaviour. We suggest that this definition of trust is useful for further exploration of IdM and e-ID. From our data, ‘trust problems’, ‘trusting’ and other instantiations of the concept of trust can be interpreted as a desire to reduce relational risk. When e-ID is employed, it is expected that the risk of undesired outcomes is reduced.

Clearly, for profit e-Commerce can also be viewed as a situation where trust needs to operate in both directions. However, identification of users in such contexts is generally under the specific control of the commercial organisation, whose use of customer data (e.g., credit card information) may be dictated by legislation or commercial practices. Moreover, consumers can choose to interact with providers in a market, taking their custom elsewhere as they desire. Users of e-Government systems and e-ID may not have a choice, and similarly governments have to provide services for all citizens, not simply those that self-select to be customers.

Defining trust in terms of relational risk better frames our understanding, but does not address the question of whether it is an outcome or a moderator, or both. As noted above we struggled to map some of our data onto the Grimsley and Meehan framework, in particular, identifying possible differences between tangible intermediate outcomes (e.g., cost reduction) and intangible outcomes (e.g., better national security). One way ahead might be to consider everything as an outcome from IdM-enabled services, but to recognise that different outcomes will only emerge over time. Table 2 suggests one categorisation, using the well-known World Bank maturity model of e-Government. (See Andersen & Henriksen (2006), for an explanation of the model, and a considerable extension of it.)

In this model, e-Government moves from simple interactivity with citizens, and the development of the supporting IT, through transaction-based services and delivery of government (i.e., public value) outcomes. It is debatable where some of our outcomes are placed on this model, and further work could suggest what else needs to be visible in this model to fully capture the expectations for IdM. A causal model could also be developed and tested; a challenge for stakeholders is to consider what outcomes and expectations they desire, or can deliver, at different stages of maturity, and the causal relationship between them.

## Notions of public value

Relating our findings back to Moore (1995), we can see the two differing public value activities of deploying ‘the money and authority entrusted to them to produce things of value to particular clients and beneficiaries’ and ‘establishing and operating an institution that meets citizens’ desires for properly ordered and productive public institutions’ in play. Our respondents are trying to produce things of value to clients and beneficiaries: online services coupled with transaction convenience that provide trustworthily applications that increase public value. But, a properly ordered and productive public institution should increase public value through reduction in costs. Moreover, what is perceived to be efficient IT management has a role to play in order and productivity. The desires to retain legacy systems and/or employ COTS, and a focus on process integration, appear to be fully sensible goals within the context of public value as the management of a productive institution.

Although our search for a viable framework led us to the concept of public value, the link between Grimsley and Meehan’s framework and Moore (1995) is tenuous at best. Moore provides an overarching strategic view that can be used to guide managerial behaviour, or interpret current behaviours. He does not explicitly address trust, and how the conceptualisation of trust in Grimsley and Meehan flows from Moore is not clear. Generating any specific model or framework that represents public value or includes ‘public value’ as a concept is challenging.

## National differences

As a final piece of analysis, it is worth considering the extent to which our data and analysis exhibits any national differences (or otherwise) between stakeholders. Seltsikas & van der Heijden (2010) compare government IdM approaches at a national level using three distinct country cases (U.S.A., New Zealand and the Netherlands). Their work suggests an essentially macro-level taxonomy of three different IdM implementation approaches. The outcomes and expectations that we have identified would appear to apply to all of Seltsikas and van der Heijden’s national IdM approaches.

This might suggest that despite the lack of common method or definitions in practice, the set of outcomes that we identify are common across different stakeholders and governments. However, we would caution researchers that this needs further consideration and testing. The link between expectations, outcomes and specific technologies will unfold over time. It is possible, for example, that a particular country- (or region-) specific approach may provide the outcomes identified in this paper quicker than in other countries, and in so doing may well alter the expectations in other countries.

## Conclusions

Very large amounts of money are being spent by governments, both local and national, in implementing e-ID and IdM technologies, processes and policies. Technological approaches at the user interface range from SINs through to password protection systems and national security cards. Similarly, vendors are engaged in the production and marketing of IdM and e-ID ‘solutions’.

The actual conceptualisation of IdM is, however, sophisticated and critical. There is a realisation that existing technology has been rebranded as IdM. The concept of IdM as a means of fully and consistently identifying all citizens, businesses or other transacting entities is not accepted by the stakeholders we engaged with. A view of IdM as risk mitigation prevails, such that the risk of incorrect identification of citizens is reduced, or the risk of potential malicious acts by citizens is reduced. There is an understanding that full interoperability might not be desired in the e-Government arena: citizens or government departments do not want sensitive information integrated with the mundane.

Our attempt to provide some order and theory to IdM has suggested that the notion of trust can be central to the conceptualisation of electronic IdM. However, we suggest this should be defined in terms of relational risk. When stakeholders deploy e-ID they are attempting to reduce the risk of undesired outcomes. IdM and e-ID are being deployed in systems which (at least in theory) must be accessible to all citizens, and must provide beneficial outcomes to both government and citizen users. The context is subtly different from that of e-Commerce where consumers have a choice of service providers – in e-Government trust must be established within an often single (or even mandatory) service provision. We suggest that there are considerable opportunities for further research that delineates the catalysts or inhibitors of trust in public service relationships. Such findings could help guide future implementations of IdM systems.
