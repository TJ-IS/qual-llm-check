# A theoretical model of intentional social action in online social networks

Christy M.K. Cheung <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Matthew K.O. Lee <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Finance and Decision Sciences, Hong Kong Baptist University, HKSAR, China

<sup>b</sup> Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, HKSAR, China

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 19 November 2008 Received in revised form 5 October 2009 Accepted 21 December 2009 Available online 4 January 2010

Keywords: We-intention Social in<sup>fl</sup>uence Social identity Subjective norm Group norm Online social networks IS adoption IS continuance

## a b s t r a c t

Online social networks (Facebook, MySpace, LinkedIn and the like) have become truly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant new phenomena in human communication and interaction patterns and may have a profound impact in the way people communicate and connect with each other. In this study, the decision to use an online social network is conceptualized as intentional social action and the relative impact of the three modes of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence processes (compliance, internalization, and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation) on intentional social action to use (collective intention) is examined. An empirical study of Facebook users (n=389) found that collective intention to use a social networking site is determined by both subjective norm and social identity. Further, social identity is found to be a second-order latent construct comprised of cognitive, evaluative, and affective (<sup>fi</sup>rst-order) components. Implications for research and practice are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The growth and public popularity of Web 2.0 applications have created a new world of collaboration and communication. More than a billion individuals around the world are connected. They support themselves through wikis, podcasts, discussion posts, comments, and instant messaging. New generation online social networks (a Web 2.0 application), as enabled by the widespread diffusion of high-speed Internet, have even emerged as a mainstream communication and interaction modality with ever-increasing signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in the information society in which we live today. Facebook, MySpace, LinkedIn, and other similar online social networking sites provide online spaces where individuals can create a profile and connect that profile to others to create a personal network. In online social networking sites, social interaction and connection is the objective. These online social networking sites give everyone a place to share their personal stories, in words, pictures, and videos with their friends. They also connect people with friends and others who work, study, and live around them. They help people learn more about events, parties, and other social functions. Participation and continuance in online social networks represents a new social phenomenon that depends largely on the interactions with other users in a personal network.

While recent information systems (IS) studies have started to address the issue of online social networks [15,20,21], these studies basically adopted the classical individual-based models to investigate the use of this new or complex communication and interaction phenomena. The classical individual-based models (I-Intention Models) assume that the use of an information system solely depends on individual personal reasons and perceived social pressure [8,9,26]. This classic approach has also been widely used in IS research to study the continuance of an information system (i.e., whether users will continue to use an IS is depended solely on their personal motivations) [5,6].

Bagozzi [1] however argued that “Much of human behavior is not best characterized by an individual acting in isolation. (p. 247)”. In particular, the decision to use online social networking technologies represents a social phenomenon that largely depends on the interactions among users and the use of social technologies can make sense only when a group of individuals are willing to use and continue to use the technology together. For example, a friend plans to use an instant messenger for doing group projects together. However, the use of an instant messenger for group projects is depended on whether other friends are willing to use it. In addition, their usage experiences will create a norm for them to continuously use it for their other group projects. Thus, it would be more appropriate to examine the decision to use online social networks using the intentional social action perspective.

The main objective of this study is to develop and empirically validate a research model on intentional social action in online social networks. In this study, Collective Intention (We-Intention) is adopted to study the use of online social networks. Previous studies in philosophy focused mostly on the conceptual and logical aspects of collective intention [7,13,25]. Not until recently, Bagozzi and his colleague [4] developed the measurement and conducted the hypothesis testing of collective intention models. The second objective of this study is to borrow the measures developed by Dholakia et al. [10] and to validate their measures of collective intention and the three modes of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the context of online social networks

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. The next section addresses the theoretical background and research hypotheses. The third section describes a survey study of users of online social networking sites (e.g., Facebook) to empirically test our research model. The fourth section discusses the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our empirical study. Finally, the paper is concluded by discussing the implications for both research and practice.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses development

Prior research on group, cultural, and social aspects of technology use has been scant in the IS literature [1]. In this research, the participation and continuance in social networking sites (e.g., Facebook) is conceptualized as an intentional social action where users regard themselves as part of the social fabrics of online social networks. This type of group-intention to jointly participate in a social action is referred to as “We-Intention”. The concept of intentional social action will be <sup>fi</sup>rst introduced.

Social in<sup>fl</sup>uence has been widely used to explain group and collective behavior [4]. The research model explores the role that compliance (subjective norm), internalization (group norm), and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (social identity) play in explaining intentional social actions in online social networks. A description of the model and its research variables, as well as justi<sup>fi</sup>cations for the research hypotheses will be addressed. The research model of this investigation is developed as depicted in Fig. 1.

## 2.1. Intentional social action

According to Bagozzi and Lee [4], there are three levels of explanation on decision making in social research, including (1) classical individual based models (a personal intention to perform an individual act by oneself), (2) contingency consistency and other normative based models (a personal intention to perform an individual act but with consideration of the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence), and (3) a group-based model (the model comprises of both personal intention and social intention to perform a group act). Two important concepts arise in this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, I-Intentions (classical individual-based models) and We-Intentions (group-based models).

In the past two decades of Information Systems (IS) research, the predominant focus has been put on the classical individual based models [8,9,26]. The intention to use (I-Intention) a new technology depends on individual reasons and perceived social pressure for using the new technology. This approach is particularly useful to the previous studies, as the focus is mostly standalone information systems. Decision makers are more likely to form an intention, or to act in concert with their attitudes, insofar as perceived norms encouraging making such a decision accordingly. Web 2.0 creates a new world of collaboration. People come together to create, collaborate, and contribute their expertise and wisdom. They support themselves and each other through comments, discussions, and instant messaging. Unlike the traditional individual-based approach (personal intention to perform an individual act), social interaction and connection is the objective in Web 2.0 technologies, including online social networks. Associated with these new phenomena in human communication and interaction patterns, we believe that We-Intention, encapsulating social behaviors by the collectivity, is a more appropriate approach to study user participation in online social networks.

Tuomela [25] de<sup>fi</sup>ned We-Intention as “commitment of an individual to participate in joint action, and involves in an implicit and explicit agreement between the participants to engage in that joint action” (p.2). This de<sup>fi</sup>nition emphasizes the collective commitment among group members and the collective acceptance of joint action. Once members are collectively committed to performing the group action jointly, they are socially committed to each other and cannot be released from the interdependent obligations merely by changing their minds. Different from traditional individual intention (I-Intention), We-Intention emphasizes collective agreement and commitment involved in the performance of a group behavior. In addition, people with We-Intention view a group activity holistically, instead of in an atomistic sense [4]. For instance, “We intend to play an online game together” refers to the case that a person sees the self as a part of the team and takes the online game as a group action rather than a personal act. Obviously, it is different from I-Intention to perform an individual act (e.g., I intend to check my email message box), where other people are not involved as essential parts of the behavior. Compared with I-Intention, We-Intention highlights the individual commitment in collectivity and the social nature of group action. Therefore, We-Intention is a more appropriate approach to study online social activities (e.g., online social networking).

## 2.2. Social influence processes

Social in<sup>fl</sup>uence has been widely used to explain group and collective behavior [4]. Prior IS cognitive behavior models however consider only one aspect of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, namely subjective norms, which re<sup>fl</sup>ect social pressure from signi<sup>fi</sup>cant others to perform a behavior. In the context of group activity (group behavior), it is important to take other types of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence into account. Kelman [18] proposed three modes of social in<sup>fl</sup>uences, including compliance (subjective norm), internalization (group norm), and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (social identity). All three modes of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence might function to different degrees depending on the circumstances. For example, compliance is particularly in<sup>fl</sup>uential in determining user's initial decision to use a group technology (i.e. technological platform for supporting group activities) since the user has no prior usage experience and hence will tend to rely more on prevailing subjective norms to decide whether to try out the new technology. Once the user has started adopting the group technology and interacting within the referent group, internalization comes into play more prominently in determining continuing usage behavior. After extended group usage, a sense of social identity may emerge which in turn will affect continuous usage behavior. Although the effects of these three modes of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence do overlap at any point in time, their relative strengths depend on the stage of adoption process. The impact of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence on online social technologies has started gaining attention in academic research. For instances, Dholakia et al. [10] proposed and empirically tested a social in<sup>fl</sup>uence model of virtual communities. Bagozzi and Dholakia [3] proposed a group-referent intentional actions model of user participation in Linux User Groups. Song and Kim [22] extended theory of planned behavior with social in<sup>fl</sup>uence theory to investigate the acceptance of an avatar service.

The social in<sup>fl</sup>uence underlying subjective norm re<sup>fl</sup>ects the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of expectations from signi<sup>fi</sup>cant others and represents what Kelman terms “Compliance”. In IS adoption research, the compliance process appeared to be paramount. Before users have any actual usage experience with a new system, second-hand information, particularly from the primary reference groups (family or friends), are important for their usage decisions. After users gain <sup>fi</sup>rst-hand experiences with the system, the compliance process is expected to play a less in<sup>fl</sup>uential role. Karahanna et al. [16] and Taylor and Todd [24] compared the adoption decision models between both experienced and inexperienced users, and both studies found that subjective norm no longer plays an important role in explaining usage decision for experienced users. However, it remains to be seen whether this is true for users of highly interactive online social network systems since, in contrast with standalone systems, these users are more exposed to other people's in<sup>fl</sup>uences as they interact in the social network.

H1. A stronger subjective norm leads to a higher level of We-Intention to participate in an online social networking site.

The second mode of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence characterized by group norm is similar to the term “Internalization” as suggested by Kelman [18]. Internalization is the adoption of a decision based on the similarity of one's values with the values of other group members. In other words, group norms are shared values or goals perceived by the user between himself/herself and other group members. Kelman [17] also suggested that if the values of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing agents are congruent with the recipients’ own value systems and the recipients <sup>fi</sup>nd the content of the behavior intrinsically rewarding, they will be willing to engage in the target behavior because they want to. Similarly, Bagozzi and Dholakia [2] argued that if a person realizes that he/she and other members share one or more common values or goals, the person will form an intention to participate in the social group. Thus, if a user realizes that he/she shares similar values or goals with other users in an online social networking site, we expect his/her We-Intention will increase.

H2. Stronger group norm leads to a higher level of We-Intention to participate in an online social networking site.

The third mode of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, refers to the selfawareness of one's membership in a group, as well as the emotional and evaluative signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of this membership [23]. There are three major components of social identity [11], including cognitive social identity, evaluative social identity, and affective social identity. Social identity may be conceptualized as a second-order latent construct with cognitive, evaluative, and affective (<sup>fi</sup>rst-order) components [2].

▪ Cognitive social identity: The self-categorization process renders the self stereotypically interchangeable with other group members, and stereotypically distinct from outsiders.

▪ Evaluative social identity: The evaluation of self-worth on the basis of belonging to a particular group.

▪ Affective social identity: A sense of emotional involvement with the group, which is characterized by identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with, involvement in, and emotional attachment to the group.

Each of the three components of social identity is expected to have main effects on We-Intention of using the online social networking site. In online social networking sites, social identity arises through interaction with other users in the personal network as well as in online social groups. Thus, if a user holds strong social identity toward an online social networking site, we expect his/her We-Intention will increase.

H3. A stronger social identity leads to a higher level of We-Intention to participate in an online social networking site.

## 3. Research method

Facebook (http://www.facebook.com), an online social networking site, was used in this study to examine intentional social actions in online social networks. We believe that Facebook is appropriate for the current study as it surged in global popularity since it opened its social network beyond colleges and universities in Spring 2007. In addition, its features encourage users to interact and create their own networks and expand new networks through the participation in online social groups. It is a good example of new generation online social network which is highly interactive.

## 3.1. Data collection

A convenience sample of Facebook users (student groups) were created by inviting volunteers to participate in our study. An invitation message with the URL to the online questionnaire was posted on a number of online social groups of Facebook (student groups). To increase the response rate, an incentive of supermarket vouchers was offered as lucky draw prizes. A screening question was used to ensure that the respondents were current active users of Facebook. In addition, they were told to follow an instruction before <sup>fi</sup>lling out the questionnaire:

“Imagine that you are logging on to the Internet to engage in the group interaction and communication that you described above. You have a number of friends within Facebook that you regularly interact with. Please picture brie<sup>fl</sup>y in your mind the name and image of each online friend. Then, write your nickname and their nicknames in the table below (Please put at most <sup>fi</sup>ve online friends)”.

These instructions were designed to capture the respondents collectivity of using Facebook. In addition, these are the online friends that they should refer to when answering other questions (See Appendix A for details).

## 3.2. Measures

The constructs of interest to this study were We-Intention, Subjective Norm, Group Norm, Cognitive Social Identity, Affective Social Identity, and Evaluative Social Identity. The measures of the constructs in this study were borrowed from previous literature (See Appendix A). All constructs were measured using multi-item perceptual scales. That means each construct was measured by a few items for construct validity and reliability.

## 3.3. Sample profile

A total of 389 useful online questionnaires were collected in this study. Among the 389 respondents, 54% were male and 46% were female. A majority of the respondents (74%) aged 19–23. 12% respondents used Facebook everyday and 60% used Facebook every 2 days. The top three most-frequently used functions were photo sharing (29%), posting comments (22%), and sending free virtual gifts (14%). Around 40% respondents had 101–150 friends on Facebook.

## 4. Data analysis

LISREL 8.80 was used in the current investigation. LISREL is one of the most widely used Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) techniques in IS research. Following the two-step analytical procedures [14], the measurement model was <sup>fi</sup>rst evaluated and the structural model was then assessed. The rationale of this two-step approach is to ensure our conclusions on structural relationship drawn from a set of measurement instruments with desirable psychometric properties.

## 4.1. Measurement model

Convergent validity indicates the extent to which the items of a scale that are theoretically related should correlate highly. A composite reliability of 0.70 or above and an average variance extracted of more than 0.50 are deemed acceptable [14]. Table 1 summarizes the item loading, composite reliability, average variance extracted, mean, and standard deviation of the measures of the constructs of our research model. All measures ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the recommended levels, with the composite reliability ranging from 0.78 to 0.97 and the average variance extracted ranging from 0.64 to 0.94.

Discriminant validity is the extent to which the measure is not a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of some other variable. It is indicated by low correlations between the measure of interest and the measures of other constructs. Discriminant validity of the measures is demonstrated when the squared root of the average variance extracted for each construct is higher than its correlations with all other constructs [12]. Table 2 shows that the square root of average variance extracted for each construct is greater than the correlations between the constructs and all other constructs. The results suggested an adequate discriminant validity of the measures used in the current study.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

The motivation of this study is to examine the role of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence processes in determining the collective social action in the context of online social networks. Participation in an online social networking site is conceptualized as an intentional social action where users regard themselves as part of the social fabric. The research model is developed based on previous literature. Intention to use online social networking sites together is explained in terms of the three social in<sup>fl</sup>uence processes (subjective norm, group norm, and social identity). The measurement model is con<sup>fi</sup>rmed with adequate convergent and discriminant validity of all measures, and the structural model explains 32% of the variance. Except for the path of group norms to We-Intention to use online social networking sites, all path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients are found statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in this research model.

## 5.1. Implications for theory and research

While the social and collective nature of Web 2.0 is apparent, there is relatively little theory-driven empirical research available in the IS literature to address these new complex communication and interaction phenomena. This study is one of the very <sup>fi</sup>rst studies attempts to explain the collective behavior in Web 2.0 applications using intentional social action. Over the past two decades, IS researchers have demonstrated considerable interest in measuring personal intentions in traditional information systems adoption where the usage experience does not depend on other users [5,6,8,9,26]. In contrast, online social networking sites connect people with friends and others and help people learn more about events, parties, and other social functions. Interaction and connection is the objective of most users. This study is one of the very <sup>fi</sup>rst studies that measure the intentional social action (encapsulating social behaviours by the collective) in the context of online social networks.

The research model is built on the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence theory. In this speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of online social networks, collective intention is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly explained by two of the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence variables, including subjective norm and social identity. The results of this study not only indicate that intentional social action (We-Intention to use online social networks) can be explained by the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence processes, but also demonstrate that the three modes of social in<sup>fl</sup>uences might function to different degrees depending on the circumstances. Our results have actually challenged and extended the existing theories to some degree. This has added a contingency perspective to the relevant theoretical lens.

In this study, subjective norm is particularly important in determining user decision to use an online social networking site. The result does not support the conventional thinking about the role of subjective norm in the post-adoption (continuance) stage, when users already gain <sup>fi</sup>rst-hand experiences with a new technology and tend to depend less on others’ recommendations to make their own decision. One possible explanation is that Web 2.0 is all about interaction and collaboration. Users are more exposed to other people's in<sup>fl</sup>uences as they interact in the social network. A second kind of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence processes, social identity, is also found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in explaining intentional social action in online social networks. The construct of social identity consists of three interrelated components: selfawareness of one's membership in a group (cognitive social identity), evaluative signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of one's membership in a group (evaluative social identity), and emotional signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of one's membership in a group (affective social identity). Thus, this calls into question the generalizability of prior research on IT continuance and suggests a contingency approach may be more appropriate.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

Apart from the theoretical contributions, the results of this study also provide some insights to practitioners. In particular, online social networks create a lot of business values and opportunities. For online social networks, keeping members actively involved in their networking sites will have a higher chance of raising fund and attracting top advertisers. For example, UK-heavy social network Bebo got US \$15 million funding in 2006 and was acquired by AOL in 2008 for US \$850 million. For other companies, understanding why and how people come and use these online social networks can help companies exploit these networks and stay competitive in the global market. In particular, it is important to use online social networks as customers do. The driving forces behind online social networks are connections and community, and the usage is largely depended on subjective norm and social identity. Companies should use these online social networks to better reach and understand their customers. The sales people can come in contact with their customers and better understand them in online social networks. They can also join the many available communities of practice and af<sup>fi</sup>nity groups in social networking sites and contribute to groups by answering questions and creating values in groups. This may result in a positive response from other users (and potential customers). Customers will probably treat them as “friends”. As shown in our study, users are greatly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by subjective norms. Thus, the indirect results of this mutual understanding will likely be increased sales for companies and enhanced customer relationship.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

In interpreting the results of this study, one must pay attention to a number of limitations. First, to keep the model parsimonious, the proposed research model only focuses on the impact of the three modes of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence (subjective norm, group norm, and social identity) on intentional social action. The model explains 32% of the variance, though it is considered as high in social sciences studies, future studies should continue to enrich the existing model by adding social technological factors, such as social presence, media richness, and the like. Second, the study represents mostly student users of online social networks. Care must be taken when extrapolating the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other types of online social networks that target different groups of users (e.g., professionals in LinkedIn). According to the Press Room of Facebook (www.facebook.com/press/info.php?statistics), there are more than 350 million active users in Facebook. It is not feasible to reach the whole population and conduct an online survey based on a simple random sampling method. In future studies, researchers should stratify Facebook users based on interest groups (e.g., university student groups, sport groups, professional groups, and etc.) and take a random sample in each subgroup. Using this sampling method, a more representative sample of Facebook users can be obtained and the results will be more generalizable. Third, because of the cross-sectional nature of the study, spurious cause-effect inferences may be presented. A longitudinal design is needed in the future for valid cause-effect inferences. Particularly, it would be interesting to examine the relationship between We-Intention and actual group behavior. Although prior studies have examined I-Intention and we argue that in the context of social networks We-Intention will be more appropriate predictor of behavior, future studies should also incorporate other factors (e.g., habit [19]) in a longitudinal setting to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the dominant set of factors at play in predicting collective behavior in online social networks.

Web 2.0 application facilitates many kinds of collective and collaborative activities around us. For examples, we jointly create and write weblogs for information sharing. We create knowledge base together with wikis. We collaborate and work on research projects using instant messaging. Group-based models represent a relatively unexplored area in the IS literature. Considering that this study has raised many interesting questions, it is believed that the current study triggers additional theorizing and empirical investigation aimed at a better understanding of intentional social actions in online social networks. Further, future research should continue this line of research by investigating other Web 2.0 applications, such as wiki, weblog, MSN, social bookmarking, and other social networking services.
