# Bringing Machine Learning Systems into Clinical Practice: A Design Science Approach to Explainable Machine Learning-Based Clinical Decision Support Systems

Luisa Pumplun,<sup>1</sup> Felix Peters,<sup>2</sup> Joshua F. Gawlitza,<sup>3</sup> Peter Buxmann<sup>4</sup>

## Abstract

Clinical decision support systems (CDSSs) based on machine learning (ML) hold great promise for improving medical care. Technically, such CDSSs are already feasible but physicians have been skeptical about their application. In particular, their opacity is a major concern, as it may lead physicians to overlook erroneous outputs from ML-based CDSSs, potentially causing serious consequences for patients. Research on explainable AI (XAI) offers methods with the potential to increase the explainability of black-box ML systems. This could significantly accelerate the application of MLbased CDSSs in medicine. However, XAI research to date has mainly been technically driven and largely neglects the needs of end users. To better engage the users of ML-based CDSSs, we applied a design science approach to develop a design for explainable ML-based CDSSs that incorporates insights from XAI literature while simultaneously addressing physicians’ needs. This design comprises five design principles that designers of ML-based CDSSs can apply to implement user-centered explanations, which are instantiated in a prototype of an explainable ML-based CDSS for lung nodule classification. We rooted the design principles and the derived prototype in a body of justificatory knowledge consisting of XAI literature, the concept of usability, and an online survey study involving 57 physicians. We refined the design principles and their instantiation by conducting walk-throughs with six radiologists. A final experiment with 45 radiologists demonstrated that our design resulted in physicians perceiving the ML-based CDSS as more explainable and usable in terms of the required cognitive effort than a system without explanations.

Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, Clinical Decision Support Systems, Explainability, Usability, Cognitive Effort Perspective, Design Science Research

## 1 Introduction

The application of machine learning (ML) holds great promise for medicine. The technology is increasingly being used to support various process steps in medicine, such as hospital admissions, diagnosis, and treatment (Sutton et al., 2020). In this context, recent research has investigated how ML can help develop drugs against COVID-19 or detect cancer at an early stage (McKinney et al., 2020; Mohapatra et al., 2020). The size of the ML market in healthcare has already multiplied elevenfold since 2014, and estimates show that ML might be able to save the US healthcare industry up to \$150 billion per year by 2026 (Collier & Fu, 2020). As a subfield of artificial intelligence (AI), ML enables systems to learn from data autonomously. This is accomplished using algorithms that allow the ML system to analyze incoming data for patterns and store them in models. The trained ML models can then be applied to new data to make predictions about the malignancy of a lung nodule, for example (Ardila et al., 2019; Venkadesh et al., 2021).

Due to ethical, legal, and validation aspects, ML systems in medicine are currently only conceivable as clinical decision support systems (CDSSs) to assist human users such as physicians in their workflows, making them faster and more effective (Roshanov et al., 2013). Indeed, the need for such ML-based CDSSs exists because clinicians need to work swiftly and precisely to provide adequate care to all patients, despite increasing challenges such as the rising age of the overall population and the COVID-19 pandemic (Caley & Sidhu, 2011; McCabe et al., 2020). However, beyond legal and validation hurdles, the opacity of ML-based CDSSs and their susceptibility to errors continues to prevent their widespread use (Adadi & Berrada, 2018). Not only are ML-based CDSSs based on statistical pattern recognition, which can produce erroneous outputs, they are also often incomprehensible due to the large amounts of data and complex algorithms used to train the underlying ML models (Diakopoulos, 2016; Rudin, 2019). This is particularly problematic for applications in medicine because of the high costs associated with errors in patient diagnosis or treatment based on suggestions of ML-based CDSSs (Lebovitz et al. 2022).

Under the umbrella term explainable AI (XAI), a wide variety of approaches have been developed to make the inner functionality of ML systems more tangible, typically in the form of human-understandable explanations (Arrieta et al., 2019). An ML system is considered explainable if it “produces details or reasons to make its functioning clear or easy to understand” (Arrieta et al., 2019, p. 7). In this regard, explainability focuses on the comprehensibility of ML systems’ inner workings to humans (Arrieta et al., 2019; Guidotti et al., 2018). However, this discussion has thus far been primarily conducted from the perspective of technical professionals and has largely ignored the fact that users of ML-based CDSSs are not only data scientists, mathematicians, and developers but end users like physicians (Meske et al., 2022). As a result, the available explanations are often complex and challenging to grasp for those without a statistical background, which may lead physicians to misjudge ML-based CDSSs and overlook severe errors in clinical applications (Adadi & Berrada, 2018; Bhatt et al., 2020; Lyytinen et al., 2021).

As part of the long-standing concept of usability, scholars have placed end users and their satisfaction at the center of IT system development (Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002; Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015; Venkatesh & Ramesh, 2006). Special care has been taken to ensure that IT systems are user friendly, implying that users should not be overwhelmed by interacting with them (Bevan et al., 1991). To ensure that physicians can also interact with ML-based CDSSs quickly and safely and provide the best possible treatment to patients, designers of these systems need to reflect more strongly on which explanations are useful and how they should be presented to increase the explainability of ML-based CDSSs without cognitively overstraining physicians. To address this trade-off, this study seeks to facilitate the design of more explainable ML-based CDSSs that simultaneously maintain systems’ usability in clinical practice. We thus investigate the following research question:

RQ: How can better explainability of ML-based CDSSs be enabled without reducing their usability for physicians?

To consider the end-user perspective, we relied on a design science approach consisting of six phases: namely awareness of the problem, suggestion, development, demonstration, evaluation, and conclusion (Peffers et al., 2007; Vaishnavi & Kuechler, 2007). These phases were iterated twice to develop a design for explainable ML-based CDSSs, consisting of five design principles for user-centric explanations and instantiations. To this end, we first derived a problem-oriented goal of the design in the form of meta-requirements based on the existing literature in the field of XAI and usability. We then consulted the identified literature and amplified it with the help of an online survey study involving 57 physicians to suggest four initial design principles that address the derived meta-requirements. To render the design principles more tangible and allow for evaluation, we implemented these in a wireframed prototype of a more explainable ML-based CDSS that can be applied to detect tumors on computed tomography (CT) images of the thorax. To conclude the first design cycle, the derived design was evaluated via walk-throughs with six radiologists. The results of the evaluation served as the basis for the second design cycle, in which we refined and expanded the existing design principles to five principles and used them to create a web-based prototype. Based on an experimental survey study employing 45 radiologists, we successfully evaluated the results against the metarequirements at the end of the second design cycle.

While the derived design principles can guide developers on how to design explanations for MLbased CDSSs in a way that increases explainability but does not overwhelm physicians, the instantiated prototype offers concrete suggestions for implementing ML-based CDSSs in clinical practice. In doing so, we take a deeply user-centric perspective to provide a blueprint for researchers and designers in the medical environment who are striving to develop more applicable, explainable ML-based CDSSs.

The paper is structured as follows: We first derive the meta-requirements for explainable ML-based CDSSs from the related literature on XAI and the concept of usability. Next, we present an overview of the conducted design science process. We then describe how the design principles and instantiated prototype were created and present the respective outcomes, which we evaluate afterward. We conclude by setting our findings in the context of current research and practical endeavors and discussing what limitations and further research opportunities arise from the conducted design science research.

## 2 Deriving the Meta-Requirements from Literature

It has been about 50 years since early CDSSs were developed based on explicitly formulated rules to support medical professionals (e.g., MYCIN; Shortliffe, 1976). Currently, new powerful computational approaches based on ML are being widely discussed in research to support physicians in coping with increasing numbers of patients and complex cases and making diagnosis and treatment decisions more quickly and effectively (Miller & Brown, 2018). Especially for radiological diagnostic tasks, many image-based ML-based CDSSs could soon become relevant (Shen et al., 2019). Specific systems may assist radiologists, for example, in diagnosing breast cancer, stroke, or intracerebral hemorrhage by alerting physicians to anomalies detected on CT images (Cheng et al., 2016; Reyes et al., 2020). Yet only a few ML-based CDSSs have been implemented in clinical practice, preventing their potential from being fully leveraged—a profound problem marking the entry point of our design science process. In this process, the meta-requirements that must be met in order for the problem to be solved must first be defined. In this context, meta-requirements indicate the overarching design goal (Gregor & Hevner, 2013), i.e., the desired effect of a solution (Kuechler & Vaishnavi, 2012). In the following, we derive the meta-requirements from the existing literature, which shows that ML-based CDSSs are not being used primarily because they tend to be black boxes, making physicians reluctant to accept advice from such systems (Lebovitz et al., 2022; Reyes et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019). Therefore, scientists are increasingly calling for explanations of ML systems, especially in high-risk environments such as medicine (e.g., Lebovitz et al., 2022). Research on XAI is responding to this call.

## 2.1 Explainable Artificial Intelligence

XAI, a research field at the intersection of computer science, human-computer interaction, and social sciences seeks to develop ML systems whose outputs are understandable to humans (Arrieta et al., 2019; T. Miller, 2019). Here, the primary interface between ML systems and humans is explanations, which provide reasoning for the decisions made by ML systems with the primary goal of increasing explainability (Miller, 2019). Apart from explainability, other terms are discussed in the context of XAI research—for example, transparency and interpretability, which are often used but not always clearly distinguished from each other (Rosenfeld & Richardson, 2019). We follow Rosenfeld and Richardson (2019) and refer to ML systems as transparent if they are already inherently understandable due to the selected ML models, such as decision trees, and do not require additional explanations, making the term less relevant for this research. Interpretability is very similar to the notion of explainability. However, while interpretability describes how well users can predict what output an ML system will provide as changes are made to inputs and parameters, explainability emphasizes understanding the underlying mechanism. Thus, users of interpretable ML systems can predict what will happen, while explainable ML systems also explain why (Gall, 2019). Interpretability thus contributes to the accessibility of ML systems to users, but explainability emphasizes making ML systems comprehensible to humans through explanations (Arrieta et al., 2019; Asatiani et al., 2021; Meske et al., 2022; Rosenfeld & Richardson, 2019). Therefore, explainability is at the core of XAI; thus, we refer to explainability throughout this research.

XAI research has been dealing with the technical realization of explanations for ML systems for some time, which has led to a variety of different explanatory approaches. To gain an overview of existing explanations, Table 1 provides a synoptic ontology, including a two-level categorization of explanations separated into higher-order categories and more concrete types presented in the first two columns, example implementations for explanation types in the third column, and sources for these implementations from the XAI literature in the last column. At the first level, the ontology distinguishes three overarching explanation categories according to their functionality: (1) explanations that concern the ML model and its performance, (2) explanations that contribute to a global understanding of ML systems, and (3) explanations that aim to make specific, local outputs of the systems more comprehensible. At the second level, it further divides explanations into more concrete types. While the XAI literature identifies various types for model and local explanations, there is no further distinction for global explanations. This is mainly due to the lack of sophisticated technical approaches for global explanations in modern ML systems (Guidotti et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2018).

Deploying explanations such as those found in the ontology serves three goals. First, generating explanations for ML systems can help data scientists, mathematicians, and developers better detect errors and improve ML model performance (Bhatt et al., 2020; Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2017; Lipton, 2018; Meske et al., 2022). Second, explanations may be required to comply with legal or regulatory demands, such as the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (Bhatt et al., 2020; Cheng et al., 2019; Reyes et al., 2020). In organizational contexts, explanations might further be used to pass ML systems’ internal or external quality assurance tests, referred to as ML model audits (Bhatt et al., 2020; Reyes et al., 2020). Third, explanations might help to enhance the interaction between an ML system and the end user. Recently, IS research has begun to delve into this aspect in more depth, concluding that explainability must encompass not only the technical aspects of implementation but also the needs arising from potential end users (Asatiani et al., 2021; Berente et al., 2021). If these things are taken into account, we expect that increased explainability will be associated with several positive effects, such as the increased ability to evaluate the ML system (Jussupow et al., 2021; Lebovitz et al., 2021), more effective communication with the system (Kane et al.,

2021), increased trust-building (Benbya et al., 2021; Schuetz & Venkatesh, 2020), and opportunities for learning (Jussupow et al., 2021; Lebovitz et al., 2021). In addition, the explainability of ML systems can increase end users’ confidence in following the advice of these systems while maintaining their perceived authority and understanding of the domain (Asatiani et al., 2021; Strich et al., 2021; van den Broek et al., 2022). As a result, prior IS research has suggested that explanations for ML systems will have far-reaching consequences for end-user interactions.

Despite the growing literature on XAI, the technical approaches to implementing explanations, and the potential impacts on users, no explicit guidelines for the appropriate design of explanations have yet been identified. Indeed, several technical limitations still exist in generating explanations for complex ML systems (Bhatt et al., 2020; Liao et al., 2020). Most explanations for ML systems are generated by statistical algorithms and can thus become very unintuitive to humans and may contain incorrect information (Miller, 2019; Rudin, 2019). While researchers and practitioners agree on the overarching goals of deploying XAI, it remains largely unclear how to design explanations in practice to enable seamless user interactions with ML systems. Indeed, “creating machines that divulge and explain their behavior in a way humans can interpret is difficult” (Lyytinen et al., 2021, p.12). Yet designing ML-based CDSSs to be more explainable is inevitable if they are to be used in clinical practice where human lives are at stake. In particular, given that the existing XAI literature promises various beneficial effects of increased explainability, our first meta-requirement is to maximize the explainability of ML-based CDSSs. Possible lessons for the user-friendly design of more explainable ML systems may be found in the decades of literature in the field of usability, as discussed below.

## 2.2 The Concept of Usability and the Cognitive Effort Perspective

The usability of IT systems is considered a highly relevant predictor for user acceptance, making it an eminently relevant concept in IT and its use. Researchers from various disciplines have established fundamental theories of human-computer interaction, derived recommendations for application design from them, and developed and validated procedures for methodologically evaluating usability (Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002; Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015; Shneiderman, 1996; Venkatesh & Ramesh, 2006). IS research has adopted a user-centric viewpoint, which is concerned with designing IT systems in a way that reduces the cognitive effort required to operate the system (Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002; Bevan et al., 1991). To explain why this research stream seeks to minimize cognitive effort, we invoke the cognitive effort perspective. This perspective captures the idea that individuals are limited in their overall mental resources but need to expend cognitive effort to complete a given task. Cognitive effort is defined as the number of elementary information processes that an individual needs to engage in to accomplish this task (Gregor & Benbasat, 1999; Johnson & Payne, 1985; Todd & Benbasat, 1991). Having limited mental resources, individuals perform a cost-benefit analysis and weigh the required cognitive effort against the benefits they will likely derive from a course of action to decide what to focus on (Mao & Benbasat, 2000; Payne et al., 1993).

Gregor and Benbasat (1999) applied the cognitive effort perspective to examine the role of explanations for IT systems. When designing explanations, the main challenge is balancing their completeness and soundness, as explanations are inherently helpful but too much information can also cause cognitive overload for users (Eiband et al., 2018; Kizilcec, 2016). Despite their benefits, explanations for IT systems can thus lead to such a high amount of required cognitive effort among users that they discard the explanatory aids (Gregor, 2001; Mao & Benbasat, 2000). While prior IT systems are based on human-made rules and therefore exhibit expectable behavior, ML systems are prone to error and frequently opaque. Thus, explanations are even more crucial for ML systems than for conventional IT systems in order to make informed choices (Diakopoulos, 2016; Rudin, 2019). Despite the urgency of explanations for ML systems, several scholars have emphasized that understanding explanations for ML systems requires significant cognitive effort (e.g., Arrieta et al., 2019; Bunt et al., 2012; Lebovitz et al., 2022) since explanations for ML systems are typically complex and challenging to grasp for users and thus differ significantly from explanations for conventional IT systems (Lyytinen et al., 2021; Meske et al., 2022). Therefore, users perform a cost-benefit analysis to decide on the use of explanations for ML systems, weighing the exacerbated cognitive effort against the potentially greater benefits of explainability due to the specific characteristics of ML systems. Especially in low-risk scenarios, previous research has indicated that the required cognitive effort exceeds the potential benefits of explanations for ML systems and that explanations are only selectively accessed—if at all (e.g., Adadi & Berrada, 2018; Bunt et al., 2012; T. Miller, 2019). In our context, however, physicians rely heavily on explanations to safeguard the lives of their patients. On the other hand, they already use many systems in parallel and must work very efficiently in their clinical routines. Thus, increasing explainability must not be accompanied by excessive cognitive demands on physicians (Reyes et al., 2020). Instead, explanations for ML-based CDSS must be designed to minimize the cognitive effort required for clinicians to operate the system. This goal represents our second meta-requirement for the following design science process.

## 3 Overview of the Design Science Process

The design science approach is employed to develop and evaluate designs within a structured process (Ågerfalk & Karlsson, 2020; Hevner et al., 2004; Jones & Gregor, 2007; Niederman & March, 2012). Specifically, we chose the iterative design science approach according to Peffers et al. (2007) and combined it with that of Vaishnavi and Kuechler (2007), resulting in six design science phases, namely awareness of the problem, suggestion, development, demonstration, evaluation, and conclusion. While the approach of Vaishnavi and Kuechler (2007) facilitates integrating the top-down foundations of literature with the bottom-up needs of users at an early stage in the design science process, Peffers et al. (2007) allow for distinction between the demonstration and evaluation of the design (Meth et al., 2015).

Overall, we went through the design science phases twice to establish five principles for a more explainable design of ML-based CDSSs and an instantiated prototype thereof. An overview of both conducted design cycles can be seen in Figure 1 and is described below.

The meta-requirements for designing user-centric explanations were derived from existing literature on XAI, usability, and cognitive effort and apply to both design cycles. By consulting the literature to derive the meta-requirements, we anchored our design science research in a known problem context and derived research objectives based on established justificatory knowledge (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; vom Brocke et al. 2020). In line with the design science approach of Vaishnavi and Kuechler (2007) and consistent with the usability concept, we further recognize the importance of involving potential users of a system in the design science process from the early phases on (Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002; Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015; Venkatesh & Ramesh, 2006). Therefore, in addition to the identified literature, we surveyed 57 physicians in the suggestion phase of the first design cycle to help formulate the four initial design principles that address the meta-requirements. The study participants were 40 years old on average, 64.9% male and 35.1% female, predominantly senior physicians (43.9%) or resident physicians (31.6%) from a wide range of medical specialties (e.g., radiology, anesthesiology, internal medicine, surgery) and with different degrees of prior ML knowledge (40.4% novices, 35.1% advanced beginners, 12.3% proficient, 8.8% skilled, 3.5% experts). We employed a questionnaire that allowed physicians to, first, indicate explanatory needs if using ML-based CDSSs and, second, assess the explanations identified from XAI literature and summarized in the ontology according to their relevance on 7-point Likert scales. In formulating the design principles, we drew extensively on the structure developed by Gregor et al. (2020) and closely considered Iivari et al.’s (2021) evaluation framework. Specifically, this means that we placed emphasis on phrasing the design principles in such a way that they are understandable to researchers but more importantly to practitioners (i.e., accessibility), address the important underlying problem of providing adequate care to patients (i.e., importance), are meaningful to the problem (i.e., novelty and insightfulness), and can be realistically executed (i.e., actability and guidance).

To show how the derived principles can be practically implemented and to make them tangible for physician evaluation, we next developed a wireframe for an explainable ML-based CDSS that adhered to the established principles. Thus, we applied the tool Balsamiq, which allows a quick, sketched representation of the ML-based CDSS’s functioning and the generation of click dummies (Balsamiq, 2022). The resulting wireframe sketched an explainable MLbased CDSS that can be applied by radiologists to detect and classify pulmonary nodules on CT images of the thorax. We chose to develop a wireframe in the field of radiology because this medical discipline has a particular affinity for technical developments (Reyes et al., 2020). In addition, tumor detection is a common task for radiologists, and initial ML-based CDSSs are already available to support this purpose. These systems, which do not yet contain explanations, served as a basis to better understand the working process of radiologists and design a wireframe that focuses on the introduction of explanations while respecting the standard design practices of existing systems. Among the ML-based CDSSs considered are AI-Rad Companion by Siemens Healthineers (Siemens, 2021), Lung AI by Arterys (Arterys, 2021), and Veye Lung Nodules by Aidence (Aidence, 2021; Armato et al., 2011). The wireframe underwent a detailed examination of technical feasibility by four IS researchers proficient in computer science and a professional review of its practicality by a radiologist.

After a successful demonstration, it was evaluated as an instantiation of the design principles using guided walk-throughs with six radiologists. The interviewed radiologists had diverse backgrounds, some working in clinics, others in private practices, ranging from 1.5 to 30 years of professional experience and varying prior expertise in the field of ML. To ensure the most realistic process possible while still allowing any aspects of the wireframe to be evaluated, we employed three scenarios closely aligned with diagnostics in clinics to guide the radiologists through the complete wireframe: First, participants were asked to review the patient’s file to develop a general understanding of the case. We then invited the radiologists to take a closer look at the sketched ML-based CDSS and learn about its features.

Finally, we challenged the physicians to classify the detected lung nodules, taking into account the MLbased CDSS’s suggestions and explanations. In all scenarios, participants were invited to express their thoughts aloud. These thoughts were audio-recorded and summarized in protocols. The walk-throughs lasted an average of 44 minutes.

By reflecting on the qualitative results from the evaluation, the third and fourth design principles were adjusted and extended by one further principle in the second design cycle, resulting in a total of five design principles. Because the radiologists indicated that the appearance of the wireframe still deviated too much from existing ML-based CDSSs in clinical practice due to its sketched representation, we translated it into a web-based prototype called RadiologyAI to create a more realistic user experience during the second development phase. The correctness of the implemented code and the functionality of the webbased prototype were independently verified by an IS researcher and an IS student. Subsequently, we evaluated the web-based prototype as an instantiation of the design principles in an experimental survey study among 45 radiologists. As suggested by vom Brocke et al. (2020), we referred to measures derived from the identified justificatory knowledge, namely explainability and cognitive effort. The second design cycle and thus the entire design process was completed with the successful evaluation of the derived design against the established meta-requirements.

## 4 Designing Explainable ML-Based CDSSs

The following describes in detail how the literature and empirical data were used to develop the five design principles and to realize these principles in the form of a web-based prototype to support the future design of more explainable ML-based CDSSs. The final set of the derived design principles is summarized in Table 2.

## 4.1 Deriving the Design Principles

To begin, we drew on the ontology of explanations synthesized from the XAI literature. This ontology formed the basis for surveying 57 physicians about preferred explanations. Matching the physicians responses against the ontology revealed that no explanatory approaches beyond the ontology were desired by the physicians, indicating its completeness. Moreover, all explanations included in the ontology were considered essential (mean values of all explanations’ relevance ≥ 5.5 on a scale from 1 = “not important at all” to 7 = “very important”), which indicates the conciseness of the ontology.

As all the explanations contained in the ontology were found to be important, designers should make sure they include explanations with different functionalities in their explainable ML-based CDSSs. To do so, designers could follow the explanation categories that have been established in the ontology: An ML-based CDSS should display model explanations that help physicians verify its performance, allowing for a comparison between the system and themselves. However, global explanations about the overall reasoning behind the ML-based CDSS and local explanations of the system’s specific output should also be presented in a way that allows physicians to compare the ML model’s way of working with their own, enabling them to draw conclusions about the correctness of the suggested results. In this way, a comprehensive picture of how the ML-based CDSS works can emerge that meets the diverse explanatory needs of physicians and contributes to fulfilling the meta-requirement for maximized explainability. In summary, we derived the principle of multipurpose explanations, which can be found in Table 2.

For deriving the second design principle, we relied on the identified literature in the field of usability, which shows that similar and functionally related information should be displayed in local proximity to each other (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015; Shneiderman 1996). Indeed, grouping information has been associated with a need for less cognitive effort for users to grasp and process the content (Malinova & Mendling, 2021). This demand can also be applied to explanations shown to physicians operating the ML-based CDSS. In line with the second meta-requirement of minimizing cognitive effort, having explanations that serve related functions positioned close to each other helps keep the complexity of the ML-based CDSS low, even if sophisticated explanations are introduced. Arranging the explanations according to their purpose can again be guided by the ontology and the categories of explanations it contains, namely model, global, and local explanations. This results in the principle of visual separation of explanations (see Table 2).

The third design principle draws on the insights obtained from the literature on usability, which states that certain information for IT systems should only be supplied on request. Shneiderman (1996) emphasizes in his wellknown mantra that details are to be provided on demand whenever they are actually needed. This is also evident in research on the perspective of cognitive effort, particularly with regard to explanations. It states that developers can actively influence users’ willingness to engage with explanations through their design. Specifically, explanations should only be displayed optionally— when users doubt a system’s results and actively seek explanations—in order to reduce the amount of information that needs to be processed (Ausubel, 1985; Everett, 1994; Gregor & Benbasat, 1999). These insights from the literature were verified and refined by the walk-throughs of the wireframed MLbased CDSS with six radiologists. From the recorded protocols, it appears that radiologists prefer not to see all explanations at once but rather to receive certain information only as needed to reduce the complexity of the ML-based CDSS and thus the required cognitive effort. This can be deduced from the following statement: “That’s pretty general here [referring to model explanations]. I’d be interested in that once now, but it’s not something I need to have displayed every time.” Here, the radiologist highlighted the explanations in the wireframe prototype that can be classified as model explanations in the ontology and that are not subject to change due to their static nature. In this sense, static explanations such as model and global explanations are usually read only once to fundamentally evaluate the MLbased CDSS and should be provided for review only if the overall functionality of the system is in doubt. In contrast, local explanations must be displayed at all times since the error-prone outputs of ML-based CDSSs have to be verified by physicians on a case-by-case basis. These explanations should thus be visible by default. Therefore, to satisfy the second meta-requirement for minimizing the cognitive effort of using ML-based CDSSs without withholding important information, the principle of on-demand availability of explanations (see Table 2) should be followed.

The core of the following design principle derives from the concept of usability and the related cognitive effort perspective, according to which familiar terms should be used to communicate with users in order to reduce the cognitive effort required to understand the content (Gregor & Benbasat, 1999; Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). We extended this concept using the qualitative findings from the walk-throughs with radiologists so that they accounted not only for the formulation of explanations but for physicians’ entire knowledge base and common decision-making processes. For example, one of the radiologists stated that the ML-based CDSS became more explainable after it was disclosed that it is informed by the same criteria that apply to the workflow of radiologists: “You can see that the criteria, especially these spicules, which would also be malignancy criteria for us, that the ML is using those as well. And that definitely makes it more explainable already.” This is also reflected in the currently limited user-centered research in the field of XAI, which calls for making explanations of ML systems easy to grasp, accounting for users’ cognitive abilities and prior knowledge (e.g., Lebovitz et al., 2021; Meske et al., 2022; Strich et al., 2021). To summarize, we postulate that explanations for the ML-based CDSS should be aligned with physicians’ workflows and expertise to create familiarity. This mainly means that even though they are derived from probabilistic methods, explanations should be presented in a humanunderstandable way, e.g., as combinations of natural language and visualizations and reference concepts that physicians use in their daily decision-making processes. Thus, explanations should be less machineoriented and more responsive to physicians’ prior knowledge, leading to reduced cognitive effort, as mandated by the second meta-requirement. This is articulated in the principle of mental alignment of explanations, which can be found in Table 2.

The final design principle again stems from the requests expressed by the radiologists in the walkthroughs and was added during the second design cycle. Given that the explanations for ML systems developed in the extant XAI research are often based on statistical methods and may consequently be prone to error (Miller, 2019; Rudin, 2019), the radiologists sought reliable aspects in the explanations for the MLbased CDSS, according to our protocols: “I think it’s extremely important that you only report cases that you know are validated. So only biopsy-confirmed cases. ... And then it can be really helpful.” These hard facts helped physicians gain confidence in the evaluation of the ML-based CDSS and required them to spend fewer cognitive resources to ascertain the validity of the explanations provided by the ML-based CDSS in addition to the system itself. Therefore, we propose the principle of determinism of explanations (see Table 2), which stresses that explanations should not be based exclusively on probabilistic algorithms but should be complemented with deterministic information from reliable sources whenever possible. This helps physicians spend fewer cognitive resources on the use of ML-based CDSSs, thus fulfilling the second metarequirement.

## 4.2 Deriving RadiologyAI as the Overall Result of the Design Science Process

To show how the design principles can be operationalized, we demonstrate a web-based prototype named RadiologyAI below that incorporates and manifests the concepts from the principles. The two main components of the prototype are an underlying ML model for lung nodule classification and a web application that displays the output of the ML model. The ML model is trained on the LIDC-IDRI dataset (Armato et al., 2011). It contains 1010 CT scans from US patients with a total of 2,625 lung nodules that were segmented and annotated by radiologists in terms of their malignancy, size, and additional characteristics (e.g., texture, margin). We chose this particular dataset because it is representative of actual patients and contains multiple nodules that vary in their difficulty of diagnosis. We extracted the nodule volumes and conducted image preprocessing similar to prior studies (Ardila et al., 2019; Venkadesh et al., 2021). Additionally, we prepared the malignancy annotations as a binary classification feature (i.e., benign and malignant). The data were separated into a training and validation set according to a 90%/10% split. We then trained a classification model, specifically a deep neural network with the DenseNet architecture, which uses the 3D nodule volume as input and generates a corresponding malignancy prediction. Given the limited training data, the model achieved acceptable performance on the validation set (accuracy 0.79, AUC 0.83). The established ML model served as the basis for generating predictions and explanations, which were stored in JSON format alongside all metadata for the selected patient case. The JavaScriptbased web application used the JSON data to render the user interface.

The resulting prototype for an explainable ML-based CDSS comprises three screens—two are depicted in Figures 2 and 3 (see Appendix A, Figure A1 for RadiologyAI’s introductory screen). Figure 2 represents an overview of the selected patient case. It contains the complete scan (slider allows scrolling through the 3D volume) and a list of the nodules detected by the underlying ML model. For each nodule, the calculated measurements and the ML and physician diagnosis are displayed. The second depicted screen details a single nodule and can be reached from the nodule list (see Figure 3). This screen contains the nodule volume and the outputs of the ML model, including the malignancy classification. Finally, a dropdown menu allows the physicians to input their classification.

The aforementioned design principles are implemented in our prototype as follows: Regarding the principle of multipurpose explanations, we made sure that physicians could comprehensively verify the performance of the ML-based CDSS (model explanations) and also receive additional information about the generated specific output (local explanations). As physicians considered all explanations relevant in the survey study, we aimed to integrate all explanation categories of the ontology into the prototype. However, global explanations and thus feature importances were not added, as there are no established technical approaches for generating this kind of explanation for 3D image data (Kim et al., 2018; Yeh et al., 2020).

General insights into the performance of the ML-based CDSS can be drawn from the model explanations, which were added as a model card that contains four tabs, one for each explanation type (see Figure 4 for validation and Appendix A, Figures A2, A3, and A4 for general information, datasets, and performance). The model card is accessible on request from all screens and contains all content listed in the “Examples” column of Table 1. Moreover, info boxes on the overview and details screen inform the physician about the main components of the prototype (see Figures 2, 3, and 5).

Furthermore, we added local explanations in the form of uncertainty estimates for each classification, feature attributions, and example-based explanations to allow physicians to assess the output of the MLbased CDSS. Uncertainty estimates were implemented as output probabilities on the overview and details screen (see Figures 2 and 3). Feature attributions were added on the details screen as a heatmap over the nodule volumes and additional predictions of nodule characteristics (see Figure 3). The heatmap depicts parts of the volume that increase the malignancy probability and was created using the integrated gradients algorithm (Sundararajan et al., 2017). The nodule characteristics represent additional ML model outputs regarding concepts physicians typically incorporate into their decision-making processes, such as shape (e.g., spiculation, margin) and substance (e.g., texture, internal structure with solid components).

Classifying each nodule according to these characteristics provided additional information to the physicians about how the respective malignancy classification was generated. Furthermore, the details screen also incorporates example-based explanations in the form of similar nodules from the dataset for which a diagnosis has been confirmed via biopsy (see Figure 5). Similar nodules were determined using a k-nearestneighbors model, relying on the annotated nodule measurements and characteristics outlined above.

As stated in the principle of visual separation of explanations, explanations were visually segregated in the ML-based CDSS according to the explanations categories provided in the ontology. While model explanations, such as the performance of the ML model, are located on the model card (see Figure 4), local explanations are provided on the overview (e.g., uncertainty estimates; see Figure 2) and details page (e.g., heatmap; see Figure 3). As described earlier, global explanations were not added to the prototype due to the lack of technical approaches available to provide global explanations for ML models based on 3D image data (Kim et al., 2018; Yeh et al., 2020).

The principle of on-demand availability of explanations expands on the principle of visual separation of explanations in that the visually separated model explanations are provided only upon physician request via the model card (see Figure 4). Within the model card, the different explanation types (general information, validation, datasets, and performance; see Figure 4 and Appendix A, Figures A2, A3, and A4) can be accessed individually. Local explanations, in contrast, are displayed during the physician’s classification process by default (see Figure 2 and 3).

The principle of mental alignment of explanations is realized in several places in the prototype. First, we included metrics in the model card’s performance tab, which physicians commonly use to evaluate diagnostic performance (e.g., sensitivity, specificity; see Appendix A, Figure A4). Second, the generated heat map adheres to the usual decision-making process undertaken by physicians, as it highlights potentially malignant nodule regions that contributed to the ML model’s outcome (see Figure 3). Third, we included local explanations by letting the ML model classify each nodule in terms of additional characteristics, i.e., human-understandable concepts which are commonly used in this particular diagnostic process (e.g., spiculation, texture; see Figure 3). Thus, the physician could get a sense of the correctness of the ML model’s output. Fourth, the similar cases panel was included as it is common practice for physicians to look at previously examined cases in online and offline imaging archives (see Figure 5).

Examples of implementing the principle of determinism of explanations are the provision of similar cases as example-based explanations whose diagnoses have been confirmed by a biopsy and the inclusion of peer-reviewed publications that examine the diagnostic performance of the ML-based CDSS under different boundary conditions (see Figures 4 and 5). In addition, the data basis is shown transparently and explained extensively. These aspects represent reliable components in addition to statistically generated explanations.

## 5 Evaluating Explainability and Cognitive Effort

We chose an experimental survey study to evaluate the web-based prototype RadiologyAI as an instantiation of the five design principles. The experimental setting allowed us to control the participants’ environment, exclude confounding variables, and measure the plain effect of the explainable design on users (Venable et al., 2016). Nonetheless, we kept the setting as realistic as possible by accounting for the usual work processes of physicians and relying on existing ML-based CDSSs to design RadiologyAI so that the evaluation’s results would be transferable to natural scenarios to the greatest extent possible. As required by Iivari (2020) and vom Brocke et al. (2020), we further referred to the accumulated justificatory knowledge from the literature to identify metrics against which to evaluate the design and meta-requirements compliance. This revealed (1) the explainability of RadiologyAI and (2) the cognitive effort required of physicians when using the prototype as meaningful measures of its performance.

We followed the guidelines of Mettler et al. (2014) for conducting controlled experiments to evaluate designs, and compared RadiologyAI modeled after the design principles with a version of the prototype that does not include any explanations. To prevent the priming of participants, we chose a between-subject study design in which participants were randomly assigned to one of the two groups. The experiment was conducted online and embedded in a questionnaire that included demographic questions and standard scales for target variable explainability and cognitive effort, which were slightly adapted to fit the context (see Table 3). The reflective latent measures in Table 3 were used to assess the target variables on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”).

The questionnaire further comprised an attention check to detect inattentive participants. In addition, cursor data were recorded during the experimental procedure to verify that the radiologists recognized the explanations and that the manipulation of the experiment was successful (Aronow et al., 2019). Cursor behavior was thus assumed to approximate physicians’ eye gaze patterns (Huang et al., 2011). We referred to the three scenarios previously tested during the mockup evaluation to guide participants through the web-based prototype. We contacted 2,186 radiologists from clinics and practices in three European countries; of these, 115 started the survey and 49 participants completed the experiment, resulting in a response rate of 2.24%. Based on the analysis of the demographic data and the attention check, we excluded four respondents who did not pass the attention check or provided conflicting information about their education. Among the 45 resulting participants, 22 were assigned to the interface with explanations, while 23 received no explanations. The manipulation proved successful for all participants who received the explanations. 75.6% of the radiologists were male and 24.4% female. On average, the radiologists were 38.2 years old; 40.0% were resident physicians, 17.8% were specialists, and 42.2% had senior status. Most radiologists worked for clinics (86.7%) and a minority (13.3%) worked in practices. Prior ML knowledge was broadly distributed among the radiologists (8.9% novices, 31.1% advanced beginners, 28.9% proficient, 17.8% skilled, 13.3% experts). Overall, we are confident that the sample was appropriate, given that ML-based CDSSs will be used primarily initially in large clinics and will need to accommodate a variety of training levels and prior ML knowledge.

The collected responses to the explainability of the ML-based CDSS did not contain extreme outliers. They were normally distributed in both groups (i.e., prototype with explanations and prototype without explanations) as indicated by the Shapiro-Wilk test (p > 0.05) (Shapiro & Wilk, 1965). Furthermore, variance homogeneity was found, allowing a t-test to be applied in SPSS 26. Overall, we were able to show that the radiologists perceived the explainability of the prototype with explanations as higher (M = 4.916, SD = 1.122) than the one without explanations (M = 3.037, SD = 1.553) and that the difference between both groups was significant with t(43) = 4.633, p = 0.000. The effect size was large with Cohen’s d = 1.382 (Cohen, 1992).

Analysis of the data concerning cognitive effort showed that there were no outliers and both groups, i.e., those with and without explanations, were normally distributed according to the Shapiro-Wilk test (p > 0.05) (Shapiro & Wilk, 1965). Again, variance homogeneity was present. Applying a t-test in SPSS 26, we observed that the cognitive effort was lower for the prototype with explanations (M = 2.121, SD = 0.651) than for the prototype without (M = 2.580, SD = 0.924), with t(43) = -1.917, p = 0.062. The effect size measured by Cohen’s d resulted in a value of -0.57. Thus, the effect is marginally statistically significant at a medium effect size (Pritschet et al., 2016).

In summary, the results of our evaluation show that compared with ML-based CDSSs without explanations, the web-based prototype RadiologyAI designed according to the design principles has higher explanatory power and requires less cognitive effort for physicians to operate, satisfying the established meta-requirements.

## 6 Discussion

Physicians work in a unique environment, especially regarding how critical their decisions are. In clinical environments, physicians work closely with people and machines and must make quick and accurate patient-care decisions. Against this distinctive background, the created design principles represent an improvement in terms of the design science research knowledge contribution framework by Gregor and Hevner (2013). While it is well known that the application of ML-based CDSSs in medicine is challenging due to the opacity of these systems, we synthesized the existing literature and complemented it with the help of empirical studies to establish five design principles that can make these systems more applicable to clinical practice (see Table 2). We thereby show how explanations should be designed to ensure the explainability of ML-based CDSSs without overwhelming physicians. According to these principles, designers should incorporate model-based, global, and local explanations into ML-based CDSS rather than merely using isolated metrics (e.g., accuracy), as has often been the practice to date (Lebovitz et al., 2021).

While these metrics reflect the performance of a system, they do not provide a deeper overall picture of how the system works. Moreover, the explanations should be grouped according to the respective explanatory categories so that physicians can swiftly gain an overview of the ML-based CDSS, even though the typically statistics-based explanations are commonly complex (Meske et al., 2022). The principles further require that while model and global explanations should be provided only when needed, local explanations must be continuously displayed. Because the individual outputs of these systems may be erroneous (Adadi & Berrada, 2018), it is essential that physicians are repeatedly reminded to validate the provided results according to the local explanations at hand. Previous research has already shown that explanations should contain familiar wording (Gregor & Benbasat, 1999; Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015)—our principles begin there but then go a step further to extend this to the entire knowledge base of physicians. All explanations, whether model, global, or local, should strive to consider and actively address physicians’ common work processes and related expertise existing prior to the development and usage of ML-based CDSSs. In addition, the inclusion of hard facts in explanations, as called for in the design principles, can not only enable physicians to make more certain diagnoses but also contributes to the trust relationship, an aspect that has been frequently discussed and explored in previous XAI research (Benbya et al., 2021; Schuetz & Venkatesh, 2020).

The RadiologyAI prototype makes the design principles more concrete and presents a practical blueprint for implementing the principles in an MLbased CDSS. For example, the prototype shows how the distinct categories of explanations can be separated and integrated according to their functions, which explanations harmonize with physicians’ typical workflow (e.g., typical metrics for physicians, heatmap), and how to incorporate hard facts alongside probabilistic explanations (e.g., similar cases, peerreviewed publications).

## 6.1 Theoretical Contributions

Previous research from the field of XAI has focused on technically realizing explanations that can be applied by data scientists, mathematicians, and developers (Meske et al., 2022). Recent research from the IS field has begun to investigate goals that can be achieved using modern XAI techniques (e.g., Asatiani et al., 2021; Benbya et al., 2021; Lebovitz et al, 2021). Our design principles contribute to this research by showing how the respective goals can be achieved in the medical domain. This task is not trivial, due to the complexity of explanations of ML systems and the limited statistical expertise of physicians, and requires a dedicated reflection considering the needs of end users. The resulting design principles not only offer researchers of ML-based CDSSs a clear path showing which explanations to consider and how to arrange them when studying these systems but also provides appropriate rationales for the underlying mechanisms, adding to an understanding of how explanations can be tailored to the needs of physicians. In this way, the developed design principles fill a gap in XAI research, which must better account for the interests of end users to facilitate beneficial applications of the technology.

With this design science research, we further translate previous research on the usability of conventional IT systems to the ML era. We demonstrate that the design principles implemented in the prototype RadiologyAI allow physicians to perceive an ML-based CDSS as more explainable and reduce the cognitive effort the need to expend compared to a version of the prototype without explanations. These effects are surprising, as they contradict prior assumptions of the cognitive effort perspective (Gregor & Benbasat, 1999) and, according to Baskerville et al. (2018), may thus make this research particularly valuable for further design theorizing.

While the perspective has thus far presumed that consuming explanations for conventional IT systems leads to increased cognitive effort (Gregor, 2001; Gregor & Benbasat, 1999; Mao & Benbasat, 2000), our research indicates that explanations for ML-based CDSSs that are appropriately conceptualized can lead to lower demands on mental resources. One explanation for this finding could be that physicians working with opaque ML-based CDSSs, as opposed to predictably acting and often blindly trusted rule-based IT systems (Davis, 1986), are aware of ML-based CDSSs’ susceptibility to errors and must devote high levels of cognitive resources to figuring out how these systems work and evaluating their accuracy without good reference points. This process is commonly referred to as abductive reasoning and has been deemed relevant to user Interaction with opaque ML systems in previous research (Miller, 2019). If appropriately designed explanations are included, physicians will thus not perceive them as a burden but as a relief; even though understanding the explanations involves additional effort, it can reduce their sense of groping around in the dark.

Our results not only challenge the transferability of the cognitive effort perspective for this high-risk application domain but also contribute to a better understanding of the underlying cognitive processes involved in the use of ML-based CDSSs. After all, our research reveals that the meta-requirements described above need not be mutually exclusive but can be satisfied simultaneously if the explanations for MLbased CDSS are appropriately designed.

Our results also differ from previous research on user interaction with ML systems and related explanations in low-risk environments. Users in low-consequence scenarios often perceive the benefit of explanations for ML systems to be low, causing them to discard this additional information (e.g., Adadi & Berrada, 2018; Bunt et al., 2012). In contrast, the physicians in our study appreciated the additional clarifications. Physicians even actively requested explanations for the ML-based CDSS to better understand the inner workings of the system and thereby prevent potentially fatal consequences. This differs from scientific findings on ML systems in low-risk scenarios, as the costs of errors are significantly greater in the medical field and the impact of decisions thus more farreaching, resulting in physicians bearing a higher degree of responsibility. By outlining design principles for a more explainable ML-based CDSS, we thus enable physicians to fulfill their obligations and respond to the call of Young et al. (2021) to create designs that contribute to a better future.

## 6.2 Practical Contributions

In practice, the implemented prototype can be used as a hands-on guide for the development of more explainable ML-based CDSSs. Not only does it provide explanations that are essential to physicians but it also demonstrates concretely how these can be implemented without increasing the mental overhead associated with operating ML-based CDSSs. In this way, we help to address one of the biggest challenges of applying ML-based CDSSs in medicine, namely their lack of explainability combined with their errorproneness and the resulting rejection by physicians and patients. The user-centered, explainable design accommodates the stressful day-to-day lives of physicians, while also helping to make diagnoses more effectively and efficiently without risking misdiagnoses, thus better leveraging the potential of ML for medicine. This contribution is highly relevant: not only is healthcare of utmost personal importance for the population, it also represents one of the largest economic sectors in the US (Collier & Fu, 2020).

Moreover, the experimental results conducted with the help of the prototype indicate that explanations are valuable to users in high-risk environments such as medicine and thus may serve as a rationale for integrating explanations, even if this requires additional expenditures of time and money during development. Our findings should thus encourage developers to invest more diligently in the explainable design of ML-based CDSS to ensure eventual applicability in the clinical environment.

In addition, the introduction of understandable explanations, as shown in the prototype, enables compliance with legal demands in high-risk settings. The lack of explainability of ML-based CDSSs is currently a point of discussion for many regulatory bodies, such as the Food and Drug Administration (Patel, 2021), which has called for physicians to be empowered to make informed decisions when using ML-based CDSSs. RadiologyAI shows a clear path to fulfill this demand by providing a validated blueprint for more explainable ML-based CDSSs. Thus, our research contributes to bridging the current gap between the technical capabilities of ML-based CDSSs and their actual implementation in clinical practice.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

Like any research, our research has limitations. First, we chose a particular use case from medicine as part of our design science approach to motivate our research subject and make it more tangible. Specifically, we provide a design for an ML-based CDSS with explanations in the field of radiology. Thus, our design principles, the instantiated prototype, and the final evaluation’s results relate to the selected use case, which calls into question their transferability to other application scenarios, especially low-stakes domains with substantially lower error costs in which explanations are less relevant to users (Adadi & Berrada, 2018; Bunt et al., 2012). While the applicability of our results to low-risk settings is questionable, the selected use case is an archetypal example for high-risk environments such as medicine, The growing medical applications of ML will dramatically impact the professional work of physicians where the greatest value of all is at stake—human life (Lebovitz et al., 2022). In addition, the design principles are largely derived from existing literature that is not focused on medicine and represent more general ideas for improving explainability while limiting cognitive effort. These ideas are designed to be transferable to the design of ML systems in other high-risk areas, such as other diagnostic or treatment settings in medicine and beyond—for example, in aviation or policing. Nevertheless, this research should be replicated in other contexts to provide empirical evidence of its transferability to other high-risk areas or to potentially identify domain-specific explanatory needs.

In addition to the primary use case, we also selected a specific patient scan when displaying the prototype. This choice could have influenced physicians’ impressions of the ML-based CDSS. However, we were careful to select a realistic patient scan that included different nodules of varying malignancy and, according to assessment by a medical practitioner, was moderately difficult to diagnose in terms of daily diagnostics. In addition, we varied only the presence of explanations in the experiment and kept the patient scan constant to exclusively examine the effect of the more explainable design. However, future studies could investigate how patient scans with different levels of tumor-detection difficulty affect physicians need for explanations.

Moreover, we did not include global explanations in our web-based prototype due to the lack of established technical approaches for 3D image data. Therefore, future research should focus on investigating how this type of explanation could be included in ML-based CDSSs. Promising avenues for global explanations on image data include concept vectors (Kim et al., 2018; Yeh et al., 2020) and radiomics-based models (Afshar et al., 2019; Thawani et al., 2018), which are presently at a very early stage of research.

Finally, the group of possible participants in our experimental evaluation was severely limited due to the required educational background, and the sample was specifically based on hospital radiologists with public email addresses. Future studies could expand this sample and identify, for instance, differences between the needs of radiologists from hospitals and practices or between radiologists with different levels of training.

Our research reveals how explanations for ML-based CDSSs could be designed without overburdening physicians. However, this is only the first step in a series of possible future investigations. To examine, as suggested by Iivari et al. (2021), the effectiveness of the design principles on the applying organization, future studies could look at the impact of adequately designed explanations on physicians’ acceptance and continued use of ML-based CDSSs, the adoption of ML-generated diagnostic recommendations, and the ability to learn from them in practice—for example, in the form of a longitudinal case study. In particular, it has long been known from the didactic literature that adequately designed explanations contribute to learning success (e.g., Bobek & Tversky, 2016). The extent to which this could be transferred to explanations for ML-based CDSSs and how to prevent physicians from learning incorrect information via error-prone probabilistic algorithms could be a primary starting point for evaluating the effectiveness of the suggested design principles.

However, such future studies should not only investigate the impact of explainability on dependent factors such as learning or the acceptance and use of ML-based CDSSs, as it is known from early research on CDSSs that even transparent systems (e.g., based on decision trees) are not always fully integrated into clinical practice (Chang et al., 2007; Trivedi et al., 2002). Therefore, in addition to appropriately designed explanations, other factors, such as the compatibility of the ML-based CDSS with the existing clinical IT infrastructure or the reliability of the system, should be considered.

Further, our research focused on making explanations for opaque ML-based CDSSs more user friendly. In the future, computer scientists should strive to develop

ML-based CDSSs that are transparent from the outset rather than retrospectively offering explanations (e.g., Rudin, 2019). This could paint an even more complete picture of the inner workings of ML-based CDSSs than is possible with after-the-fact explanations. In developing such systems, however, cost-benefit tradeoffs beyond those between cognitive effort and explainability will be at play. Transparent ML systems tend to be less accurate because, according to statistical learning theory, higher accuracy generally requires a higher complexity of the models underlying the systems (Adadi & Berrada, 2018; Asatiani et al., 2021; Breiman, 2001). How and under which terms this trade-off can be solved most effectively should be part of future technical research in the field of MLbased CDSSs.

## 7 Conclusion

To answer our established research question, we integrated justificatory knowledge from pertinent literature and demands from physician end users and (1) derived five principles that can guide the design and future research on explainable ML-based CDSSs, (2) developed a prototype that shows a feasible operationalization of an explainable ML-based CDSS, and (3) evaluated the prototype as an instantiation of the design principles against the established metarequirements. The evaluation revealed that the established design actually leads to increased explainability while reducing the necessary cognitive effort of physicians, indicating increased usability of the system for clinical practice. These insights provide valuable guidance for developers of ML-based CDSSs seeking to enable physicians to work efficiently with ML-based CDSSs and benefit from their immense capabilities while avoiding potentially fatal errors in diagnostics. As a result, our design can help improve patient care, enabling a better future for all.
