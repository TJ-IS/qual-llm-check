# DANCING TO THE #CHALLENGE: THE EFFECT OF TIKTOK ON CLOSING THE ARTIST GENDER GAP IN THE MUSIC INDUSTRY<sup>1</sup>

Yifei Wang Department of Decision Sciences, Peter T. Paul College of Business and Economics University of New Hampshire, Durham, NH, U.S.A. {yifei.wang@unh.edu}

Jui Ramaprasad

Decision, Operations & Information Technologies, Smith School of Business University of Maryland, College Park, MD, U.S.A. {jramapra@umd.edu}

Anandasivam Gopal Division of Information Technology and Operations Management Nanyang Business School, SINGAPORE {anandasivam.gopal@ntu.edu.sg}

This study investigates how “Hashtag Dance Challenges” (HDCs), a phenomenon popularized on the short-video platform TikTok, are instrumental in helping music artists gain traction in the digital music marketplace. HDCs represent an appealing combination of music and dance, designed to engage users and achieve virality, thereby benefiting artists whose music is featured. This research focuses on how HDCs contribute to the success of women artists, as compared to men, in an industry known for its diversity but challenged by gender inclusivity. We apply role congruity theory to posit that women artists are in a better position to derive benefits from being featured on HDCs, relative to male artists, particularly in cases of gender concordance—when both the creator and the artist are women. We measure the benefits of HDCs using daily changes in the artist’s followership on Spotify, a leading music streaming service, and test our hypotheses using song and artist-level data collected from Spotify and TikTok. We found that artists featured in a new HDC achieve a significant increase in followership on Spotify, relative to similar artists not featured in an HDC. Further, we observed that women creators drive this effect, enhancing the daily growth of Spotify followers by approximately 3% more for women artists, underscoring the value of gender concordance. Our findings shed light on the role of short videos, especially through the vehicle of HDCs, in advancing women artists, while also promoting inclusivity within the digital music industry.

Keywords: Social media, viral product design, short-form video, women, discrimination, music industry

## Introduction

It is well-established that the entertainment industry suffers from a lack of equal representation across multiple demographics, particularly in the music and film sectors. The underrepresentation of women within these sectors is especially noteworthy, given the presumed central role afforded to women artists within mainstream culture. A recent Billboard report underscores this disparity, revealing that women constituted less than one third of all performers and only 12.5% of songwriters in the top 800 songs from 2012 to 2019 (Bain, 2022). Additionally, between 2013 and 2020, women made up just 11.7% of Grammy Award nominees, resulting in a gender ratio of 7.5 men to every one woman nominee (Grammys, 2019). Furthermore, a study tracking over 10,000 popular songs from 2012 through 2018 found that women were vastly outnumbered as artists, songwriters, and producers, comprising only 21.7% of artists, 12.3% of songwriters, and a mere 2.1% of producers (Smith et al., 2020). Research on women in the American music industry suggests that the observed underrepresentation does not necessarily stem from a lack of opportunities at the entry level—there is no evidence of gender disparity in music school enrollees and college music majors in the U.S. (College Factual, 2023)—but rather from the discrimination and barriers encountered by women once they enter the industry (Prior et al., 2019).

Many reasons have been postulated for why these gender disparities exist in the music industry. One such reason is systematic discrimination, characterized by unequal opportunities for women artists and the undervaluing of their artistic abilities, which significantly hampers their ability to gain visibility and recognition (Kennedy, 2023). Additionally, unwanted stereotyping and sexualization further compound these challenges by shaping perceptions that hinder effective self-promotion efforts (Hamer, 2024; Kraus, 2021). Moreover, the presence of negative studio cultures and a dearth of role models creates environments that are less supportive for women artists (Smith et al., 2020). Collectively, these factors create a complex landscape where women artists experience challenges when they enter the industry, which are further intensified as they try to generate traction for their musical talent and output.

Within this landscape, social media has emerged in recent years as a potential channel that can help women artists establish a direct connection with their audiences and expand their fan base. Social media allows artists to reach their target demographic of consumers and fans using their own voice. One of the core functions of social media is self-promotion, enabling artists to showcase their work and achievements (Karra, 2023). From this perspective, social media appears promising for women music artists, as it provides a means to bypass potential institutional barriers within the music industry, allowing them to directly reach their fans and promote their work. Platforms like YouTube and Facebook can potentially facilitate these direct connections between artists and their audiences.

However, both research and anecdotal evidence highlight that even on these platforms, women music artists encounter disproportionate negativity compared to men. Research illustrates that women musicians face heightened criticism when openly sharing their achievements online, ranging from abusive comments on YouTube (Wotanis & McMillan, 2014) to accusations of arrogance on Twitter (Peng et al., 2022). Even a well-known artist like Selena Gomez faced widespread mockery when she posted Instagram photos celebrating her single reaching #1 on iTunes; critics claimed she was bragging and trying too hard to prove herself (Kraus, 2021). Similarly, singer Madison Beer encountered backlash when she tweeted about her song debuting at #1 on the iTunes pop chart, with people accusing her of “shoving her success in everyone’s faces” (Henry, 2021). Country singer Kacey Musgraves experienced criticism when she tweeted about her album going gold, with trolls accusing her of being full of herself and excessively self-congratulatory (Young, 2019). These instances underscore the challenges women artists continue to face, even within the seemingly democratizing space of social media.

One of the reasons the challenges faced by women artists on social media persist is gender stereotypes that associate selfpromotion with masculine behavior. When women artists selfpromote their achievements, they create role incongruity. This clash occurs when women defy traditional gender roles that expect them to be communal, humble, and supportive rather than dominant or authoritative (Rudman, 1998). This incongruity leads to backlash and social penalties against women who assertively self-promote their achievements, amplified by the online nature of social media environments, where encounters with trolls are inevitable (Buckels et al., 2014). As a result, even on democratized social media platforms, women artists continue to experience difficulties gaining traction, compared to male artists. However, are there specific features on social media platforms that may give women artists an advantage and allow them to generate reasonable traction for their work even in the presence of potential role incongruity?

In this paper, we address this question by examining the potentially beneficial aspects of a relatively recent technological phenomenon called the “Hashtag dance challenge” (HDC), made popular by the short-form video social platform, TikTok, which was launched in 2017. Our focus is on understanding how the HDC, as a unique feature of newer forms of social media, can utilize role congruity to facilitate the promotional process for women artists. HDCs are short video clips in which an original piece of music by an artist is re-created in combination with a series of dance steps by an HDC creator and posted on the platform for others to enjoy or emulate. Interestingly, the HDC creator can either be the artist or an independent user who chooses to feature the artist’s music in the HDC. Subsequently, other users can film themselves performing the same dance steps and share their videos on TikTok, responding to the HDC using the corresponding hashtag. The hashtag serves to link all videos featuring the same music and dance steps, creating easily shareable content that can be viewed and enjoyed by all users on the platform (see Appendix Figure A1 for an example).

HDCs have grown in popularity and have become a significant driver of traffic for TikTok, as well as other social media platforms that have now incorporated TikTok-like short video features, such as Instagram and Facebook Reels. These short videos typically possess all the features associated with viral content (Aral & Walker, 2011)—they are creative, are easily viewed and shared, evoke emotional reactions in the audience, and encourage imitation and sharing. Given these qualities, it is not surprising that HDCs are largely wellreceived and remain popular, with a variety of users on TikTok applying their own creative styles to the music and participating in perpetuating and sharing them. More importantly, their relevance and utility in enhancing the profile of musical artists whose music is featured within them has yet to be examined in the literature on social media, a gap that we address in this paper.

We argue that artists benefit from the wave of amateur content creators engaging with and through HDCs for three reasons. First, HDCs function as virtual advertisements for the underlying music they feature. Moreover, due to their viral nature, they increase the likelihood that users will share content with their peers, thereby boosting the visibility of both the music and the original artist. Second, since the music is almost always credited to the original artist (see examples in Figure A2), there is a direct link to enhancing the artist’s brand appeal and popularity. Third, and importantly, given the viral nature of HDCs, the artist is likely to receive a surge of attention. This could translate into concrete action, such as the average user signing on as a follower of the artist’s music on musical platforms like Spotify rather than simply displaying a passive interest. We aim to capture this demonstrated increase in popularity of the original artist in our analysis, regardless of the artist’s preexisting popularity or following. In other words, whether an artist is established or emerging, we explore whether being featured in an HDC is associated with a significant increase in market traction. Thus, our main research question is: Does the introduction of an HDC enhance the artist’s popularity in terms of followers on streaming music platforms?

Beyond the main effect of the HDC, we examine the nuanced dynamics shaped by the gender of the HDC creator—whether it’s the artist or a “regular” TikTok user. Grounded in role congruity theory, we argue HDCs initiated by women creators likely possess greater intrinsic appeal because modern dance is an art form strongly congruent with feminine features (Jeschke, 1991). Consequently, HDCs initiated by women are anticipated to elicit a more favorable response from the audience compared to those initiated by men. We further propose that the appeal and impact of HDCs may be even higher when gender fully aligns—specifically, when a woman creator dances to a song clip credited to a woman artist—due to the heightened role congruity. Finally, we explore how

HDCs can help ease the promotional process for women artists by examining the extent to which women experience any self-promotion penalty. To do this, we distinguish between cases where women artists serve as the HDC creators for their own music, as opposed to other users featuring their music. These analyses allow us to better gauge the extent to which HDCs are truly instrumental in assisting women artists attract attention and followers within the music industry.

To study these research questions, we manually constructed a dataset of songs released between January 1, 2020, and April 30, 2020. We identified songs that have been used in TikTok videos, both with and without an HDC, by collaborating with a TikTok API authorized analytics company. We supplemented this with song- and artist-level data such as gender <sup>2</sup> and genre from Chartmetric, a music analytics company that tracks artists’ social media activity and follower growth on Spotify. We focused our measurement of popularity, as a dependent variable, on Spotify because it is one of the world’s largest music streaming service providers, with over 381 million monthly active users, and serves as an effective proxy measure of an artist’s creative impact (Spotify). While there are other ways to follow an artist’s musical work, following the artist on Spotify represents an accessible and practical way to measure an artist’s commercial appeal in the marketplace (Fruci, 2020). Our dependent variable captures the percentage change in the artists’ followership on Spotify, a naturally normalized measure of the artist’s popularity that allows comparisons across artists. To identify the effect of an HDC on an artist’s followers on Spotify, we compared songs that had an HDC initiated on TikTok during our observation window to similar songs that did not have an HDC on TikTok. We used propensity score matching (PSM) in conjunction with a difference-indifferences (DID) econometric specification to conduct our empirical analysis in several stages, as described later in the paper (Liu & Lynch, 2011; Smith & Todd, 2005).

Our findings show that, on average, the presence of an HDC enhanced the daily Spotify followership of the focal artist by around 1.37% during our study period. More notably, HDCs initiated by women exhibited a higher impact than those by men, increasing average daily Spotify followers of the focal artists by close to 1% regardless of artist gender. Even more compellingly, we found that HDCs initiated by women and credited to women artists resulted in the most substantial increase for the focal women artists, showing an average daily growth of 3% in Spotify followership. In contrast, we found that HDCs created by men and credited to male artists tended to negatively impact the focal artists’ Spotify follower growth.

This aligns with our theoretical arguments suggesting that because dance is considered to be less role congruous with masculine traits, when men incorporate dance content, they face a penalty due to perceived deviation from gender roles (Moss-Racusin et al., 2010). Finally, we found no differences between user- and artist-initiated HDCs in cases where both the creator and credited artist were women. This result suggests that HDCs can attenuate the anticipated backlash that women artists face in self-promoting their work on social media, at least in the short term.

Our work contributes to the literature in three distinct ways. First, we address the immediate question of how music artists can leverage newer forms of social media to gain visibility and traction. Prior research has established how viral content can facilitate social influence, in turn affecting user behavior (e.g., Aral & Walker, 2011; Phelps et al., 2004). Research has also demonstrated how the broadcasting nature of viral content can boost adoption of products by users (Chen et al., 2011; Chen & Berger, 2016), increase user engagement (Huang et al., 2020), and attract media attention (Berger & Milkman, 2010). Building on this foundation, we demonstrate that HDCs, rooted in the short-form video platform context, are particularly effective in generating attention and attracting potential fans. We argue that these results highlight both the appeal of short-form video contexts and the relative democratization and decentralization of social media’s impact, where even users can help artists gain popularity. Second, and more importantly, our work offers empirical evidence that role congruity matters, specifically highlighting how dance can significantly aid women artists in promoting their work. Intriguingly, HDCs appear to be more effective for women artists in gaining popularity because they mitigate the stigma of self-promotion, building on the congruity between dance and women artists, at least in the short term. Whether these effects will persist in the future remains an open question for future work.

Lastly, at a macrolevel, our work addresses broader questions of gender parity, particularly within the digital music industry (Born & Devine, 2015; Wang et al., 2021) and in creative labor more broadly (e.g., Bielby & Bielby, 1992; Lutter, 2015). Studies have documented discrimination and bias toward women in digital music (e.g., Ferraro et al., 2021; Lesota et al., 2021; Wolfe, 2019), but research on mitigating these biases is relatively scarce. While social media has the potential to rectify some of these imbalances by offering women a safer and more collaborative platform, research shows that this is not always the case (Wotanis & McMillan, 2014). Our study explores how emerging forms of social media that leverage role congruity can ease the promotional process for women artists, ultimately increasing the visibility of their work.

## Theory and Hypothesis Development

## The Role of HDCs on Artist Popularity

Social media serves as a direct channel for artists to connect with their audiences. Platforms such as Instagram provide artists with the opportunity to share behind-the-scenes glimpses into their creative process through images, offering fans unique and personal insights into the evolution of their work; Twitter and Facebook enable artists to post updates and receive immediate feedback from their audience. Engaging in real-time conversations with fans, whether through comments, direct messages, or live sessions, fosters a sense of community. Social media not only strengthens the bond between artists and their audiences by helping to cut out the middleman but also serves as a powerful tool for expanding the artist’s fan base (Mangold & Faulds, 2009). While social media platforms differ in their functionalities and features, they each arguably help foster a direct line between the content creator and their audiences, thereby helping artists gain traction within their target community using an authentic and original “voice.”

Within the context of social media platforms and the functionalities they offer, HDCs are uniquely designed to strengthen the connection between artists and their audience. Emerging as a trend on the short-video platform TikTok, HDCs clearly exhibit characteristics that have long been associated with viral content—they are attractive and short, in addition to being affect-rich and easily broadcasted (Berger & Milkman, 2012; Henke, 2013). Extensive research supports the notion that affect-rich content is likely to achieve virality. For instance, a study analyzing news articles over three months found a relationship between emotion and virality: Content that is affect-rich is more likely to secure a place on most-emailed lists, irrespective of whether the conveyed emotions are positive or negative (Berger & Milkman, 2010). In daily conversations and social anecdotes, the potential for content virality is attributed to its high entertainment value; sharing surprising information can positively reflect on the sender, implying possession of interesting, surprising, and unconventional knowledge (Heath et al., 2001; Palka et al., 2009; Peters et al., 2009). Along these same lines, HDCs incorporate affect-rich features that are designed to evoke strong, positive emotions of enjoyment and happiness within users, thereby inviting wider dissemination and participation by design. In addition, the TikTok platform facilitates easy viewing and broadcasting of HDC video content, attracting a larger user base. This not only encourages users to adopt and engage in creating content contributing to HDCs (Aral & Walker, 2011; Chen et al., 2009) but also ensures increased user engagement with the original HDC content (Shi et al., 2014). Thus, HDCs and their content are particularly designed to be viral in nature and assimilate quickly through the TikTok network in an exponential manner.

The exponential growth in viewership of an HDC not only makes the content easily accessible but also enhances its recall value. A rich stream of literature on availability heuristics has consistently shown that the availability of information plays an important role in its consumption (e.g., Hamilton & Thompson, 2007; Menon & Raghubir, 2003; Raghubir & Valenzuela, 2006). Topics or events with more available information are not only more likely to be consumed but are also likely to be endowed with positive qualities (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973). This phenomenon is evident in consumer behavior, where products with more available information tend to be the preferred choice (Chernev, 2003). Similarly, the accessibility of information about a brand’s achievements and testimonials enhances customer trust (Wiles, 2007). In the context of songs featuring HDCs, the heightened availability sets them apart from songs without HDCs. This increased accessibility significantly raises the probability that a focal user will remain positively influenced by the HDCs. Consequently, when an HDC is created for a specific song, there is a strong likelihood that the focal user will actively seek out the original song (typically credited within the HDC) and decide to follow the credited artist. The virality of HDCs thus likely induces purposive action on the part of the user rather than passive engagement that may not result in any observable benefit.

Therefore, as a baseline, we expect that the introduction of an HDC will lead to an organic increase in the number of followers of the original musical artist featured therein, regardless of their longevity or preexisting popularity. The increased attention to the artist is likely to manifest across multiple platforms and domains, but we use followership on Spotify as our measure of the benefits of the HDC, given that Spotify is a leading platform dedicated to music consumption and provides a reasonable measure of an artist’s market appeal. Thus, we formally hypothesize:

H1: The presence of an HDC is associated with an increase in the featured artist’s popularity on Spotify.

## Differential Effects of HDCs by Creator Gender

We argue that the enhanced value generated from HDCs, as per H1 above, may not apply uniformly to all artists, given that the appeal of an HDC is intricately tied to its capacity to draw attention to the artist and can therefore vary significantly. To understand these potential variations and their underlying mechanisms, particularly in relation to gender dynamics, we turned to role congruity theory, which allowed us to examine how the gender of HDC creators might influence the effectiveness of HDCs in boosting artist popularity.

Role congruity theory provides a framework for understanding how gender stereotypes and societal expectations influence perceptions and evaluations of individuals in various roles. The theory posits that individuals are perceived more favorably when their characteristics align with the stereotypical requirements of their social roles, i.e., when there is role congruity (Eagly & Karau, 2002; Koenig & Eagly, 2014). Recent research has expanded the application of role congruity theory to explore how it can be leveraged to promote positive outcomes for women across various domains (Koenig & Eagly, 2014). For instance, research has found that in politics, women candidates who emphasize stereotypically feminine traits and issues in their campaigns are more likely to win elections, suggesting that role congruency can be strategically used to one’s advantage (Herrnson et al., 2003). Similar findings in the fields of entrepreneurship and education suggest that the presence of role-congruent models can enhance performance and potentially reduce gender gaps in male-dominated fields (Eddleston et al., 2016; Guarino & Borden, 2017). These studies indicate that by leveraging role-congruent behaviors, women can potentially achieve greater success in various professional contexts. We refer the interested reader to a review of this literature by del Carmen Triana et al. (2024).

Extending this framework to the music industry, from the perspective of role congruity theory, we contend that HDCs combining music and dance are likely to be the most appealing when they are showcased by a woman creator. This is because behavior consistent with an individual’s expected gender roles or those determined by gender stereotypes tends to elicit a more positive and reinforcing response (Moss-Racusin et al., 2010). Conversely, the response can be more negative when there is perceived incongruity between gender roles and the individual (Eagly & Karau, 2002). Modern and contemporary dance, which tend to be largely prevalent in HDCs, are more commonly and stereotypically associated with women than men (Gard, 2001; Jeschke, 1991; Risner, 2018), especially among younger users (Holdsworth, 2013). Social attitudes frequently link the attributes of dance, such as fluidity, coordination, and expressiveness, to natural feminine traits (Jakubowska & Byczkowska-Owczarek, 2018). These links lead to significant gender stereotyping, with women dancers generally being viewed more positively since there is clear role congruity between the art form and their gender. In contrast, when men engage in modern and contemporary dance, it tends to challenge traditional masculine norms that prioritize strength, solidness, and stoicism (Rochlen et al., 2009). This gendered association in societal perceptions can significantly influence the audience’s expectations when assessing the appeal of HDCs. In effect, gender-based role congruity can paradoxically benefit women more since the observed actions fit with gender stereotypes, while men in such contexts are more likely to be viewed as incongruous and may even attract mild derision.

We argue that these dynamics can influence how HDCs are perceived and the extent to which they can influence the focal artist’s followership. We focus attention on the creator of the HDC. When a woman (user or artist) creates an HDC that combines elements of music and dance in a synergistic manner, the fact that the creator is a woman is likely to enhance the appeal of the HDC through role congruity, all else being equal. In contrast, male creators, even when gifted, are more likely to be viewed as role-incongruous by the average platform user. If the male creator is of average creativity (which is to be expected), the benefits that accrue to the original music artist will generally be lower. Therefore, given the general hypothesized effect of HDCs on the growth of followership for the credited music artists, we anticipate a stronger treatment effect when initiated by women creators, based on role congruity between dance and women. We formally hypothesize:

H2: An HDC initiated by a woman creator (whether artist or user) is associated with a higher positive impact on the featured artist’s popularity on Spotify, relative to an HDC initiated by a male creator.

## Differential Effects of HDCs by Gender Concordance

Recall that although an HDC creator may be a woman, the featured song clip may be credited to an artist who is a man, and vice versa. Our hypotheses thus far have not addressed the gender of the original artist whose music is featured on the HDC and have only established the role of the creator’s gender. We now consider the artist’s gender in addition to the creator’s gender. Specifically, we explore the impact of genderconcordant dyads, where both the creator and the credited music artist are of the same gender, on the treatment effect of HDCs. Gender concordance has been addressed as an important factor in successful collaborative work across multiple contexts. For example, in the context of education, project teams comprising individuals of the same gender have been observed to contribute to increased class engagement and foster supportive behavior across both genders (Dasgupta et al., 2015; Woolley et al., 2015). Similarly, within organizations, gender concordance in leadership teams is linked to improved communication and decision-making, ultimately leading to more effective and cohesive outcomes (Bear & Woolley, 2011; Eagly & Karau, 2002). In the music industry, partnerships characterized by gender concordance capitalize on an intrinsic understanding of behavior, aesthetics and norms, thereby enriching collaborative contributions (Kilduff & Krackhardt, 2008; Lincoln & Miller, 1979; Perry-Smith & Mannucci, 2015); bands with samegender members experience smoother acceptance compared to integrated groups (Reger, 2014), while collaborations between same-gender vocalists and producers often result in a higher level of creative synergy (Lincoln & Miller, 1979).

As previously argued, dance is associated more closely with women than men. We thus ask: How can gender concordance provide value in this context, characterized by cases where the creator of the HDC and the original artist are of the same gender? An HDC initiated by a woman creator featuring a song clip by a woman artist may be especially effective in helping the artist gain followers on Spotify because of the benefits of role-congruence. The combination of a woman artist alongside a woman creator who can choreograph the music to attractive dance steps is more likely to generate higher levels of appeal. In contrast, HDCs initiated by men featuring male artists, though gender concordant, likely do not typically enjoy the benefits of role congruence. Therefore, we expect that among all possible combinations of creator-artist gender dyads, the dyad consisting of a woman creator and a woman artist will be the most effective in helping the focal artist gain traction, measured by the focal artist’s Spotify followers. We formally hypothesize:

H3: The introduction of a woman-concordant HDC, where the artist and creator are both women, is associated with a higher positive impact on the featured artist’s popularity on Spotify, relative to the other three possible gender dyads.

## Differential Effects of Artist-Generated vs. User-Generated HDCs: The Specific Case of Woman-Concordant Dyads

While H3 above argues for the benefits of gender concordance, especially in the case of women artists and HDC creators, we note that these observations can be divided into two types. In one type, the creator of the HDC is the artist herself, thereby ensuring gender concordance. In the second type, the artist and the creator of the HDC are different individuals but of the same gender. These differences within woman-concordant HDC dyads allow us to tease out the effects, if any, of the self-promotion penalty associated with women. Existing research has underscored the disproportionate challenges faced by women when they choose to promote their own achievements and creative pursuits through social media. As discussed earlier, women showcasing their accomplishments often encounter more critical comments, abuse, and accusations of arrogance compared to their male counterparts (Rose et al., 2012; Wotanis & McMillan, 2014). This differential treatment accorded to women may be rooted in discrimination, bias, structural inequities across fields, or indeed even in role congruity, with women being expected to be modest and selfeffacing, relative to men (Moss-Racusin & Rudman, 2010; Rudman, 1998). Regardless of the reasons for these dynamics, our context allows us to examine whether these selfpromotion penalties are likely to manifest here when we differentiate between woman-concordant HDC dyads where the creator is the artist (i.e., engages in self-promotion) versus when the creator is a user.

Given the documented self-promotion penalty that women typically face on social media, woman-concordant HDCs created by artists are more likely to face this penalty, relative to HDCs where the creator is not the focal artist, i.e., when they are another TikTok user. Artists who use their own songs in creating HDCs under their own names will be viewed as promoting themselves, thereby leading to some potential backlash. Thus, on the one hand, these HDCs are likely to generate less traction for the artists, all else being equal. On the other hand, role congruity may be of particular value in this context, especially since the woman artist generating an HDC for her own music through dance is likely to be viewed as more appealing since it conforms to feminine gender stereotypes (Eagly & Karau, 2002). Viewed in totality, it is arguable that any self-promotion penalty applied to women artist-generated HDCs will be nullified by the fact that the HDC benefits from gender-concordant role congruity. In essence, HDCs generated by women artists face two competing forces—an intensified self-promotion penalty, arising from their dual roles as creator and artist, and a potentially reduced backlash due to dance being role congruent. We can disentangle these forces by comparing user-initiated HDCs with those initiated by artists within the sample of woman-concordant HDCs. If the self-promotion penalty persists, HDCs created by women artists should show lower performance in terms of Spotify followership growth compared to HDCs generated by women users. Discovering no difference would indicate that role congruity elicited through dance helps to offset any potential self-promotion penalties for women in this context. We therefore propose:

H4: When there is a “woman creator–woman artist” concordance, the impact of an artist-generated HDC should be no different than that of a user-generated HDC on the artist’s popularity on Spotify.

## Empirical Methodology

## Data

To conduct our empirical analysis, we focused on HDCs created on the TikTok platform. Recall that in order to create and share an HDC, the user must first choose an existing song clip on TikTok or upload a new song clip. Once the song clip is selected, the user can record a dance video, ranging from 15 seconds to 1 minute, using the song clip and post the video with a formatted hashtag (#songnamechallenge). These are often created independently by users, without any coordination with the original artist, but can also be created by the artist themself. To test our hypotheses, we compared HDCs introduced on TikTok in a specific time period with similar songs that were present on TikTok but without an HDC associated with them, thereby isolating the effect of the HDC itself on the focal artist’s followership on Spotify. To conduct this analysis, we compiled a unique dataset by triangulating across song-level, artist-level, and TikTok platform-level data from different sources, as described below.

Song-level data: We first obtained 2,949 newly released songs on Spotify between January 1, 2020, and April 30, 2020, in Canada and the United States. For each song, we obtained song-related information, including release date, artist name, and music genre. We also captured underlying musical attributes for each song, including measures of beats per minute, danceability, causticness, energy, liveness, valence, speechiness, and duration from Spotify. These characteristics are useful in assessing the intangible aspects of the song, e.g., song quality. We only considered singles—songs released separately from an album and songs by individual artists, omitting songs from bands to reduce the effect of band heterogeneity on the analysis. Using the artist popularity index provided by Spotify, we removed the top 10 most popular artists from our sample to avoid any bias introduced by the celebrity effect. Celebrities typically have followership on Spotify in very high numbers, potentially skewing any analysis. This step resulted in the identification of 1,873 unique songs.

Artist-level data: For the 1,873 single songs we identified, we used a database of 19,305 North American artists from Chartmetric to collect specific information about the corresponding artists. For this study, we exclusively focused on solo artists and coded each artist’s gender as a binary variable. Since some artists may adopt a stage name, we crosschecked gender coding based on the artist’s profile on Chartmetric. Following this strategy, we were able to identify the gender of all 1,873 artists. To obtain additional artist-level data, we extracted time-invariant information such as artists’ main genres and time-varying information such as daily Spotify followers between Dec 1, 2019, and May 31, 2020.<sup>3</sup>

## Variable Descriptions

Dependent variable: Our primary dependent variable of interest was the daily percentage change of Spotify followers at the artist level. We log-transformed the daily Spotify followers to adjust for the right skewness of the variable. Then we took the first differences to obtain the percentage change in daily Spotify followers (in log). Effectively, the variable was normalized, since it captured the (logged) percentage growth in followership across artists, thereby allowing for reasonable comparisons across music artists.

Independent variables: Our key independent variable was the HDC status of a song on TikTok (denoted HDC), which took the value of 1 if an HDC was associated with the song and 0 otherwise. PostHDC was defined as the time before (PostHDC = 0) and after (PostHDC = 1) an HDC was posted.

Control variables: We controlled for both time-variant and time-invariant variables at both the song and artist levels. Song-level control variables included time-invariant music attributes (i.e., danceability, speechiness, acousticness, instrumentalness, liveness, valence, tempo, loudness, and primary genre; see Table 1 for variable descriptions) and timevariant variables, including the number of days since the song had been released on Spotify, as well as the song’s tenure on TikTok. We included artist gender dummies (WomanArtist), coded 1 for women and 0 otherwise, and HDC-initiator gender dummies (WomanCreator), coded 1 for HDCs initiated by women, regardless of whether they were artists or users, and 0 otherwise. ArtistGen was coded as 1 if an HDC was initiated by the artist, 0 otherwise.

Table 2 provides a detailed description of the variables and summary statistics. In the sample, women artists constituted 33.9% of the artist sample. This is somewhat consistent with the typical gender distribution in the digital music industry, which is roughly 21.7% (Smith et al., 2020). The majority of the HDCs were initiated by women (54.8%), regardless of whether they were artists or users. Users initiated most of the HDCs (63%), relative to the artists themselves. ## Empirical Specification

To examine the causal effect of the HDC on an artist’s daily Spotify follower growth, we adopted a difference-indifferences (DID) model (Meyer, 1995). In our case, the treated group represented songs with an HDC, while the control group included songs on TikTok but without an HDC. Since the treatment in our context was staggered (HDCs may occur at different times), we followed prior research (e.g., Gao & Zhang, 2017) and normalized the time dimension to simulate an “event study” approach to identification (Callaway & Sant’Anna, 2021; Goodman-Bacon, 2021). We set the day a song first appeared with an HDC to Day 8, with Days 1-7 as pre-treatment (PostHDC = 0) and Days 8-15 as post-treatment periods (PostHDC = 1). We identified an observation window of 15 days to assess the short period of the viral dance challenge effect. Since viral content is designed to gauge immediate attention, the life span of viral content is relatively short (Vaish et al., 2012). Therefore, choosing a window of 15 days allowed us to assess the immediate impact of the HDC, consistent with the definition of virality in the literature.

Songs that received the treatment in our setting were not randomly assigned; we therefore had to account for the process by which songs were selected for an HDC. We used PSM to address this selection bias (Abadie, 2005). PSM has been employed in various nonexperimental settings when the assignment of treatment is not random (Dehejia & Wahba, 2002) and used in combination with DID analysis for causal inference of the effect of non-exogenous interventions (e.g., Kumar et al., 2018; Mayzlin et al., 2014; Tan & Netessine, 2020). We first predicted the propensity score of a song receiving the treatment (HDC) using a logistic regression on key observable covariates in Table 2 on the day prior to the song’s release on Spotify. For every treated song, we apply one-to-one nearest neighbor matching without replacement on the propensity score to identify a matched control song (Austin, 2010), as shown in Figure 1. Given that each song was associated with one unique artist, the matching process resulted in 62 artists who had songs with an HDC and a matched sample of artists with songs not associated with an HDC.

We note that in the context of creative work like music, finding exact matches to a treated observation within a set of control observations cannot fully control for the inherent quality and appeal of each individual song. While matching closely allows some mitigation of these differences in unobservable (or unmeasurable) quality across songs, we acknowledge that perfect identification of the causal effect in this context could only be generated through a randomized experiment. Conducting a randomized experiment in this context is practically infeasible as such experiments would, first, not be permissible on platforms and, second, would potentially affect the professional performance of the artists involved. In such contexts, we used matching methods to match treated units to control units as closely as possible and included all the matching covariates in the final regression. Finally, we performed a series of robustness tests to show that the identified effect of an HDC on artist followership remained in place under different regression and sample specifications. We next describe how we showed balance between control and treated samples.

Table 3 provides the balance check and shows that the treated and control groups were similar in all observable song-level and artist-level covariates after matching. Figure

2 shows the distributions of the propensity scores for the two subsamples before and after matching, confirming that the control and treatment samples were similar post-matching. We also compared the daily percentage change in Spotify followers between groups prior to the treatment (t-stat = - 0.352, ns). These checks provided confidence that prior to treatment, the treatment and control groups were largely similar in terms of trends and characteristics.

We first ensured that the parallel trends assumption was met, i.e., the paths of the outcome variable over time were parallel between the control and treatment groups prior to the treatment. It is important to ensure that this assumption was not violated in our dataset so that the treatment effect can be cleanly identified. We employed the approach suggested by Angrist and Pischke (2008) and estimated the interactions of treatment and the pre-treatment period on the daily percentage change of Spotify followers (results presented in Table 4). The estimated coefficients were statistically indistinguishable from zero in the pre-treatment periods, indicating that the parallel trends assumption was not violated in our context, adding strength to the inference that any observed differences post-treatment are attributable to the treatment, i.e., the introduction of an HDC.

## Robustness Checks

## The Effect of TikTok: Testing for Alternative Explanations

Our primary argument here is that HDCs make artists’ music salient, thereby leading to increased followership on Spotify. However, we also considered an alternative explanation—that it is the presence of a song on TikTok that leads to an increase in Spotify followership and not the HDC per se. Recall that our baseline analysis compares songs on TikTok with an HDC to songs on TikTok without an HDC; for a robustness test, we checked if the presence of a song on TikTok itself can lead to an increase in followership. To identify this effect, we isolated songs on TikTok without an HDC and matched them with songs that appeared on Spotify at the same time but were not on TikTok. Effectively, this contrast captured the effect of a song being available in some form on TikTok on an artist’s Spotify followers. Following the same propensity score matching approach described above, we matched songs using song-level attributes, song genre, and artists’ Spotify popularity pre-song release, resulting in a sample of 214 songs—107 songs on TikTok and a set of 107 matched songs that were on Spotify but not on TikTok. Like the earlier analysis, we performed covariate balance checks to ensure equivalence between the control and treatment groups, the results of which are available upon request. To ensure consistency, we used the same 15-day observation window and estimated the following baseline model:

Furthermore, it allowed us to consider unobserved characteristics, such as artists strategically choosing to release songs at specific times with a higher likelihood of going viral.

## Confounding Effects Within the Observation Window

Our original analysis used an observation window of 7 days preand post-treatment to examine the effects of the HDC on Spotify followership. This analysis assumed that we had visibility into a song on TikTok for at least 7 days prior to the introduction of an HDC. However, in some cases, a song may be added to TikTok and subsequently used in an HDC within the 7-day pretreatment period. In such cases, the effect of the HDC we estimated could have been confounded by the effect of the song being added to TikTok itself. Although we found no significant effect from a song being added to TikTok itself (from above), we still considered the potential for confounding arising from a song being added to TikTok and thereafter featured in an HDC within 7 days, a situation depicted in Figure 4.

## Discussion and Conclusion

How can artists navigate a crowded marketplace to gain recognition for their artistic endeavors? Breaking through in popular culture remains a persistent challenge for all artists. However, it is worth noting that this struggle is often more intense for women artists. Organizational and instructional factors, such as studio culture, workplace discrimination, and the absence of women role models collectively contribute to barriers that impede the ability of women artists to effectively gain traction, compete on an level playing field with men, and achieve parity within the music industry. Significant industry data exists to show the downstream effects of these disparities in opportunities and outcomes associated with women artists, especially in the early stages of their careers (Prior et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2020).

The advent of social media and user-generated content, with the ability to democratize discourse and allow underrepresented voices to be heard, has been viewed as a positive step towards achieving gender parity, especially in the creative arts. However, even in this domain, it has been observed that women have markedly different experiences compared to men (Buckels et al., 2014; Fang, 2023). For instance, research has shown that the responses to women (compared to men) on platforms such as YouTube and Instagram are systematically more biased (Peng et al., 2022; Wotanis & McMillan, 2014). Explanations for the systematic gender-based biases on these platforms in many cases are based on gender stereotypes or role congruity theory, suggesting that actions that appear at odds with stereotypically feminine behavior will be responded to in a harsh manner (Moss-Racusin & Rudman, 2010). Thus, even on social media, where it is possible to promote one’s own work, women artists are often hamstrung by gender-related penalties associated with self-promotion and acting contrary to stereotypes.

We propose that HDCs, presented through TikTok’s short video sharing platform, can serve as a pathway for women and other underrepresented music artists to gain salience and attention. Using a novel dataset of songs and artists collated from diverse sources such as the Chartmetric musician database, Spotify, and TikTok, our analysis shows that the presence of an HDC featuring music from an artist increases the visibility and salience of artist and is thus associated with a short-term increase in followership on Spotify. <sup>5</sup> Theoretically, it is worth noting that HDCs possess many of the properties associated with viral content—they are designed to be appealing, to incorporate positive affect, and to evoke strong emotional responses from viewers and can be easily broadcast and forwarded (Chen et al., 2009). Furthermore, unlike other social media platforms like YouTube and Facebook, HDC content is short, focused, and does not incorporate much text or material requiring evaluation (Herrman, 2019). Thus, HDCs are ideal vehicles for generating attention and salience for artists since they function as short, appealing advertisements that have the capacity to go viral.

To investigate the drivers behind the main effect, we examined whether creator gender moderates the impact of HDCs on artists’ Spotify follower growth, building on arguments from role congruity research. Our findings confirm that the positive effect of HDCs is primarily driven by HDCs created by women, given that dance and music have deep associations with stereotypically feminine traits and roles. As such, the attractive combination of music and dance in an HDC created by women appears to be more appealing. Importantly, we empirically show that the presence of HDCs, especially those created by women, enables the corresponding women artists to achieve substantial gains in their Spotify followership of approximately 3% daily. We further disentangled the potential self-promotion penalty for women and found no noticeable difference in the impact of the HDC depending on whether it is user-initiated or artist self-initiated, particularly in woman-concordant cases where one might anticipate a more severe penalty. This contrasts with prior research showing that women suffer backlash for selfpromoting on social media platforms like Twitter and YouTube (Wotanis & McMillan, 2014). Our work suggests that the unique and appealing format of HDCs, as well as the benefits of role congruity surrounding women and dance, may help attenuate the typical negative response that is usually associated with women’s self-promotion.

Our study is the first to examine the HDC phenomenon, allowing us to provide some implications for both platform managers and musical artists seeking to gain traction through their social media activities. With respect to artists, it is often assumed that successful social media campaigns require significant resources to produce content in a professional manner, including a social media ad plan and production staff. Our work shows that short video viral content like the HDC can achieve similar successful outcomes at a much lower cost, albeit with a lot more user participation. While HDCs can generate a large number of views on TikTok itself, which can be beneficial even for highly established musicians (Burke, 2020), we show that the benefits can also manifest in terms of the number of users who follow the artist on Spotify, arguably a more important and proximal economic outcome for the artist. For women artists who may otherwise face hostility on platforms like YouTube (Wotanis & McMillan, 2014), the short-video HDC format appears to be particularly beneficial in terms of drawing eyeballs, attention, and salience.

One factor that we did not consider in our analysis is whether TikTok’s algorithm intentionally promotes women artists, relative to male artists, thereby indirectly creating the enhanced salience for women artists that we identified. However, this seems unlikely (Perez, 2020) since the content on a user’s page is largely driven by user input. Specifically, the algorithm considers the set of previous videos a user has liked or shared, the diverse accounts a user follows, the comments a user posts, and the content created by the user to generate what is shown on a user’s page. Thus, TikTok’s algorithms are designed to keep the “For You” page fresh and diverse and aim to remove repetitive content and spam. We see no evidence for gender-based differential promotions here, suggesting that this issue is likely not particularly salient in our context.<sup>6</sup>

It is also important to note that while HDCs may offer immediate benefits in reducing gender disparity in terms of gaining traction, they are not a comprehensive solution to the broader issues of gender bias. While the short-term benefits that accrue to the artist are evident in our analysis, it is important to consider the potential long-term consequences of relying on gender-congruent strategies (Wang et al., 2024). Gender congruence unfortunately builds on long-held stereotypes about behavior that tend to be resistant to change. Therefore, in this particular case, while gender congruence is viewed as an advantage for women artists, we acknowledge that this may reinforce gender stereotypes in the long run. For instance, the association of dance with femininity (and women artists) could potentially limit the perceived range of artistic expression for women artists in other contexts. Similarly, the effect of gender congruence and gender-based stereotypes could have adverse effects on male artists. We believe more research is needed to examine the long-term effects of gender stereotypes within the creative arts, even if they are helpful to specific populations, to avoid reinforcing potentially harmful and restrictive gender stereotypes.

The impact of HDCs and the associated mechanisms that we study here are potentially generalizable to other platforms that have introduced similar short-video functionalities: Instagram and Facebook, for instance, have also introduced “Reel” functionalities that includes short videos, while YouTube recently introduced the “YouTube Shorts” functionality for short video content, which often also features dance and music. To the extent that platforms can keep the short-video format free of gender-based bias and hostility, it is likely to lead to enhanced activity on the platform. Bias and negative reviews on social media platforms remain a persistent problem, which also typically impacts women more than men. Our work here suggests that such social media platforms should work towards retaining the appeal and engagement of viral HDC content, ensuring a supportive environment, and reducing any prevalence of bias and hostility. Finally, highlighting the beneficial spillover effects of HDCs and other forms of short-video activity on other platforms (such as Spotify) may help enhance the value of these platforms. Our work provides a first-order estimate of where these spillover benefits may come from. Future research could explore whether repeated engagement with HDCs or the strategic timing of HDCs might extend their impact. Additionally, investigating how artists can leverage the initial boost in followership to obtain more enduring career benefits could provide valuable insights.

Our work here is subject to some limitations. First, although we provide some evidence of the positive effect of HDCs on artists’ popularity through the number of followers on Spotify, we cannot provide a definitive estimate of what each additional follower represents in terms of either music streams or revenue. This data is not available at the level of granularity needed and remains a limitation. Second, we are limited by the scope of our study since the artists in our sample were either from the United States or Canada. Therefore, the results may not fully generalize to other countries. Third, we only considered individuals and not songs from bands to keep the analysis relatively simple—future work could extend our work to develop a fuller theoretical structure that accommodates bands and other collectives. Fourth, given that the level of virality varies across different HDC campaigns, it would be advantageous to include the number of TikTok users participating in each HDC to better gauge engagement levels. Unfortunately, due to data limitations, such information was unavailable to us. Furthermore, using a dummy treatment variable simplified the propensity score matching process and allowed us to better estimate the short-term effects of HDCs. However, future research addressing a more continuous measure of virality would be useful in providing a more complete understanding of the benefits of HDCs for artists.

Fifth, given our relatively short observation window (6 months) and the relative recency of the HDC phenomenon on TikTok, we recognize that we had a relatively small sample of songs to work with. On the one hand, this allowed us to rule out other sources of time-based heterogeneity that may affect demand. On the other hand, we were constrained by the sample size in terms of the power of our analysis. We believe that our analysis is robust and provides a reliable first look at the value of HDCs for women artists but acknowledge that more work is needed to fully flesh out the nature of the HDC phenomenon. Future longitudinal research is needed to track artists’ careers over extended periods to understand the lasting impact of viral social media phenomena like HDCs on gender disparities in the music industry.

We note that the COVID-19 pandemic formed the contextual backdrop of our study; however, the shock from this global event impacted both the control and treated groups. We acknowledge that it was not entirely possible to remove the influence of the pandemic in our dataset; therefore, our results must be interpreted with this point in mind. Finally, we could not completely isolate the intrinsic music attributes of the song and the artist’s gender, since these are codetermined. This remains an issue in all research examining the creative industries, where the intrinsic attributes of the artifact are hard to separate from the attributes of the artist. We attempted to find “similar” artifacts using matching, but acknowledge this limitation. Indeed, as in any archival data analysis, there remain potential sources of endogeneity that we may not have captured in our specifications. We acknowledge these and provided a series of robustness tests to bolster our analysis. That said, achieving perfect identification here remains challenging, and we acknowledge this as a limitation.

Despite these limitations, our work is an early attempt to examine the motivation and identify the role of technologydriven platforms that empower women in music production to succeed in their careers. Future steps will include finding mechanisms to motivate these platforms to be more inclusive and attractive to women in digital music production. We believe that our findings are critical for academic audiences as well because they help us understand how a technologydriven phenomenon can encourage and empower women music artists. The gender disparity in the music industry is well recognized, and actionable technology-driven remedies are needed to mitigate this gap. Our research takes the first step in exploring such a remedy. We show that in settings with low levels of female representation due to systemic industry biases, a technology-driven, democratized music promotion process can engender positive and meaningful changes. Our work also speaks to the power that social media platforms may have in improving gender parity in the creative industries. We hope that our work here sparks more interest in both the shortvideo HDC format and in broader research studying gender equity issues in the creative industries and on social media platforms.
