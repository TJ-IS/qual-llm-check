## Highlights

 We study Human Flesh Search (HFS) behavior.

 We establish three contagious mechanisms driving HFS behavior.

 We establish two non-contagious mechanisms driving HFS behavior.

 We shed insights on the technology affordances and constraints.

## Abstract

Human Flesh Search (HFS) has profusely proliferated in recent years. We adopted the multiple-case, replication design approach, to investigate 21 HFS cases spanning from 2007 to 2014, comprising 204,008 posts from 67,375 posters. Our preliminary analyses depicted consistent patterns across categories of legal, moral, and entertainment. We established three contagious mechanisms driving HFS behavior (i.e., fueled emotionalism, blind acceptance, and collective amnesia) and two non-contagious mechanisms (i.e., isolated dissension and sluggish update). We also shed insights on the technology affordances and constraints. Critical theoretical contributions and important practical implications for HFS initiators, facilitators, and designers of forums are discussed.

Keywords: Human Flesh Search; Online Community; Contagious and Non-Contagious Mechanism; IT Affordances and Constraints

## 1. Introduction

“Last month a Chinese official in charge of Internet surveillance gave notice that mobs of web users who turn on individuals and make their lives a misery will not be tolerated. In China it happens often and on a massive scale, earning the phenomenon the title of the ‘human flesh search engine’ ...” (BBC 2014)

Human Flesh Search (HFS), literally translated from the Chinese phrase “ren rou sou suo,” connotes “people-powered” search, as opposed to simply “google” or “automated engine” search (Wang et al. 2010a). Given the paucity of research, there is no agreed upon

#

definition. Nonetheless, assimilating the various definitions, three underlying characteristics can be identified, namely (i) first, HFS attracts a massive pool of voluntary participants via online platforms (Pan 2010; Wang et al. 2010b). However, not to be confused with simply a “hot topic,” HFS defers in the next two critical characteristics; (ii) second, HFS demands a concerted effort by online participants, hence the name “people-powered” search, to achieve an ultimate goal in response to an inquiry or an issue (Wang et al. 2010b). Succinctly expressed by Bu (2013: p.2), it portrays “one asks, thousands answer” or “one issue, thousands explore.” It is worth noting that this effort is often collaborative and cumulative as participants typically build upon earlier findings (Chen and Sharma 2011; Wang et al. 2010b); and (iii) third, the goal is either to identify a target (such as “cyber manhunt” for a hit-and-run driver) (Chen and Sharma 2011) or to unravel the truth (such as fake pictures of “South China Tiger”) (Wang et al. 2010; Cheng et al. 2012). In view of these three characteristics, “2017 Oscar Blunder,”<sup>1</sup> for example, is considered a “hot topic” by generating much online buzz, but it does not constitute a HFS. Our research is driven by four important motivations.

First, HFS has profusely proliferated. Not only has it burgeoned in China, it has also gained traction overseas, such as Japan, South Korea, Singapore, and other countries (Wang et al. 2010b). Opinions are, however, divided regarding HFS, terming it a double-edged sword (Bu 2013). Some laud it for providing a channel to seek justice (Fang and Lin 2010) and realize civil superintendence (Yuan 2009), while others frown upon it as “becoming out of control” (Liu 2008), with punitive consequences such as detainment by police (Chen and Sharma 2011), loss of employment (Chao and Tao 2012), cyber violence (Liang and Lu 2010), and even suicides (Dennis 2008). Despite its escalating phenomenon, there is a lack of academic investigation (Chao and Tao 2012).

Second, given that some online communities, such as Crowdsourcing, Wikipedia, and Open Source community, also seem (i) to amass a large group of online participants (Sai and

Orito 2011), (ii) to demand concerted effort, (iii) to achieve some unified goal (e.g., fund innovation in crowdfunding, contribute content to Wikipedia, perform coding in open source) (albeit different from the unified goal of target identification or truth revelation in HFS), some researchers may erroneously misconstrue HFS to be no different. However, the similarity ends there, as HFS differs in three important ways. One, while other online communities typically observe visible contributions (e.g., monetary funds, programming codes, etc.) from a majority of the members to foster identity (Bagozzi and Lee 2002), emotional affinity, and passion (Cova and Cova 2002), the percentage of HFS participants that literally contributes to useful cumulative information (e.g., leads, clues) that guides the eventual identification of the target is alarmingly low. Two, while other online communities are driven by consumers, which may not be as conspicuous (e.g., readers of Wikipedia or users of Open Office), the support demonstrated by HFS consumers is often overtly demonstrated (Dholakia et al. 2004; Shen et al. 2011). Three, while other online communities may boast of a more unified goal, HFS may occasionally have marplots and the so-called “galleryites” to detract others from their common cause (Sai and Orito 2011). Without scrutinizing the mechanisms of HFS at work, academics and practitioners may erroneously assume that HFS behaves exactly like other online communities.

Third, extant research, still in its infancy, has predominantly focused on either the antecedents and motivations (Wang et al. 2009(2011) or the consequences and impacts (Chen and Sharma (2011) of HFS, but has gravely overlooked the mechanisms driving HFS. It is worth noting that Cheng et al. (2012) have analogized HFS to that of an infectious disease spread. Three similarities are highlighted, namely (i) like infectious disease, there is a call for participation; (ii) participants who get involved in HFS are likened to those infected by the virus; and (iii) it can be depicted by the susceptible-infectious-recovered (SIR) model (Kermack and McKendrick 1927) (see Section 5 and Figure 5 for details). Hence, it is critical that we elucidate the contagion mechanism underlying HFS. Traditionally, contagion research can be dichotomized into the studies on emotional (i.e., the spread of mood and affect) (Doherty 1997; (Hatfield and Cacioppo 1994) and behavioral (i.e., the spread of action and behavior) aspects. By unraveling the mechanisms (contagious and non-contagious) of HFS, we seek to bridge the research gap to provide a more comprehensive understanding of HFS.

Fourth, beyond extant research work that focuses on the psychology, sociology, morality, or legality of HFS (Cheung 2009a), it is critical that we examine the mechanisms driving HFS, for which Information Technology (IT) affordances and constraints play a critical role (Leonardi 2011; Zammuto et al. 2007). As IT has conferred us with much interactivity and dynamism (Murugesan 2007), it has exerted imperceptible influences on the way people behave (Fogg 2002) and is claimed to be instrumental in orchestrating HFS (Wang et a 2010a), but it falls short of further elucidation. Without unraveling this “black box” of IT affordances and constraints, the amazing workings of HFS will always be shrouded in mystery. In view of the aforementioned motivations, our research questions are:

(RQ1) What are the mechanisms (contagious and non-contagious) driving HFS?

(RQ2) How are these mechanisms afforded or constrained by IT?

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1 Human flesh search

Originated in China in 2001 (Wang et al. 2010a), HFS has spread to other countries (Chao and Tao 2012; Chen and Sharma 2011). Although it is typically triggered by news regarding wrongdoers whose misbehavior infuriates the public (Cheung 2009a), HFS episodes do cover a wide range of topics, ranging from extramarital affair to entertainment, and from people/animal abuse to individual/business activities (Wu 2011). Consequently, HFS often evolves into a massive campaign, involving hundreds or even thousands of participants, and necessitates a concerted effort to either identify a target or unravel the truth. Amazingly, HFS is effective in identifying targets within a short period of time, at times even more efficient than the authority. For example, in October 2008, the netizens only used less than 11 hours to find out the identity and detailed personal information of the target when a video of a middle-aged man harassing a teenager girl was posted on the Web (Cheng et al. 2011).

##

Still in its infancy, three streams of HFS research dominate. The first stream examines the antecedents and motivations of participants in HFS. Some researchers find that participants are mostly motivated by intrinsic factors, such as volunteerism (Chen and Sharma (2011) and a strong sense of justice and surveillance (Wang et al. 2009), especially when they derive nationalistic sentiment in exposing government scandal or corruption, or when they identify evil-doers (Cheng et al. 2012). Over time, HFS is motivated not only by participants who are righteous and seek justice but also by participants who want to placate their curiosity, voyeurism, or other personal needs (Wu 2011). Apart from intrinsic factors, other researchers find that participants in HFS may be driven by virtual currency (Pan 2010). Applying expectancy theory, Sai and Orito (2011) propose information prospectability to explain the motivation on information-provision by HFS participants, which includes three elements: the expectancy for the attainment of desired performance goals, instrumentality for gratitude from others as a reward, and valence on the reward received.

The second stream explores the consequences and impacts of HFS. Predictably, there are two camps of thoughts. On one hand, there are proponents of HFS who underscore the potential positive outcomes of HFS. For example, HFS enables the solicitation of public help to search for missing family members (e.g., the young who are abducted or the old who have lost their way) (Liu, 2008b; Liu, 2008a); also, HFS helps combat illegal behavior and deter unethical behavior (Chen and Sharma (2011), and in a way, HFS serves to empower citizens by offering them the freedom of speech, so as to open up access to government and promote transparency (Bu 2013; Chen and Sharma 2011; Wu 2011). On the other hand, there are opponents of HFS who caution against the potential negative outcomes of HFS. Topping the list is the concern over privacy invasion (Chen and Sharma 2011; Sai and Orito 2011), be it to individuals or their family members ((Tao and Chao 2011). If the HFS episodes entail ethical or legal issues, such a revelation of information related to the individuals or their family members is tantamount to public shaming (Bu 2013). Just as dire is the fact that, sometimes, the impact will spill over from online to offline (Liu 2008). Some punitive consequences include the loss of employment (for adults) and disciplinary actions by the school (for teenagers) (Chao and Tao 2012) or even real-life harassment and death threats (Liang and Lu 2010)! Hence, some researchers have labeled this phenomenon “Internet mob” or “online mob” (Wang et al. 2010b), with a call for law and legislation to be revised to provide a more effective protection against the intimidation and harassment resulting from HFS (Bu 2013; Cheung 2009a, 2009b).

The third stream, which is more in line with our research investigation, attempts to figure out how HFS evolves (Tao and Chao, 2011). Some researchers embark on a computer simulation to model the individual interaction and network growth in HFS, such as through a dynamic network structure along dimensions of network density and distribution degree (Wang et al. 2009). Different from them, Cheng et al. (2011) use epidemic models to simulate HFS-evolving process (likened that to the spread of an infectious disease). In line with this idea of an infectious disease, we are keen on the mechanisms driving HFS that are either contagious or non-contagious.

Traditionally, social contagion research can be dichotomized into the studies on emotional contagion (Doherty 1997; Hatfield and Cacioppo 1994) and behavioral contagion (Stephenson and Fielding 1971; Wheeler 1966). Emotional contagion refers to the process in which expressed moods are infectious to others (Doherty 1997; Hatfield and Cacioppo 1994; Hatfield et al. 1993). It is defined as “a process in which a person or group influences the emotions or behavior of another person or group through the conscious or unconscious induction of emotion states and behavioral attitudes” (Schoenewolf 1990: p.50). It can occur unconsciously, via automatically mimicking others’ expressions, vocalizations, postures, and movements (Hatfield et al. 1993; Neumann and Strack 2000). Others view it as a conscious emotional comparison process, whereby people consciously synchronize their own emotions with the emotions expressed by their peers in order to facilitate social interactions (Gump and Kulik 1997; Schoenewolf 1990; Sullins 1991). Earlier research on emotional contagion indicates that it mainly relies on non-verbal communication, in which people mimic one’s expressions, vocalizations, postures, and movements with others (Hatfield et al. 1993). A more recent work gathers evidence from massive-scale field studies on Facebook and Twitter to suggest that emotions can also be transferred to others through networks via text-based computer-mediated communication, in the absence of non-verbal cues in direct interpersonal contact (Ferrara and Yang 2015; Kramer et al. 2014).

Behavioral contagion refers to the process in which certain behaviors exhibited by an actor are imitated by an observer, through either interpersonal communications or mass media coverage (Stephenson and Fielding 1971; Wheeler 1966). This process is attributed to a variety of different factors, such as reduction of fear or restraints (Wheeler 1966), identity of the actor (Stephenson and Fielding 1971), characteristics of the observer (Ogunlade 1979), and social norms (Gino et al. 2009). Prior research on behavioral contagion typically focused on undesirable behaviors that spread through crowds, such as aggressive behavior (Wheeler and Caggiula 1966; Wheeler and Levine 1967; Wheeler and Smith 1967), rule violation behavior (Ennett et al. 1997; Rowe et al. 1992), and even deliberate self-harm (Gould et al. 2003; Higgins and Range 1996; Taiminen et al. 1998).

## 2.2 Information technology and human flesh search

Technology affordances can be conceptualized in various ways. Earlier definitions are, however, nebulous about whether technology affordance is “a property of an object or of the relationship between an object and an actor” (Volkoff and Strong 2013: p.822), with the latter being dominant. Recently, Robey et al. (2013) perform a comprehensive review to distinguish among three conceptualizations of technology affordances.

First, the materiality of IT has often been downplayed or marginalized in the past in favor of social construction (Leonardi 2011; Orlikowski 2010), but has now resurfaced with renewed interests. Robey et al. (2013) hence suggest that traditional views be revised to capture the influence of IT’s “material properties” (p.386) which refer to “the objective properties of artifacts and natural objects rather than the interpretations or interventions of social actors” (Robey et al. 2013: p.385). Materiality can refer to computers, database, software, and other IT artifacts. Regardless of the possibility of social construction in using technology, it is opined that materiality sometimes compel people to use the system or features “as intended.” Hutchby (2001) concurs by underscoring that the interpretation of technology is “not unbounded,” and there are “some limits on what is possible to do with, around, or via the artifact” (Hutchby 2001: p.453). To illustrate how “materiality” of IT provides technology affordances, some have attributed the proliferation of HFS to the ease of “access” (e.g., through IT artifacts such as the Internet and IT features that facilitate “sharing,” “cut and paste,” etc.) as well as “interactivity” (e.g., through IT features that allow “post versus reply,” etc.) (Herold 2009).

Second, technology affordances can be interpreted as relational concepts (i.e., potential interactions between people and technology) as advocated by most socio-technical researchers (Faraj and Azad 2012; Leonardi 2011). This contrasts with the “materiality” conceptualization (which views IT artifact as independent characteristics) by emphasizing that it is situated in the context of interactions between people and technology (Leonardi 2011). While the materiality of IT does not change, the affordances of an IT artifact might vary across different context (Leonardi 2011). Thus, people may either regard technology as affording distinct potential for action in some context or consider it as constraints that prevent their goal fulfillment (Leonardi 2011). Hence, Majchrzak and Markus ((2013) define technology affordance as “an action potential, that is, to what an individual or organization with a particular purpose can do with a technology or information system.” Conversely, they define technology constraint as “ways in which an individual or organization can be held back from accomplishing a particular goal when using a technology or system” (Majchrzak and Markus (2013). In essence, IT artifact alone is a necessary but insufficient condition for conceptualization. Researchers have found that people do use the same technologies very differently across organizations, or even within organizations. Sometimes, the usage is aligned with the intentions of the IT artifact designer; sometimes it is not, simply because people choose to ignore or work around things. Moreover, it has been noted that multiple affordances can arise from a single actor and material relationship (Volkoff and Strong 2013: p.822). To illustrate this “relational” conceptualization, de-individuation results due to the ability of netizens to recognize and exploit online anonymity (conferred by IT artifact) (Hinduja

#

2008). This inevitably equips netizens with the perfect disguise and excuse to destructively act in HFS as they have little fear of punishment (Schweingruber 2000). Likewise, although both HFS and microblogging rely on “Internet technologies,” the technology affordances and constraints in HFS do not necessarily apply to microblogging because people interact with these “Internet technologies” in different ways for different goals (Leonardi 2013; Volkoff and Strong 2013).

The third conceptualization, albeit similar to the second conceptualization in terms of being “relational,” has a subtle but critical difference. Researchers contend whether it should be the “relationships” between the IT artifacts and actors (second conceptualization) or the “emerging relational properties” of the IT artifact–actor system (third conceptualization), as advocated by most sociomaterial researchers (Orlikowski 2010). Indeed, Orlikowski (2007) even proposes the elimination of the dichotomy between the social and the material, to suggest a unified conceptualization that incorporates both under the label of sociomateriality (Orlikowski 2007; Orlikowski and Scott 2008). This conceptualization emphasizes that both the social and the material are intertwined in the ongoing, situated practice. Aptly put across by Bloomfield et al. (2010: p.420), “The affordances of technological objects are typically “co-presence” ... of other social actors and other objects …” To illustrate, the way Facebook is initially configured to be used by people, and the way people subsequently reshape its uses (e.g., beyond network to embrace marketing) to tempt Facebook into revising (add/delete/modify IT artifacts), exemplifies this intertwined relationship.

In sum, to interpret technology affordances and constraints in HFS, examination should be placed in the particular context of HFS with reference to technology, people, as well as their interactions (Zammuto et al. 2007).

Population and sample. To unravel the mechanisms driving HFS, we probed at where HFS emerged and thrived (i.e., online forums in China) (Wang et al. 2010b). Having defined our population and context, we proceeded with sampling. As in most qualitative research, we adopted “theoretical sampling” (as opposed to random sampling) (Eisenhardt 1989; Glaser and Strauss 1967) which is defined as “cases are selected because they are particularly suitable for illuminating and extending relationships and logic among constructs” (Eisenhardt and Graebner 2007: p.27). Yin (1994) adds that cases are chosen with theoretical intentions, such as “replication, extension, contrary replication, and elimination of alternative explanations.” It is a purposeful selection. the entire process of data analysis is iterative (rather than linear, even though we detail in steps). During “open coding” (Strauss and Corbin 1998), in the first cycle, we went through every single line of text to “code” them. For example, we have initial codes like “make inquiry,” “repeated inquiry,” “share information,” “repost old information,” “target identified,” “provoked sentiments,” etc. to characterize what was happening in HFS. In the second cycle, in an iterative manner, bearing in mind that we were keen on mechanisms (rather than antecedents and outcomes), we tried to identify all the “concepts” that emerged relating to plausible mechanisms at work and grouped similar ones into “categories.” For example, we deliberated and categorized “repeated inquiry,” “repost old information,” and the alike under “sluggish update.” Likewise, we deliberated and categorized “provoked sentiments,” “heated debate,” and the alike under “isolated dissension.” During “axial coding” (Strauss and Corbin 1998), we took an intensive scrutiny at all our concepts and categories. Iteratively, we did go back to our original data to confirm the precision in our identified “concepts” and “categories.” More importantly, we also explored how these “concepts” and “categories” might be related. As we cycled to-and-fro from the data, codes, and categories, some categories reflected common “themes.” A theme is defined as the “outcome of coding, categorization, and analytic reflection, not something that is, in itself, coded” (Saldana 2013)! In our case, we eventually identified “contagious” and “non-contagious” mechanisms driving HFS. We stopped our coding and data analyses when “theoretical saturation” was attained (Eisenhardt 1989). Theoretical saturation refers to the point at which there is a marginal incremental learning due to the fact that no new phenomenon is observed (Glaser and Strauss 1967).

Despite our attempt to detail the steps in coding and data analyses, we have to concur with Miles and Huberman (1984: p.16) that it is often difficult to phantom how a researcher got from thousand odd pages of field data to arrive at the final conclusions, despite being “sprinkled with vivid quotes.” Hence, readers reading the next few sections (on findings) have to be cognizant that the researchers have undergone many “iterative” rounds of analyses and deliberation (i.e., at times agreeing, at times challenging one another).

## 4. Phase I – preliminary analyses

Prior to our main findings (see Section 5), we conducted some preliminary analyses to have a better grasp of the massive amount of data. It is worth noting that these preliminary analyses do not attempt to draw any causality conclusion; to assert causality, a quantitative examination (which is not our focus) is more appropriate. Instead, we simply wish to have an overall visualization of any potential relationships that may prevail. In particular, we seek to achieve these two objectives: (i) prior to understanding the mechanisms driving HFS, we wish to assess if all the HFS cases follow a similar intensity (or pattern) in their activities; if not, what are the differences in intensity (or pattern); (ii) aforementioned, HFS embraces a goal that demands the concerted effort of online netizens; hence, we wish to figure out if the posters’ participation and the amount of post-informativity would accelerate the attainment of the goal. If yes, it would lend credence to our research which focuses on identifying the mechanisms.

First, as Eisenhardt (1989: p.540) has advised, “One tactic is to select categories or dimensions, and then to look for within-group similarities coupled with intergroup differences.” We hence decided to follow the classification by Wang et al. (1997), to group the 21 cases into 3 different categories based on their content.<sup>2</sup> In particular, “Legal” incidents are those that in spite of our individual opinions and positions, the legal norm can be enforced, that is, there are laws and legislations on these issues. However, “Moral” norms are not sanctioned by the state or law. It is worth noting that although it is often a thin line between “Legal” and “Moral,” if a case does transcend both “Legal” and “Moral,” we would classify it as the former which indicates a more serious offense! If the case does not infringe on “Legal” or “Moral,” we would classify it as pure “Entertainment.”<sup>3</sup>

Next, after we have categorized all the HFS cases, we want to assess whether and how the posts proliferate over time. We hence plot the graphs (see Figure 2) of “Relative number of posts” against “Duration” to visualize the general trend of post-intensity across time for all the 21 HFS cases. Interestingly, there are visible patterns exhibited for each category.

## 5. Phase II – main findings

After gaining a preliminary understanding and visualization of our data, we embarked on the “coding” and “data analyses” as guided by Strauss and Corbin (1998) and Miles et al. 2014). When our analyses of the 21 HFS cases attain theoretical saturation (Eisenhardt 1989), we elucidate an important irony: counter-intuitively, the rapid proliferation of HFS is not only propelled by contagious (Le Bon 1896; Polletta and Jasper 2001) but also aided by non-contagious (i.e., the failure to influence others) mechanisms!

For each mechanism driving HFS, we shall also deliberate on the technology affordances and constraints (Robey et al. 2013). For clarity, we hereby term the three conceptualizations of technology affordances as follows: (i) “Type 1” refers to technology affordances attributed to the materiality of IT artifacts (Robey et al. 2013); (ii) “Type 2” refers to technology affordances attributed to the relationship between people and technology as advocated by most socio-technical researchers (Leonardi 2011); and (iii) “Type 3” refers to technology affordances attributed to the emerging relational properties of the IT artifact–actor system as advocated by most sociomaterial researchers (Orlikowski 2010).

## 5.1 Contagious mechanisms driving HFS behavior

## 5.1.1 Mechanism 1 (affective): fueled emotionalism

Fueled emotionalism is hereby defined as “the increase in provocation and intensification of sentiments.” Although physical crowds tend to be emotional (Le Bon 1896), some researchers have dismissed it as stereotyping (Couch 1968). Adding to the contention, it is unclear whether emotional contagion in physical crowds (Hatfield and Cacioppo 1994) would translate to online crowds whereby participants are not physically collocated and online. An analysis of our HFS cases reveals consistent evidence of fueled emotionalism among participants, via verbal cues and pictures, to impact one another (Raafat et al. 2009). As we have found during our “coding” of data, and shall illustrate later, individuals in online settings are less inhibited and more extreme in their choice of words and expressions. Conformity to the emotional atmosphere (Reicher 1996) that has been created and maintained by other HFS participants and the conscious effort to synchronize own emotions to the others (Gump and Kulik 1997) play a pivotal role to the cascading effect.

To illustrate, in Case #21 (Child Abuse), not only is there an evident emotional contagion, there is also an emotional intensification from the initial feeling of “angry” and “sad” felt by one participant to the subsequent stronger choice of words such as “inhumane” and “vicious” by other participants to the eventual calling of names, such as “worse than beast” and “worse than a dog.”

IT affordances and constraints: Unlike physical crowd, “fueled emotionalism” is afforded or constrained by technology in these four ways: (i) the ease of resurfacing text/pictures (Type 1); (ii) the inability to identify oneself (Type 2); (iii) the detachment from reality (Type 2); and (iv) being “lost” in cyberspace and “benumbed” (Type 2). First, in terms of the materiality of the IT artifact (i.e., “cut-and-paste” function, “pin-on-top” feature, etc.), this confers HFS participants with much “ease” in resurfacing the same piece of a text or a picture, either by the same participant or different participants, to induce a few rounds of intensive discussion, focusing on different aspects of the original post. Believing that they are absolved of responsibility and accountability individuals (who) would like to get into the fracas but who wait for the cloak of anonymity” (Momboisse 1967). Third, the ability of HFS participants to realize and capitalize on the materiality of the IT artifact (e.g., Internet link) again enables them to be “virtually present,” but “physically absent.” This can ironically exacerbate (rather than alleviate) the emotional harm. As online participants do not witness the potential consequences of their behavior “live,” such as “bloodshed” or even “death,” they are more unrestrained in their call for vigilance and vengeance. In addition, unlike a physical crowd whereby one can only witness the limited events happening around oneself, in HFS, which is online, anyone can easily see everyone else’s post to gain a holistic view and be imbued with cumulative and pent-up emotion! Fourth, however, the materiality of the IT artifact (e.g., online distractions, massive post, etc.) may affect HFS participants by “losing” them in cyberspace! As a result, this IT constraint may compel participants to engage in stronger words and emotions, so as to capture attention and response. After all, the avalanche of HFS posts (from the thousands to the millions, see Table 2) poses a virtual competition for participants to pit against one another in soliciting views and comments on the phenomenon (Wang et al. 2009). Compounding the problem, the greater exposure to online pictures and videos of violence (i.e., materiality of the IT artifact) has often been castigated to benumb individuals; so HFS participants will heighten their intensity to get the same points across and to attract readership. In sum, the “crowd mind” (Schweingruber 2000) aided by the affordances and constraints of IT helps propel HFS.

## 5.1.2 Mechanism 2 (behavior): blind acceptance

Blind acceptance is hereby defined as “the admission of posted information at face value without questioning.” Unlike netizens who may be skeptical toward online information from unknown sources (Ba 2001), HFS participants are surprisingly receptive, ignoring the need to verify the authenticity of the information or the credibility of the sources. When new information is shared, most HFS participants either explicitly concur or add on to the information (thereby suggesting tacit agreement). Once the critical mass is achieved, the rest will follow suit in the belief without further questioning as people tend to believe what most people believe, “even if it is wrong” (Deutsch and Gerard 1955)! Though undesirable, it is infectious in HFS. Upon blind acceptance, participants proceed to utilize this unverified and potentially erroneous information to embark on their subsequent futile search for the target. Consequently, reports have surfaced about victimizing innocent people. A few mistaken identities have been so overwhelmed by the traumatic experience of verbal abuses and threats such that they commit suicide (ChinaDaily 2013). Indeed puzzling, few posts, if any, ever challenge the veracity of the information. The occasional doubt often does not gain traction and soon fade into oblivion. Hence, “blind acceptance” is a vital mechanism propelling HFS.

To illustrate, in case #16 (Zuo Li’s Girlfriend), even though the secret sender is unknown, most participants simply assume that the discovered information is prosaic truth.

As surfing the Internet (i.e., the materiality of IT) and accessing social media sites (i.e., the materiality of IT) become habitual, be it to seek information or socialize (i.e., ability of actor), many have gotten accustomed to accept at face value “most,” if not all, of the shared content. Resultantly, the reliance over online technology and its community, without much thinking, is expected (Friedman et al. 2000). It is worth noting that technologies initially dictate how people should use them, but often than not, over time, people reshape the usage and technologies get redesigned. The cycle hence repeats. Third, many participants would have some experience partaking (i.e., ability of actor) in at least an online community (i.e., the materiality of IT). Among like-minded people (Sunstein 2002), they typically share a common goal (e.g., serving as vigilantes in HFS cases) and exercise good citizenship. Sabotaging the community is dysfunctional, so is doubting the community. In their fervent quest to breakthrough and identify the target as soon as possible, haste takes precedence over caution to result in “blind acceptance.” It is worth noting that online communities (i.e., the materiality of IT) and the activities of the members (i.e., ability of actors) are very much intertwined as they help shape and reshape each other.

## 5.1.3 Mechanism 3 (behavior): collective amnesia

Collective amnesia is hereby defined as “the ease in which participants collectively forget prior mistakes.” It characterizes HFS and ensures its momentum. In their desire to identify the target (Liu 2008), participants exhibit little effort to reflect on prior mistakes, if any, and are too quick to rebound back into action upon a mistake. It is unfathomable that HFS participants are not exercising more care and caution in their online behavior (Wallis 2011). Moreover, analyses of our data further reveal that some of the HFS participants even end up repeating prior mistakes (e.g., jumping into conclusion).

## 5.2 Non-contagious mechanisms driving HFS behavior

## 5.2.1 Mechanism 4 (affective): isolated dissension

The impetus driving HFS behavior is not only driven by contagious mechanisms but also smoothened by some non-contagious mechanisms. The former helps propel it forward, whereas the latter prevents it from coming to a halt. Challenging the myth that crowds (be it online or offline) will always get infected by one another’s action or thoughts, we find that HFS participants (being online) are not always affected by everything and anything.

Isolated dissension is hereby defined as “the indifference towards conflicts or squabbles among a small faction of participants.” This differs from offline mob, whereby crowd theory (Lang and Lang 1968) predicts that crowd members would be spontaneously gathering other members to defend themselves and to act in concert. Consequently, crowd members in offline mob would exacerbate the conflict as one by one gets involved, resulting in the continuous spread of conflicts (Reicher 1996). However, this has not been observed in the online mob of HFS participants. Online HFS participants typically would not band together or take sides; neither would they try to appease the antagonistic parties. In essence, opposing voices were localized and did not spread to others. The majority of HFS participants were immunized and not infected.

For instance, in Case #9 (Rabbit Harm), two participants hurled insults at each other, but other HFS participants do not provoke the situation further or try to placate them.

IT affordances and constraints: The isolation mechanism can be attributed to these few possibilities: (i) the quick submergence of posts (Type 1); (ii) the assumption of no physical harm done (Type 1); and (ii) probable manifestation of bystander effect, albeit online (Type 2). First, in terms of the materiality of the IT artifact, the voluminous posts in HFS would differently. Second, as the dissents or squabbles happen online, HFS participants might have assumed that the conflict is at most “textual” or “pictorial” in form (i.e., the materiality of the IT artifact) and does not inflict the actual physical harm. Third, technology has set up a barrier to physical contact as participants may be very spatially apart. Uninvolved participants may be quick to dismiss the conflict as others’ business (Walther 1996) and none of their business (Matzat 2009). Soon, the conflict fades into obscurity and HFS continues to thrive.

## 6. Discussions and contributions

Unlike offline mobs where contagion is said to be an inherent characteristic (Polletta and Jasper 2001), the mechanisms driving HFS behavior are not all contagious. We have unraveled three which are contagious and two which are not. For the former, the mechanisms driving HFS are “fueled emotionalism,” “blind acceptance,” and “collective amnesia.” They work in tandem as follows: “Fueled emotionalism” lays the emotional foundation, diffuses and intensifies a shared emotional atmosphere within the online mob. As HFS propels forward, participants continually receive and build on new information with “blind acceptance.” Erroneous information may infuse and innocent people may be victimized, but HFS participants often do not reflect, demonstrating a state of “collective amnesia.” With such contagious behaviors, HFS is in full throttle, efficiently seeking the identification of targets (Cheng et al. 2012).

Counter to popular belief, not all mechanisms driving HFS behavior are contagious, and we unravel two which are not. They are “isolated dissension” and “sluggish update.” Paradoxically, by being non-contagious, the smooth trajectory of HFS is ensured. They work as follows: “Isolated dissension” helps localize and contain conflicts to avoid messing up HFS. Limited individuals are dragged into the conflicts, as majority remains indifferent, unaffected, and focused. “Sluggish update” creates an illusion of sustained interests. At times, it does make the HFS seems more “popular” than it really is, due to repetitions and redundancy.

Despite our systematic multiple-case approach (Eisenhardt 1989), we admit plausible limitations. First, due to the fact that we gather our primary data from China, where HFS is conceived, future studies may want to add cases from other countries to assess potential differences, if any, due to either cultural differences (Hall et al. 1998; Hofstede 1997) or attitudinal differences toward “privacy” (an important element of HFS) between the East and the West (Ess 2005). Second, compared to our in-depth qualitative analyses in Section 5, our quantitative analyses in Section 4 are only descriptive and preliminary in nature, without formal statistical models and tests. Although we have followed a scientific and systematic way to operationalize the variables (to ensure their meaningfulness and appropriateness) and conduct the descriptive analyses, we acknowledge that other operationalizations of the variables are possible and we are far from asserting causality. Future quantitative research is hence another promising direction.

Our study has several critical theoretical contributions. First, contributing to the field of information systems, we have accentuated the essential role of IT in providing technology affordances and constraints to the mechanisms driving HFS. In particular, we underscore that IT is not just a context or a platform of HFS, but it boasts of materiality (Type 1 of technology affordance) and relationships with the participants (Types 2 and 3 of technology affordance) that may help or hamper HFS. In addition, we establish that the same materiality could be enabling on one hand and simultaneously constraining on the other. For example, the voluminous amount of online posts (i.e., a materiality of the IT artifact) can be “enabling” by compartmentalizing local conflicts and at the same time “constraining” by making one feel “lost” in cyberspace. More importantly, we have also shown that technology affordances might not always be “positive” (despite the choice of word), while technology constraints might not always be “negative” (despite the choice of word). For example, when technology affordance confers HFS participants with a sense of detachment from reality, it may be negatively perceived. As another example, when technology constraint confers HFS participants with the notion that targets will not be physically harmed, it may actually prevent local squabbles from getting out of hand and hence positively perceived. In essence, we contend that the ambivalent nature of technology affordances and constraints implies both an empowerment and a helplessness (Volkoff and Strong 2013). In sum, we add to the understanding of technology affordances and constraints in online community.

Second, we challenge the past understanding of collective efforts found in other different online communities (i.e., HFS versus Crowdfunding, Wikipedia, Open Source community, etc.). Re-iterating, the success of target identification in HFS does require participants to collaborate and build upon earlier findings. This parallels the collective efforts demanded by crowdfunding, open source programming, and the alike. However, in the latter cases, contributors are expected to add “materially” (e.g., monetary funds, programming codes, etc.), and this is done by the majority of participants (Wang et al. 2009). Although this conventional understanding is not entirely wrong, it is being challenged in the context of HFS. Emphatically, we unravel that in HFS, most participants do not really contribute by adding “valuable information” (e.g., leads) or “correct information” (e.g., identity). Yet, their importance and participation cannot be undermined. This is because both constructive roles (e.g., information provider) and destructive roles (e.g., repetition requests) may unwittingly help drive HFS. In sum, the notion of collective efforts (Howe 2006) may need to embrace a broader definition and meaning.

Third, we contribute to the discourse on “crowd behavior,” especially regarding contagion online. We find that contagion (Le Bon 1896) may not be a property that is universally applicable to all crowd behavior (Polletta and Jasper 2001). Hence, researchers should be conscientious about making such an assertion. On the one hand, we are consistent with prior researchers who manage to observe both affective contagion (Doherty 1997; Hatfield and Cacioppo 1994) and behavioral contagion (Stephenson and Fielding 1971; Wheeler 1966). We too find “fueled emotionalism” and “isolated dissension” to be affective in nature and “blind acceptance,”, “collective amnesia,” and “sluggish update” to be behavioral in nature. However, we differ from prior work, in that we establish that not all crowd behavior is contagious! Hence, a lucid understanding of the contagious and non-contagious mechanisms driving HFS, coupled with the affordances and constraints offered by IT, will help us better exploit HFS.

Apart from theoretical contributions, our paper has important practical implications. For HFS initiators and facilitators, our elucidation of contagious and non-contagious mechanisms driving HFS behavior can assist them in effectively regulating HFS. For example, recognizing the existence of “blind acceptance,” HFS initiators and facilitators should be cognizant and vigilant about erroneous information such that whenever they emerge, intervention (e.g., in the form of rumor refutal or even legal actions) as soon as possible to prevent further dissemination is encouraged, so that only relevant and factual information is propagated to aid in target identification. Likewise, recognizing the existence of “collective amnesia,”

whereby HFS participants quickly forget and repeat prior mistakes, HFS initiators and facilitators should proactively keep up with the entire target identification process to prevent unconfirmed identities or misidentified targets from being traumatized or harassed by HFS participants. Moreover, recognizing the existence of “isolated dissension,” HFS initiators and facilitators should direct HFS participants from time to time to refocus their attention back to the original HFS cause, rather than be mired in any dysfunctional behavior, even though it could be contained.

For designers of forums which are being exploited for HFS, they could either follow Mop’s model<sup>9</sup> to dedicate specialized sub-forums for HFS (so as to better separate HFS from “hot topics”) or introduce a “flag”/“indicator”/“icon” or the alike to allow posters to denote HFS-related posts. Furthermore, our revelation of IT affordances and constraints may offer some clues as to how IT artifacts can be enhanced to better facilitate HFS, especially if there are dedicated specialized sub-forums for HFS (cf. Mop). For instance, to avoid overloading HFS participants with irrelevant information (i.e., leading to “sluggish updates”), designers for HFS platforms could seek ways to allow accentuation of important updates (e.g., highlight posts of target identification) so that it becomes more prominent. To combat “blind acceptance” of unverified information, designers could add features that allow HFS participants to indicate information that awaits verification or has been verified. In doing so, the risk of HFS participants jumping into erroneous conclusion may be ameliorated.

In sum, we hope our paper will excite other researchers to examine this contentious HFS phenomenon.
