# Disaggregation of the IT capital effects on firm performance: Empirical evidence from an IT asset portfolio perspective

Tae Hun Kim<sup>1</sup>, Matt Wimble<sup>2</sup> and Vallabh Sambamurthy<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Broad College of Business, Michigan State University, 632 Bogue Street, East Lansing, MI 48824, USA; <sup>2</sup>College of Business, University of Michigan–Dearborn, 19000 Hubbard Drive, Dearborn, MI 48126, USA

## Abstract

Although prior research has frequently focused on aggregate IT capital, most firms invest in specific types with different goals. Each type of capital represents a distinct factor of a firm’s production function. Drawing on a theory-ofproduction framework, we disaggregate overall IT capital into specific types to examine their unique effects on firm performance over time. We categorize these IT-specific production factors into a firm’s installed personal computers for individual information access, servers for collective information access, storage capacity for information stock, and nodes for information flow. We investigate when and how each IT capital type contributes to firm performance by analyzing the 5-year panel data of 1,548 US firms. Our findings show that individual information access capital and collective information access capital have immediate effects on profitability through cost efficiency or sales growth. By contrast, information stock capital has a lagged effect on profitability. In addition, information stock capital complements individual information access capital in improving profitability, as well as contributing to sales growth and cost efficiency equivalent to firm size. These results extend the existing research on firm-level effects of IT investments by demonstrating that different IT capital effects have unique ways of affecting firm performance. European Journal of Information Systems (2017).

Keywords: disaggregate IT capital effects; firm performance over time; profitability improvement; sales growth; cost efficiency; theory of production; panel data method

## Introduction

Since the introduction of Solow’s (1987) seminal attention to the ‘‘productivity paradox,’’ information system (IS) researchers have examined how and why information technology (IT) investments affect firm performance (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Dedrick et al, 2003). Initially, the theory of production provided a framework for researchers to differentiate IT capital as a separate factor of production from ordinary capital, often using the Cobb–Douglas (i.e., constant elasticity of substitution) production function (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Dewan & Min, 1997; Tambe & Hitt, 2012). This long tradition has been observed in the literature. Typical applications of production theory separate production inputs into two factors, capital and labor, and expand to identify further disaggregate factors into six or seven production inputs (Binswanger, 1974; Douglas, 1976). The theory-of-production framework, which has been created to separate IT capital from ordinary capital, also implies that the IT capital itself can be disaggregated into its distinct effects on firm performance; this would offer fine-grained implications that are missing in prior studies that advance our understanding of IT business value.

Initial studies about the role of IT as a production factor have focused on either the aggregate stock of IT investments made by a firm (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Kleis et al, 2012; Tambe & Hitt, 2012) or firm-leve investments in specific IT applications (e.g., Banker et al, 2006; Mukhopadhyay et al, 1997). Recent research has advocated the need to focus on disaggregating IT capital (Aral & Weill, 2007; Ross & Beath, 2002; Ross & Weill, 2002; Xue et al, 2012). As Schryen (2013) has noted, ‘‘Key limitations of current research are based on the ambiguity and fuzziness of IS business value, the neglected disaggregation of IS investments, and the unexplained process of creating internal and competitive value (p. 139).’’ We respond to these important observations regarding IS business value to address these limitations. That is, a theory-of-production lens disaggregates IT assets based on a meaningful typology and then examines the differential effects on business value in terms of profitability. We provide a view of the value creation process by looking at how the associations between IT assets and firm performance play out over time.

Prior studies have shown that organizational learning and adaptation are necessary to enhance the capability of firms to leverage their IT capital (Curley & Pyburn, 1982). IT investments require time to manifest positive returns (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2003). Investments in the form of business process redesign, training, or organizational changes are expected to benefit from IT capital. Thus, time is an inherent dimension of how IT creates the business value.

To expand the existing findings, we examine the relative effects of different IT capital types on firm performance by addressing the following research questions: Does the association between IT and firm performance vary across different types of IT capital? If so, then does it also vary over time? In doing so, we adopt the theory-ofproduction framework which was originally proposed by Cobb & Douglas (1928) to estimate the increase in production associated with changes in the quantity of labor and capital. This theoretical framework allows for an analysis of the relative influences of labor and capital on production over time. The production factors, by definition, are required to produce outputs. It is important to note that the inputs of the original Cobb–Douglas framework have been multiplied and are inherently linked to one another. Stated in another way, the production theory suggests a mechanism to estimate the relative effects of different production factors.

The theory-of-production framework has been used across a wide range of research contexts and forms the basis for the business value of IT as a production factor (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Dewan & Min, 1997). Drawing on this approach, we disaggregate IT capital into separate production factors to show their relative and differential effects on firm performance over time. In this manner, firm-level IT capital is categorized into four distinct types: a firm’s installed personal computers (PCs) as individual information access, servers as collective information access, storage capacity as information stock, and nodes as information flow capital. The rationale behind disaggregation is that each form of IT capital contributes differently to profitability improvement by facilitating organizational information processes. For empirical evidence, we analyze a panel dataset of 1,548 US firms for 5 years (2005–2009). We expect our findings to fill in the gaps from prior studies that have considered the overall effect of aggregate IT capital on firm performance (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2003; Brynjolfsson & Yang, 1996; Mithas et al, 2012).

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. We draw upon the related literature to hypothesize when and how disaggregate IT capital types influence firm performance. Subsequently, we investigate their relative progressive effects on profitability over time as a measure of firm performance. Additionally, we draw upon the work of Mithas et al (2011) to examine their disaggregated effects on sales and operating costs and to explain whether the profitability effects of each IT capital type are driven by either the sales growth or cost efficiency pathway. We also test potential complementarities between the different IT capital types and their interactions with organizational factors to further understand the effects of IT value. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications of this research.

## Related literature

Prior studies on IS business value have adopted an aggregate IT capital perspective. Their empirical results show that the aggregate IT capital of a firm positively affects final outcome measures, such as productivity (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Tambe & Hitt, 2012), customer retention and new customer targeting (Mithas et al, 2005; Ray et al, 2005), process outputs (Mukhopadhyay et al, 1997), and market value (Bharadwaj et al, 1999), as well as intermediate outcome metrics, such as innovation capability (Kleis et al, 2012), product variety (Gao & Hitt, 2012), plant performance (Banker et al, 2006), inventory turnover (Barua et al, 1995), interorganizational collaboration (Mukhopadhyay & Kekre, 2002), and process cycle time (Mukhopadhyay et al, 1997). However, the existing literature has not yet simultaneously examined multiple forms of IT capital and performance dimensions over time. That is, the prior findings are limited, considering the ambiguity and fuzziness of IT value (Schryen, 2013). Aral & Weill (2007) suggested that differences in firm performance might be explained not just by the total IT investment, but by its individual components disaggregated along strategic dimensions given they have different purposes and functions. The current paper aims to provide a means to fill in these gaps in the literature.

Although several studies have investigated the effects of separate IT capital types on firm performance, they have focused mainly on a few specific IT applications. For example, prior studies have disaggregated IT capital, but focused on single IT applications, such as enterprise resource planning systems (Hayes et al, 2001; McAfee, 2002; Poston & Grabski, 2001), IT infrastructure (Aral et al, 2012; Chatterjee et al, 2002; Rai et $^ { a l , }$ 1997), e-commerce applications (Subramani & Walden, 2001; Tan et $^ { a l , }$ 2010; Zhu & Kraemer, 2005), supply chain management systems (Kim et $^ { a l , }$ 2006; Vickery et $^ { a l , }$ 2010), IT functions for interfirm relationships (Rai et $^ { a l , }$ 2012), and customer relationship management applications (Setia et $^ { a l , }$ 2013). Their findings could not identify potential complementarities among individual IT capital effects because of the neglected bundles of IT assets (Schryen, 2013).

The literature calls for dividing the monolithic construct of IT capital and suggests the importance of the time dimension in understanding how the value from IT changes over time. We adopt an IT asset portfolio perspective (Xue et al, 2012) to analyze disaggregate IT capital effects, using a set of IT-specific production inputs, across multiple firm performance dimensions over time. As shown in Figure 1, our approach separates IT capital into: a firm’s installed PCs as individual information access, servers as collective information access, storage capacity as information stock, and nodes as information flow capital. Our IT asset portfolio perspective identifies specific and measurable IT capital categories, thereby avoiding the potential measurement problems of a firm’s aggregate IT capital. Consequently, the immediate and lagged effects of disaggregate IT capital types can be investigated to explain their different temporal effects on profitability. In addition, we examine the performance using multiple firm performance measures to determine whether the immediate or lagged effect on profitability of each disaggregate IT capital type is driven by its intermediate contribution to sales growth or cost efficiency (Mithas et $^ { a l , }$ 2011, 2012; Xue et $^ { a l , }$ 2012). We believe that our IT asset portfolio perspective is an economical and feasible way to measure the effects of separate IT capital and to test their relative and differential effects over time.

## Disaggregating a firm’s IT capital effects

To disaggregate the IT capital, we adopt the Cobb– Douglas production approach with two factors (labor and capital inputs), as follows:

$$
Y = A K ^ {\alpha} L ^ {\beta}
$$

where Y is total production output, A is total factor productivity, K is capital input, L is labor input, and a and $\beta$ are the output elasticities of capital and labor, respectively. The parameters $\mathscr { X }$ and $\bar { \beta }$ are estimated by the empirics. In this application, there are more than simply two production factors. Such an approach has a long history in both economics and IS literature. It is a practical way to disaggregate relative effects of inputs in an integrated system. From an engineering perspective, each component of an IT system (e.g., PCs, routers, and servers) is needed for the system to function. A Cobb–Douglas production function is structured, so the terms are multiplied together; a zero for any production factor would result in zero output. IT plays a critical role in improving a firm’s ability to utilize prior knowledge and to recognize the value of new information. Furthermore, IT enables the outcomes to be assimilated and applied to new knowledge and organizational capabilities. The adoption and use of IT impacts not only the ability to channel and utilize information within and outside the firm (Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005), but also the effectiveness of an organizational design for information stock and flow within and across firm boundaries (Ruiz-Mercader et al, 2006). We propose four different types of IT capital. The first two of these include a firm’s installed PCs for individual information access and servers for collective information access. The IT capital of a firm also facilitates the accumulation of organizational information (i.e., information stock) and the regulation and circulation of accumulated information (i.e., information flow) (DeCarolis & Deeds, 1999; Dierickx & Cool, 1989). These crucial IT roles for organizational learning identify the other two IT capital types, i.e., a firm’s storage capacity for information stock and its installed nodes for information flow.

Therefore, we conceptually define disaggregate IT capital types, drawing on the idea that each IS can be used effectively for its unique nature and purpose (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013). Each disaggregate IT capital type is explained by physical structure, which specifies the mechanical aspects of IS in an organization, and in terms of different natures and purposes of IS, as summarized in Table 1.

More specifically, individual members in an organization use PCs to process organizational information, i.e., inputs via keyboards and output via monitors. The individual-level system usage (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006) is defined as an activity with three elements, i.e., an individual user (the subject to use) operates a PC (the object to be used) to process information (the task to be done). In addition, servers facilitate the individual-level access to organizational information at the collective level in an organization, i.e., a server system connects PC users with each other by enabling multiple computations to be distributed over a group of PCs. Centralized server systems provide information services and resources requested by PC users, thus supporting the collectivelevel access to organizational information. The uses of PCs and servers are determined by their distinct natures and purposes at the different levels in an organization. The multilevel nature of system use (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007) disaggregates IT capital into individual information access via the use of PCs and collective information access via the use of servers.

Furthermore, the strategic value of organizational information can be conceptualized in terms of its stocks and flows (Dierickx & Cool, 1989). An organization needs to accumulate its information and build efficient information networks in order to be competitive with firmspecific knowledge (DeCarolis & Deeds, 1999). A firm’s effective information stock and flow are key requirements to assimilate organizational information into knowledge assets. The physical structure of IT capital identifies a firm’s storage capacity as an important driver of effective information stocks. Storage capacity in different forms of memory is a fundamental IT component to accumulate digital data for information stock in an organization. It provides a firm with data reservoirs for a stock of organizational information. At the same time, the accumulated stock of organizational information needs to be transported to users in the right place at the right time. Nodes build up efficient networks for a flow of organizational information. They also distribute organizational information and facilitate data communications across spaces. Therefore, a firm’s storage capacity and nodes are conceptualized as the other disaggregated IT capital types are facilitating stocks and flows of organizational information, respectively.

Individual information access capital: PCs installed by a firm A firm’s installed PCs represent its individual information access capital. A PC enables an organizational member to individually access taskrelated information. Its fundamental features are then adopted and directly used by individual users within organizations. For individual information access, users must adopt, utilize, and adapt PCs for specific task requirements. The process of structuring technology in use with interactions among institutional properties, individuals’ use of technology, and technology itself requires individual users’ adjustments and episodic changes over time (Orlikowski, 1992). Individual users should spend a certain amount of time learning how to utilize a variety of PC features. Such human–computer interactions may delay the effect of individual information access capital, i.e., a firm’s installed PCs.

PCs are typically a general-purpose technology in organizations (David, 1990). Most of the value of general-purpose technologies originates from individual users’ customized changes in how to facilitate the use of technologies, rather than being the direct benefit of their automation (Bresnahan & Trajtenberg, 1995). Although IT directly impacts productivity by automating manual processes, general-purpose technologies additionally have an augmented impact in facilitating mechanisms for interpersonal coordination in organizations (Brynjolfsson et al, 2002). The IT impact as a coordination mechanism yields greater returns than its direct productivity of automation (Gurbaxani & Whang, 1991; Malone et al, 1989). This is possible when general-purpose technologies complement other organizational capital (e.g., process redesign, training, hiring a more skilled workforce, and new organizational designs) for such expected returns. For the complementary assets to be successful, individual users need time to customize their IT usage to the given practical tasks and organizational contexts (Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997). For example, users must engage in not only formal training, but also in informal sharing experiences and interpersonal interactions to customize their IT usage and to master workrelated skills that eventually contribute to the complementary assets in organizations.

While new applications written today often reside on the server side using 3-tier or multitier architecture, the reality is that most organizations are laden with legacy applications as well (Ellison et al, 2014; Ulrich, 2002). The most prominent examples take the form of mainframe applications used for transaction processing, common in a majority of large banks, airlines, and insurance companies, as well as in large organizations that existed when mainframes were common. In the case of PCs, there may be client-specific, client–server applications that run client-side or firm-specific applications using generic software. Additionally, in modern applications where the application logic and data reside on the server, the most common way that a human interfaces with the application in a business environment is still through a PC. Thus, it is not necessarily the PC itself which takes time to learn, but rather it takes users time to learn applications that are tailored to the organization in some form. In this sense, users need time to learn before the technology yields business value. Therefore, we propose the lagged effect of PC-oriented capital on profitability, as follows:

Hypothesis 1: An increase in PCs installed by a firm is positively associated with profitability after a time lag.

Collective information access capital: servers installed by a firm We characterize a firm’s installed servers as a production factor for collective information access in organizations. To access organizational information, individual users of PCs must be connected via server systems. Such server systems support and control the individuals’ collective access to organizational information. That is, organizational information is sent to personal devices and users through server systems.

The process of metastructuring, technology in use with technology-use mediation, may explain the performance effects of servers as collective information access capital. In the metastructuring process, a few decision makers shape interactions with technology, modify IT features, and alter the context of use (Orlikowski et al, 1995). For example, the more the servers that are installed in an organization, the larger the amount of data that users can process immediately. This condition may be attributed to servers benefiting from users’ information processing, not through human–computer interactions at the individual level, but through the establishment and reinforcement of technology use at the collective level. Such a mediation process to promote collective information access through servers immediately generates value without any substantial human–technology interactions. Also, there is a more practical reason why servers are likely to yield immediate returns: Changes in servers are often made as a direct result of collective capacity constraints, which result in degraded performance, such as slow server response times (Almeida & Menasce´, 2002;

Kant & Won, 1999; Lam & Chan, 1987). In such a situation, firm performance is likely to improve immediately because a capacity constraint on an organization is lifted quickly. Thus, we propose the prompt profitability effect of server-oriented capital for collective information access.

Hypothesis 2: An increase in servers installed by a firm is positively associated with profitability immediately.

Information stock capital: storage capacity expanded by a firm We define a firm’s storage capacity as information stock capital. Storage capacity facilitates the accumulation of organizational information over time. A firm’s storage capacity is a disaggregate IT capital for accumulating historical and organizational inferences that evolve into routines and beliefs that guide the future behavior of organizational members (Huber, 1991; Levitt & March, 1988). Furthermore, Brynjolfsson & Yang (1996) offered a simpler example of how lagged returns to storage investments might occur. As they explained, the server installation of a firm must precede its database software installation, which, in turn, precedes data acquisition. This indicates that multiple years might be required to accumulate sufficient data to improve decision making based on the initial IT purchase. Greater data storage capacity facilitates exploitative learning through the accumulation of documentation, thereby codifying knowledge which is characterized by mechanistic structures, tightly coupled systems, path dependence, routinization, control and bureaucracy, and stable markets and technologies (Bharadwaj, 2000; Dierickx & Cool, 1989; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Teece et al, 1997).

Exploitative learning activities require a certain period to accumulate organizational information and to achieve their positive effects on profitability. We propose that the information stock value of a firm’s storage-capacityoriented capital has delayed effects on performance because of the time required for learning to occur.

Hypothesis 3: An increase in storage capacity expanded by a firm is positively associated with profitability after a time lag.

Information flow capital: nodes installed by a firm We defined network capacity, measured by a firm’s installed nodes, as a production factor for information flow. IT provides pipelines through which information flows across the entire organization. This information flow is facilitated by nodes specifically. The network capacity is known to scale with nodes (Almeida & Menasce´, 2002; Kant & Won, 1999; Lam & Chan, 1987). Node-oriented capital promotes the smooth circulation of organizational information accumulated by a firm’s storage capacity. Consistent with prior literature, we use a firm’s installed nodes as a proxy for network capital. According to the Harte-Hanks CI Technology Database (CiTDB) manual, the proxy refers to total LAN nodes, which includes LAN devices (PCs, printers, workstations, etc.) and LAN file servers, installed annually in a firm (Harte-Hanks, 2006). Although this may differ conceptually from how a practitioner would view it, this is consistent with a long line of literature in IS (Dong & Yang, 2016; Forman, 2002; Guadalupe et al, 2014; Ray et al, 2006; Xue et al, 2017; Zhu, 2004).

Through support from nodes, information flow facilitates explorative activities represented by organic structures, loosely coupled systems, path breaking, improvization, autonomy and chaos, and emerging markets and technologies (Ancona et al, 2001; Brown & Eisenhardt, 1998; Lewin et al, 1999). Explorative learning activities provide a firm not only with the capabilities to adapt, integrate, and reconfigure organizational information for a short-run competence, but also with the capabilities to match information outputs into changing environments for a competitive advantage (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Helfat, 1997; Lavie, 2006; Teece, 2007; Teece et al, 1997). Like servers, nodes are also likely to yield immediate returns because the changes in network capacity via node installation are often made in response to capacity constraints, such as difficulties accessing a network (Almeida & Menasce´, 2002; Kant & Won, 1999; Lam & Chan, 1987). Performance improvements should accumulate quickly because the capacity constraint on a firm is lifted. That is, changes in information flow capital do not require substantial organizational learning nor information stock to build up yield returns. We propose that the information flow value of node-oriented capital generates its immediate effect on profitability, as follows:

Hypothesis 4: An increase in nodes installed by a firm is positively associated with profitability immediately.

## Operationalization

Firm performance was measured by multiple indicators, including annual net income (for profitability), annual revenue (for sales), and operating expenditure (for operating costs) per employee (Mithas et al, 2012). Figure 2 shows the trajectories of our study’s firms’ annual percapita averages of their performance measures for 5 years (2005–2009).

The IT capital of a firm was disaggregated into four types that were conceptually independent of each other. Their measures were the annual per-capita quantities of PCs, servers, storage capacity, and nodes installed by a firm. Such measures were reflective of the unique and distinct properties of the different IT capital types and used to test their relative and differential effects on firm performance over time. For example, changes over time in the number of PCs installed per employee adequately represent changes in other computer terminals, such as laptops, tablet computers, and mobile devices, which are used by individuals to access information in organizations. In addition, unlike that of other recently emerging new devices, data on the number of per employee PCs installed by a firm were consistently available during our panel data period (2005–2009). ## Panel analyses and results

Our research model with four disaggregated inputs of IT capital and firm performance outputs could be expressed in the Cobb–Douglas production function (Cobb & Douglas, 1928), as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Firm   performance } = \alpha \cdot \text { PCs   installed } ^ {\beta_ {1}} \cdot \text { Servers   installed } ^ {\beta_ {2}} \\ \cdot \text { Storage   capacity } ^ {\beta_ {3}} \cdot \text { Nodes   installed } ^ {\beta_ {4}} \cdot Z ^ {\gamma_ {1}}. \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

The statistical model, which was estimated for firm i at time t, could be rewritten, as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Firm   performance} _ {i, t} = \alpha \cdot \text { PCs   installed } _ {i, t} ^ {\beta_ {1}} \\ \quad \cdot \text { Servers   installed } _ {i, t} ^ {\beta_ {2}} \\ \quad \cdot \text { Storage   capacity } _ {i, t} ^ {\beta_ {3}} \\ \quad \cdot \text { Nodes   installed } _ {i, t} ^ {\beta_ {4}} \cdot Z _ {i, t} ^ {\gamma_ {1}} \cdot \varepsilon_ {i, t}, \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where $\varepsilon _ { i , t }$ is a multiplicative stochastic error term. For estimation, the model was transformed into a log-linear form with controls $Z _ { i , t } ,$ as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln \left(\text { Firm   performance } _ {i, t}\right) = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \ln (\text { PCs   installed } _ {i, t}) \\ \quad + \beta_ {2} \ln (\text { Servers   installed } _ {i, t}) \\ \quad + \beta_ {3} \ln (\text { Storage   capacity } _ {i, t}) \\ \quad + \beta_ {4} \ln (\text { Nodes   installed } _ {i, t}) \\ \quad + \gamma_ {1} \ln (Z _ {i, t}) + \varepsilon_ {i, t}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

We applied the empirical specification to the fixedeffects model to estimate the contemporary-year effects of disaggregate IT capital types on the multiple firm performance measures with dummy year indicators, as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \ln \left(\text { Firm   performance } _ {i, t}\right) & = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \ln (\text { PCs   installed } _ {i, t}) \\ & + \beta_ {2} \ln (\text { Servers   installed } _ {i, t}) \\ & + \beta_ {3} \ln (\text { Storage   capacity } _ {i, t}) \\ & + \beta_ {4} \ln (\text { Nodes   installed } _ {i, t}) \\ & + \gamma_ {1} \ln (Z _ {i, t}) + \text { Year } + \varepsilon_ {i, t}. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

Given available data from 2005 to 2009, we could test the lagged effects of disaggregate IT capital types on profitability for 3 years in addition to their contemporary-year effects. ## Additional analyses and results

Our panel data analyses did not show any effect of nodes installed on profitability over time. Therefore. we further explored the effects of node-oriented information flow capital by testing its possible interactions with other variables. Our additional analyses for such potential interactions were established from the idea that the profitability effect of information flow capital might be explained, not by its main effects, but by the effects of its interactions with the other variables, i.e., PC-oriented individual information access capital and firm size.

IT-driven networking effect Node-oriented information flow capital supports information sharing activities among organizational members, thereby generating an IT-driven networking effect. Such an effect may be generated by the complementarity between node- and PC-oriented capital. Knowledge outputs from the access of individual members to organizational information (through PCs installed) will provide a larger knowledge pool with a variety of expertise across diverse domains because the knowledge network of a firm, mediated by nodes installed, connects more members and further facilitates their mutual knowledge sharing (Zahra & Nielsen, 2002). That is, node-oriented capital augments the effect of PC-oriented capital on firm performance, i.e., an IT-driven networking effect, by enabling individua members to share their information via the knowledge network of a firm. To show the contemporary-year augmenting role of nodes on the effect of PCs on profitability, we estimated an interaction term between PCs installed<sub>i,t</sub> and Nodes installed<sub>i,t</sub>, as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \ln \left(\text { Profitability } _ {i, t}\right) & = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \ln \left(\text { PCs   installed } _ {i, t}\right) \\ & + \beta_ {2} \ln \left(\text { Servers   installed } _ {i, t}\right) \\ & + \beta_ {3} \ln \left(\text { Storage   capacity } _ {i, t}\right) \\ & + \beta_ {4} \ln \left(\text { Nodes   installed } _ {i, t}\right) \\ & + \beta_ {5} \ln \left(\text { PCs   installed } _ {i, t}\right) \\ & \cdot \ln \left(\text { Nodes   installed } _ {i, t}\right) + \gamma_ {1} \ln \left(Z _ {i, t}\right) \\ & + \text { Year } + \varepsilon_ {i, t}. \end{array} \tag {1.1}\tag{6}
$$

IT-driven economies of scale To succeed in simultaneously developing both radical and incremental knowledge bases, i.e., explorative and exploitative learning modes, excessive efforts are required from firms, primarily because of their limited resources (Levinthal & March, 1993). Therefore, a firm needs to decide how much of its attention and resources should be invested in different learning modes for its sustainable competitive advantage (He & Wong, 2004). A firm can increase viability by achieving a balance between incremental exploitation and radical exploration through the punctuated equilibrium, in which the firm alternates temporal cycles of either exploration or exploitation rather than simultaneously pursuing both learning modes (Burgelman, 2002). We expect that firm size determines the balance between exploitation and exploration in organizations. The larger the firm size, the more positive the networking effect provided by its installed nodes, i.e., IT-driven economies of scale. The IT-mediated knowledge network of a firm, the size and scope of which it increases in proportion to firm size, is what determines economics of reuse and decreases searching cost by reconfiguring the codified knowledge of a firm (Teigland & Wasko, 2003) with easy access and comprehension (Kyriakopoulos & de Ruyter, 2004). Hence, as the size of a firm increases, the more organizational members join its knowledge network, and the more valuable the knowledge network becomes given its positive feedback loops with diverse knowledge sources across organizational boundaries. Therefore, the value of nodeoriented information flow capital may be considerably more crucial for large-scale organizations than for medium- and small-sized firms.

## Robustness checks

As explained by Wooldridge (2012), fixed-effects estimators are widely used when ceteris paribus effects are estimated, whereas random-effects estimators allow explanatory variables to be consistent over time. The fixed-effects approach removes any time-constant explanatory variables by excluding the unobserved effect. If explanatory variables of interest are consistent over time, then the random-effects approach is proper because of its assumption that the unobserved effect is uncorrelated with all the explanatory variables. Thus, we included industry type (NAICS ) as a control variable in the random-effects models but excluded it from the fixed-effects models because the fixed-effects estimator does not allow for the addition of such a time-invariable variable as a right-side variable to its estimated models would. In this sense, our panel data analyses based on the fixed-effects estimator provided empirical evidence that was consistent with the results from the random-effects estimator, thereby further supporting the robustness of our findings. In addition, we used robust standard errors clustered on firm, thereby ensuring that our results were free from any potential problems regarding serial correlation or heteroskedasticity (Wooldridge, 2012).

## Discussion

Although Xue et al (2012) did not actually use disaggregate IT capital measures for their analyses, their portfolio perspective calls for analyzing the disaggregate IT capital effects to extend the current understanding on IS business value and to explain how a firm’s IT asset portfolio can be allocated to improve firm performance (Aral & Weill, 2007; Ross & Beath, 2002; Ross & Weill, 2002; Schryen, 2013; Xue et al, 2012). In this sense, we disaggregated IT capital into a firm’s installed PCs as individual information access, servers as collective information access, storage capacity as information stock, and nodes as information stock capital. Consequently, our empirical evidence supported some of our theoretical arguments, Hypotheses 2 and 3, and differentiated the timing of disaggregate IT capital effects by showing that the association between IT capital and firm performance varies according to the form of IT capital. Furthermore, our findings provided details on the dynamic value mechanism of IT capital, i.e., a sales growth or cost efficiency pathway toward profitability improvement. We also provided evidence on the IT-driven networking effect and economies of scale by testing the interactions of node-oriented capital with PC-oriented capital and firm size. Table 10 summarizes the results of our hypotheses testing and additional findings.

First, PCs as individual information access capital have an immediate positive effect on profitability, which contrasted with our expectation proposed in Hypothesis 1. This unexpected finding implies that to sufficiently utilize PCs, current users no longer require a substantial amount of time. As computer terminals, PCs were exploited by humans, i.e., the end users in organizations, who experienced almost-immediate success with fewer trials and errors recently. Compared with individual users in the past, these days, people across generations are becoming familiar with continuously emerging terminal devices, thereby reducing the time required to utilize IT capital by adopting new PCs and adapting to the personal technologies. In addition, PC-enabled individual information access capital is a promising variable in terms of cost efficiency but not sales growth because PC manufacturers are probably able to sell new emerging terminals and personal devices for reduced prices over time. Individual information access capital measured by a firm’s installed PCs (a representative of diverse computer terminals for individual use in organizations) is supplied by contemporary computer manufacturers at a decreasing price per unit over time. From our findings, this cost efficiency of PC-enabled capital may be explained by its insignificant association with operating costs.

Second, servers as collective information access capital had a significant contemporary-year association with profitability, but the effect would disappear after a year, as proposed in Hypothesis 2. To obtain the value of server systems, a firm does not require any direct human– computer interactions, which require a certain period for users to adapt to the technologies. That is, although server-oriented capital, activated and improved through a firm’s adoption of the latest technologies, immediately improves firm performance, the profit achieved by new server systems cannot be sustained in the end. We also found that server-oriented collective information access capital was promising for sales growth opportunity because of its intangible IT-based outputs combined with the effect of PCs, i.e., individual information access capital. From the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm, the interplay between different IT resources explains organizational competence (Wade & Hulland, 2004). Similarly, the effect of servers used for collective information access augments the value of PCs used for individual information access, thereby contributing to the competitive advantage of a firm. For example, an increased number of servers may collectively enhance the positive benefits from human–computer interactions via the individual use of PCs in organizations by overcoming the off-the-shelf characteristics of PCs and generating intangible IT-based outputs from mixed IT capital types. Such an intangible advantage from the augmenting effect of servers may be explained by the resource interplay, i.e., a key driver of a firm’s sales growth rather than cost efficiency. Thus, we suggest that the intangible property of server-oriented collective information access capital may eventually explain its immediate effect on profitability.

Third, storage capacity as information stock capital positively affected profitability over 2 years after a 1-year lag, as proposed in Hypothesis 3. Storage-capacity-oriented capital affected profitability when organizational information was accumulated via storage technologies over time, thereby generating the long-term performance improvement of a firm. In addition, our empirical evidence suggests that the information stock value of storage capacity is a promising variable for the cost efficiency of a firm. This finding may be explained by ITdriven organizational learning via exploitative activities, of which benefits can be clearly understood by a firm’s strategy to reuse existing knowledge and reduce operating costs.

Finally, the effect of nodes, i.e., information flow capital, on profitability was immediately negative. This finding is inconsistent with Hypothesis 4. A possible explanation for this unexpected finding is that the negative profitability effect of nodes may be the omitted interactions of node-oriented capital with other IT capital types and organizational characteristics (Schryen, 2013). In this sense, our additional analyses identified the IT-driven networking effect by testing the complementarity between node- and PC-oriented capital in terms of profitability: The node-oriented information flow capital complements the PC-oriented information flow capital in terms of profitability improvement. We also found that the individual effect of node-oriented capital on profitability is oriented toward its revenue growth contribution. Furthermore, our additional analyses showed that the effect of node-oriented capital was contingent on firm size, thereby implying the IT-driven economies of scale for both sales and operating costs: Nodes contribute to sales growth and cost efficiency in proportion to firm size. In our view, these additional findings suggest that node-oriented information flow capital plays a crucial role in enabling the interconnection of organizational members who are indirectly connected via IT. That is, nodes form a knowledge network, in which organizational members can share, combine, and reuse their different knowledge and information, and facilitate a firm’s innovation process and knowledge creation for new products and services (Nerkar & Paruchuri, 2005). Furthermore, node-oriented capital helps a firm capture customer needs via its in-depth interactions with customers (Zahay et al, 2004). The value-added output, i.e., a firm’s on-time understanding of customer needs, leads to innovations for new products and services, thus generating the competitive advantage based on creativity.

## Theoretical contributions

This paper may theoretically advance the current literature-based understanding of initial performance dips (Clark & Fujimoto, 1991; Hayes & Clark, 1985) and organizational learning theory (Huber, 1991; Levitt & March, 1988) by explaining how disaggregate IT contributions relate to firm performance. The literature explains the presence of initial dips in the performance of firms that adopt and utilize technology, thereby providing a theoretical explanation for the lagged effect of IT on firm performance. That is, the concept of initia dips in performance describes a common phenomenon in which an organization immediately experiences a decrease in firm performance during the initial stage of technology adoption and implementation (Clark & Fujimoto, 1991; Hayes & Clark, 1985). The organization consequently performs better after the initial performance dip. In a similar course, organizational learning theory indicates that an organization improves performance through direct and indirect experiences (Huber, 1991; Levitt & March, 1988). In this sense, Brynjolfsson & Hitt (1996) suggested that time lag would be an important factor in examining the effects of IT capital. Empirical studies have also proved that the IT capital of a firm has lagged effects on firm performance (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2003; Brynjolfsson & Yang, 1996) because a certain period is necessary to leverage the capital with organizational learning and adaptation (Curley & Pyburn, 1982). Despite IT investments, a firm requires time to learn, adjust, and restructure organizational IT. Such time lags may disable early returns from representing the IS business value. The effects of learning and other complements can amplify the impact of IT capita on firm performance over time (Anderson et al, 2003; Brynjolfsson et al, 2002). In this regard, our disaggregate IT capital approach and hypothesis tests cover existing theories and exceed the general implications of prior studies. First, our disaggregation of IT capital effects on firm performance confirms the existing finding of previous studies (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2003; Brynjolfsson & Yang, 1996): The IT investment of a firm generates value in terms of firm performance despite the IT value requiring a certain period for organizational learning.

Second, our findings can significantly provide further definitive explanations on IT value over time, i.e., the different timing of performance effects across disaggregate IT capital types, which is based on how each type is used and interacted with by the users in an organization. Furthermore, our detailed findings explain whether each disaggregate IT effect on profitability follows a sales growth or cost efficiency pathway (Mithas et al, 2012).

Apart from the main findings, our additional evidence on the IT-driven networking effect and economies of scale, i.e., the significant interactions of nodes with PCs and firm size, supports the extended RBV (Mathews, 2003), which captures the dynamics of exchange and circulation of technologies, know-how and intangible assets, and tangible assets and capital goods. The extended RBV accounts for resource dynamics, such as the propagation, diffusion, imitation, replication, and recombination of organizational resources (Mathews, 2003). Similarly, we can address the IT capital value with variation, selection, and retention by providing empirical evidence on the synergy/conflict between disaggregate IT resources and their lagged effects on firm performance. Such an advanced approach may improve the understanding of complex and transformative IT value over time.

## Empirical contributions

The period for learning, adjustment, and restructuring IT in organizations, i.e., time lags, has not been considered in assessing firms’ output in prior relevant studies. Such lags disable early returns from representing the IT value (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996). In terms of the robustness for empirical evidence, we estimated the fixed-effects models with robust standard errors clustered on firm to test the differential effects of disaggregate IT capital over time. Such econometric techniques used in this paper do not only show the dynamic adjustment of IT capital in relation to multiple firm performance measures over time, but also address the serious problem of omitted variables with panel data, satisfy the strict exogeneity assumption, and eliminate serial correlation or heteroskedasticity problems (Wooldridge, 2012). The econometrics can reduce the risk of reverse causality, solve endogenous variable bias and omitted variables, and overcome other measurement issues. Our empirical evidence from the fixed-effects estimators was consistent with the results from the random-effects estimators, thereby satisfying robustness in estimating firm-level IT value lags.

We also empirically tested potential interactions between IT effects and organizational characteristics, as Chwelos et al (2010) identified the relationship between IT capital and other ordinary capital as production inputs. In this sense, we addressed the dynamic interactions of nodes with PCs (i.e., IT-driven networking effect) and firm size (i.e., IT-driven economies of scale), thereby compromising conflicting arguments on whether firmlevel investment in IT improves business profitability (Hitt & Brynjolfsson, 1996) based on an IT asset portfolio perspective on firm-level disaggregate IT capital.

Finally, our disaggregate approach avoided mismeasurement problems. That is, this paper overcame a common limitation of previous studies, i.e., the inconsistency in how key constructs are conceptualized and how their interrelationships are hypothesized (Melville et al, 2004), by clearly conceptualizing different IT capital types, which were accurately measurable, and robustly analyzing their potential interactions with a set of 5-year panel data. Consequently, our findings addressed the uncertainty and contradiction in when and how IT contributes to firm performance with specific IT capital aspects. In addition, we considered the argument of Bharadwaj et al (1999) that historical accounting measures would be problematic in assessing IT effects on firm performance because of their limitations. That is, given that discount rate equates the present value of the net cash stream from the investment projects of a firm/business unit with the initial outlay, observing future cash flows is impossible using such accounting measures (Chen & Lee, 1995). In this sense, we used multiple aspects of firm performance, i.e., profitability, sales, and operating costs, to investigate whether the profitability effect of each form of IT capital is driven by sales growth or cost efficiency pathway (Mithas et al, 2011). Thus, our findings do not only explain when the different forms of IT capital exhibit profitability but also whether the values are generated by sales growth or cost efficiency pathways in a single study.

## Practical implications

Our findings may provide a strong overall justification for continued IT investments across different IT capital types by suggesting differentiated and sustainable effects of both IT-enabled revenue-centric and cost-centric projects. Our empirical evidence simultaneously shows the differential effects of disaggregate IT capital types on profitability, sales, and operating costs. That is, each IT capital type requires a different duration to achieve its value on firm performance because each type has unique characteristics. Such findings may guide managerial decisions on IT investments and the allocation of IT assets.

In particular, generating the value of PC-enabled IT capital for individual information access may not require a long period because most individual users quickly grasp how they can properly adapt to and fully utilize its IT features for the given tasks in their work contexts today. Server-enabled IT capital for collective information access will also promptly generate its value because a long period of direct human–computer interactions is unnecessary. By contrast, the IT capital for information stock, such as storage capacity, gradually generates its value after a certain period of exploitative learning to accumulate a sufficient pool of organizational information over time.

Decisions on IT investment can be guided to fit with business strategy according to our findings on whether the profitability effect of each IT capital type follows a sales growth or cost efficiency pathway. That is, the profitability effect of individual information access capital, such as PCs, is driven by its contribution to cost efficiency because of its off-the-shelf characteristics. Server-oriented capital for collective information access capital generates profitability because of its intangible advantage. For example, servers catalyze the profitability effect of PCs, which contributes to both sales growth and cost efficiency. Information stock capital, such as storage capacity, increases the profitability achieved by its contribution to cost efficiency. Node-enabled capital for information flow contributes to sales growth rather than to cost efficiency.

In addition, the decisions on a firm’s IT asset allocation require careful attention to information flow capital. PCenabled capital sufficiently affects profitability only when a sufficiently large quantity of nodes is installed by a firm. Compared with small-sized firms, large-scale companies are likely to generate further sales growth and cost efficiency through their investments in server-oriented capital for information flow.

Overall, we believe that our findings may provide managerial decision makers with practical and feasible implications that are considerably more detailed and advanced compared with the general implication of Mithas et al (2012) that the aggregated IT capital is promising for a firm’s sales growth opportunities rather than cost reduction opportunities. From a top management and board perspective, our findings suggest that each IT capital type should receive a significant and distinct attention to govern and allocate diverse IT resources to improve profitability, which can be driven by sales growth or cost efficiency.

## Limitations and future directions

Our IT asset portfolio perspective is only one of the possible frameworks to disaggregate IT capital. For example, Aral & Weill (2007) and Weill (1992) identified four classes of IT asset portfolios: infrastructure, transactional, information, and strategic IT systems. However, the difference between our work may be that while their concepts of disaggregate IT assets are relatively abstract, our disaggregate IT capital types are considerably more specific. Such a clear conceptualization enables us to measure disaggregate IT capital types economically and objectively as well as provide feasible implications for practices. In addition, unlike Aral & Weill (2007) and Weill (1992), we could analyze effects over time with panel data rather than just contemporaneously using cross-sectional data. Furthermore, our data comprise a more recent dataset across nearly all industry sectors, thereby updating the understanding of disaggregate IT values and providing findings that are comprehensive across industry sectors. In this sense, future studies should propose more exhaustive frameworks than ours, not only based on strong IT value theories but also with tangible measures of IT asset types, to further advance the understanding of IT capital’s differential effects on firm performance.

In regard to nodes, we only found significant interactions with PCs and firm size. Our analyses still omitted other potential complementarities and substitutes between disaggregate IT capital types and their possible interactions with other non-IT capital components and organizational characteristics. Thus, follow-up studies will allow the discovery of diverse research opportunities by investigating such additional complementary and substitutable IT capital effects on firm performance. In particular, we would encourage future studies to investigate the evolving relationships among IT and non-IT capital types: the shift from their substitutive relationships to complementary relationships, or vice versa (Chwelos et al, 2010).

In addition, the conceptualization of network capita using the quantity of nodes installed in a firm, while consistent with the literature, represents an interesting avenue for future research given the important role of network capital in modern computing architecture. It appears a gap exists between how IS research conceptualizes such equipment and how it is viewed in practice.
