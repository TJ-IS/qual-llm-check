## a b s t r a c t

Fads, from a cognitive perspective, are caused by informational cascades. It describes a situation in which people observe the actions of others and then make the same choice, regardless of their own information. Previous research on informational cascades suggested that behavioral conformity prevents information aggregation, and therefore the stability of an informational cascade need not increase as more people join the bandwagon. However, under information overload conditions, where there is more information than a person can handle, any one individual's information is a sample of the whole information pool. When assessing an existing informational cascade an individual can rule out the possibility that predecessors had enough information to shatter the cascade, as the cascade continues. In this way, information (i.e., that the cascade has not been rejected) is injected into cascade even when a new decision-maker simply follows predecessors' behavior. Taking this into consideration, we analyze the information threshold for rational conformity/deviation behavior, and propose a model that will measure information aggregation and evaluate the stability of informational cascades under an overloaded information setting. The results captured two primary behavioral aspects of informational cascade: information aggregation and diminishing sensitivity. The in<sup>fl</sup>uences of product complexity and information revealing are also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Enterprises use various competitive strategies to maintain their dominance in different markets, whether monopolies or oligopolies. Some enterprises dominate the market by maintaining core-technological advances (e.g., Intel); some dominate the market with unique operational modes (e.g., Dell); and others dominate the market with innovations focusing on function and user-experience (e.g., Apple). Apple's competitive advantage is product innovativeness and its use of fads. Product innovation is easy to imitate, but fads seem unpredictable and uncontrollable. Customers who buy Apple's products are not exhibiting pure consumption behavior, but are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by fads and the consumption atmosphere. Successful marketing strategies of this type are not about technical thresholds or business operational modes, but about how to create and nurture consumption fads. One managerial problem for this strategy is evaluating the stability of fads.

As suggested by Bikhchandani et al. [3], fashion and fads can be attributed to herd effect. Although the underlying mechanisms of herd effect have been studied extensively, its stability is not well understood. As a result, we use the herd phenomenon and the mechanisms behind it to explain many things, but we do not know how to alter herd behavior. For example, when we are in the wrong herd, we do not know what efforts are required to overcome it. This is partly because the underlying mechanisms of herd behavior have emotional, cognitive and behavioral components, and understanding the mechanisms requires input from the disciplines of economics, sociology and psychology. Banerjee [2] and Bikhchandani et al. [3], have used a cognitive approach to explore the rational aspect of herd behavior and have uncovered the informational cascade mechanism. An informational cascade occurs when an individual's action does not depend on his private signal. This mechanism, together with sanction on deviant [11], positive payoff externalities [1], conformist transmission [10], and communication channels [22], constitute the primary mechanisms behind herd effect.

The effects of informational cascades have been widely recognized in behavioral economics, <sup>fi</sup>nancial markets [5], labor markets [13], technology adoption [18,23], innovation [15], online investment [12] and real estate [19]; they also affect social <sup>fi</sup>elds such as politics [4] and legal issues [8]. A typical illustration of an informational cascade is an auction in which, once there is a critical mass of bidders, others will join in [20]. Some scholars have investigated the negative impact of informational cascades. For example, Farnsworth [8] claims that the negative effects of informational cascades sometimes become legal concerns and laws should be enacted to neutralize them.

Traditional theories explaining informational cascades ignored the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of decision-makers after the cascade had started. This made it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to explain why the more popular a product is, the more dif<sup>fi</sup>- cult it is for individuals to disobey an information cascade. For example, Duan et al. [6] suggest that consumers pay more attention when evaluating less popular products. Traditional theories also failed to explain why the longer the bandwagon continues, the more robust it becomes.

In our study, we consider cognitive processes in the analysis of the herding sequence. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of all of the individuals in informational cascades will be examined by measuring the information aggregation over the cascade sequence. The stability of the informational cascade will be evaluated by the amount of information needed to disobey it. We primarily focus on the rational aspect of herd behavior, and try to bridge the gap between descriptive behavioral economic theory and prescriptive managerial practice.

The rest of this article proceeds as follows: in Section 2, we provide a conceptual framework, set up decision scenarios to illustrate assumptions of behavioral patterns, and develop several hypotheses about herd behavior; in Section 3, we do analytical analysis; in Section 4, we conduct a computational analysis to test our hypotheses; and in Section 5, we conclude with some remarks about our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 2. Theoretical framework

## 2.1. Conceptual background

Informational cascades occur when people, after observing the behavior of others, choose to behave in the same way, regardless of their own information. In most situations, a person's private information is inaccessible to others; individuals in an informational cascade make decisions based on predecessors' actions only. Thus the effect of information is supposed to be negligible once a cascade starts. Bikhchandani et al. [3], in their evaluation of the stability of informational cascades, argued that the “depth” of an information cascade does not increase with the number of followers. Once a cascade has started, any further herding decisions would be uninformative. This proposition is based on the rationale that while behavior can be observed, the information that leads to that behavior cannot be observed, so informational cascades actually prevent information aggregation. This conventional informational cascade theory, despite its simplicity and strong explanatory power, contradicts some observations of daily behavior.

Let us consider a simple case of choosing a smart-phone; the product set includes Nokia, Google's Android and Apple's iPhone. Suppose you have an information source indicating that an Android is better than an iPhone, but you observe that more people bought iPhones than Androids. If the difference between iPhone and Android consumption is slight and your private information is abundant and strong, you may keep the opinion that an Android is better than an iPhone. However, when the difference between the number of iPhone users and the number of Android users increases, you may become increasingly suspicious of your private information. When the difference reaches a certain threshold, you may decide that an iPhone must be better. This example suggests that the more popular a cascade is, the more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult it is to disobey it, and that size contributes to the stability of a cascade. This is different from the above statement that there is no information aggregation in a cascade.

Another counteract phenomenon of the traditional informational cascades is that consumers usually discount the impact of other's choices as bandwagon continues. For instance, an increase in iPhone users from 2000 to 20,000 is more in<sup>fl</sup>uential than a subsequent rise from 20,000 to 38,000. In both cases, the increase is 18,000, but the initial rise has more in<sup>fl</sup>uence on consumers. As the bandwagon grows, consumers will heuristically discount the number.

These two cases suggest that informational cascade theory may need revision to satisfy the following criteria: <sup>fi</sup>rst, the more popular a cascade is, the more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult it is to disobey, which also means that each individual contributes to the stability of the cascade; second, individuals have diminishing sensitivity with the length of the bandwagon, (i.e., followers contribute less to the stability of informational cascade than earlier decision-makers).

To investigate these two forms of behavior, we introduce the concept of information acquisition effort as an aspect of the judgment process. Today, we can always access more information than we can process, especially with access to e-commerce platforms (e.g., Amazon, Taobao, Apple Application Store, etc.). Under this condition of information overload, every unit of acquired information is a sample of the whole information pool. We suggest that under these conditions, we should explore how in informational cascades a decision-maker utilizes predecessors' behavior and her own information acquisition effort to optimize her choice in a sequential decision-making process. In such a decision process, we assume that there are three prescriptive conditions: decisions are sequential; decision-makers combine their private information with information inferred from previous decision-makers' actions to optimize their choice with Bayesian rationality; the information pool is large enough that every individual's information can be treated as a sample from the pool.

Although, predecessors' information is not observable, a rational decision-maker could maximize the utility of predecessors' information by making a decision based not only on the predecessors' behavior, but also on the perception of the predecessors' information. With this sequential decision process, the cascade will continue if either of two conditions are met – an individual's judgment is consistent with the cascade or an individual's judgment differs from the cascade, but individual has insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient information to reject the cascade. When a decision-maker follows a cascade, this becomes additional information added to the cascade, making the cascade increasingly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to reject.

This suggests that the statistical characteristics of predecessors' information can be inferred from their behavior. The analysis of information cascades in previous literature takes no consideration of this characteristic, thus leading to the conclusion that decision-makers in a cascade have no contribution to the stability of information cascade. This makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to explain why the more popular a product is the more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult it is to shatter the cascade. In our analysis, we distinguish the perceived information from acquired information. Using the statistical characteristics of perceived information, we consider all individuals' contribution to the stability of the information cascade, as subsequent decision-makers can always rule out the possibility that any predecessor had enough information to reject the cascade.

## 2.2. Decision scenarios

Consumers today deal with informational cascades in information overload settings. In their product evaluation and selection process, they are trying to acquire relevant information about an uncertain event, but that event generates information more than an individual can handle, even with maximum effort. This scenario is especially common for consumers on intermediaries, as there are a large variety of product choices. Consumers lack the knowledge to evaluate all aspects of these products, and do not have the time to acquire all of the necessary information. That is to say, consumers are bounded rational when choosing products, as well as judgment and information acquisition. This kind of information overloading in the process of product evaluation is comparable to information overloading in computers. We capture this point by setting an individual's information acquisition ability so that for any unit of information, there is a probability α, that the unit re<sup>fl</sup>ects the true state of the product.

When consumers are choosing a product that they are unable to evaluate, they put more weight on the choice of others. This phenomenon is especially common in e-commerce, as the choices of others are aggregated in the form of product choice ratings and rankings, which are much easier to evaluate than other attributes of the product. This form of information aggregation is quite common in online markets [6]. Of course, personal knowledge, experience and other psychological attributes of the decision-maker also play an important role in her reasoning process [9]. These attributes will determine the probability of an individual making the best choice.

We capture this point by setting an individual's judgmental ability so that the probability of an individual making the best choice independently is p.

In this study, we investigate information aggregation in an informational cascade by examining the psychological status and judgmental process of decision-makers. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne their decision modes in terms of whether the cascade is true or false, whether the individual's judgment is true or false, and whether the information that an individual acquired is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to shatter the informational cascades. Based on these three decision conditions, we get a $1 ~ 2 \times 2 \times 2 { - } 2$ decision scenario set (two scenarios were removed, because when a cascade and an individual judgment are the same, a decision-maker will certainly follow the cascade). The six scenarios are as follows:

1) The cascade is false and the individual's judgment is also false;

2) The cascade is false, the individual's own judgment is true, but the decision-maker has insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient information to disobey the false information cascade;

3) The cascade is false, the individual's own judgment is true, and there is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient information for the decision-maker to disobey the false information cascade;

4) The cascade is true and the individual's judgment is also true;

5) The cascade is true, the individual's own judgment is false, and the decision-maker has enough wrong information to disobey the true information cascade; and

6) The cascade is true, the individual's own judgment is false, but the decision-maker has insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient information to disobey the information cascade.

Of the above decision scenarios, informational cascades will continue in Scenarios 1, 2, 4 and 6, but will shatter in Scenarios 3 and 5. These decision scenarios are based on expected utility theory [17]. When the probabilities (p) re<sup>fl</sup>ect decision-makers' beliefs rather than scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge, the calculation produces subjective expected utility [24]. This calculation usually incorporates not only the belief about others, but also others' beliefs. An analogous rationale can be found in Mertens and Zamir's work [16], which suggests that the presence of incomplete information raises the possibility that we may need to consider a player's beliefs about other players' preferences, his beliefs about their beliefs about his preferences, and so on, in the spirit of rationalizability. Although this idea is used to explain simultaneous decision making, the basic concept of “decision-makers should consider a player's belief about other players' preferences, and so on” could also apply to sequential decision making processes in informational cascades.

Taking the classical model of informational cascade for example, if the nth decision maker simply considers that her predecessors are making decisions based on their own information and judgment, her subjective Bayesian probability of the state of the world i $\vdots { \frac { p ^ { n - 1 } } { p ^ { n - 1 } + \left( 1 - p \right) ^ { n - 1 } } } .$ . Thus the information aggregation is n 1 when the length of bandwagon is n − 1, which suggests a linear function of the bandwagon effect. With this reasoning, this nth decision maker will need n pieces of information to shatter an information cascade. That is, in the naive herding context, where people naively believe that each previous person's action re<sup>fl</sup>ects solely that person's private information, naive herders over-weight early movers' private signal [7,21]. However, if this nth player has higher order beliefs about others, she will consider the fact that others may also choose to follow others. That is, interim herders' actions also embed signals of predecessors [7]. As a consequence, her subjective Bayesian probability of the state of the world is $\frac { p ^ { 2 } } { p ^ { 2 } + ( 1 - p ) ^ { 2 } }$ whatever the length of the bandwagon is. Thus information will not increase in the bandwagon.

The two aforementioned scenarios demonstrate that taking others beliefs into decision consideration will have an impact on the herding model. They also demonstrate that herd behavior is dependent on decision makers' order of beliefs. Recall the case of Apple's product, we will see that these two scenarios should be the boundary condition of herd behavior – later Apple buyers contribute to the stability of the bandwagon, but less as compared to those earlier buyers. That is, information will aggregate in the bandwagon, but not in a linear way.

In a sequential decision process, the cognitive decision process can be broken down into four steps: observe the information cascade, perceive the predecessor's psychological status, make a quick initial judgment, and acquire cross-sectional information to evaluate the judgment. If the decision-maker's judgment is the same as that indicated by the cascade, the decision-maker will not seek extra information; however, if the judgment is different from the cascade, the decision-maker will seek information to con<sup>fi</sup>rm or discon<sup>fi</sup>rm her judgment. In general, this process will only stop when either the decision-maker fails to solicit supportive information, resulting in her decision to follow the herd, or <sup>fi</sup>nds supportive information suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to reject the cascade. This process is illustrated as a <sup>fi</sup>nite state transition in Fig. 1.

Based on this model, decision makers will evaluate predecessors' decision process and effort in making their decisions. For example, four decision makers, A, B, C, and D, have to make their decisions sequentially. Assume that A and B have both chosen 0. C follows and also chooses 0. When D faces the same decision, D might evaluate C's decision details – whether C's initial judgment was 0 or C's initial judgment was 1 but had to choose 0 due to insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient information to deviate from his predecessors A and B. If D's initial judgment is also a 0, he may avoid information seeking process to minimize his decision effort. However, if D faces confusion as his initial judgment deviates from the majority others, he may become more cautious with his initial judgment and proceed to information-seeking process to assess his initial judgment. Hence, our decision model suggests that initial judgment is a mandatory process as everybody will have a quick initial judgment after observing an event, but information seeking can be optional, depending on whether or not the decision maker's initial judgment aligns with the cascade.

## 2.3. Hypothesis

Decision-makers may utilize their perceived information combined with their own private information to update their beliefs. When the private information is strong enough, it may override their perceived information and shatter the informational cascade. Bikhchandani et al. [4] suggest that informational cascades can be shattered by external shock. A shock is actually information disclosure beyond what was expected, or information that is strong enough to contradict perceived information. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne the amount of information needed to shatter a cascade as a measure of the stability of an informational cascade. Information above a certain threshold provides a shock strong enough to shatter the cascade. Intuitively, the higher the threshold, the more stable the cascade is.

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 1. The stability of an informational cascade is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the information threshold needed to shatter it.

A decision-maker's following behavior indicates that her acquired information is below the threshold needed to shatter the cascade. As she has considered the possibility that a false cascade has occurred and then rejected that possibility, her conformity choice suggests to later decision-makers that the cascade is more likely to be true. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1a. New information will be injected into informational cascades when new decision-makers join the cascade.

As the bandwagon grows, more information is aggregated into the cascade, resulting in a more stable cascade, making it harder for subsequent decision-makers to deviate from the cascade. This leads to Hypothesis 1b:

Hypothesis 1b. Later decision-makers need more information than earlier ones to disobey the cascade.

As the informational cascade continues, it becomes increasingly stable, and later decision-makers are less likely to shatter the cascade in the absence of a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant shock. As a result, later decision-makers will rely more on the cascade and less on their private information. This pattern suggests that less information will be injected into a cascade by later decision-makers. In addition, later decision-makers need more information to shatter the cascade, thus increasing the probability that they will follow the cascade. In other words, their decision to follow the cascade would be less informative than an earlier follower. Therefore, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 2a. Later decision-makers contribute less to the stability of information cascades than earlier ones.

Each individual's contribution to the stability of an informational cascade is the marginal stability of the informational cascade. As less information is injected into a cascade by later decision-makers' following behavior, the marginal stability increases slower. Hence, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 2b. The marginal stability of an informational cascade decreases as the cascade continues.

Another issue is related to judgment ability. Consider the acceptance of this article; if three well-recognized professors think this paper is acceptable, the editor will probably accept it, but if three undergraduate students accept this paper, the editor might hesitate and the cascade might shatter. This simple story illustrates that individuals follow their leaders in an informational cascade. Leaders have superior information and are more likely to make better decisions. If the early decision-makers in an informational cascade have a high probability of making the best choice independently, the informational cascade will be more reliable and its stability will be higher. Therefore, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 3. The stability of informational cascades is positively related to an individual's probability of making the best choice independently.

Farnsworth [8] analyzed the legal aspects of informational cascades and found that of<sup>fi</sup>cers in many military courts voted in reverse rank order, that is the of<sup>fi</sup>cer of the lowest rank votes <sup>fi</sup>rst. He explained that this was done to prevent lower-ranked of<sup>fi</sup>cers from simply following the more senior of<sup>fi</sup>cers, who they believed to have better judgment or authority.

If we consider this voting case in a forward manner, we would probably <sup>fi</sup>nd that senior of<sup>fi</sup>cers' judgments are more likely to be accepted by other of<sup>fi</sup>cers. That is to say, later decision-makers would be tempted to follow the cascade, and depend less on their private information. Therefore, their contributions to the cascade, in the form of information and judgment, would be reduced. Hence, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 4. Once a cascade is formed, decision-makers with a high probability of making the best choice independently will make the cascades more stable, and reduce the value of later decision-makers' information and judgment.

When decision-makers have a high probability of acquiring true information to support their decisions, their judgment will be more reliable and they will trust their own judgment. This is particularly true when the cascade is in its early stage. However, a higher probability of acquiring true information will probably accelerate information accumulation, and expedite the stability of the informational cascade. A simple illustration for these two competing effects is as follows. As an early decision-maker with valuable pertinent information to support your judgment, you might be better off following your own judgment. However if you are making the same decision as many others, it is entirely possible that others have also acquired accurate information to support their own decision making, thus abiding by your own judgment may be naïve. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 5a. It is easier to shatter an informational cascade in its earlier stage, if the probability of acquiring true information is high.

Hypothesis 5b. It is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to shatter an information cascade in its later stage, even if the probability of acquiring true information is high.

If a decision-maker's information acquisition ability is high, he may be more likely to reject a false cascade. If he chooses to follow, it suggests that the cascade is more likely to be true. In such a scenario, subsequent decision-makers will be better off if prior decision-makers have high information acquisition abilities. That is to say, the high probability of predecessors acquiring true information increases their contribution to subsequent decision-makers. Thus, we conclude the following:

Hypothesis 6. An individual's contribution to the stability of an information cascade is positively related to the probability of acquiring true information.

These hypotheses consider informational cascades in both macro and micro perspectives. From the macro perspective, we treat the informational cascade as a whole to study its attributes, such as sequential length, information environment, external shock, stability and marginal stability. From the micro perspective, we focus on individual behavior and optimized decision making with the assumption that some statistical characteristics of predecessors' information are perceptible or can be estimated.

## 3. Analytical model

## 3.1. Model setup

In setting up the analytical model for evaluating the above hypotheses, the problem is formulated as follows: N Bayesian rational decisionmakers sequentially choose a product from a binary set [7,21]. The state of the world is either $A \succ B$ or $B \succ A .$ . Decision-makers follow the behavioral pattern discussed in Section 2.2.

To reduce the complexity of the problem, we make an assumption that all of the decision-makers are identical, that is to say their cognitive and judgmental ability are the same. It is a strong assumption to treat every decision-maker as a homogeneous agent, but it is to some extent reasonable to assume that people within a group have similar backgrounds. All of the decision-makers make a judgment, and will try to acquire external information to support their judgment. When their judgment is different from that indicated by the cascade, and the external information is strong enough to support their judgment, a shock forms and the cascade shatters. Otherwise, the cascade continues.

In constructing our analytical model, the following notations were used:

i) p Denotes the probability that an individual's own judgment is true. It is a value related to personal knowledge and experience and it re<sup>fl</sup>ects the chances that an individual will make the best choice individually.

ii) α Denotes the probability that the cross-sectional accessed information is true.

iii) $\lambda _ { i }$ Denotes the amount of information needed to overcome the information cascade for the ith decision-maker.

iv) $\lambda ^ { * }$ Denotes the threshold for stability.

Under overloaded information conditions, information seeking activity can be treated as repeated experiments on the binary set. $\lambda _ { i }$ is equivalent to the number of supportive experiments needed to reject the cascade. The behavioral pattern can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as λ repeated experiments $\{ I _ { 1 } , I _ { 2 } , . . . , I _ { \lambda } \}$ . This information-seeking activity will begin only when a decision-maker's judgment is different from that indicated by the cascade. It will stop at λ when either $I _ { \lambda }$ is different from $I _ { k } ,$ given $k < \lambda \mathrm { o r } \lambda > \lambda ^ { * }$

## 3.2. Sequential analysis

This section analyzes sequential decisions under the conditions of false and true cascades. Given a false cascade, the probability that an individual makes a true judgment without observing others' behavior is $p ;$ and the probability that an individual makes a false judgment without observing others' behavior is $\left( 1 - p \right)$ . The probability of acquiring enough true information to reject a false cascade is $\alpha ^ { \lambda } .$ Therefore, the probability that a judgment is true and information is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient is $\alpha ^ { \lambda } p ,$ whereas the probability that a judgment is true but information is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient is (1-α<sup>λ</sup>) p.

Given a true cascade, the probability that an individual makes a true judgment without observing others' behavior is p; and the probability that an individual makes a false judgment without observing others' behavior is (1-p). The probability of acquiring enough false information to reject a true cascade is $\left( 1 - \alpha \right) ^ { \wedge }$ . Therefore, the probability that a judgment is true and the information is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient is $( 1 - \alpha ) \ ^ { \wedge } p ,$ whereas the probability that a judgment is true but the information is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient is $\left( 1 - \left( 1 - \alpha ^ { \lambda } \right) \right) p .$

D M _ {3} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} 1 - p & \Big (1 - a ^ {\lambda_ {3}} \Big) p & a ^ {\lambda_ {3}} p \\ p & (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {3}} (1 - p) & \Big (1 - (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {3}} \Big) (1 - p) \end{array} \right]
$$

$$
C M _ {3} = \left[ \begin{array}{l} (1 - p) ^ {2} \Big ((1 - p) + \Big (1 - a ^ {\lambda_ {3}} \Big) p \Big) \\ p ^ {2} \Big (p + \Big (1 - (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {3}} \Big) (1 - p) \Big) \end{array} \right]
$$

$$
c _ {2 1} a _ {3 3} > c _ {2 2} a _ {3 5}: a ^ {\lambda_ {3}} p (1 - p) ^ {2} > (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {3}} (1 - p) p ^ {2} \rightarrow \lambda_ {3} > \log \frac {a}{1 - a} \left(\frac {p}{1 - p}\right)
$$

For the 4th decision-maker:

$$
D M _ {4} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} 1 - p & \Big (1 - a ^ {\lambda_ {4}} \Big) p & a ^ {\lambda_ {4}} p \\ p & (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {4}} (1 - p) & \Big (1 - (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {4}} \Big) (1 - p) \end{array} \right]
$$

$$
C M _ {4} = \left[ \begin{array}{l} \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {4} (1 - p) + \Big (1 - a ^ {\lambda_ {i}} \Big) p) \\ \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {4} \Big (p + \Big (1 - (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {i}} \Big) (1 - p) \Big) \end{array} \right]
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} c _ {3 1} a _ {4 3} > c _ {3 2} a _ {4 5}: a ^ {\lambda_ {4}} p (1 - p) ^ {2} \Big ((1 - p) + \Big (1 - a ^ {\lambda_ {3}} \Big) p \Big) \\ \quad > (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {4}} (1 - p) p ^ {2} \Big (p + \Big (1 - (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {3}} \Big) (1 - p) \Big) \to \lambda_ {4} \\ \quad > \log \frac {a}{1 - a} \left(\frac {p}{1 - p}\right) + \log \frac {a}{1 - a} \left(\frac {p + \Big (1 - (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {4}} \Big) (1 - p)}{(1 - p) + (1 - a ^ {\lambda_ {4}}) p}\right) \end{array}
$$

Using the same analysis for every decision-maker that follows, we conclude that for the nth decision-maker:

$$
\lambda_ {n} > \left\{ \begin{array}{l} 0, n = 1, 2 \\ \log \frac {a}{1 - a} \left(\frac {1 - p}{p}\right) + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n - 1} \log \frac {a}{1 - a} \left(\frac {p + (1 - (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {i}}) (1 - p)}{(1 - p) + (1 - a ^ {\lambda_ {i}}) p}\right), n \geq 3 \end{array} \right.\tag{3}
$$

The contribution of the nth decision can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as:

$$
\begin{array}{l}\delta_ {n} = \lambda_ {n} - \lambda_ {n - 1} = \log \frac {a}{1 - a} \left(\frac {p + (1 - (1 - a) ^ {\lambda_ {n - 1}}) (1 - p)}{(1 - p) + (1 - a ^ {\lambda_ {n - 1}}) p}\right), n \geq 3\\\lim _ {n \rightarrow \infty} \delta_ {n} = 0\end{array}\tag{4}
$$

## 4. Computational analysis

To illustrate the property of the information threshold and test its robustness, we conduct several computational analyses based on the above mathematical derivations.

## 4.1. Marginal analysis

If the decision sequence is long enough, the individual contribution will be very small. In a short time span, this kind of information aggregation is almost negligible. This special circumstance coincides with the conclusions of Bikhchandani et al. [3]. However, over a long time span, the accumulation effect on the aggregated information cannot be ignored. This accumulation effect is somewhat comparable to the story of non-perceptible differences in individuals' preferences provided by Mas-Colell et al. [14].

Product complexity has two effects: <sup>fi</sup>rst, it may increase consumers' dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in making independent judgments; second, it may increase the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of acquiring truly supportive information. To analyze the impact of product complexity on informational cascades, we examine the information threshold and its margin with regards to α and $p ,$ which are illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4 respectively.

Although p has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the stability of information cascades at the beginning, it has a subtle negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on individual contribution once a cascade is formed. These results are consistent with H3 to H6, suggesting that information aggregates in and stabilizes the cascade as the bandwagon grows.

Our studies also <sup>fi</sup>nd that product complexity tempers information aggregation. This property suggests that complex products are more likely to succeed with a fad-based marketing strategy. Product complexity can be evaluated in terms of the quantity of features and the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in assessing each feature. Product complexity decreases consumers' ability to make the best choice independently for three reasons. First, when the product is <sup>fi</sup>rst launched into the market, relevant information about the product quality and features may be limited. Second, information about a complex product is less accurate as the features are more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to evaluate. Third, ordinary consumers, without professional knowledge or technical background, have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty evaluating the product even if they have the necessary information.

When consumers are unable to make the best choice independently, they rely more on the behavior of others, as they believe that others' choices re<sup>fl</sup>ect their private information and preferences. That is to say, if there are options, consumers are more likely to choose the product chosen by more consumers, and this effect is strengthened when product complexity increases.

A similar issue can be found in <sup>fi</sup>nancial markets. When <sup>fi</sup>nancial derivatives are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to evaluate, bubbles caused by herd behavior are more severe. This was the case with the mortgage-backed security (MBS) and collateralized debt obligation (CDO) instruments that caused the recent <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis. The complexity of these instruments made it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for investors to understand before investing in them. Therefore, product complexity can sometimes lead to systematic error and cause welfare loss.

## 5. Conclusion

In this study, we proposed an analytical model to evaluate the stability of informational cascades, with an effort to connect descriptive behavioral economic theory to prescriptive managerial practice. In today's dynamic business environment, executives are often forced to develop business strategies under overloaded information settings. Informational cascades may form when the decision-makers see that a number of predecessors have made identical choices. This observational evidence may outweigh an individual's judgment based on her private information. By separating the decision-making process into the stages of initial judgment, observational learning, information seeking and <sup>fi</sup>nal judgment, we proposed the concept of information threshold to simulate the decision effort required to clarify the decision confusion caused by a discrepancy in the individual and cascade's judgment. Technically speaking, below a certain information threshold, people lack the information necessary to shatter the herd behavior with Bayesian rationality. This is the essence of informational cascades. Informational cascades can only be shattered when the decision-maker has information strong enough to overcome the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of observational learning.

This study developed a method to measure information aggregation and to evaluate the stability of informational cascades under overloaded information conditions. It contributes to the theory of sequential decision making by analyzing the information threshold for rational conformity/deviation behavior. When the decision sequence is long enough, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that traditional informational cascade theory is a good approximation in a restricted time span, under overloaded information conditions. This study con<sup>fi</sup>rms the herd phenomenon as the underlying mechanism, while stressing the fact that there is information gained even in a cascade, especially in the initial phase, but the effect tapers off as the cascade grows.

Furthermore, this study analyzed the effect of product complexity on informational cascades. In doing so, it provided a method to evaluate the stability of informational cascades with respect to product complexity and popularity, which may help executives to evaluate the risk of entering a market dominated by herd behavior. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that a good marketing strategy in such a market is to combine product complexity and herd behavior. Our analysis and <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding information revealing effect may help governments to evaluate the risks of herd behavior, and identify policies strong enough to shatter unhealthy herding.
