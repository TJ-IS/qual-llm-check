## a b s t r a c t

Online merchant rating is an important determinant of online merchant success, and plays an essential role in building consumer trust. Based on a rich data set collected from Yahoo's merchant review system, we applied content analysis to identify the most important factors that affect the overall rating of online merchants. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that consumers in general put more weight on post-transaction services than onlinetransaction services when they rate online merchants' performance. This research can help e-commerce practitioners understand potentially problematic areas with their business. This research will also provide some easy-to-implement strategies for e-tailing managers to improve their service quality as well as their overall rating in the online feedback systems.

## 1. Introduction

Online retailing (e-tailing) has been growing rapidly in recent years. According to the U.S. Census Bureau, the total U.S. e-commerce sales for 2007 were estimated at \$136.4 billion, an increase of 19.0% from 2006 [37]. Despite strong overall growth of e-tailing sales, however, e-commerce sales in 2007 accounted for a mere 3.4% of total sales [37]. Evidence has shown that the service quality of e-tailing has been one of consumers' serious concerns. ForeSee Results Inc., a research <sup>fi</sup>rm in Ann Arbor, Michigan reported that online retailers scored 69 out of 100 on customer satisfaction index — the equivalent of a “C”. According to ForeSee Results's survey, 11% of the subjects said they were extremely dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with online retailer service [38]. Consequently, online retailers face a critical challenge in electronic markets (e-markets): how to build consumers' trust in online retailers' service?

As a viable way of promoting consumer trust, the online feedback system has become an important venue in e-markets [10]. Generally speaking, online feedback systems can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two categories: feedback systems in consumer-to-consumer (C2C) auction markets and feedback systems in business-to-consumer (B2C)

markets. As online auction is a hot topic in both business practice and academia, the <sup>fi</sup>rst category of online feedback systems has been extensively studied (e.g., [2–4,10,40]). Yet, the second category of online feedback systems has not been fully explored.

Yahoo's merchant review system (Yahoo's system) is an important mechanism to promote consumers' trust in online stores listed at Yahoo. It is also a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant representative of feedback systems in online B2C markets. Consumers can evaluate a Yahoo merchant after <sup>fi</sup>nishing an online transaction; other consumers can also check a merchant rating before conducting a transaction with the merchant. Yahoo's system consists of the following dimensions: Each online merchant has an overall rating, as well as <sup>fi</sup>ve-category ratings that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the merchant's performance in each speci<sup>fi</sup>c area — price, shipping options, delivery, ease of purchase, and customer service. All the ratings are based on a 1–5 star scale. Not only can customers rate online merchants, they can also leave text comments about their purchasing experience. The recommended length for a comment is 250 to 500 words. With ample space for customers to describe their complete experience with online merchants, Yahoo's system not only functions as a tool to foster trust, it also provides extremely valuable information. Online merchants can discover business problems, improve service quality, increase repetitive purchase, and promote customer loyalty using Yahoo's system. Fig. 1 provides a screen shot of the Yahoo's merchant review system.

Through Yahoo's system, online merchants could generally <sup>fi</sup>nd out consumers' evaluation about their services. However, with the current

Yahoo's system, it is not clear how a single merchant attribute, such as price, shipping options, delivery, ease of purchase, or customer service affects the merchant's overall rating. Nor is it clear which attributes consumers consider to be more important when evaluating a merchant. Most importantly, when reading consumers' comments in the review system, we can clearly notice that the current dimensions used by Yahoo's merchant review system are overly broad and simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed. For example, the category of “customer service” includes almost every aspect of post-transaction activities such as product exchanges or returns, customer service accessibility, and so on. This phenomenon is indeed very common in online merchant review/feedback systems. We found that most other commercial online rating sites use just one category – “customer service” or “customer support” – to cover various types of customer services. However, more detailed information about customer services is particularly valuable to online merchants because customer service is one of the most important means to create consumer trust. Consumers' text comments may convey rich information about an online merchant's previous transactions which cannot be wholly captured with Yahoo's current merchant review system. Therefore, studying the text comments in Yahoo's system can provide more insight into the formation of online merchant reputation. This research applies content analysis to the text comment in Yahoo's system. We seek to answer the following research questions: What are the most important factors affecting online merchants' satisfaction rating? How can online merchants signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve their customers' satisfaction?

The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the research is twofold. From a practical perspective, consumers rely heavily on online merchant ratings to make purchase decisions in B2C electronic markets. Our research identi<sup>fi</sup>es the factors that constitute online merchants' reputation by studying real consumers' comments. The results can shed light on what operational components online merchants should concentrate on to effectively improve their overall rating. For online merchants, especially small and unknown online merchants, this research can help them improve their services and gain competitive advantages in electronic markets. Second, from a research perspective, this is a new effort to explore B2C online feedback systems. Better knowledge about the criteria consumers use to evaluate an online merchant and the weight consumers put on different criteria can help us improve the design of B2C online feedback systems.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the related literature as well as compares the current study with the prior literature that applies content analysis in C2C markets. Section 3 discusses the data collection method, the categorization scheme of online merchant services and the detailed content analysis methodology we used in this research. Section 4 discusses the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings based on the regression analysis, and Section 5 concludes the paper with discussion of the research implication.

## 2. Literature review

In the past years, we have witnessed a proliferation of studies focusing on online transactions. From the beginning to the end, an online transaction includes “information search, Web site navigation, ordering customer service interactions, delivery and satisfaction with the ordered product” ([39], p. 183). Accordingly, prior literature mainly examines one part of the online-transaction process such as Web site interface (e.g., [5,6,25,26,31,41]), customer information search [29], customer complaints [17], customer service [42], and customer satisfaction [11,13,14,22,30,36]. A more comprehensive review of the prior literature along this line can be found in Wol<sup>fi</sup>nbarger and Gilly [39] and Field, Heim and Sinha [13].

In the following section, we will mainly review literature closely related to the present study. Table 1 lists the dependent variables, independent variables and research methodology used in the previous studies.

Concentrating on the service quality of e-tailing, Wol<sup>fi</sup>nbarger and Gilly [39] develop a conceptual framework for both de<sup>fi</sup>ning and measuring e-tailing quality. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that four factors – Web site design, ful<sup>fi</sup>llment/reliability, privacy/security and customer service – are strongly predictive of customer evaluations of e-tailing quality and satisfaction. Devaraj, Fan and Kohli [11] study consumer satisfaction with e-commerce channel using three established frameworks: Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Transaction Cost Analysis (TCA), and Service Quality (SERVQUAL). They <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence supporting the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of perceived ease of use and usefulness in TAM, ease of use in TCA, and assurance in Service Quality in determining consumer satisfaction. Kotha, Rajgopal and Venkatachalam [22] study the role of online buying experience as a competitive advantage, and claim that Web site usability and product selection can be easily lost to competitors via imitation, whereas customer con<sup>fi</sup>dence and relationship services can lead to a sustainable competitive advantage. Posselt and Gerstner [34] investigate the effects of customer satisfaction with services delivered in different stages on repurchase intention and overall service rating. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that compared to pre-sale service, the post-sale service affects customer repurchase intention and overall service ratings in a much stronger way.

It is important to point out that most prior literature generally dwells on Web-customer satisfaction using controlled experiments with student subjects (e.g., [21,29]) or online surveys where respondents are online customers who are not necessarily randomly drawn (e.g., [11,36,39]). Because student subjects do not actually make the purchase, the issues related to customer services and product delivery cannot be addressed. Online surveys may face the problem that a representative sample of the target population may not have the opportunity to respond. Moreover, some research (e.g., [11]) uses online surveys, but undergraduate and graduate students still represent a big part of online shoppers in the study. To date, very few studies examine online consumer satisfaction or online merchant performances using real customer data collected from online B2C markets. The aforementioned two studies [22,34] analyze real market data in their studies, but the independent variables in their research are severely restricted by the existent categories used by Gomez and BizRate, respectively. In the current research, we collect data from Yahoo's merchant review system, use content analysis to generate our own independent variables, and analyze the impact of those selfgenerated variables on Yahoo merchants' ratings. Using real customer comments in B2C feedback systems allows us to document the most current concerns of customers and helps us identify the potential problematic areas for e-tailing.

It is worth noting that content analysis has been used recently in studies focusing on online auctions. For instance, MacInnes [27] and MacInnes, Li and Yurcik [28] study the nature of disputes on eBay and explore whether some factors (e.g., the number of bids, the reputation of the seller, the length of the auction, and the <sup>fi</sup>nal price) are more likely to lead to a particular type of dispute. Also concentrating on eBay's online auction site, Gregg and Scott [16] investigate the role of reputation systems in documenting, predicting, and reducing fraud. Pavlou and Dimoka [33] examine the feedback text comments on eBay and their role in promoting a buyer's trust in a seller's benevolence and credibility, both of which may affect the price premiums that a seller gets from buyers. However, all the aforementioned literature focuses on online C2C markets. Although some sellers on eBay operate as a full-time business, eBay is still generally considered as one of the largest online C2C markets. C2C and B2C markets are different in many aspects, which lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different <sup>fi</sup>ndings. For example, the transaction process in Yahoo's B2C market is different from that in eBay's C2C market. Yahoo basically provides a market for many other online merchants such as Target, Tele<sup>fl</sup>ora.com, Zappos.com and so on. Those merchants list their products at Yahoo, and when a buyer clicks on a product listed at Yahoo, he or she will be directed to the seller's storefront. The transaction will be completed at the merchant's storefront as well. Although eBay's auction site also provides a market for buyers and sellers to meet, the buyers conduct the bidding process at eBay's auction site, and the buyers and sellers complete their transactions either through eBay's payment system or <sup>fi</sup>nish the transaction completely of<sup>fl</sup>ine. Therefore, eBay sellers do not have to have a Web site to conduct their business transaction. As a result, the criteria to evaluate merchants in Yahoo's market are quite different from those used in the evaluation of eBay sellers. For instance, ease of purchase, Web interface design, and Web site security are criteria commonly used to evaluate online merchant's performance, but are not relevant for individual sellers in a C2C market. These factors are managed by the market host (e.g., eBay) and do not differ among sellers. Moreover, the scope of practice is quite different for online retailers and individual sellers. Online retailers generally have a larger scope of practice than individual sellers (e.g., sales volume, marketing expenses, inventory, and variety in products carried). Accordingly, issues such as supply chain management and marketing practice are more relevant to online retailers than to individual sellers. This also explains why we found factors such as the effectiveness of service representative and post-transaction spam important in shaping an online merchant's reputation, while these issues in general are not prominent in a C2C market. As a matter of fact, the current research complements the literature based on content analysis in C2C market, and contributes to the trust literature. It also provides insights for online merchants to effectively improve their merchant ratings in B2C electronic market.

## 3. Methodology

To investigate the factors affecting consumers' valuation of online retailers' services, we collected data with real consumers' text comments from Yahoo's system. The data collection and processing details are described below.

## 3.1. Data collection

We obtained data from Yahoo (http://shopping.yahoo.com) on March 10, 2004. Clicking on the digital camera subcategory under electronics, we were able to obtain a list of 721 digital cameras. Clicking on the <sup>fi</sup>rst digital camera, which was a Canon Powershot S400 Digital ELPH camera, a list of 74 merchants who were selling this product was displayed. 54 of the merchants had at least one text comment available and the total number of text comments was around 2700.

To reduce the workload of reading the unbelievably large amount of consumers' comments, we picked 27 online merchants listed with an even number. For the 27 online merchants, we collected all the merchant review information, including Yahoo merchant ratings and text comments. The merchant rating includes an overall rating and <sup>fi</sup>ve-category ratings on a scale from 1 star to 5 stars, with 1 being the worst and 5 the best. We then cleaned the information by eliminating irrelevant comments (e.g. comment is about product feature not about online merchant performance) and merchant ratings with missing information (e.g. merchant rating for price component is not available). The <sup>fi</sup>nal data set contains 1001 comments and corresponding merchant ratings, each of which has relevant content in the comment and complete information in the merchant ratings. It is worth noting that although we collected our sample by searching a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of digital camera, the consumers' text comments collected were not about the digital camera. Yahoo pooled all the merchant ratings and comments from consumers together for each online merchant. Those consumers may have bought a digital camera, a bunch of <sup>fl</sup>owers, a book or a CD, but they did not evaluate the particular product in the feedback system. What they evaluated was the online merchant's performance according to their shopping experience with that online merchant. Therefore, the merchant ratings and text comments were about the online merchant's performance, not that of the product itself. Although most of the online merchants in our data set are from the electronics sector, we do not think this is a serious generalization problem. There are good reasons to focus on the electronics market: First, electronics products form a large sector of electronic markets. Second, the market for electronics is very competitive, so online merchants have a strong incentive to improve their reputation. Third, an online merchant's reputation plays a more important role for electronics than reputation plays for a commodity product, given the relatively strong look-andfeel nature of an electronics product [9].

## 3.2. Online merchant service categorization scheme

Yahoo has a <sup>fi</sup>ve-category merchant review system (price, shipping options, delivery, ease of purchase and customer service). However, it is not clear whether those categories have captured the service components customers are most concerned about. Moreover, some categories in Yahoo's system are too broad to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently reveal information about online merchant performance. To solve those problems, we developed our own online merchant service categories by examining the text comments collected at Yahoo's merchant review system and by following the literature on online merchant performance in B2C marketplaces. We drew on the literature and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several dimensions measuring online merchant performance. These dimensions include issues related to Web site design (e.g., Web site usability [22]), privacy/security [39], on-site resource [22], selection and clarity [34], shipping fee and shipping options [34], price [22], and post-sale services such as availability, tracking, ontime, expectation, and support [34] and reliability [39]. These dimensions provide us with the basic understanding about measuring online merchant performance. We then applied the content analysis approach to the text comments we collected and generated a list of 23 service categories that had been mentioned in the consumers' text comments about online merchants' services. Finally, we examined the occurring frequency of those categories and removed some categories with very low occurring frequency in the data, because those categories generally do not represent major issues consumers cared about in evaluating online merchants. Therefore, our <sup>fi</sup>nal number of categories is 14. These categories are listed below (category with the highest frequency is listed <sup>fi</sup>rst). The de<sup>fi</sup>nition of each category can be found in Appendix A.

(1) Quality of order processing

(2) On-time delivery

(3) Product price

(4) Effectiveness of service representative

(5) Quality of delivered product

(6) Ease of purchase

(7) Order tracking service

(8) Customer service accessibility

(9) Product delivery accuracy

(10) Shipping cost

(11) Product availability as promised

(12) Post-transaction spam

(13) Ease to return product

(14) Shipping options.

Due to the complexity of the information in feedback comments, it is not enough to simply code each feedback comment into different categories as that does not capture the rich information in the comment. Therefore, we further assigned an ordinal measure to each comment for each nominal category. We used a scale of −5 to 5 for the ordinal coding: a positive 5 means extremely positive experience; a negative 5 indicates extremely negative experience.<sup>3</sup> For example, if a customer wrote “the price is the lowest that can be found”, we would assign a 5 to the category of product price for this observation; if a customer wrote “there is no way that I can track my order after I submitted it”, we would give a −5 to the category of order tracking service for this observation. We assigned a value of zero to categories not mentioned in a comment for that observation. Zero is in the middle of our ordinal coding scale and thus represents a neutral feeling. This is consistent with the measurement of consumer perceptions of service quality (SERVQUAL) [32]. The researchers de<sup>fi</sup>ned service quality (SERVQUAL) as “the discrepancy between consumers' perceptions of services offered by a <sup>fi</sup>rm and their expectations about <sup>fi</sup>rms offering such service” [32, p. 14]. By the same token, we believe if a customer does not mention a particular aspect of his/her shopping experience in the feedback, that means there is no discrepancy between the consumer's perception of service in this category offered by a <sup>fi</sup>rm (e.g., on-time delivery) and his/her expectation about the <sup>fi</sup>rm offering such service. Therefore, the customer feels neutral about the merchant's performance in this category and does not mention it in the merchant review.

To summarize, the categorization scheme we developed is not only nominal (the 14 categories) but also ordinal (the coding scheme is ordinal from −5 to +5 within each of the 14 nominal categories). Since the majority of prior content analysis research only employs nominal categories (e.g., [15]), our categorization scheme thus is much more sophisticated. Moreover, our approach makes it possible to apply regression analysis to study the factors that affect customer satisfaction in online shopping.

## 3.3. Content analysis

Content analysis has been extensively used in social sciences, decision sciences and information systems [1,8,15,20,35]. This methodology develops data sets using a systematic procedure of coding documentary evidence [18,19]. Following content analysis procedure [10,15,19,20], one author and one research assistant worked on the coding.

First, the two coders went through an iterative training process. They were provided with the categorization scheme and a description of each of the nominal and ordinal categories. A sample of 100 text comments (not overlapping with the 1001 comments in the formal analysis) was used as a training sample to ensure consistent understanding of the categories between the two coders. Then, following the coding instruction for the nominal categories and ordinal scales, each of the two coders processed all the 1001 comments independently and generated two data sets. Following standard content analysis procedure, we computed two commonly used measures of inter-coder reliability: Cohen's Kappa [7] and Krippendorff's alpha [23]. First we used Cohen's Kappa to evaluate the reliability of nominal categorization; that is, whether the two coders put each comment in the same nominal categories. Second, we used Cohen's Kappa to evaluate whether the two coders shared the same perception (i.e., positive, neutral or negative feeling) about each nominal category in each comment. For the above two tests, all of the categories have Kappa value greater than 0.75, implying excellent reliability according to Landis and Koch [24]. Last, we used Krippendorff's alpha to evaluate whether the two coders put each comment into the same nominal and ordinal category. All of the categories have an alpha value above 0.70, also indicating great reliability [23].

After the two coders <sup>fi</sup>nished the independent coding, the research team met to discuss disagreements between the two coders and chose a mutually agreeable coding. Thus, we obtained the <sup>fi</sup>nal data set. The descriptive statistics can be found in Table 3. The distribution of the 27 online merchants in the sample of 1001 feedbacks can be found in Appendix B.

Among the 14 categories, seven have negative mean values, which represent potential problematic service components. Buyers seem to be mostly unsatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the customer service accessibility, followed by product availability as promised. Product price and on-time delivery received the highest satisfaction on average.

There are two major advantages using our categorization based on text comments, compared to the Yahoo rating system.<sup>4</sup> First, categories in Yahoo's star ratings are too broad to capture consumers' speci<sup>fi</sup>c concerns. For example, many comments in our sample are about an online merchant's failure to deliver a status notice before the consumer receives the product. Since information regarding status notices is not included in Yahoo's <sup>fi</sup>ve-category rating, most consumers expressed their concerns instead by giving a lower rating to the customer service. This comment was collected directly from Yahoo's Web site. “They sent no notice that it was shipped, so I didn't know if anything was happening…”. This customer thus gave two stars to customer service category and four stars to the other four categories. In another observation, the customer gave <sup>fi</sup>ve stars to all other categories, but only two stars to customer service. “I purchased a digital camera along with a couple of accessories,” the customer wrote in comments. “The UV lens …didn't <sup>fi</sup>t the camera. Now I have to <sup>fi</sup>gure out how to exchange or return it….” Clearly the customer is concerned speci<sup>fi</sup>cally about product return, not general customer service.

Similarly, the delivery category in Yahoo's merchant review system can have very different meaning as well: time (Was the product delivered within a time frame promised at the point of purchase?), product (Was the delivered product actually what was ordered?), and package (Was the delivered product well protected in good packaging?). It is too general to compress those different aspects into only one category, although it is understandable that online merchant feedback system designers want to keep their systems simple. Therefore, our detailed categorization based on consumer's text comments can help etailing managers to accurately pinpoint their customers' speci<sup>fi</sup>c concerns and thus to improve online merchant's operations.

## 4. Regression analysi

Using the data discussed in the above section, we conducted regression analyses to identify factors that have major impacts on customers' evaluation of online merchants. In our models, we use the overall rating from Yahoo's system as the proxy for customers' evaluation.

The results are reported in Table 5. Model 1 is the base model from the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimation. All fourteen variables have the expected sign, and most are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. In the base model, we assume a constant marginal effect of all service factors on the overall rating. To capture the potential decreasing or increasing marginal effects in Model 2, we included quadratic function terms for the 6 most in<sup>fl</sup>uential variables based on Model 1. As can be seen, most quadratic terms are negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, indicating that the marginal impact on overall rating from additional improvement of a particular service is diminishing.

For our data, it is likely that the problem of heteroskedasticity exists, which will result in invalid test statistics and inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the OLS estimation. We tested for heteroskedasticity using Breusch– Pagan/Cook–Weisberg test.<sup>6</sup> The LM statistics in the test is 63.66 (pvalueb.0001), which con<sup>fi</sup>rms the problem of heteroskedasticity. To improve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, we correct heteroskedasticity by applying the Weighted Least Squares (WLS) estimation using a general form of heteroskedasticity. In our treatment, we assume that heteroskedasticity is of unknown form but is affected by all of the explanatory variables included in the model. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we assume that the variance of regression error var(ɛ ∣x) is a function of x, i.e., h(x ), where x is the vector of all explanatory variables in the model. For generality, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne h(x )=exp(x ′δ) to estimate h(x ) and then apply WLS with the weight $\sqrt { h ( { \pmb x } ) } . ^ { 7 }$ The results from WLS are reported in Table 5 (Model 3).

In general, WLS is more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than OLS if heteroskedasticity exists. However, since each online merchant is different in terms of market status, <sup>fi</sup>nancial status, Web site design and management, there may be some unobservable factors that affect a consumer's rating. If such unobservables are correlated with the factors included in the model, the resulting estimation will suffer from omitted variable problem. As each online merchant has a number of customers in our data, it is possible to apply panel data techniques (<sup>fi</sup>xed effects model) to control for the omitted variable problem. The results from <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model (Model 4) and random effects model (Model 5) are reported in Table 5. The Hausman test suggests that a <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model is appropriate in this analysis. Furthermore, the results of <sup>fi</sup>xed effects regression and WLS regression are similar. We will focus on the results from <sup>fi</sup>xed effects (Model 4) in the following analysis.

All variables are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant except shipping options and square of on-time delivery. Variables that have major effects on online merchant overall rating (stronger ones are listed <sup>fi</sup>rst) include post-transaction spam, on-time delivery, effectiveness of service representative, product availability as promised, quality of order processing, and customer service accessibility<sup>8</sup>. Negative signs on most of the quadratic terms imply that those variables have diminishing marginal effects on overall rating. For example, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the post-transaction spam variable suggests that, <sup>fi</sup>xing other variables, if the performance of sending post-transaction spam improves from −5 to −4 in a scale of −5 to 5, the overall rating on a scale of 1 to 5 is expected to increase by 0.34. Improving performance from −4 to −3 will increase the overall rating by 0.30, and so on.<sup>9</sup> If an online merchant's behavior related to post-transaction spam improves from negative to neutral, the overall rating will increase approximately half a star point on a scale of 1 to 5; improving from 0 to 1, the overall rating is expected to increase by about another half a star point.

The rest of the categories can be grouped into measurements of an online merchant's posttransaction performance (Table 6). We de<sup>fi</sup>ne an online-transaction to be the part of a transaction in which the consumer has interaction with the online merchant's Web site. Post-transaction is the part of the transaction after a consumer makes the purchase online. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, product price, ease of purchase, shipping cost, and shipping options can generally be grouped into online-transaction. Quality of order processing, on-time delivery, quality of delivered product, order tracking service, product delivery accuracy, product availability as promised, effectiveness of service representative, customer service accessibility, post-transaction spam, and ease to return product can be grouped into post-transaction.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

Online-transaction service versus post-transaction service is a critical issue for online merchants. Online merchants have to decide where to invest to improve their service quality and thus to improve their reputation rating in electronic markets. In this study, we apply a content analysis approach to identify 14 categories and to examine what categories are the most important determinants of the online merchant rating. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that consumers in general put more weight on post-transaction services than online-transaction services when they evaluate online merchants' performances. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the most important factors impacting consumers' evaluation of an online merchant's reputation rating as post-transaction spam, on-time delivery, effectiveness of service representative, product availability as promised, quality of order processing, and customer service accessibility.

Compared with the literature, our study, in a sense, reveals the evolution of consumers' evaluation of e-tailing performances. Wol<sup>fi</sup>nbarger and Gilly [39] identify four factors (Web site design, ful<sup>fi</sup>llment/reliability, privacy/security and customer service) to be the strongest predictors of customer evaluations of e-tailing quality and satisfaction. However, our study demonstrates that two of the four factors are not practically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in our regression results. Web site design is statistically, but not practically, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Privacy/security is not even included in our categories. Based on content analysis, the occurrence frequency of privacy/security in the comments is very low, indicating very few customers have concerns about the privacy/security issue when they shop online even if it is the <sup>fi</sup>rst time customers use that Web site. One possible explanation is that given the post-purchase data collected in the research, it represents the consumers who have chosen to shop on this site, thus privacy/security is already not a problem.<sup>10</sup> Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are in line with the results of Kotha, Rajgopal and Venkatachalam [22]. They claim that competitive advantages gained by Web site usability and product selection can be erased via imitation, but customer con<sup>fi</sup>dence and relationship services represent a sustainable competitive advantage.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are also consistent with the results of Posselt and Gerstner [34]. Posselt and Gerstner [34] used pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed variables identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by BizRate and found that the post-sale service impacts customer repurchase intention and overall service ratings in a much stronger way than the pre-sale service. However, Posselt and Gerstner's analyses were restricted by the pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed variables. For example, BizRate's categories still favor pre-sale factors. Among the 13 categories BizRate currently in use, there are 8 pre-sale variables (i.e., ease, selection; clarity; price; look; ship-fee; shipoptions; charge), and only 5 post-sale service variables (availability; tracking; on-time; expectation; support). By applying content analysis methodology, we identify 14 service categories — 10 about posttransaction services. Our analyses demonstrate that the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variables affecting online merchants' reputation ratings are the post-transaction services. Most importantly, we identify some variables that the existing reputation ratings have never recognized, such as post-transaction spam.

Our study can provide insight into online merchants' business practices in electronic markets. Two of the six categories that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect online merchants' satisfaction ratings were related to online merchants' strategies to make more pro<sup>fi</sup>ts: An online merchant may mark unavailable products as available and take a consumer's order, but later on send a notice to the buyer that this product has been back ordered. This is a harmful strategy, which may result in a consumer's strong dissatisfaction with the merchant. Moreover, online merchants should be very cautious about trying to sell more related products to a buyer who just made an order from the online merchant. Our results show that consumers are annoyed by this type of behavior — they generally view it as post-transaction spam, which has a strong negative effect on online merchant reputation. In addition, competitive price is important, but electronic commerce is not just about price competition. In our analysis, product price is statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in affecting consumer evaluation, but the magnitude of this impact is relatively small.

Our study can also shed light on the design of online feedback systems in B2C electronic markets. We <sup>fi</sup>nd two signi<sup>fi</sup>cant limitations of the current Yahoo merchant review system.<sup>11</sup> First, while three categories (price, shipping options, and ease of purchase) out of <sup>fi</sup>ve in Yahoo's existing system focus on online-transaction variables, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that all the variables that most affect online merchants' reputation ratings belong to the post-transaction category. For example, consumers want their products to be delivered as promised and on-time (on-time delivery and product availability as promised); consumers expect good consumer care after transaction (customer service accessibility and effectiveness of service representatives); but consumers do not want to be bothered by sales marketing after the transaction from online merchants (post-transaction spam). Therefore, the B2C feedback system should add more categories about post-transaction service.

Second, the two categories “customer service” and “delivery” in Yahoo's star ratings are too broad to capture consumers' exact concerns. For instance, the delivery category could include time aspect (on-time delivery), product aspect (the delivered product is actually what has been ordered) and package aspect (the delivered product is well protected in good package), etc. Our study reveals that it is the time aspect rather than the product or the package aspect that affects the consumers' evaluation. Moreover, for customer service, consumers are mostly concerned about the effectiveness of service representatives and customer service accessibility. Therefore, more detailed categories should be added into the evaluation system.

Our results are mainly limited to pure online merchants (those with only online presence). Future research can compare the determinants of merchant ratings for pure online merchants and for “click and brick” merchants (those with both physical store and online presence). Additionally, although we extended our research by studying the <sup>fi</sup>rm's post-transaction and online-transaction performance, we did not go further to study the performance pattern of online merchants. For example, it might be of interest to investigate whether online merchants that have good online-transaction performance also perform well in post-transaction. Finally, online-transaction variables were measured in the current study after a real transaction was <sup>fi</sup>nished, similar to Posselt and Gerstner [34]. Thus the recency effects could be strong due to the measurement issue. That is, when consumers form their evaluation of an online merchant, they may value post-transaction factors much more than the onlinetransaction factors because of fresh memory.

## Appendix A. Category de<sup>fi</sup>nition

1. Quality of order processing: How good and how smooth is the whole ordering process? A positive example in this category includes “the whole ordering process is smooth and error-free.” Negative examples in this category include, “merchants have wrong customer information,” “lost order,” “cancelled order without notifying customers,” “charged credit card before shipping out product,” “failed to include discount as promised,” “delivered product without invoice,” etc.

2. On-time delivery: Did customers receive products on time? Comments re<sup>fl</sup>ect customers receiving their products within the time frame promised by the merchants, how fast merchants deliver products, etc.

3. Product price: The price at which a customer buys the product, not including shipping cost. Comments in this category re<sup>fl</sup>ect how the customer feels about the price charged by a merchant.

4. Product delivery accuracy: Was the product delivered as advertised? Examples of comments in this category include, “ordered new but received refurbished product,” “product received is part of what had been ordered,” etc.

5. Quality of delivered product: How is the product packaged and how is the product functioning?

6. Effectiveness of service representative: How effective are the service representatives? Comments re<sup>fl</sup>ect representatives' attitude and effectiveness helping customers.

7. Ease of purchase: How easily can customers purchase from an online merchant? Comments re<sup>fl</sup>ect online store layout and the ease of searching for, <sup>fi</sup>nding and ordering products.

8. Order tracking service: Will customers receive order con<sup>fi</sup>rmation? Can customers track the shipping progress of their orders? How easily can customers track their orders?

9. Shipping cost: The actual expenses of shipping.

10. Product availability as promised: Is a product really available as advertised? Comments can re<sup>fl</sup>ect when an online merchant's Web site has shown a product to be in stock even though the product is out of stock, requiring a back-order to be placed after consumers place orders. This circumstance could result in delayed shipping.

11. Ease to return product: How easy for customers to return a product?

12. Customer service accessibility: How easy for customers to reach customer service? Comments re<sup>fl</sup>ect concerns about the availability of merchants' toll-free number or email contact, customer service hours of operation, hold time for customer service representatives and how fast a customer service representative replies to a customer's email requests.

13. Post-transaction spam: Online merchants attempt to cross sell after customers contact customer service or after customers place orders.
