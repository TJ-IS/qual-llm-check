# TOWARD A PROCESS-BASED, INTERPRETIVE UNDERSTANDING OF HOW COLLABORATIVE GROUPS DEAL WITH ICT INTERRUPTIONS $^{1}$

Sanna Tiilikainen
School of Arts and Design, Aalto University,
Espoo, FINLAND {sanna.tiilikainen@aalto.fi}

Virpi Kristiina Tuunainen
School of Business, Aalto University,
Espoo, FINLAND {virpi.tuunainen@aalto.fi}

Suprateek Sarker
McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia,
Charlottesville, VA, U.S.A. {sarkers@virginia.edu}

Ilkka Arminen
Faculty of Social Sciences, University of Helsinki,
Helsinki, FINLAND, {ilkka.arminen@helsinki.fi}

Contemporary knowledge workers collaborating in hybrid work environments need to frequently deal with ICT interruptions. This can potentially lead to disruptions in the collaboration process, particularly in synchronous collaboration contexts. How do knowledge workers deal with such interruptions to ensure the smooth continuation of their collaboration? Previous studies, for the most part, suggest that interruptions are likely to negatively affect the process of collaboration, yet we see that in many instances, knowledge workers continue to collaborate smoothly despite receiving ICT interruptions and even attending to them. In other words, ICT interruptions do not always discernibly affect the collaborative process. In this paper, sensitized by concepts from Goffman's work, we offer a novel understanding of how group members can seamlessly handle ICT interruptions, avoiding and, in certain cases, recovering from serious disruptions in collaboration.

Keywords: ICT interruptions, distributed work, collaboration, knowledge work, symbolic interactionism, qualitative research, interpretive study, multimodal interaction analysis, video analysis

## Introduction

Contemporary collaborative knowledge work is a complex sociotechnical activity that often involves multiple individuals working jointly on nontrivial, knowledge-related tasks supported by information and communication technologies (ICTs) (e.g., Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015; Bailey & Konstan,

2006). To accomplish such tasks, knowledge workers must remain engaged with their various collaborators at any given moment, acting in a collectively acceptable manner to avoid the risk of breakdown or a serious disruption in the flow of their collaboration (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015, 2018b; Goffman, 1959; Junglas et al., 2013). To complicate matters, knowledge workers can work in physical proximity to each other, in a digital space (e.g., in collocated or distributed environments), or in combinations of the two (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015; Davenport et al., 1996)—referred to as “hybrid” digital-physical environments (Griffith & Neale, 2001). Furthermore, they can collaborate synchronously or asynchronously (e.g., Sarker & Sahay, 2003).

During a collaboration, which we define as multiple individuals working jointly toward a shared goal, members of the collaborating group often receive ICT interruptions (Jett & George, 2003; Gupta et al., 2013) in the form of text messages, emails, phone calls, notifications, software update requests, battery power alerts, etc. In fact, research suggests that over the course of an average workday, knowledge workers may spend almost a third of their work time dealing with such interruptions (D'Arcy et al., 2014; González & Mark, 2004; Spira & Feintuch, 2005). In this paper, we are only concerned with ICT interruptions. Consistent with past definitions and in line with the collaborative group context of our study, we define an ICT interruption as a discrete event in the form of a message delivered to a member of the collaborating group through ICT that: (1) is unanticipated in relation to the ongoing collaboration from the perspective of the recipient with respect to its timing, and possibly the source and/or the medium of delivery, and (2) enters the attentional space of the recipient such that the recipient experiences a distraction or break in focus in the task at hand—even if only briefly.

Given the practical relevance of collaborating in hybrid knowledge work environments and the frequent ICT interruptions that collaborators might encounter, it is essential to investigate how seamless collaboration is possible despite interruptions potentially causing a break in focus for the interruption recipient, highlighted in extant research on this topic.

Previous studies on interruptions (see Appendix A) have focused mainly on individuals engaged in discrete tasks rather than on group collaboration (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015, 2018b; Chen & Karahanna, 2018; Dabbish & Kraut, 2008). With some exceptions, studies have typically highlighted the negative effects on the interrupted individual's task performance (e.g., Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015; Gupta et al., 2013) due to the individual's cognitive limitations that result from dealing with ICT interruptions (e.g., Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018a; Chen & Karahanna, 2018; Tams et al., 2018). There is, however, limited research examining interruptions in group contexts. This is a significant gap, given that past research has shown that the understanding of a phenomenon at an individual level does not necessarily translate to an understanding of what happens at the group level (e.g., Sarker & Valacich, 2010). The few studies focusing on groups suggest that the individual-level effects of interruptions can cascade at the group level (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018b; Gupta et al., 2013); in particular, the theoretical work of Addas and Pinsonneault (2018b) suggests that ICT interruptions' effects tend to "ripple" up to the group (e.g., through mechanisms such as emotional contagion and social loafing). The theoretical reasoning and assertions in this work offer a solid foundation for understanding how the ICT interruptions received by an individual might affect the group and its actual task performance. Yet the current theoretical perspectives do not satisfactorily explain how the ripple that is expected to transfer the effects of interruptions from the individual to the group can be potentially suppressed, avoided, or—more broadly—managed by group members such that there are no noticeable effects on the collaboration. Understanding the process by which individuals belonging to a group routinely and naturally deal with interruptions is of particular importance because there is a mismatch between anecdotal observations (and indeed our empirical material) of how the phenomenon of handling an interruption in a group context actually unfolds in practice and existing theoretical models, including those derived through a synthesis of the literature. Such a mismatch between field observations and theory is, however, not unusual for most emergent IS phenomena, and may arise, for instance, due to: (1) the focus of the theory (e.g., individual-level vs. group-level), (2) the assumptions underlying the theory (e.g., rationality vs. symbolism), and, most importantly, (3) the unique contextual elements (Johns, 2006) associated with an empirical setting (e.g., synchronous vs. asynchronous collaboration, group engaged in individual-level tasks that are additive in nature vs. group-level unitary tasks) that may not have been considered when formulating the theory. While no theory is perfect (Walsham, 1997), different theoretical frames bring to light different aspects of a phenomenon, and we believe that the dramaturgical lens—featuring concepts such as performance, stages, and tactful inattention, which we discuss later—provides a potentially valuable foundation for revealing aspects of interruption processing in a group context that may seem self-evident to some of us but have been thus far overlooked in the literature. This prompted us to undertake an empirical study and fresh theorizing, even though there is existing theory.

Our work recognizes the paucity of intensive examinations of the phenomenon of interest and the incongruence between the relatively scant theoretical literature on the topic and the many real-life instances of smooth, ongoing collaboration despite interruptions, and seeks to offer a midrange, empirically grounded understanding addressing interruptions in synchronous collaborative groups engaged in knowledge work. We reiterate that our focus in this study is on the process through which the recipient of an ICT interruption and their collaborators handle the interruption and the implications this has on the collaboration rather than on the outcomes of the collaboration, such as the quality of the work accomplished, which can depend on factors beyond how the interruption is handled.

To interpretively reveal how the processing of ICT interruptions unfolds in a group engaged in contemporary knowledge work, we view collaboration as dramaturgical performance, a key concept from the symbolic interactionist tradition (Goffman, 1959). As a quick summary, within Goffman's theater metaphor, dramaturgical performance refers to all observable, coordinated activities related to the ongoing collaboration (which, in our study's context, is the joint accomplishment of relevant knowledge work) enacted by a group comprised of relevant dramaturgical actors (i.e., the collaborators). These actors seek to sustain the definition of the situation, which includes not only their motivation and goal for the dramaturgical performance but also their anticipation of and implicit agreement on the appropriate behaviors for each team member to keep the performance going smoothly (Goffman, 1959). Interruptions during the performance tend to challenge the existing definition of the situation. In such circumstances, the recipient and, when appropriate, the other collaborators in the group need to make sense of these interruptions and prevent them from causing a significant rupture in the definition of the situation; this is often accomplished through suitable impression management and tactful inattention (Goffman, 1959). As long as the definition is maintained effectively, the collaboration—or the dramaturgical performance—continues seamlessly. In light of the above discussion, we seek to address the following question: How do participants engaged in collaborative knowledge work deal with ICT interruptions so that their collaboration process does not experience trouble or, even worse, a breakdown?

To this end, we interpretively study multiple ICT interruption-processing episodes (i.e., the period between an interruption's arrival and when it has been dealt with) using Goffman's perspective as a lens to frame the collaboration sessions and interpret how the interruptions were handled by the recipient of the interruption in conjunction with the other group members.

This paper provides a number of potential contributions, most notably, an empirically based theoretical and process understanding of how (1) collaboration among knowledge workers can continue unaffected even when one of them receives ICT interruptions, (2) the collaboration may encounter trouble or move toward a breakdown, and (3) work groups might make themselves less vulnerable to breakdowns due to ICT interruptions. In addition, this paper highlights some concepts fundamental to how groups collaborate that could inform future work in this area. More broadly, this study seeks to contribute to the area at the intersection of ICT interruptions and collaboration, outlining a sociotechnical process enabled by the social ingenuity of the human actors and the parallelism afforded by the ICTs through which interruptions are handled. Our study also suggests some design implications for ICTs enabling collaboration in similar contexts. In the following section, we present our theoretical background and concepts in some detail, which allows us to frame the phenomenon of interest in a novel manner. This is followed by a description of our empirical study and the methodology, the theory development along with corresponding empirical illustrations, and a discussion of the contributions and potential limitations of the study.

## Theoretical Foundation

As noted in the introduction, our theoretical perspective is based on Goffman's (1959) ideas within the broader tradition of symbolic interactionism (Aakhus et al., 2014; Blumer, 1969; O'Brien, 2006). We view collaborative work as a dramaturgical performance by a group of human actors. According to Goffman (1959), the “theater metaphor helps illustrate ‘the structure of social encounter’ that occurs in all social life” (Adams & Sydie, 2002, p. 170). The context of this drama pertains to a “performance team,” defined as “any set of individuals who co-operate in staging a single routine” (Goffman, 1959, p. 79). A core point here is that sustaining such a performance requires the creation and maintenance of a suitable definition of the situation through a working consensus between collaborators (Goffman, 1959). According to Crossman (2019):

The definition of “the situation” is what people use to know what is expected of them and what is expected of others in any given situation ... It is the agreed upon, subjective understanding of what will happen in a given situation or setting, and who will play which roles in the action. The concept refers to how our understanding of the social context of where we may be, like a movie theater, bank, library, or supermarket informs our expectations of what we will do, who we will interact with, and for what purpose. As such, the definition of the situation is a core aspect of social order.

The importance of the definition of the situation is evident in Goffman (1959, p. 212):

I have considered some major forms of performance disruption—unmeant gestures, inopportune intrusions, faux pas, and scenes. These disruptions, in everyday terms, are often called “incidents.” When an incident occurs, the reality sponsored by the performers is threatened. The persons present are likely to react by becoming flustered, ill at ease, embarrassed, nervous, and the like ... When these flustering or symptoms of embarrassment become perceived, the reality that is supported by the performance [the definition of the situation] is likely to be further jeopardized and weakened.

In this connection, Goffman (1959, p. 12) also notes:

When these disruptive events occur, the interaction itself may come to a confused and embarrassed halt.... the participants find themselves lodged in an interaction for which the situation has been wrongly defined and is now no longer defined. At such moments the individual whose presentation has been discredited [due to the disruption] may feel ashamed while the others present may feel hostile, and all the participants may come to feel ill at ease, nonplussed, out of countenance, embarrassed, experiencing the kind of anomie that is generated when the minute social system of .... interaction breaks down.

In summary, the definition of the situation concept refers to the overall symbolic meaning of the collaboration. It is the collaborators' understanding of the essence of their joint activity that holds their collaboration together, and when the definition of the situation is ruptured, the dramaturgical performance (i.e., the collaboration) disintegrates.

Obviously, different types of performance have different demands. For instance, there is less flexibility in performances related to critical, time-bound projects with a rapidly approaching deadline or those related to very consequential activities such as surgery compared to work activities with relatively little time pressure or those that are not as consequential (Goffman, 1959; Mead, 1962). Thus, the challenge in maintaining the definition of the situation can vary based on the nature of performance.

Within symbolic interactionism, action follows meaning that is socially derived and negotiated through interaction with others; such meanings are influenced (though not governed) by norms or pre-expectations that act as a reminder of what previously happened in similar situations (Blumer, 1969; Goffman, 1959; O'Brien, 2006). A symbolic meaning is a sign system that guides an individual's action by conveying messages about how to respond to an event, individual, or thing or how to remember, plan, and foresee actions beyond the immediate, concrete environment (Blumer, 1969; O'Brien, 2006). Based on the symbolic meaning of actions, individuals take each other into account during interaction and collectively fit together the lines of their behavioral patterns to form a definition of the situation (Adams & Sydie, 2002; Blumer, 1969), which then indicates how each participant might contribute to sustaining the definition of the situation (Goffman, 1959; O'Brien, 2011; Park & Burgess, 1921). In the event of an interruption during the collaboration, the interruption recipient may need to deal with the interruption and, in doing so, may threaten the definition of the situation. Consequently, breakdowns in the collaboration can occur in certain circumstances (Park & Burgess, 1921).

While interruptions can have many origins and carriers, as mentioned earlier, we specifically focus on ICT interruptions, which are interruptions delivered through ICTs. ICT interruptions in a group context are particularly interesting, not only because they occur quite frequently in today's information age but also because (1) incoming interruptions and their contents or even their sources may not be visible/audible/noticeable to other collaborators, and (2) the entry of such interruptions tends to be more controllable by participants (i.e., it may be easier to prevent the ICT interruptions from reaching a given recipient that is difficult to achieve in the case of physical interruptions, such as knocking on an office door or waving and shouting a message from outside a conference room).

The dramaturgical framing suggests that an ICT interruption could occur when an actor from the offstage, backstage, or even frontstage (see Figure 1, Table 1) sends a message and/or requests information from an individual who is engaged in the (dramaturgical) performance (i.e., an ongoing collaboration session) through one or more communication attempts (e.g., text messages). Such ICT interruptions enter the attentional space of their recipient and can undermine the definition of the situation. For example, a group member being visibly distracted by an incoming message, especially when other members are expecting their critical input, can endanger the performance.

According to Goffman's dramaturgical theory (1959), a performance unfolds on a metaphorical $frontstage$ , where, within the context of collaborative knowledge work, the workers are engaged in joint action in line with an appropriate definition of the situation (Adams & Sydie, 2002). A key aspect of the frontstage is the use of $impression\ management$ —i.e., expressing the behavioral attributes required for successfully staging one's character—while performing, drawing on the group's working consensus for maintaining the definition of the situation among the participants. $Backstage$ defines a supportive metaphorical space where, according to Goffman (1959), “the performance can be ‘knowingly contradicted,” and a performer “can relax … drop his front, forgo speaking his lines, and step out of character” (Adams & Sydie, 2002, p. 174). Additionally, the backstage is an unofficial channel that is hidden from the public eye where the actors can practice or experiment with upcoming frontstage activity, disagree with some aspect of the ongoing performance on the frontstage, or express dissatisfaction with and even plot to change the definition of a situation. Finally, the outside—or $offstage$ —refers to a metaphorical space that does not have any obvious relationship with the team's frontstage performance. Drawing on Goffman (1959), Figure 1 illustrates the stages in which interruptions originate and are received. Given that Goffman's work predates the extensive use of ICTs, frontstage performance implies face-to-face interactions in this case.

Interruptions often originate from the offstage (Arrow A). To simplify the discussion (and subsequent theorizing) in this paper, we assume that only one dramaturgical actor involved in the frontstage performance is interrupted at a time. The interrupted actor could ignore the interruption or exit the frontstage to deal with it. Alternately, there could be interruptions arising from the initiation of side conversations between, for instance, two frontstage actors, where one of them directs the attention of the other to some unrelated topic (Arrow B). This could be ignored by the interruption recipient, or both actors could withdraw to the backstage to discuss their issues informally. Finally, once on the backstage (temporarily), these dramaturgical actors could interrupt others (Arrow C) involved in the frontstage performance.

A performance can withstand the effects of interruptions to a certain degree because the dramaturgical actors can give each other some latitude for processing them (Goffman, 1959). Defensive strategies are often utilized to safeguard the definition of a situation (Goffman, 1959) and thus support some degree of tolerance to interruptions. Furthermore, an important aspect of the latitude that actors give each other is closely linked to the concept of tactful inattention—the extent of the distraction on the part of the interrupted individual that can be ignored (or temporarily overlooked) by their collaborators (Goffman, 1959). Because collaborating in the real world often does not unfold exactly according to expectations, tactful inattention is a necessity for maintaining the definition of the situation; we believe this is a particularly important insight given that, in modern ICT-enabled environments, social expectations are sometimes not universally shared and are frequently put under strain by interruptions (Ayaß, 2014; Bryant, 2010).

Even though tactful inattention is a working solution for overlooking certain digressions from a collaborator's expected behavior during a performance, it is nevertheless temporary: those engaged in the frontstage performance expect their interrupted collaborator's behavior to return to expected patterns quickly; otherwise, they may become offended, confused, and even stop performing (Goffman, 1959). To avoid such a situation, the interruption recipient needs to signal to their collaborators that they are still very much engaged in the performance. In this regard, the members of the group—especially the interruption recipient—can enact certain defensive strategies, as mentioned above (Goffman, 1959). These strategies are employed in anticipation of or upon receiving an interruption. They include seeking in-group solidarity, inculcating close ties and values (e.g., mutual respect, tolerance, and confidentiality) within the group, the “ability to cope with dramaturgical contingencies as they arise” (Goffman, 1959, p. 216), avoiding gaffes, being tactful, tolerating gaffes within certain limits, choosing appropriate team members, and so on. ## Methodology

We adopt an interpretivist approach (Klein & Myers, 1999; Schwandt, 2006; Walsham, 1995, 2006) to formulate an understanding of the process by which collaborative knowledge workers deal with ICT interruptions within certain boundary conditions.

## Data Collection

Our data comprises 114 hours and 25 minutes of video recordings of authentic, real-life knowledge work collected at six unrelated sites of knowledge work, ranging from business organizations and municipalities to academia.

Video recordings can capture the sequential and temporal organization of social behavior in detail (see Knoblauch & Schnettler, 2012; LeBaron et al., 2017). Such research associated with a given phenomenon, even if sometimes mundane in nature, highlights the importance of everyday actions in producing social outcomes (Aakhus et al., 2014). We chose to use video recording-based observations as the empirical material because we wanted to understand how social outcomes unfold as sequences of interactions in real social life. Video data is rich, capturing the details of the research phenomena as they take place. This contrasts with interviews, which comprise participants' own interpretations and deliberate representations of what happened and why (Hindmarsh & Llewellyn, 2018). Interviews do have a place in research when focusing on aspects of collaboration that are not directly observable, such as the historical or political aspects of technology use. Video recordings, on the other hand, reveal how subjects authentically accomplish their tasks in the very situation they are in, in which they often make ad hoc contextual decisions that may not always be consistent with what they might believe they would do or be expected to do under such circumstances (Hindmarsh & Llewellyn, 2018). As human attention and memory are selective, it is typically difficult for collaboration participants to take note of all the details of their collaborations and technology use while accomplishing their tasks and then recall them in future interviews (Garfinkel, 1967; Hindmarsh & Llewellyn, 2018). Especially when performing mundane group tasks and using familiar technology, subjects tend to overlook the minute details of what is happening and how (Garfinkel, 1967; Hindmarsh & Llewellyn, 2018). Video recordings can capture these details, serving as a permanent, authentic record of the interactions that can be reviewed later.

In our study, the collaboration sessions were captured using either four separate synchronized video cameras (at two sites) or by positioning one camera to capture as much of the work as possible (at four sites). The participants varied on several dimensions including age, culture (a single cultural context or international collaborations), and work profiles (senior vs. junior positions). The participants were captured in varied distributed settings (e.g., at a workplace or visiting the premises of a research organization) with different work objectives and time constraints. However, these all involved synchronous collaboration. Appendix B provides an overview of the site details, illustrating the variations in the teamwork contexts, tasks, types of teams, and types of ICTs involved. Capturing these variations is potentially valuable in that all these characteristics can affect the ways in which individuals deal with ICT interruptions (Wang et al., 2013); additionally, it can help increase the generalizability of the results.

## The Data Analysis Process

We note that data analysis was undertaken in two logical steps. The first step involved immersing ourselves in the data and identifying key themes and empirical patterns suggested by the data. This step was conducted primarily by the first author and last author. We started by screening all the videos to identify ICT interruption occurrences. Next, we sampled the occurrences, tagging their various origins, settings, and influences on the collaborating processes until we were convinced we had covered the various contextual circumstances associated with the phenomenon of interest. This step resulted in the identification of 183 ICT interruption instances (occurring in over 809 minutes of video), which constituted the sample set for our observation and analysis of interruption processing in microlevel detail.

Our approach to observation and analysis in this study was informed by key ideas from applied ethnomethodology/conversation analysis (EM/CA), where “ethnomethodology” and “conversation analysis” refer to the “schools of sociology which focus on the mechanisms by which people use commonsense knowledge in structuring their day-to-day encounters to construct shared meanings and social order from their conversations and interactions” (Payne & Payne, 2011, p.76). Accordingly, we used methods from EM/CA multimodal visual-auditory analysis, which are widely used by the EM/CA tradition (Jewitt, 2014; Sidnell, 2007; Sidnell & Stivers, 2013).

To manage the data and synchronize the videos taken by the several cameras, we used ELAN video annotation software (Brugman & Russel, 2004). The video recordings and associated annotations allowed us to revisit and repeatedly observe the microlevel details of each instance of interruption processing as they took place in real life (see Mondada, 2008). We iteratively observed and analyzed these instances until no additional patterns or concepts emerged. Saturation was reached after examining 70 ICT interruption processing episodes.

To aid in the analysis, we also used a series of screenshots to document the collaboration conditions prior to the ICT interruption and identify the transitional moments in the interruption processing, which are defined as moments of change in the interaction flow (e.g., a knowledge worker noticing an ICT interruption) (Langley, 1999; Schegloff, 1996, 2007).

After identifying, tagging, and describing the transitional moments, we analyzed their specific details, transcribing the content of each selected video episode at a resolution of 0.1 seconds (Heath et al., 2010; Mondada, 2008) per the transcription guidance of Atkinson and Heritage (1989). Here, we investigated the speech of each knowledge worker involved, specifically examining what they said and how as well as their accompanying body language and gestures. In addition, we documented the knowledge workers' key interactions with ICTs when these appeared to play a prominent role in the group interaction.

While the first step (described above) was predominantly data-centric, the second step, as is characteristic of many interpretive studies, involved creative “mental leaps” (e.g., Van de Ven, 2013) and an interpretation characterized as imaginative (Sarker et al., 2018). The focus was on developing a plausible interpretation based on the interaction between the empirical material that we fractured/coded and the sensitizing concepts of dramaturgy. This is consistent with the approach suggested by scholars associated with the symbolic interactionist tradition. Specifically, Schwandt (2006, p. 12, emphasis added) notes that “the process of actors’ interpretation is rendered intelligible not merely through description of word and deed, but by taking that rich description as a point of departure for formulating an interpretation of what the actors are up to.”

Our data provided a gateway through which to encounter the instances of interruption processing. This allowed us to use our scientific imagination to interpret the subjective experiences of the participants we observed, informed by symbolic interactionism. We developed microlevel impressions of the episodes addressed; based on these, we formulated our interpretation via an iterative process (Agar, 1985; Klein & Myers, 1999). We repeatedly examined the videos and transcripts that captured verbal utterances related to, for instance, social acceptance, offense, disapproval, indifference, or rejection while also paying attention to nonverbal gestures, such as nods, frowns, and the gaze of the collaborators. Identifying these signs and responses was important for several reasons: First, they are used by humans to communicate their symbolic meanings and organize their actions accordingly (Goffman, 1959; Levinson, 2006; Sacks, 1995). Second, humans also use these cues to reveal whether they are aware of what the others in their group are doing, whether the situation is progressing as expected, and whether they are tolerating and approving of each other's activities (Levinson, 2006; Sacks, 1995). In our study, this information revealed the state of tactful inattention; the dramaturgical stages, breakdowns, repairs, the presence of any trouble; and the collaborator's comfort in handling interruptions (Goffman, 1959). We paid close attention to both the video and audio components of the recordings to formulate and validate the interpretations based on both the participants' spoken accounts as well as their nonverbal behavior. Multiple interpretations of the authors were considered (e.g., Klein & Myers, 1999), actively debated, and reconciled when possible, and, true to the ontological/epistemological assumptions of the adopted approach, no notion of interrater reliability was used. Furthermore, to increase the credibility of our interpretations, we subjected our interpretations to the data-session evaluation and critique protocol widely used in EM/CA (e.g., Have, 2007; Jordan & Henderson, 1995). The participants included researchers familiar with EM/CA who were not part of our research project. ## Findings, Theory Development, and Illustrations

Before moving forward, it will be helpful to highlight some of the boundary conditions (based on our own assumptions and the nature of our empirical material) of the study. They include the following:

First, many different types of interruptions exist, but we only considered ICT-delivered interruptions. We did not consider other types of interruptions, such as physical interruptions (e.g., a visitor showing up and knocking on the door or a light bulb blowing out) or self-interruptions (e.g., taking a break). Additionally, we also considered human-initiated, ICT-delivered interruptions.

Second, the interruptions we studied were received by one individual in a group. While the ideas presented could be adapted to a small proportion of group members who are simultaneously interrupted, interruptions specifically received by the entire group would need to be studied separately. Third, the collaborative groups we studied were involved in knowledge work, which, as we discerned, possesses some specific characteristics that are markedly different from other types of tasks. Different types of tasks undertaken by such groups would likely change the results and implications of our work.

Fourth, we acknowledge that different team members involved in collaboration may be engaging in different levels of collaboration-relevant activity based on their roles when the interruption is received. If the individual's role involves a certain type of performance (e.g., making a live presentation), their capacity to attend to an interruption on the side that collaborators would tactfully ignore may vary accordingly. Finally, our cases involved collaborative knowledge work in synchronous virtual, hybrid, or face-to-face meetings.

We recall that the framing of collaboration using Goffman's (1959) ideas features the following: The collaborators are actors engaged in a collaborative performance on a frontstage, which could be face-to-face, distributed, or a combination of the two. The performance involves joint action on the part of the individual actors and is held together by the definition of the situation. If an interruption triggers action “that defies the definition of the situation” (Crossman, 2019), the performance can run into trouble or even hurtle toward a breakdown. Next, in the following section, we discuss three concepts—augmented multi-frontstage performance (AMFP), partial focal frontstage performance (PFFP), and the tactful inattention period (TIP)—as well as some supporting ideas that emerged through our interpretation. Thereafter, we use empirical material (the cartoons) to illustrate how these concepts helped us understand the process by which the interruptions are dealt with.

## AMFP, PFFP, and TIP: The Key Emergent Concepts

Based on our interpretation of the instances of collaboration processes in our data, we formulated three concepts. To start with, we devised the notion of augmented multi-frontstage performance (AMFP) as a way of performing (in a dramaturgical sense) enabled by the parallelism of ICTs that makes it possible for the collaborators to perform (i.e., engage in collaboration) on more than one frontstage simultaneously. We observed that this allows the interruption recipient to seamlessly deal with interruptions during the collaboration. Figure 3 introduces the idea of AMFP. For example, one of the participants on the focal frontstage (A1) is shown participating in three additional stages (i.e., four parallel stages) and another (A2) is shown in two parallel stages.

In contemporary knowledge work, ICT interruptions can originate from any of the dramaturgical stages. First, ICT interruptions can arise from and impact the focal/original frontstage (Arrow A) (e.g., when a colleague taking part in the frontstage performance texts about a task-relevant matter). ICT interruptions can also arise from the backstage and impact the focal frontstage (Arrow B) (e.g., when a colleague sends a text message commenting on something unrelated to the performance of collaborative work, such as dinner plans). In addition, ICT interruptions can arise from the offstage and impact the focal frontstage (Arrow C) (e.g., in the form of a request for information from a collaborator working on a different project or from a family member inquiring about intended vacation planning or someone sending junk mail).

We found that knowledge workers can process ICT interruptions by forming parallel frontstages and seamlessly performing on them in addition to performing on the focal (original) frontstage. This is made possible by the properties of ICTs that allow for shared space and parallel interactions and enable the formation and maintenance of multiple frontstage performances, thereby augmenting the original performance; this is where the term AMFP (augmented multi-frontstage performance) derives its name from. In AMFP, such parallel performances are managed so that the focal actor's performance on each parallel frontstage is found acceptable by collaborators on the respective frontstages as per socially constructed standards (including the tactful inattention period [TIP], defined and discussed below). Further, AMFP requires the focal collaborator to be simultaneously aware of each stage and handle their respective performances on each stage via impression management to ensure that the actors on the other stages are unaware of the effects of the received interruption—at least not to the extent that it disturbs the collaboration. For AMFP to remain viable, impression management needs to be effective, and the focal collaborator should not commit major gaffes, as this would make tactful inattention on the part of the other collaborators difficult. Most importantly, the focal actor (i.e., the actor who receives the interruption) needs to ensure that their contribution to the performance is not missed on the frontstage beyond a certain time period (TIP, discussed as the third concept below).

The second concept that emerged during our analysis was partial focal frontstage performance (PFFP), which, as developed from our observations, refers to situations in which the performance on the focal frontstage (i.e., the frontstage with the original team's original collaboration) proceeded despite the focal actor (i.e., the actor performing on the original frontstage who received the interruption) being temporarily absent from the focal frontstage performance. PFFP captures the situations where, despite the temporary absence of the interrupted actor from the original (focal) frontstage, the performance on the focal frontstage continues, even if imperfectly (hence, the word “partial” in PFFP). While such an exit of the focal actor from the focal frontstage may result in a minor and temporary impact on the group, the collaboration process is not materially affected as long as the exit duration remains within TIP. In other words, the absence of the focal actor from the ongoing performance on the frontstage can be temporarily tolerated or excused by the collaborating actors.

The third emergent concept, the tactful inattention period (TIP) is inspired by Goffman's notion of tactful inattention in light of patterns in the data that highlighted the role of time. We define TIP as the maximum socially constructed time of tactful inattention allowed for someone (the focal actor) not playing their role in the performance on the frontstage—from the point of view of collaborators on the same frontstage. TIP affords an interrupted focal actor a limited amount of time to return to their frontstage interaction without damaging the definition of the situation (Goffman, 1959). We do not conceptualize TIP in terms of an objective clock time (for different conceptualizations of time, see Ancona et al., 2001; Blumer, 1969; Venter et al., 2014); rather, our constructivist conception of TIP may be characterized as the amount of time the focal actor can be absent from the frontstage before the definition of the situation becomes unlikely to be maintained. We emphasize that TIP typically represents an implicit agreement among the collaborators for a given collaboration context. Accordingly, TIP may be understood as the focal actor's awareness of the socially acceptable period available for their ICT interruption processing. Such a period of time is seen as limited, and there is a possibility of the focal actor exceeding TIP in attending to the interruption received. We must note that TIP is not a constant but can vary dynamically depending on the task demands, situation, participating actors, functions of ICT, and the nature of the interruptions. For example, in time-critical, protocol-based collaborations, TIP “allocated” to an individual may be practically nonexistent; however, in most cases, collaborators do allow for some TIP. Furthermore, sometimes, renegotiation of TIP is possible. In the context of the collaboration process, TIP for an individual may be negotiated before the start of the performance or emerge during the collaboration through appropriate impression management, courtesy, and other defensive strategies.

In summary, upon receiving an ICT interruption, the focal actor in a collaboration can (1) ignore it, (2) exit the frontstage to deal with it on the backstage or offstage, and return within an acceptable period of time (while a partial performance continues on the focal or original frontstage [i.e., PFFP]), or, more frequently, (3) seek to deal with the interruption, which could necessitate temporarily becoming a part of another performance in parallel to the original performance (AMFP). TIP serves as an important constraint for both PFFP and AMFP. We will next present more specific empirical examples of how knowledge workers use PFFP and AMFP to deal with ICT interruptions while maintaining their collaboration.

## Using Defensive Strategies to Block Interruptions

To ensure that interruptions do not seriously affect the definition of the situation, collaborative groups may use a variety of defensive strategies that include encouraging the development of close ties and values among each other (e.g., mutual respect, tolerance, and confidentiality) and exercising prudence and foresight in collaboration (Goffman, 1959). In many cases, knowledge workers are aware beforehand that they may receive ICT interruptions sometime during the collaboration. In such cases, they may choose to inform the collaborators about this possibility, who then may agree to overlook the ICT interruption and its effects on the collaboration to the extent possible. If the interruption is interpreted as being reasonable by the collaborators, this could result in a greater allowed TIP for the actor who needs to exit the frontstage to deal with the interruption. Obviously, such negotiations tend to be more effective when defensive strategies, such as the development of close ties, are implemented.

While the defensive strategies discussed above are suggested in the literature, we must acknowledge that many were not directly found in our empirical material. This is understandable because many of the defensive strategies were implemented before the collaboration (for which we have a video record) started. A defensive strategy that was apparent in our cases was that the knowledge workers prepared for interruptions by adjusting/closing the stage doors (see Goffman, 1959). Examples of closing doors or keeping them ajar (to various extents) that are particularly relevant to ICTs include adjusting the volume and vibrational intensity of one's ICTs (e.g., mobile device), deciding on which social media applications to keep in the foreground of one's view (e.g., on a personal computer or laptop), and presetting the spam filters and pop-ups related to alerting the user about an email or update (e.g., on personal tablets). In our data, we observed keeping the doors ajar the most frequently (e.g., hearing a vibration alarm from incoming messages but no actual sound that would be evident to other collaborators). Indeed, when there was serious time pressure on the frontstage, when a participant's role (e.g., chair, presenter) in the collaboration demanded it, or when high-profile actors were involved in the collaboration, the "doors" to the performance were shut to prevent ICT interruptions from intruding (e.g., participants were asked to shut down their personal applications, email, and smartphone during a meeting.)

## Actualizing and Maintaining the AMFP

Instead of necessarily “closing the doors” to all interruptions, the participants in the collaboration often acknowledged and interpreted the ICT interruption as encountered and decided whether any action was needed or, alternatively, whether the interruption could be ignored for the moment or altogether. Assuming that the ICT interruption needed to be dealt with, the focal collaborator invoked AMFP, with one or more additional stages becoming simultaneously relevant as parallel frontstage(s) in addition to the focal (or original) frontstage. In our cases, the focal actor appeared natural in interacting in the resulting work environment, juggling the multiple frontstages of AMFP through the use of impression management as necessary. In maintaining AMFP, the focal actor sought to give an impression of being engaged with all of the parallel frontstages. Such impression management played a key role in sustaining the performance on each of the parallel frontstages. In addition, TIP “allocated” to the focal actor by the collaborators for each of the frontstages on which the focal actor was performing started counting down as soon as the focal actor’s absence from the performance was felt by the other collaborators. To maintain AMFP, the performers monitored each of the frontstages on which they were performing, implicitly prioritizing and timing their participation. Of course, we acknowledge the fact that text-based media (e.g., text messaging, emailing) is more capable of facilitating the deployment of AMFP than voice or video, because it can be more demanding for a given actor to interact synchronously on multiple parallel frontstages using video or audio media, especially when an active performance is required.

## Breakdown and Repair of the AMFP

The path to the breakdown of the performance on multiple frontstages involves the following: a focal collaborator attending to an ICT interruption (on a parallel frontstage) for a prolonged period of time and not paying appropriate attention to the focal frontstage or not effectively managing impressions for their collaborators on the focal frontstage. In such situations, the growing dissonance among the collaborators on the focal frontstage can begin to affect the AMFP, compromising the focal frontstage performance. The situation then moves to the state of PFFP (i.e., partial frontstage focal performance) because the focal actor is, in effect, missing from the original (focal) frontstage. Some collaborators from the compromised focal frontstage may become irritated and may even create a scene, while some may begin to lose focus. As the collaborators' definition of the situation becomes increasingly blurred, the focal performance starts to disintegrate, and participants on the focal frontstage grow progressively uncertain about what they are trying to achieve together and whether it is even possible with the increasingly distracted, uncommitted, and/or free-riding participants. Exceeding TIP at this point can be fatal to the collaboration. If the interruption processing by the focal collaborator continues as TIP nears its limits, especially without the focal collaborator attempting to renegotiate TIP and repair the performance through a credible apology or a clear signal that the behavior will not be repeated, the shared working consensus among the collaborators in the focal frontstage will dissolve, their definition of the situation will be lost, and the collaboration will break down. However, because the collaborators tended to effectively manage their performances on multiple stages using impression management and other strategies, we observed that such breakdowns rarely occur. When near-breakdowns did arise, they were often repaired through appropriate defensive strategies, such as apologizing or explaining the urgency of attending to the ICT interruption. This led to a renegotiation of TIP, an assurance of performing in the form of PFFP for a bit longer, or a reinstatement of AMFP.

## A Process Model for Handling ICT Interruptions During Collaboration

In the previous sections, we offer three concepts (AMFP, PFFP, and TIP) and an interpretation—based on Goffman's dramaturgical theory—of how ICT interruptions are processed in groups, subject to certain boundary conditions (listed in the Findings, Theory Development, and Illustrations section). In order to provide a more concrete and integrative form of abstraction, in this section, we present an interpretively developed process model that highlights how knowledge workers appear to deal with ICT interruptions during ongoing collaboration. Our model (see Figure 6 for an overview) specifically presents a conceptualization of ICT interruption processing based on the interaction of our empirical material and the theoretical ideas in Goffman's work (1959), with the three main parts of the process model (screening, interruption processing, and handling trouble, breakdown, and repair) represented in the boxes with dashed lines below.

Interruptions can be handled broadly along three alternative process paths. Dealing with an interruption may involve these paths, either exclusively or in any combination. First, an interruption can be processed through screening only. The second path involves screening followed by interruption processing in the form of AMFP or PFFP. The third path entails screening and then interruption processing through PFFP, followed by the management of trouble, breakdown, and repair.

Figure 7 shows the entire process of managing ICT interruptions in detail, as a series of performance states (represented by rectangles), acts of the collaborators (arrows with circled text), changes in the collaboration environment (arrows with plain text), important decision points (diamonds), external sources or interruption sinks (circles), and the end of the process (grey oval). Of course, given the interpretivist stance underlying our work, the model is not intended to be deterministic (Walsham, 1995). Indeed, similar box and arrow diagrams have been used in other well-regarded interpretive studies (e.g. Levina & Vaast, 2008). Readers not interested in the details of the process may skip the rest of this section and move on to the Discussion and Conclusions section.

First, we provide an overview of the diagram, with the three main parts of the process model represented with dashed, colored lines. The first part deals with how entering interruptions (shown as the process element A) may be screened before processing (B, D, E, and F are the key process elements involved). The second part is related to processing the interruption (G, H, I, J, K, and M are the key process elements involved, although there are different path options, some of which we discuss below). Finally, the third part concerns how the performance can encounter trouble and experience breakdowns or how the situation is repaired (I', L, N, and O are the primary process elements).

At the heart of the process model are four states (rectangles labeled D, H, K, and O) that represent the different circumstances during the collaborative performance (also referred to simply as “performance”). K (partial focal frontstage performance, or PFFP) represents the state where the focal actor has temporarily become unavailable with respect to playing their part on the focal frontstage, possibly because they have become distracted in attending to an interruption. In this state, the performance is in potential jeopardy but continues on the focal frontstage, supported by the rest of the actors as if nothing has happened—at least for the moment. H (augmented multi-frontstage performance, or AMFP) represents the state where the performance proceeds smoothly (the interrupted focal actor is actually performing on multiple frontstages simultaneously) without collaborators on any of the frontstages feeling a sense of discontinuity or disruption in the collaboration. Finally, O (broken down focal frontstage performance, BDFFP) represents the state where the definition of the situation has dissolved and, hence, the collaborative performance on the focal (or original) frontstage breaks down, with one or more actors exiting the collaboration or losing interest. We now describe Figure 7 in more detail.

The input to the screening part of the process, denoted by A, is “entering ICT interruptions,” which represent events that can potentially disrupt the performance. Based on our cases, we assume that interruptions arrive from offstage. C represents ICT interruptions that are blocked by “closed doors” (B) (e.g., phone turned off, closed email reader, or disabled notifications), with only ICT interruptions that are not blocked entering the frontstage on which the performance is happening and appearing in the attentional space of the focal collaborator.

Once the interruption has been received, several things can happen. First, if the performance is completed (E), there is no need for the focal collaborator (FC in the model) to deal with the interruption (or it is beyond the scope of our model), and the process terminates (P). Naturally, a transition to P can also result if the performance is completed, regardless of whether an interruption has been received; otherwise, the D→E→F→D loop ensures that the performance continues, provided that there is no interruption that needs to be handled. Now, even if an interruption is received, the focal collaborator may choose to ignore it (F→D); otherwise, the interruption needs to be handled, which leads to the process transitioning from the screening to the interruption processing phase.

Essentially, in the interruption processing phase, there are two options in terms of the states: H or K. If the focal collaborator needs to attend to the interruption by exiting the frontstage (i.e., not performing their part during the original performance in order to attend to the interruption), the process enters the PFFP state (K). On the other hand, the focal collaborator who receives the interruption may continue parallel performances (H, the AMFP state, as explained earlier) with the original actors as well as the actor(s) who is (are) the source(s) of the interruption(s). The AMFP (H) would continue until this multistage becomes irrelevant—i.e., the interruption is seamlessly dealt with, as long as the AMFP continues to be compatible with TIP and does not start to dissolve (H→I→J→H). When the interruption processing is completed, the AMFP becomes irrelevant and the original performance continues (H→I→D). We note that the process could also exit from State H if the AMFP starts dissolving (even though it would still be relevant) when, for instance, the focal collaborator fails to manage their impression of being engaged; the process then may be seen as transitioning to the PFFP state (H→I→J→K), since the focal actor is “missing” from the performance.

The remainder of the diagram refers to the circumstances that may lead to trouble or even a breakdown of the collaboration and the ways of handling such situations. Here, the actor who is absent from the focal frontstage performance (and is thus responsible for the partial nature of the performance on the focal frontstage, or PFFP), has exceeded TIP (L). Now, unless there is an immediate repair of the situation and/or renegotiation of TIP, the collaboration experiences breakdown (K→L→N→O). On the other hand, if effective repair and/or renegotiation of TIP occurs, and if AMFP is not relevant (I') and the interruption is not yet resolved, partial performance may continue for an additional renegotiated TIP (K→L→N→I'→K). If AMFP is relevant (I') after effective repair and/or renegotiation of TIP (N), the focal collaborator continues with the reinstated AMFP (K→L→N→I'→H). Assuming that TIP is not exceeded and the interruption is resolved, the process transitions back to the screening phase, and the original performance on the focal frontstage can continue (K→L→M→D). For the sake of completion of the diagram, the last arrow, a dashed arrow O→P, denotes that the state of breakdown at some point becomes irrelevant, and the process terminates.

We reiterate that the process model resulting from our interpretive study should be viewed as our attempt to capture and abstract plausible tendencies rather than as a deterministic model (Walsham, 1995). It provides a detailed, empirically based, and theoretically informed account of how ICT interruptions can be handled effectively (or not) by collaborative groups, subject to the boundary conditions mentioned earlier. We would also like to note that, while one could think of many conditions that could potentially lead to variations in the process (e.g., frequency of interruptions, culture or power differences among members, nature of task), we do not believe that these contingencies would fundamentally change the structure of our model. Interruption processing follows the paths shown, during which the collaborators do their best to maintain the definition of the situation using tactful inattention and appropriate defensive strategies.

## Discussion and Contributions

Contemporary knowledge work often requires groups relying on ICTs to collaborate across digital, physical, and hybrid work environments. Such groups frequently encounter ICT interruptions. Past literature has mostly suggested that ICT interruptions distract groups and affect their collaboration process; however, to our surprise, in our study of groups collaborating on knowledge work-related tasks, we found this to not necessarily be the case. Consequently, we set out to theorize about how interruptions are handled in groups.

We formulate a midrange “Type II theory” (“a theory for understanding” that “explains primarily how and why some phenomena occur”) (Gregor, 2006, p. 624) in the form of the process model displayed in Figure 7 that was described in the previous section. This model is informed by the Goffmanian tradition and frames our phenomenon of interest (group collaboration) as dramaturgical performance (Goffman, 1959). This framing enabled us to achieve a potentially novel understanding of ICT interruption processing as both markedly different from yet complementary to the dominant view on the impacts of interruptions in the current IS literature, in which ICT interruptions are frequently seen as distracting from the primary task (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015; Jenkins et al., 2016).

Further, traditional coordination theory, which typically serves as the foundation for research at the group level, has been found to be somewhat limited in explaining how interruptions are handled by collaborative hybrid groups engaged in knowledge work. Informed by Goffman's ideas, we came to observe that knowledge workers in collaborative groups cope with the demands of ICT interruptions by constructing shared intersubjective meanings related to the symbolic communication among their collaborators and employ social ingenuity through the use of technological capabilities to negate the effect of the interruption on the group.

Given that it is seldom possible to isolate oneself from the offstage (i.e., the outside world) during a given collaboration in the wired world in which we live, collaborators who receive ICT interruptions seamlessly initiate multiple frontstages and perform on them, subject to constraints such as TIP (i.e., tactful inattention period). They also use effective impression management while avoiding gaffes and implementing a variety of defensive strategies. This core idea is captured in the notion of AMFP (i.e., augmented multi-frontstage performance), the state of seamlessly performing on multiple stages. In situations where the focal collaborator cannot be simultaneously present on multiple frontstages, TIP, which is potentially (re)negotiable, provides a buffer for the focal collaborator's absence from the focal frontstage before trouble appears; this state is referred to as PFFP (i.e., partial focal frontstage performance). Furthermore, our findings show that the use of ICTs fundamentally alters some important aspects of dramaturgical performance. For example, instead of the actors necessarily prioritizing the focal frontstage over other stages (Goffman, 1959), they can be involved in multiple frontstage performances.

We should note that the notion of AMFP and how it is initiated and maintained emerged from the interaction of Goffman's (1959) concepts and the empirical material we examined; however, AMFP does have some similarities with concepts such as multitasking and multicommunication. Stephens and Davis (2009, p. 65), for example, assert that “multitasking is typically conceptualized as performing two or more tasks at the same time.” Strictly speaking, however, the ability to perform tasks in parallel or simultaneously is considered rare. This is possible only with automated activities, those that are routine, or those that require a significant degree of practice, such as playing different piano keys with the left and right hands, talking to a passenger while driving a car (Salvucci et al., 2009; Wickens et al., 2015), or writing an email with a podcast playing in the background (Leroy et al., 2020). However, as we will explain below, AMFP is not the same as multitasking.

Traditionally, an interruption has been seen as a trigger to pause one task and engage in another (Adler & Benbunan-Fich, 2012; González & Mark, 2004; Paul et al., 2015). Rather than doing this in response to an interruption (which would cause a break in the flow of the group collaboration on the focal frontstage) or performing two tasks (the original task and attending to the interruption) in parallel (which would be difficult to achieve even in routinized tasks and next to impossible in complex tasks such as knowledge work collaboration), the knowledge worker can use AMFP.

The idea of AMFP involves incorporating the interruption into the collaboration as follows: (1) by creating and maintaining performances on multiple frontstages (i.e., the original or focal frontstage and the frontstage(s) associated with the interruption[s]) (2) by maintaining awareness of what is happening on all the stages, and (3) by tending to each stage to perform one's corresponding role while also (4) giving the impression that the interruption and how it is handled are indeed part of the expected course of the overall performance and the associated definition of the situation. It is clear that impression management plays an important role in ensuring that collaborators on the focal frontstage as well as on all other parallel frontstages are assured that the focal collaborator (the recipient of the interruption) is continuing to play their part as per the definition of the situation on each frontstage. While (2) above appears to be conceptually similar to “continuous partial attention,” which is considered “distinct from multitasking” (Watts, 2007), (3) appears to be related to “task interleaving” or “task rotation,” pointing to a sequence of activities rather than a parallelism (Leroy et al., 2020). Yet, according to Leroy et al. (2020):

The dividing line [between multitasking and rotation] ... can be somewhat murky depending on how one defines “simultaneous” ... As the time span of interest grows smaller, the distinction ... can become less and less clear. (p. 676)

We found that multitasking conceptually overlaps only partly with (3) and does not include the idea of (4). Overall, the notion of AMFP is more complex and nuanced and may be considered a theoretical contribution to ICT-enabled communication and collaboration.

Revisiting the literature on interruptions, we see that with few exceptions, past studies have focused on individuals (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015, 2018a; Chen & Karahanna, 2018; Dabbish & Kraut, 2008), with scant attention paid to effects on group collaboration. Specifically, much of the past research, as mentioned in the Introduction, points to individuals' cognitive limitations in dealing with ICT interruptions such as attention allocation (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015), action regulation (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018a), resource conservation (Chen & Karahanna, 2018), dual-task inference (Jenkins et al., 2016), perceived task complexity (Li et al., 2011), cognitive aging (Tams et al., 2018), demand for control (Tams et al., 2020), and so on. While these cognitive effects are undeniable for the individual recipient of an interruption, it is not clear how the individual-level effects of interruption affect the collaboration of the group the individual may be part of. In this area, by far the most comprehensive and significant scholarly work theorizing on how the effects of individual-level interruptions might influence the group and its performance is by Addas and Pinsonneault (2018b). While these authors discuss several broad mechanisms by which the effects of interruptions on individuals may be transferred to the group, our study focuses on how an interrupted individual can, through the tactful inattention of collaborators and negotiations with them, prevent the interference of the interruption in the collaboration process. Overall, previous theory has been formulated by making certain assumptions about the phenomenon (how individual effects move to the group level), the context, the nature of the interruption, and the theoretical foundation. Some of these assumptions are theoretical (e.g., coordination theory vs. dramaturgy), which are alternate frames with which to view the phenomenon, and others pertain to the specific contexts studied (e.g., face-to-face vs. hybrid collaboration), which may not necessarily have been taken into consideration by prior theorists.

What we offer to the literature is a process-based account of how collaborative groups in synchronous hybrid contexts can continue to collaborate seamlessly (i.e., continue their “performance” in a dramaturgical sense) through the use of certain mechanisms such as AMFP and PFFP. The collaborative groups we studied were involved in knowledge work, which, as we discerned, has some specific characteristics. Different types of tasks undertaken by these groups could change the results and implications of our work. Specifically, in our interruption episodes, we found that the tasks had certain features that could be described using some of Steiner’s (1972) categories. Specifically, we found that the tasks were typically disjunctive (i.e., they required group members to formulate a single solution for the entire group, such as a design or project proposal). They were not additive (i.e., the final solution was not a linear sum of individual contributions of group members). We also found contributions to be discretionary (i.e., group members could often determine exactly when and how they would contribute and/or how individual contributions needed to be combined). The process model we offer contributes to an alternate theoretical understanding of how individual-level effects may or may not transfer to the group level, going beyond mechanisms discussed in the previous literature, such as aggregation, emotional contagion, and social loafing (Sarker & Valacich, 2010; Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018b).

Another point worthy of mention is that the findings in the literature regarding the impacts of interruptions have been mixed, whether at the individual or group level. Some studies have found the effects of ICT interruptions on performance to be both positive and negative (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015, 2018b, 2018a; Chen & Karahanna, 2018), while others have found only negative performance effects (Dabbish & Kraut, 2008; Galluch et al., 2015; Jenkins et al., 2016; Tams et al., 2018). In addition, some studies point to stress (Galluch et al., 2015; Tams et al., 2018, 2020), the individual's polychronic orientation (multitasking preference) (Li et al., 2011), or the importance of communication timing (Dabbish & Kraut, 2008) in terms of its influence on the magnitude of the effects of interruptions. However, few studies elaborate on why and how such positive or negative outcomes result. Addas and Pinsonneault (2018b), for example, suggest that the individual constraints resulting from an interruption get transferred to the group through mechanisms such as social contagion. In turn, the effect of group constraints (i.e., cognitive limitations and performance inhibitors such as delays and errors arising from the rippling up of the individual-level effects of interruptions) on the group's outcomes, such as task performance, is dependent on the two coordination mechanisms of role switching and temporal coordination. We note that our focus is not on the effect of interruptions on the group's (task) outcomes, which we believe could depend on many different conditions beyond the interruption and how it is handled. Nevertheless, our work, which outlines the processes by which collaboration occurs seamlessly, runs into trouble, or breaks down, potentially offers a clue for understanding how group outcomes result as a consequence of interruptions, since having a smooth or problematic collaboration process is likely to have some relationship with the task outcomes of the group. This represents another contribution.

As mentioned earlier, our work is more contextually bound than that of Addas and Pinsonneault (2018b). We studied groups engaged in knowledge work-related tasks that were unitary/nonadditive and discretionary in nature. This contrasts with the assumptions about the characteristics of tasks made by the aforementioned authors, such as a high task interdependence with the need for specialized expertise (and hence the tasks being potentially similar to additive tasks) and explicit coordination. Thus, the two theories offer complementary perspectives on the phenomenon, which are both valuable in different contexts. All in all, we feel that the work of Addas and Pinsonneault (2018b) is foundational and offers a comprehensive theoretical view, while our work provides a valuable addition to the knowledge on the phenomenon in addition to empirical illumination.

We reiterate that an abstract theory and how situations unfold in practice are often not consistent due to the assumptions underlying the theory development and the different contexts within which a phenomenon unfolds. Our work did not seek to validate or invalidate the Addas and Pinsonneault (2018b) model; indeed, our theoretical foundations are different and thus led to new concepts such as PFFP and AMFP. In addition, our empirical context is that of knowledge work in synchronous collaborative settings and our interest is specifically in the process by which interruptions are handled by group members—sometimes effectively and sometimes ineffectively. Our interpretation of the process reveals that unlike Addas and Pinsonneault's conception (2018b) of the effect on the individual rippling up to the group level in a one-way manner that could affect the group's outcomes, the dramaturgical performance of the individual is relational and mutually constitutive of the group's dramaturgical performance; thus, the effects of the interruption on the group are influenced by how the performance incorporates the handling and management of the interruption through the use of different sociotechnical mechanisms by actors in the group.

Furthermore, neither the notion of TIP, derived from Goffman's concept of tactful inattention, nor its importance is recognized in previous multicommunication, multitasking, interruption, or group collaboration literature. We believe this is a potentially valuable concept not only for the study of interruptions in groups but also for the study of group collaboration in general. Specifically, we observed that, unless TIP is exceeded (or comes close to being exceeded), one collaborator attending to an ICT interruption does not generally appear to affect the group's collaboration process, nor do members display discernible behavioral signs of stress or distraction. This idea has not been suggested in previous individual-level studies (see Galluch et al., 2015; Tams et al., 2018, 2020). In this way, dealing with ICT interruptions like AMFP appears to neither necessarily cause deviations from the group's intended way of working, as suggested earlier (see Dabbish & Kraut, 2008; Ren et al., 2008), nor does it lead to two tasks (the original task and giving attention to the interruption) interfering with each other (see Heninger et al., 2006; Jenkins et al., 2016, for examples). Also, in contrast to the studies in which knowledge workers resort to allocating attention to one task at a time (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015; Galluch et al., 2015; Li et al., 2011; Roda & Thomas, 2006), in our study, the collaborators using AMFP appear to easily continue contributing to their multiple performances. Even when a collaborator must step out of the collaboration momentarily (i.e., exit the frontstage), the performance can continue smoothly (PFFP) as long as the interrupted member returns within TIP. All of the above, we believe, represent significant contributions to the knowledge on this topic.

Finally, the routine behavioral responses to interruptions we discerned in our study signify a fundamental change in the symbolic meaning collaborators associate with ICT interruptions (Aakhus et al., 2014). Specifically, within TIP, knowledge workers in collaborative groups associate ICT interruptions with a symbolic meaning that prompts the initiation of AMFP as the default option for proceeding when dealing with an ICT interruption. These findings help us understand how and to what extent “homo connectus” (Aakhus et al., 2014) can cope with their self-made technological environment—the relentless demands of which are always present (Aakhus et al., 2014; Faraj et al., 2011)—by incorporating these demands into a broader definition of the situation and giving an impression that all is well (Goffman, 1959).

There are a number of practical implications, in terms of awareness, operational procedures, and ideas for the design of ICTs, that emerge from this work. First, the members in a group engaged in collaboration must all acknowledge the fact that ICT interruptions are an inescapable part of contemporary work; thus, the group must consciously prepare for and develop some guidelines for dealing with them (e.g., the defensive strategies discussed in this paper).

To start with, to have some control over the nature and timing of interruptions entering the performance, it is important for team members to exercise prudence and foresight in planning prior to the start of the collaboration. This entails screening out or blocking some interruptions and allowing only specific types to enter the performance. This prudence and foresight apply to all kinds of interruptions—for example, messages and notifications can be handled by adjusting the relevant technology filters and controls. Potential technology malfunctions and updates, in turn, can be controlled by setting up some alternative collaboration channels prior to the start of the work, allowing the group, or the interrupted collaborator, to switch between them during an event. A collaborator who anticipates interruptions can forewarn their team about possible disruptions. Additionally, the collaborator should set up their collaboration technologies before the onset of the group work session. This way, the collaborator can establish multiple channels of communication and easily switch between them so that the collaborator can maintain awareness of anything relevant occurring on the other front stages. During interruption processing, the interrupted collaborator also must stay alert and open to the changing demands of the performance, remembering their part and adhering to it.

This study suggests that ICTs with parallel communication capabilities provide the potential to deal with interruptions and their demands in such a way that collaborators on the focal frontstage may not even feel the effects of the interruption. Impression management and adherence to TIP help to maintain the collaboration (i.e., AMFP) as if there had been no interruption. Whenever resorting to the PFFP, however, observing TIP is essential. When the interrupted person needs to exit the collaboration to attend to an interruption, they need to be aware of the TIP they possess. This suggests that TIP needs to be discussed within the team beforehand. Should it start to wear thin in the state of PFFP, the interrupted collaborator should swiftly modify their behavior in order to avoid exceeding TIP. However, should such a violation or any kind of gaffe occur, admitting to it, explaining it, and apologizing for it all comprise proficient impression management.

Collaborating groups will benefit from being exposed to the ideas of AMFP, impression management, and TIP as a part of their company norms. Collaborators need to realize that, in modern knowledge work, AMFP and PFFP are an inherent part of collaborating synchronously in hybrid environments. Combined with the controlled entry of the interruption into the ongoing performance and the interrupted collaborator's proficient impression management, AMFP, and PFFP can help mitigate threats to the collaboration process. Group members should be willing to participatively allocate TIP to their colleagues and be open to negotiations to increase it when appropriate, knowing that it is only a matter of time before they themselves may be interrupted and would also benefit from tactful inattention from their collaborators. Thus, collaborators can expect that any TIP they afford to a colleague would be returned when needed, whether in the present collaboration or in future collaborations. In addition, team members need to be aware that when the collaboration is degenerating due to a lack of careful handling of the interruption (e.g., TIP nearing its limits or failed impression management), this can be addressed through timely interventions. These interventions can come from the interrupted collaborator in the form of repairs as well as, for example, team members strategically creating a scene to get the collaboration back on track before it is too late.

Regarding the enhancement of collaboration technologies, we believe that features that support screening, AMFP, PFFP, and managing TIP need to be incorporated into future designs. This can entail, for example, automated suggestions for detecting and blocking certain interruptions while offering alternative channels for handling allowed interruptions. The solutions also need to provide windows to these multiple channels (i.e., frontstages for the AMFP) to enable the individual to switch between the frontstages to maintain awareness of anything relevant occurring on the other frontstages. Our concept of TIP has been conceptualized as socially constructed time, and it serves as a mechanism that holds the collaboration together. There may also be ways to implement TIP as clock time and collect inputs from group members about the appropriate TIP allocated to each individual in different work situations.

Alternatively, with sufficient training, AI approaches may be able to allocate TIP dynamically for an individual for a given circumstance. Whenever the interrupted individual invokes AMFP or PFFP, their ICT should be able to show a countdown or alert the individual about their remaining time, nudging them to return to the relevant channel or initiate negotiations for additional TIP—discreetly, with justifications—when needed. Likewise, collaboration technologies may be designed to help with impression management and the signaling of active presence in channels (i.e., the multiple frontstages) that the individual dealing with an interruption is not actively attending to at a given point in time. The capability to conveniently “close the doors” to prevent the entry of ICT interruptions (to different degrees) could also be incorporated into the collaboration system, which should be integrated with all ICTs that an individual may be using or may have access to during the meeting. Furthermore, during PFFP, the system could suggest how to best proceed with the group’s main task or other tasks from their work portfolio to make efficient use of time while the focal collaborator is away. We also believe that this work makes a methodological contribution by explicitly showing how its theory was formulated in two distinct steps: first, the intensive EM/CA analysis of videos and, second, the imaginative reading of the themes and patterns identified in the first step as informed by the dramaturgical perspective. We urge future researchers to consider using a similar approach, adapted to their own needs.

## Potential Limitations and Conclusions

While we believe that our study is revelatory, we do note some potential limitations and a number of boundary conditions of our work. First, our video observation data set is not complemented with interviews of the subjects; interviews would have allowed them to offer a rationale for their chosen behaviors. For example, we were not able to determine whether the interruptions were congruent or incongruent with the collaboration task or the motivation of the actors to act in a certain way in all cases. However, we could hear the participants (in the audio recordings) whenever they spontaneously revealed the nature of an interruption to their collaborators in the situation. For example, the focal collaborator in Dataset 5 informed his other collaborators during an intense ideating session that he had to attend to his smartphone (an incoming interruption in the form of a message) because he had to immediately comment on a design sketch he had just received from his colleague from another project. We note that, in our EM/CA methodology, the analysis focuses on studying the conversation and interactions of the subjects (i.e., how individuals do things, not why they do things); thus, interviews were not considered essential (Garfinkel, 1967; Payne & Payne, 2011; Sidnell & Stivers, 2013). We would add that interviews are not necessarily a panacea given the various critiques of the method (e.g., Van de Ven, 2013), including the tendency of interviewees to manage impressions by retrospectively constructing socially desirable responses. In addition, interpretive studies of text using hermeneutics often have similar conditions (or constraints), where the “author” who originally produced the text being analyzed is not available to confirm their original or intended meaning. Further, even if available, the originator of the text may not have been clear about the intended meaning of the text in the first place or, alternatively, the meaning of the text may have changed for the originator since its creation in that the originator’s retrospectively constructed meanings during the interviews do not actually adhere to the original meanings (Ricoeur, 1973; Hirsch, 1967; Sarker et al., 2018). Many hermeneutic scholars thus focus on the meaning of the text and not on the author’s meaning. Given that behaviors and utterances can be considered text analogues (Ricoeur, 1973), the same justification may be offered here. For us, the data was primarily comprised of observable actions and recorded words, and we have focused on constructing an imaginative but plausible theoretical interpretation consistent with the empirical material (i.e., the sample of videos on the phenomenon). That said, having video with sound data did provide us with some degree of triangulation of the visible behaviors of the actors.

Another potential limitation of this study could be the theoretical perspective chosen and its associated sensitizing frame and concepts. As with most perspectives, the dramaturgical perspective highlights certain aspects of the phenomenon and suppresses others. Our theoretical understanding, for example, does not include a strong cognitivist component. While we do not deny that cognitivist explanations have an important role in understanding the effect of interruptions, our point is that the ICT interruption processing of an individual does not necessarily negatively affect the group collaboration as might be theoretically expected due to effects such as the cognitive overload experienced by individuals. By capturing the important role of social interaction and negotiation in collaboration and the creative use of ICTs, we are able to contribute to the discipline's multifaceted understanding of the phenomenon.

We would like to note that our model captures the phenomenon well for our data set, and we did see variations with respect to the average or typical frequency of interruption; hence, we could claim that the findings are considerably generalizable across different frequencies of interruption. However, we realize that there is an upper limit regarding the number of different interruptions that an individual can possibly handle using AMFP or even PFFP. The threshold could depend on several factors, including the demands that the interruption poses, the media through which interruptions are delivered and resolved, and the group members' preferences regarding how the focal actors should demonstrate active presence (i.e., apply impression management) on the focal frontstage. Furthermore, we carefully examined the effect of variations in culture, status differences, and tasks that were evident in our data. None of the variations appeared to change the structure of the process model.

Also, while we studied ICT-delivered interruptions initiated by humans, future research could investigate interruptions specifically initiated by technology. For example, it may be worth investigating social robots and conversational artificial intelligence solutions built to closely mimic human interaction (see Jokinen & Wilcock, 2014, 2017) involved in such collaborations or those that are the source of interruptions. Another intriguing idea is related to examining multiple frontstages simultaneously in empirical work, which could yield interesting insights. Clearly, more work is needed to investigate interruption processing under the many different potential conditions, and we invite colleagues to challenge, refine, and extend our work.

Our study suggests that ICT interruption is now routine and that groups engaged in collaboration on knowledge work-related tasks are often quite resilient and can withstand incursions in the form of frequent interruptions if the interruptions are effectively processed through the collaborators' social ingenuity in capitalizing on the capabilities of ICTs. Contrary to the suggestions in the literature, despite interruptions, work can continue without a break in the flow. In this paper, we present an initial attempt at theorizing about this important phenomenon and provide conceptual tools and language for future work in this area.

Suprateek Sarker is the Rolls-Royce Commonwealth Commerce Professor at the McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia. His work has been funded by the National Science Foundation (USA), and he has received honorary doctorates from two leading institutions in Europe. ## Explaining the Video Research and EM/CA Method

## Video Recording-Based Observation as a Method

Video recordings allow for observations of all the ongoing action to be stored in persistent media (Mondada, 2008; Babbie, 2010). Because of this, collaborations can be revisited, with all the details of the collaboration visible and unchanged (LeBaron, 2005; LeBaron et al., 2017; Mondada, 2008). This allows for a deeper engagement with the data when compared to observing the phenomena without recording them. A limitation often associated with observational methods is that if only one context is studied, the generality and transferability of findings can be compromised, but this can be overcome by including several contexts (i.e., cases) (Myers 1999), as we have done in this research. Our data includes different organizations, featuring participants of various ages, educational and cultural backgrounds, and various kinds of work situations, because these can affect the ways in which individuals approach social behavior with and around ICT (see Wang et al., 2013). The data covers video-recorded real-life interactions with and around ICT from 2012 to 2017. During the data collection period, none of the researchers collecting the data or the participants knew the recordings would be used for studying how ICT interruptions are processed effectively. Because of this, we believe that the videos are relatively free of participant anticipation and bias and that the participants were trying to act in ways they thought would be socially appropriate. (see Babbie, 2010; Goodwin, 1993). Overall, the video recordings represent a unique window into the real-life processing of interruptions during collaborations.

Using video recordings enables capturing both verbal and nonverbal social behaviors in minute detail when compared to taking notes, drawing, or taking pictures of a situation, all of which produce rather patchy snapshots of a process (Mondada, 2008). Capturing collaborations in full detail via videos can be useful—given that the researcher's understanding evolves iteratively in the case of inductive, interpretive approaches (Babbie, 2010), they do not know in advance which behaviors are the most significant ones for the focal phenomenon. A significant part of human social behavior is nonverbal, yet nonverbal behavior is a major driver of social processes (Levinson, 2006), as also acknowledged in IS research (Jenkins & Johnson, 1977). However, nonverbal behavior has not been analyzed much in IS, as methods for such behavior are lacking (Andrade et al., 2015). Video recordings have been suggested as a potential way forward in this domain (Andrade et al., 2015).

## Data Collection and Analysis

In video-based research, the researcher is not necessarily physically present in the data collection situation (Knoblauch, 2006). Thus, video recordings can facilitate capturing behaviors without affecting them (Andrade et al., 2015), with the subjects often forgetting that they are being recorded, especially when hidden cameras are used and when they are placed discreetly so as not to obstruct subjects' working space (Mondada, 2008), as we have done in this research. This is much more unobtrusive compared with an observing researcher or a researcher taking part in the action (LeBaron et al., 2017; Mondada, 2008).

In this research, we were not present during the time of collaboration; rather, the recordings were made via stationary cameras set up before the subjects' arrival. Whenever there were built-in cameras in the work facility we studied (as was the case in three locations, regarding datasets one, three, and four), those were used as hidden cameras. In one location (dataset two), there was a built-in camera in one room, used for capturing the collaborations of the team working in that room, supplemented by freestanding cameras that captured the rest of the teams in other locations. The remaining two datasets (five and six) were collected using freestanding cameras.

## Analyzing Video Data

Our units of analysis are the individual and a group of individuals, where each individual potentially impacts or is impacted by the ICT interruptions and the social context of the collaboration (Eynon & Helsper, 2014; Junglas et al., 2013; Lamb & Kling, 2003; Miranda & Saunders, 2003). In the analysis, we examined the tensions and negotiations during interruption processing, focusing primarily on the individual receiving the interruption as well as the group as a whole. To code and analyze the video data, we used multimodal ethnomethodology conversation analysis (EM/CA) (Jewitt, 2014; Sidnell, 2007; Stivers & Sidnell, 2005). Through this approach, we inspected the speech and gestural behaviors of the individuals in relation to their social surroundings, examining what (and how) was being said (see Goodwin, 2000, 2007; Heath et al., 2010; Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Streeck et al., 2011).

To analyze the data in detail, we used ELAN video annotation software (Brugman & Russel, 2004). We specifically used ELAN for synchronizing the videos in cases where a dataset comprised several recordings as well as for making annotations in data. The annotations enabled us to make detailed, time-specific notes attached to the videos, while the synchronizing provided a multiwindow view into the parallel social processes simultaneously taking place across several work locations in distributed teams. ## Initial Explorations and Content Logs

We conducted the analysis in several rounds. We started by observing all the videos from our datasets to gain a working understanding of data and the social collaboration sequences within. This was important because humans fundamentally experience and structure social action sequentially, with actions unfolding one after another in time and space (Levinqson, 2006). Sequences of social action are notorious for being difficult to capture and analyze, as their details are highly granular (Langley, 1999). However, video-recorded data makes this attainable (LeBaron et al., 2017).

After our first exploration, we undertook a more detailed analysis, writing content logs about the social action observable in the data. This included describing the social actions taking place within the data, time-stamping the processes, and making annotations with ELAN about potentially meaningful moments of ICT interruption processing, such as an individual noticing a pop-up screen on their laptop. This annotation protocol helped in focusing the later analysis phases by the event type (LeBaron, 2005). Overall, this phase resulted in content logs of data that described the ongoing collaborative actions in detail along with time stamps that helped us navigate the data.

## Identifying and Analyzing Cases in the Video Data

Based on the content logs, our analysis delved deeper into the details of the collaborative actions regarding ICT interruption processing, focusing on the variations in the processes and outcomes. Here, we focused on uncovering and documenting both the visible and audible sequential organization of the collaborations, as recommended in the multimodal EM/CA video method literature (see LeBaron et al., 2017). This phase was motivated by the notion that individuals make sense of their everyday collaborative behaviors as sequences, giving meaning to them accordingly (LeBaron et al., 2017; Levinson, 2006).

In practice, in this part of the analysis, we repeatedly viewed the processes we had identified in the previous phase. Here, we wanted to identify and document the beginnings and ends of each interruption processing episode as well as the events unfolding within. We then tagged them with time stamps, took screenshots, and described what was taking place in the data in relation to each time stamp. This phase resulted in select data excerpts called “cases.” In EM/CA analysis, the term “case” refers to an example of a complete social process demonstrating the unfolding of the phenomenon of interest (LeBaron, 2005). Each of the cases we captured were about thirty seconds to a few minutes in duration.

With a series of screenshots, we then revisited the cases and formulated visual screenshot sequences to represent the social process within each case. We specifically organized the screenshots as a series of “transitional moments” covering the main points of a case from the beginning to the end. A “transitional moment” refers to a moment in a collaboration when the flow of interaction changes (Schegloff, 1996). Presenting these in their temporal order can thus reveal how the process unfolds on a moment-by-moment basis as well as how outcomes are formed (Langley, 1999; Schegloff, 1996, 2007). Each series included the preceding condition of a sequence (such as the moment just before an ICT interruption arose) and the events following it (such as one or more individuals noticing the ICT interruption, reacting to it, commenting on it or the FC actions as well as signs of social offense, approval, and disapproval). Finally, we added a screenshot highlighting the outcome of the process, such as the collaboration continuing or the individuals terminating their collaboration.

We also revisited the video clips of these cases to obtain live documentation of the social processes representative of ICT interruption processing. These clips were rather longer than the actual cases for demonstrating how each case begins and ends (see Mondada, 2008; Schegloff, 1996, 2007). We continued this protocol until the data was saturated and no more cases contributing to the phenomenon with new information could be found with further sampling within that dataset, as recommended by Langley (1999).

This cumulative evidence base, comprised of the case descriptions, series of screenshots, and video clips, led to the discovery of recurrent microlevel behavioral patterns, processes, and outcomes across the data. Next, we documented the similarities and differences between the cases. Here, we examined the contextual factors, such as the time pressure, and the ways in which the interruption processing was negotiated and resulted. Together, these case descriptions, screenshots, and video clips comprise a lasting body of evidence for the claims made in this paper based on the empirical material (LeBaron, 2005).

## Transcribing

With the representative cases covering different facets of the research phenomenon, we then analyzed the cases with our multimodal EM/CA method. We first transcribed the cases; in multimodal EM/CA, this means using a standard notation system for annotating that transforms the gestural and tonal details of video data into a written format (Bezemer, 2014). We specifically used the notation standard of Atkinson and Heritage (1989), which we explain in more detail below.

This microlevel transcription makes the smallest details of interaction explicit and aids the analyst in identifying structural details of interaction that often go unnoticed (LeBaron, 2005). In EM/CA, these transcripts are also used for peer validation of the analysis; therefore, using a standardized notation is important, as it makes the interpretations explicit and possible to evaluate in the peer validation process (Knoblauch 2006, see chapter “Validation”).

In transcribing, we first observed the cases repeatedly and in small sections to focus on the microlevel details for the transcripts. We transcribed details up to a resolution of 0.1 seconds and included all the tonal, gestural, and temporal aspects in the transcriptions, as recommended in the EM/CA literature (see Heath et al., 2010; Mondada, 2008). With the transcripts, we specifically investigated the speech and gestural acts regarding each of the individuals in relation to their group, examining what (and how) was being said together with their accompanying bodily orientations, such as the gestures the individuals used with and around objects and toward each other, signs of social acceptance, offense, or disapproval (e.g., nods, frowns, or spoken statements), and ICT-made sounds (e.g., blinking, freezing, going blank, or the appearance of text/pop-ups) in response to collaborator actions. We also used the transcripts to validate our analyses.

## Validating the Analyses

For validating our analyses, we used the standard peer validation procedure from the conversation analysis tradition (see Knoblauch, 2006). Here, the first author and last author transcribed the cases separately, comparing their transcriptions and notes to resolve any differences via discussion, until agreement was reached.

Next, the transcripts with their accompanying video clips (representative of the variation in the social processes of interest) were presented to groups of external peer experts who specialize in analyzing both f2f and ICT-mediated interaction, with a total of 11 video data session meetings. In each data session, we presented two cases. During the data sessions, the case videos were played on repeat and in sections and then compared with their respective transcripts. Each expert formed their own interpretation of the case; then, their interpretations were discussed within the expert panel and compared with those of the first and last authors, resolving any difference by negotiating until 100% agreement, as recommended by Jordan and Henderson (1995) and Have (2007). Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, since March 2020, we have been conducting the data sessions remotely via Zoom.

## Ethical Considerations

The data for this study consists of research video recordings that capture knowledge workers in their real-life work collaborations. To ensure the ethical treatment of the participants, we planned and conducted this research with the RESPECT code of conduct (The Institute for Employment Studies, 2004) together with the ethical guidelines of video research (Heath et al., 2010; Jordan & Henderson, 1995). All the participants signed informed consent forms that explained their rights during and after the research and made explicit how their data was to be stored and used in publications.

For participant privacy and anonymity (LeBaron et al., 2017), we stored the videos securely on portable hard drives protected with a password and kept them in a locked cabinet. The largest datasets were stored on secure university servers approved for this purpose by the university. In addition, the screenshots, transcripts, and video clips were stored on password-protected hard drives kept in locked cabinets. After each data session with the experts, the transcripts used during the validation protocol were retrieved and then destroyed. The video viewing during the data sessions took place behind closed doors, with the videos seen and heard only by the researchers and peer expert participants (no outsiders allowed).

To illustrate our points in research publications, we anonymized the data by using pseudonyms for the participants, blurring their faces and other relevant details, and turning the screenshots covering our cases into cartoon characters to prevent identification, as recommended by the EM/CA method literature (see Heath et al., 2010; Jordan and Henderson, 1995).

We then interpreted the subjective experiences of the participants in the light of Goffman's (1959) dramaturgical concepts.
