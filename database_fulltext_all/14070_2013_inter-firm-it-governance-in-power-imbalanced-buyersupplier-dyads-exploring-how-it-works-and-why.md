RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Inter-firm IT governance in power-imbalanced buyer–supplier dyads: exploring how it works and why it lasts

Jinghua Xiao<sup>1</sup>, Kang Xie<sup>1</sup> and Qing Hu<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Business School, Sun Yat-Sen University, Guangzhou, China; <sup>2</sup>College of Business, Iowa State University, Ames, Iowa, USA

Correspondence: Kang Xie, Business School, Sun Yat-Sen University, No. 135, Xingang Xi Road, Guangzhou, China, 510275. Tel.: +86 20-84114167; Fax: +86 20-84113687; E-mail: mnsxk@mail.sysu.edu.cn

## Abstract

Using a multiple case study design, we investigate the issue of inter-firm IT governance and its impact on information sharing in buyer–supplier dyadic relationships. We interviewed 38 managers of operations, purchasing, and IT in five dyadic relationships, and identified and examined one type of inter-firm IT governance: unilateral IT governance. In this type of IT governance, one party of the dyad dominates the relationship and the decision rights regarding inter-firm IT systems and data sharing. We find that unilateral inter-firm IT governance develops under contract-based and relationship-supplemented inter-firm governance arrangements in which significant power imbalance exists. However, contrary to the prediction of resource dependence theory, power-imbalanced governance can survive and thrive over a long period of time. We find that the inter-firm relational norms and trust that develop between these dyads constrain opportunistic and myopic behaviors in both parties, thus sustaining the seemingly unstable unilateral inter-firm IT governance. We also find that the operational necessity of the buyers and the IT capability of the suppliers are two primary factors that constrain inter-firm information sharing in these dyads. On the basis of these findings, we propose a process model for understanding and managing this type of inter-firm IT governance. ## Introduction

One of the challenges of IT governance in today’s business environment is that firms not only have to deal with internal but also external IT governance issues within their networks of business partners. For example, who should set the standards for information sharing and be responsible for implementing system integration between two partners in an inter-firm arrangement? In this Internet-enabled global economy, firms are increasingly interconnected at business process and IT systems levels with suppliers, distributors, retailers, and logistics providers. Since the boundaries of the linked IT systems span across multiple firms, interfirm IT governance is more complex, and consequently more difficult to negotiate and manage, than intra-firm IT governance in many aspects. Inter-firm IT governance must deal with many aspects of inter-firm relationships: contracts, power, trust, standards, and information sharing among partners.

However, current literature on IT governance is primarily concerned with intra-organizational IT governance (Brown & Magill, 1994; Brown, 1997; Brown & Grant, 2005; Weill & Ross, 2005; Xue et al, 2008); that is, the decision rights for resource allocations, and the subsequent accountability for IT investments and projects, within a single firm. While there has been significant progress in understanding intra-firm IT governance in the literature, critical issues related to inter-firm IT governance have not been adequately addressed. In fact, the issue of inter-firm IT governance is virtually absent from the IT governance literature.

Brown & Grant’s (2005, p. 709) assertion that researchers should ‘reframe the assumptions underlying IT governance research in order to develop alternative conceptualizations suited to the realities facing contemporary organizations’ is especially salient in today’s interconnected business ecosystem, where IT governance is no longer an intra-firm issue but critical to inter-firm relationship and performance. In this study, we examine new forms of IT governance in the inter-firm context. Our focus is no longer on finding the fit between IT governance forms and organizational contingencies, or on how different IT governance forms interact with different organizational contingencies. Instead, we focus on the following research questions related to inter-firm IT governance: (1) How does inter-firm governance shape and impact inter-firm IT governance? (2) How does interfirm IT governance work, and why does it sustain at the confluence of power, trust, and norms in inter-firm relationships? (3) How does inter-firm IT governance influence inter-firm information sharing?

Given the nascent nature of inter-firm IT governance, we pursued these research questions using an exploratory multi-case study approach. We interviewed managers of production, purchasing, and IT operations in five buyer– supplier dyads. We found that Power Imbalance (PI) in a buyer–supplier dyad significantly shapes inter-firm relationships and inter-firm IT governance. IT governance in these types of dyads are defined as unilateral inter-firm IT governance, in which the dominant party retains the decision rights for inter-firm IT issues such as the scope and platform of IT integration, and the extent and format of information sharing. This study fills a significant gap in the IT governance literature by exploring the relatively new but increasingly important phenomenon of interfirm IT governance. The findings of this study also contribute to inter-firm governance theories by showing that PI in inter-firm governance is not necessarily a destabilizing force that requires concerted managerial corrective action, as resource dependence theory suggests (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978). We find that this seemingly unsustainable governance structure can survive over a long period of time, with the parties’ mutual acceptance if reasonable degrees of inter-firm trust and relational norms exist in a dyad.

The rest of the paper is arranged as follows. We first provide a brief review of the literature related to IT governance. This is followed by a description of the multi-case study design and its execution. The main findings of the case study are then presented, along with the propositions related to the significant concepts and relationships induced from the case evidence. These discussions lead to the development of a process model of inter-firm IT governance. We then proceed to discuss theoretical and practical implications of the findings and limitations of the study. Finally, conclusions and future research directions are presented.

## Theoretical background

## Theories of IT governance

Research on IT governance can be traced back to the early attempts by IS scholars to understand what made IS functions successful when IS was emerging as a key functional area in organizations in the 1970s (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1978, 1982). The concept of IT governance gradually emerged in the 1990s with a series of publications by Brown and colleagues (Brown & Magill, 1994; Brown, 1997; Brown & Magill, 1998). These studies outlined three fundamental IT governance forms – centralized, decentralized, and hybrid – based on the locus of control. Later studies essentially used this taxonomy of IT governance and focused on the issues related to the fit between IT governance forms and competitive strategies, and other organizational contingencies (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999; Peterson et al, 2000).

In centralized governance, major IT decisions are made by senior managers at the corporate level regardless of ownership and use of the IT systems. This is in contrast to decentralized governance in which all major IT decisions are made at the business unit level where the IT systems are used. There are advantages and disadvantages in either governance structure. The centralized governance provides greater control over IT standards and increases the potential for realizing economies of scale, while the decentralized governance allows greater customization and responsiveness for business needs (Brown & Grant, 2005). The hybrid governance attempts to increase the strength and reduce the weakness of the two opposing forms by taking advantage of the efficiency of centralized decisions for corporate IT systems and the responsiveness of decentralized decisions at the business unit level. It allows centralized decisions on standards and architecture at the corporate level, and decentralized decisions on systems development and support at the business unit level.

Weill & Ross (2005) provided a more refined taxonomy in which IT governance is classified into six archetypes – business monarchy, IT monarchy, federal, IT duopoly, feudal, and anarchy – primarily based on how and where IT decisions are made within a firm. In a business monarchy, a firm’s senior management, sometimes including the CIO (Chief Information Officer), makes major IT decisions; this is the most centralized form of IT governance. In an IT monarchy, IT executives at the corporate or unit level make these decisions; thus it could be either centralized or decentralized, depending on the structure of an individual firm. In a federal structure, senior managers and representatives from the business and IT units make decisions collaboratively. In an IT duopoly, corporate-level IT executives and managers at business units work together in making the decisions. In a feudal system, business unit managers make all the decisions for their own units, a typical decentralized governance form. And finally, in an anarchy environment, users and groups make individual decisions related to their IT systems, a case of informal governance.

A more comprehensive review of the IT governance literature is provided by Brown & Grant (2005). The primary focus of the extant IT governance literature is on decision rights for resource allocations and the subsequent accountability for IT investments and projects within a firm. In the context of a supply chain, characterized by integration of key business processes among suppliers, logistics providers, manufacturers, distributors, retailers, and customers in order to provide products, services, funds, and information to the right place at the right time, the primary concern in IT governance is no longer about resource allocation. Instead, it is about decision rights and accountability for inter-firm IT system platform and architecture, and scope and extent of information sharing among the firms involved. So far, there have been few published studies in which inter-firm IT governance is at the center of concern. Studies that deal with inter-firm IT systems primarily focus on how inter-firm IT systems and data sharing benefit the parties and create value for the dyads (Subramani, 2004; Dong et al, 2009; Klein & Rai, 2009). In one study, inter-firm IT governance is treated the same as intra-firm governance and used as an endogenous construct to system performance (Wang & Wei, 2007). In another, inter-firm IT systems are conceptualized and treated as standalone systems (Gunasekaran & Ngai, 2004). This absence of inter-firm IT governance research in the IS literature forces us to go to the strategic management literature on inter-firm governance for conceptual and theoretical guidance.

## Theories of inter-firm governance

There is a rich body of literature on inter-firm governance from the perspectives of formal contract and relational governance. The contract governance perspective is primarily based on transaction cost economics (TCE) (Williamson, 1981; Williamson, 1985). TCE outlines the fundamental relationships among basic forms of governance (market vs hierarchy), characteristics of transactions (asset specificity and frequency), and extent of market uncertainty. Studies based on TCE argue that managers attempt to align inter-firm governance features with known exchange hazards, particularly those associated with specialized asset investments, difficulties in performance measurement, and uncertainty in the exchange. This effort leads to either complex contracts that define remedies for foreseeable contingencies and specify processes for resolving unforeseeable outcomes, or vertical integration if the contracts become too costly to create or enforce (Poppo & Zenger, 2002).

On the other hand, the relational governance concept largely grows out of the work of Macneil (1978, 1980). Relational governance attempts to address some of the deficiencies in contract governance: the failure to account for social structures within which the inter-firm exchanges are embedded, and the overestimation of hazardous elements in the exchange (Heide, 1994; Poppo & Zenger, 2002). Researchers observed that inter-firm exchanges often involve more than just formal contracts, and that transactions are often repeated in embedded social relationships over a long period of time (Poppo & Zenger, 2002). It is argued that in a relational governance arrangement, the enforcement of obligations, promises, and expectations – three key elements of a contract – occurs through social processes that promote norms of flexibility, solidarity, and information exchange (Poppo & Zenger, 2002).

When dealing with inter-firm relationships, it is inevitable that the issue of power balance between the parties will arise. Power in the context of inter-firm relationship can be defined as the ability of one firm to influence the intentions and actions of another firm (Maloni & Benton, 2000). When one party in a buyer– supplier dyad can exert more influence than the other, or when one firm is more dependent on the other than vice versa, power imbalance (PI) occurs in the relationship (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978; Casciaro & Piskorski, 2005). Research shows that relationships based on a perfect balance of power may not always be possible, and very often one party may have to tolerate the imbalance in order to gain and perhaps maintain lucrative business (Gummesson, 1996). In fact, PI in buyer–supplier dyads is often the norm rather than exception in supply chain relationships (Maloni & Benton, 2000; Subramani, 2004).

PI in inter-firm governance has been studied by scholars in various organizational contexts (Kumar, 1996; Maloni & Benton, 2000; Buvika & Reve, 2002; Benton & Maloni, 2005; Hingley, 2005). In a review of the influence of power in supply chains, Maloni & Benton (2000, p. 50) noted that the U.S. automobile industry ‘retains an imbalance of power as five manufacturers account for almost 90% of the market share, yet the base of supplier firms numbers in the thousands. Such an oligopolistic environment has allowed the manufacturers to authoritatively transfer responsibilities for cost reduction, product development, and inventory management back to the suppliers, forcing them to comply with strict performance guidelines or face replacement’. Hingley (2005) observed that in the U.K. fresh food industry power was generally skewed in favor of large retail buyers, and suppliers usually felt limited mutuality and imbalance of power in the relationships with their retail partners. ## Case study method and protocol

Since there is little literature on inter-firm IT governance, we adopted the case study methodology (Yin, 1989) to conduct our research. ‘Case studies are the preferred strategy when “how” or “why” questions are being posed, when the investigators have little control over events, and when the focus is on a contemporary phenomenon within some real-life context’. (Yin, 1989, p. 13) We were more interested in knowing how inter-firm IT governance works and why it is sustainable than which different forms of inter-firm IT governance exist; and we were more interested in developing a framework for future theory development and empirical testing than explaining the focal phenomenon in depth and detail. Hence, the exploratory case study methodology is appropriate (Yin, 1989; Pare´, 2004). Furthermore, we adopted a positivist view of scientific inquiry by committing to the idea that research is about discovering the objective reality of a focal phenomenon by crafting measures that will detect those dimensions of the reality that interest the researchers (Pare´, 2004).

We followed the general guidelines for conducting positivist exploratory case studies as presented in the literature (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 1989; Dube´ & Pare´, 2003; Pare´, 2004). A case study protocol was first developed based on an extensive literature research and brainstorming, which outlined the scope and objectives of the research; the nature of the research design; the characteristics of the target interviewees, firms, and industries; and the initial set of interview questions. We followed the holistic multiple case design (Yin, 1989, p. 55) because we focused on one level of analysis – inter-firm IT governance in an exchange dyad – across multiple dyads for replication. These and other design characteristics of this study, as well as assumptions and justifications, are summarized in Table 1.

A list of candidate manufacturers in the southeast region of China, the locus of Chinese, and for that matter, world’s manufacturing, was then created, based on the researchers’ knowledge about the firms and industries in the region. After initial contacts with managers in these candidate firms, five companies were identified as the focal firms for the five dyads of this study, primarily based on the criterion that each firm has an extensive supplier network. Once the five focal firms were identified, we requested the top executives in each of the firms to identify one key supplier for studying dyadic relationships. We specified that the supplier must be a strategic supplier to the focal firm, have at least 2 years of relationship with the focal firm, and be willing to cooperate with the research team. In the end, five suppliers were selected to match the five focal firms. Tables 2 and 3 show the detailed profiles of the focal and supplier firms selected.

The primary data collection method was interviews with the managers, which covered a broad range of topics: inter-firm governance, inter-firm IT governance, competitive strategies, organizational culture, and issues related to general operations in the buyer–supplier dyads. The actual interviews were conducted in July 2010, with the research team, which consisted of the three authors and three–four doctoral students, visiting each of the ten firms in the five dyads. With the consent of the interviewees, all interviews were digitally recorded for later transcription. At each site, the interviews were conducted either in a group setting that lasted 3–4 hours or with individuals that lasted about 1 hour, depending on the availability of the managers. Given the nature of the interviews and the broad issues involved, the group interview format proved to be more advantageous than individual interviews, because very often when one manager could not provide certain information, the others could jump in and fill the gap. Because no questions involved confidential information, we believed the group setting did not hinder the interviewees’ willingness to reveal true experiences and opinions. In all, 38 managers participated in the interviews, resulting in about 40 hours of recording, averaging about 60 min for each interviewee. These recordings were later transcribed by the research team into about 140 pages of documents in Chinese. Team members also took approximately 30 pages of field notes during the interviews and the firms’ presentations on their operations, IT systems, and contracts with partners. In addition, the research team collected documents and brochures about the firms and their products for background information.

The research team then read and coded the interview transcripts. We followed an open coding strategy using the text analysis software NVivo 8.0. There were no predetermined coding schemes. The passages in the transcripts were first coded as free nodes based on the central ideas, such as ‘Trust’, ‘Relationship’, ‘Contract’, ‘Power’, ‘Governance’ and so on. Each of the free nodes was later merged into tree nodes if the concepts were similar or related to other nodes in a tree. In the end, 13 root tree nodes were created and each of the root nodes has multiple child nodes that represent refined ideas. For instance, under the root node ‘Governance’, there were four child nodes: Corporate Governance, Corporate IT Governance, Inter-Firm Governance, and Inter-Firm IT Governance. The coding was carried out mostly to organize, classify, and highlight the major ideas and concepts that were embedded in the interview transcripts.

## Main findings

While the interviews involved a broad range of issues related to inter-firm governance in supply chain dyads and resulted in a rich set of data and schemes, in this study we focused on the findings related to inter-firm IT governance in the context of buyer–supplier dyads. Our conversations with the business and IT managers in the five dyads revealed a number of unanticipated results and practices. Perhaps, the most striking in these dyadic relationships is the apparent buyer dominance in interfirm IT governance. On the surface, this observation seems to challenge our preconceived notion that if there is an inter-firm IT system, there should be collaborative inter-firm IT governance for the system to function in the short run and be sustainable over the long run. However, further discussions with the interviewees and analyses of the transcripts revealed a much more complex system of checks and balances in an inherently unbalanced governance structure. Primarily due to these checks and balances, this seemingly unstable form of inter-firm IT governance not only survived but actually thrived over time, and served the dyadic firms in their pursuits of operational excellence internally and competitive advantage externally. In the following sections, we elaborate on the primary findings and develop our process model of inter-firm IT governance under power-imbalanced inter-firm governance conditions. To present the rich case evidence effectively and focus on the significant cross-case patterns, we chose the approach that organizes within-case evidence under cross-case issues (Yin, 1989, p. 135), as evident in the tables and discussions in the following sections.

## Main sources of PI in buyer–supplier dyads

In all of our case dyads, PI, as defined in the previous section, is prevalent between the buyers – which usually are large national or multinational manufacturers – and the suppliers – which usually are smaller and local manufacturers. Table 5 illustrates the nature and sources of PI in the dyads. As shown, the PI is largely structural – in each dyad, the supplier is dependent on the buyer for a significant amount of sales. In dyads A–A1 and B–B1, the buyers are the only customers of the suppliers. In the other dyads, a buyer usually accounts for 30% or more of a supplier’s total sales. Equally important, the buyer usually is the only customer for a particular product the supplier is selling. This is because the suppliers are primarily contract or original equipment manufacturers, and rarely have their own brands of products or distribution channels. Switching to alternative buyers would mean significant costs in equipment, facilities, and human resources, as well as market uncertainty. Some buyers (C, D, E) have a 30% rule in their procurement policy – no more than 30% of a product can come from a single supplier – in order to manage supply chain risk. This gives the buyer significant power to use substitution as a potential threat in contract negotiations and relationship maintenance. In the case of dyad A–A1, A1 is the only supplier for particular products, a joint venture orchestrated by A and A1’s parent firm to be an exclusive supplier for A. This is a common practice among Japanese automakers. The result is that the power of A1 in the dyad is actually limited even though it is the sole supplier for these products to A.

PI in the buyer–supplier dyads can be analyzed using the framework of resource dependence theory (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978). This theory contends that it is the asymmetry of dependence in an exchange relationship that gives power to one firm over the other. The asymmetry of dependence in the five dyads is evident in Table 5. To all five buyers, the suppliers in the dyads account for only 1–7% of their total purchase, while to all suppliers, the buyers in the dyads account for 30–100% of their total sales. Therefore, the suppliers’ dependence on the buyers for sales is much stronger than the buyers dependence on the suppliers for a particular product. To further illustrate this PI in the dyads, we adapted the power configuration grid (Casciaro & Piskorski, 2005), as shown in Figure 1.

In this grid, the dependence between two firms is depicted with 9 configurations, with PI score calculated as the differences between the two dependence scores. All cells on the diagonal of the grid have PI ¼ 0, indicating balanced power; and all cells off the diagonal have PIa0, indicating imbalanced power. A positive PI indicates buyer dominance, and a negative PI indicates supplier dominance. In our cases, all PIs are positive.

## Inter-firm IT governance in imbalanced buyer–supplier dyads

One immediate consequence of this skewed power balance in the dyads is buyer-dominated inter-firm governance and inter-firm IT governance. Table 6 shows the details of inter-firm governance structures of the dyads. In a dyad, the foundations of inter-firm governance are articulated in a general supply agreement, originated from the buyer. This agreement outlines the scope of collaboration, payment terms, logistics arrangement, quality standards, conflict resolution mechanisms, and penalties for violation of contract provisions. This agreement is usually in effect indefinitely until terminated by the parties. With this agreement, the firms then negotiate an annual supply contract that specifies products, quantity, price, delivery schedule, and quality specifications and so on. On the basis of this annual contract, purchase orders are then generated and transmitted to the supplier daily, weekly, monthly, or in some cases, semi-annually, via inter-firm IT systems.

Interviews with managers of suppliers in all five dyads revealed that the suppliers have little or no power in the negotiations of the general supply agreements and the annual supply contracts where the products, price, quantity, and quality criteria are specified. The only area in which the suppliers have some negotiating power is price.

The dominance of buyers in inter-firm governance has clearly spilled over to inter-firm IT governance in these dyads. There were no specific provisions in either the general supply agreements or the annual supply contracts in all five dyads for inter-firm IT governance. Inter-firm IT connections were established primarily to serve supply operations and reduce errors, cost, and delays in ordering and delivery. In three of the five dyads, the buyers open a Web interface to their ERP systems for suppliers to access orders and other related information. In this type of arrangement, the suppliers usually have little involvement in system designs and operations, as described by the IT Manager of A1:

I have been working in IT at this firm for over six years, and I have never had any direct contact with the [IT] people at firm A. This is because this system was not jointly developed, and they don’t need us to work on the system. They provided us with this platform, and asked us to use it. They don’t need our IT department to collaborate with them.

In E–E1 dyad, the buyer developed specialty supply chain management software for all of its major suppliers and retail outlets for better integration and information sharing to compete in a dynamic market environment. This software is hosted on the servers at the buyer’s headquarters, and the suppliers run the applications remotely from their computers in the form of SaaS (software as service). The suppliers are required to sign an annual licensing agreement that specifies the service agreement and fee schedule. In all five dyads, the suppliers have virtually no decision rights or accountability for the inter-firm IT systems.

We summarize these observations in the following proposition:

## Proposition 3 Buyer-dominated inter-firm governance is a primary contributor to buyer-dominated inter-firm IT governance in the buyersupplier dyads.

## Information sharing in buyer-dominated inter-firm IT governance

The dominance of buyers in inter-firm IT governance are inevitably reflected in the architecture and operations of the inter-firm IT systems. In all five dvads. the inter-firm

IT systems are set up by the buyers to open an interface to their ERP systems from which the suppliers can view and download specific order related data, such as purchase orders, delivery schedules and confirmations, invoices and payments, and in some cases, the buyers’ production schedules and market forecasts. Some suppliers are required to input their inventory information into the systems, and submit electronic bidding for new products and contracts via the systems. The integration between the buyers and the suppliers in the dyads are usually at the application level – data submitted by the suppliers are usable to the buyers’ ERP systems, and data downloaded by the suppliers from the interface are usable to the suppliers’ production and management information systems.

The E–E1 dyad is the only case where the two firms have accomplished full integration at the systems level. However, this case once again demonstrates the consequences of buyer dominance in inter-firm IT systems. Competing in a low entry barrier and highly dynamic market environment of consumer apparel, the buyer’s top management strongly believes in the critical role of IT in its business operations and the overall competitive strategy. Thus, firm E invests heavily in developing and operating its IT systems. As a manufacturer and distributor of upper-medium price range apparels, a key to E’s success is its capability to respond to market trends quickly with new product lines, new designs, and new materials in each of the four seasons annually. This strategy demands close coordination and control in the firm’s vast supply and distribution channels, which include about 200 suppliers and over 3000 franchised retail outlets. To ensure operational success and full integration with its corporate ERP system, E developed its own brand of supply chain management software and retail management software, and created its data and server center to host these applications as a software service (SaaS). All major suppliers and all retail outlets are required to license the software service and install Windows-based thin client software in their own locations.

Interestingly, the buyer dominance in the inter-firm IT systems and data sharing is not viewed as anything but mutually beneficial by both the buyers and the suppliers in all five dyads. The CIO and senior VP of E spoke at length of the value of its SaaS-based system. He believes that the value the system provides to the partnerships can be viewed from operational and strategic perspectives. From the operational perspective, it promotes effective communications, increases productivity, reduces and controls inventory costs in both upstream and downstream operations, improves the quality of products, and helps manage product cycle and deal with changes. From the strategic perspective, it enhances IT capabilities that benefit both partners, helps identify and resolve bottlenecks in organizations and business processes, ensures the sustainability of the relationship, and improves the ability to manage risk and expansion.

The Production Manager of E1 corroborated some of these views: ‘At the beginning we had doubts about the system. Since no one in our company, from workers to managers, understood the system, we had to designate a couple of employees to learn the operations, although we only had orders for a few million pieces [of sweaters]. However, considering E could be an important partner, we decided to give it a try. We started with the electronic packaging application, but were not used to it and felt it was complex. After a while, it started to make sense, and the problems related to mis-shipping, mis-labeling, and inventory shortage started to decline. When we compared the results of before and after, we could clearly see the difference. Although the system increased our labor cost, it decreased penalties and complaints, an indication of improved service quality. From there, we moved on to the production management application and other functions of the system’.

## Role of relational norms and inter-firm trust

With the apparent PI in all of the buyer–supplier dyadic relationships we encountered, one could not help but wonder how the inter-firm relationship can be sustained over the long run. Resource dependence theory (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978) predicts that dependence on external resources, which results in PI in an exchange, will motivate firms to manage such dependence or imbalance by taking action including mergers and acquisitions, joint ventures, appointing boards of directors, pursuing political actions, and executive successions. Interestingly, none of the five predicted actions is known to have taken place in the five dyads. In the A–A1 dyad, even though A1 is a joint venture set up primarily by A to exercise even more control, A1 expresses no desire to change the status quo. There were no signs of stress or deterioration in the dyadic relationships, all of which appeared to be stable and had been in operation for at least 8 years at the time of the study.

It is clear to us that the sustainability of inter-firm IT governance hinges on the sustainability of inter-firm governance in each of the dyads. Therefore, to understand the sustainability of inter-firm IT governance we must understand the sustainability of inter-firm governance. We were particularly interested in understanding how organizational factors affected the sustainability of interfirm governance under the apparent PI. After an in-depth examination and analysis of the interview transcripts and inter-firm governance literature, we identified two reoccurring themes that underpin the inter-firm governance in all five dyads: relational norms and inter-firm trust.

Relational norms are defined as the behavioral expectations that are shared by decision makers in the context of inter-firm relationships (Heide & John, 1992; Aulakh et al, 1996). Heide & John (1992) further asserted that relational norms have three dimensions: solidarity, flexibility, and information exchange. According to Aulakh et al (1996) and Heide & John (1992), solidarity reflects the mutual recognition that the relationship will continue in the foreseeable future, thus prescribes behavior directed specifically toward relationship maintenance; flexibility reflects the willingness of parties to adjust as circumstances change, representing an assurance that the relationship will be subject to good-faith modification if the agreed provisions prove detrimental to one party in light of changed circumstances; and information exchange reflects the bilateral expectation that the firms will proactively engage in formal and informal sharing of meaningful and timely information.

Heide & John (1992, p. 35) argued explicitly the role of relational norms as the guardian against opportunistic behavior in inter-firm arrangements: ‘A particular property of relational norms is their prescription of behaviors directed toward maintaining the system or relationship as a whole and curtailing behavior promoting the goals of the individual parties. By their very nature, relational norms constitute a safeguard against exploitative use of decision rights’. We found plenty of evidence in our interviews that all three dimensions of the relational norms are present in the dyadic relationships. When asked why E did not take advantage of its power in the dyadic relationships with suppliers in order to be more profitable, the manager of production of E replied:

We strive for win-win relationships. The suppliers have to survive as well. They have to deal with increasing cost of raw materials and labor. We must have a mutually beneficial and pleasant long-term relationship. If we don’t care about the life and death of our suppliers, we won’t be able to sustain the relationships. Win-win is part of our organizational culture. We can’t be the only winner [in the relationship].

This sentiment was echoed by the manager of production of E1 when asked how he dealt with the apparent PI in the relationship with E. He stated: ‘I think the essence of business is mutual benefits, a kind of relationship in which two parties cooperate with and then benefit from each other. But how do we promote this relationship? We must cooperate in all aspects. For example, E has very high quality standards, which are very important to them. To accomplish this, they require us to work with them on [IT] systems and supply chain. In the end, all of these cost us, but we still have enough room for profit. That’s cooperation! y. We are looking for a long-term relationship, not just one order at a time, because that would be very difficult to deal with. It is the expectation that there will be a long-term relationship that shapes how we deal with each individual order’.

Another major construct in inter-firm governance theories is inter-firm trust (Zaheer & Venkatraman, 1995; Aulakh et al, 1996; Mo¨llering, 2002; Ratnasingam, 2005; Gulati & Nickerson, 2008). Parallel to its individual level counterpart, inter-firm trust can be defined as a set of expectations between the partners regarding each other’s behavior and each partner’s fulfillment of its perceived obligations (Aulakh et al, 1996). Research suggests that this trust has at least three interrelated roles in inter-firm relationships: an important deterrent to opportunistic behavior of the partners; a substitute for hierarchical governance when ownership-control is not strategically viable or economically feasible; and a contributor to the market performance and efficiency of the firms in the relationship (Aulakh et al, 1996). When asked about the relationship with its many suppliers, the VP of firm A stated:

When the joint venture started 12 years ago, many of the Japanese suppliers which had been with the automaker for dozens of year also came over to China as suppliers of the joint venture. This formed the foundation of trust in the supplier network. Even with many new local suppliers who joined in later, our fundamental philosophy remains the same: ‘Respect individuals, respect partners, and always treat partners as equals’. We think of the suppliers, distributors, and us as a commonwealth, and our goal has been to grow together and share the wealth with each other.

However, a trusting relationship between two firms does not exclude unilateral monitoring by one party in the dyad. In fact, Aulakh et al (1996) argued that appropriate unilateral monitoring by a firm actually can enhance the inter-firm trust. They outlined three types of monitoring mechanisms: output control, process control, and social control. In output control, the focal firm monitors the results or outcomes its partner produces; process control refers to the focal firm monitoring the partner’s behavior or means to achieve the desired outcomes; and social control establishes an organizational context, such as common organizational culture, that encourages self-control by the partner. On output control and process control, the VP of A stated:

We don’t do quality checks on 100% of the components, which would be too costly for us. We rely on the quality control systems of the suppliers. We often visit and inspect the suppliers and help them improve their processes and systems, and resolve any problems identified, like they are part of the same family. If you treat them as part of your family, you will trust what they do for you, and they will do everything not to disappoint you. But this does not mean there are no quality issues in the products, which occur every month. As soon as a problem arises, we work together to solve it.

## The purchasing manager of A corroborated this:

We have a supplier visiting and monitoring mechanism. We will visit suppliers before volume production, three months after, and whenever the suppliers have problemsy. We also invite suppliers to visit us. This kind of exchange, although not specified in any contract, increases the mutual trust between us.

## Role of IT capability and operational necessity

One of the striking facts we observed during our interviews is how limited the system level integrations are between partners. Each of the five focal firms in the dyads represents the top-tier manufacturers in their respective industries, with top-notch enterprise systems and advanced management processes. Yet, with the exception of dyad E–E1 which uses the advanced SaaS technology, all others use fairly basic forms of inter-firm IT connections, as shown in Tables 7 and 10.

On the basis of the discussions in previous sections, we already know that there are high levels of inter-firm trust between the partners in each of the dyads, and the managers on one side can expect strong relational norms from the other side. Both of these factors create deterrence to opportunistic and myopic behaviors on either side. Thus, one interesting question would be: Why aren’t more data shared between the buyers and the suppliers over inter-firm IT systems? Our interviews unveiled two primary organizational level constraints:

IT capability and operational necessity of the partners in the dyads.

The IT capability of the partners, primarily suppliers, is one of the key bottlenecks for system-level integration and data sharing. Even though the buyers in all five dyads are technically and managerially advanced firms, all five suppliers are small- to medium-sized and laborintensive manufacturers, generally with only small staffs of IT professionals to support the entire operations. While some of the suppliers have their own specialty ERP systems for financial and production management, few of them have strong IT development capability, with the exception of B1, which can rely on the IT resources of its parent firm in Japan if needed. To these suppliers, system level integration and broader data sharing with their buyers are simply not feasible, as the general manager of D indicated when asked why there was no system level integration:

Technical difficulties are the primary reason. Every supplier has different systems, and each is at different stages of [technical] maturity. Large companies can invest millions for system development, but for small suppliers, that’s all their profit. So it’s impossible for them to invest that much in their IT systems. Management is another problem. Many of our smaller suppliers have problems related to business processes. We can help them redesign business processes and codify the processes using low-end ERP systems. But with so many different ERP systems, it is very difficult to accomplish system level integration.

Multiple managers in the buyer and supplier firms mentioned that information security and data confidentiality are also major concerns given the inadequate IT capabilities of the suppliers. In our interviews, it appeared to us that the suppliers have to share whatever data the buyers want for their operations, and the suppliers in general would love to have more data shared by the buyers; however, their desires are usually not met. On the other hand, if their requests for more data sharing are declined, they usually deal with it as part of doing business with an otherwise satisfactory partner and move on, as the production manager of D1 put it:

## Discussion

Inter-firm IT governance has received little attention in the IS literature as compared with inter-firm governance in the management and marketing literature. In this study, we examined inter-firm IT governance in light of the inter-firm governance literature. Our case study of five buyer–supplier dyads suggests that inter-firm IT governance is largely a derivative of inter-firm governance, at least in the domain of buyer-dominated dyads. In the dyads we studied, when the firms establish interfirm exchange relationships via contracts, inter-firm IT governance and systems are rarely considered and articulated. The PI in inter-firm governance inevitably spills over to the subsequent inter-firm IT governance and shapes the operations of inter-firm IT systems and the scope of information sharing. Inter-firm IT systems and data sharing emerge as part of the operational necessity of the two parties, primarily the buyers.

On the basis of the evidence we have observed in the five dyads, and the literature on inter-firm governance, primarily ideas from Williamson (1985), we submit that the buyer-dominated inter-firm IT governance can be described in a more generalized form – unilateral interfirm IT governance. Unilateral inter-firm IT governance is a direct product of PI in an inter-firm relationship, as we have seen in the five dyads. This type of inter-firm IT governance is characterized by the dominance of one party in the dyad in all aspects of inter-firm IT operations: integration platforms, scope and data flow in information sharing, and support and maintenance of inter-firm IT systems. Because of the dominance of one party, either the supplier or the buyer, information sharing tends to be limited as long as the operational needs of the party in power are met.

Our evidence suggests that the sustainability of unilateral inter-firm IT governance hinges on the sustainability of the power-imbalanced inter-firm governance. Literature and our own evidence show that PI itself does not necessarily destabilize inter-firm relationship, due to the sustaining forces of inter-firm relational norms and trust. We submit that the unilateral inter-firm IT governance form can be stable and long lasting regardless of the power distribution between the two parties, as long as stable inter-firm governance exists. Our findings from this multi-case study have some significant theoretical and practical implications.

## Theoretical contributions

We believe that this is among the first studies that investigate inter-firm IT governance from the lens of inter-firm governance theories. We described a form of inter-firm IT governance, and identified its origin and factors that sustain its existence and constrain its effectiveness. Although we only found one form of inter-firm IT governance in all five dyads we studied, we suspect that the unilateral form is the most common inter-firm IT governance, given the primary cause of its formation – strong buyers and weak suppliers, or vice versa, in buyer– supplier dyads.

Second, we highlighted the relationship between interfirm IT governance and inter-firm governance. The formal contract-based inter-firm governance contributes to the buyer-dominated inter-firm IT relationship and thus the unilateral inter-firm IT governance form. Yet the long-term-oriented inter-firm relational norms and trust ensure the sustainability of the seemingly unstable interfirm relationship. This is in contrast to the predictions of resource dependence theory (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978), which argues that in a power-imbalanced relationship, the less powerful party will seek to correct the imbalance to survive over the long run. Our findings show that inter-firm relationships can survive over a long period of time under sustained PI without taking apparent corrective actions. In essence, we extended resource dependence theory by showing that the weaker firms in power-imbalanced exchanges do not have to resort to actions prescribed by Pfeffer & Salancik (1978) to survive, and the structurally imbalanced exchanges can be sustained if strong relational norms and inter-firm trust have been established and maintained by the parties who are motivated by their own survival and success.

Third, we developed a theoretical framework for understanding the antecedents and consequences of PI in inter-firm IT governance, allowing future research to glean variance theories, and empirically test and validate the core propositions of inter-firm IT governance and its relationship to inter-firm governance. Although the framework is defined based on buyer-dominated exchange dyads, there is no theoretical hurdle to extend the model and the propositions to supplier-dominated dyads. This framework could lead to the development and validation of a comprehensive inter-firm IT governance theory that explains what inter-firm IT governance entails, how it emerges and evolves, why it survives and sustains, and what it impacts in the context of inter-firm relationships.

Finally, our cases suggest that the extant intra-firm IT governance theories are inadequate for understanding inter-firm IT governance. This is mainly because in intrafirm IT governance the focus is on resource allocation – that is, who has the decision rights to initiate, fund, implement, and evaluate IT projects and applications – while in inter-firm IT governance the focus is on information sharing, that is, who has the decision rights to set the standards for the inter-firm IT platform and information sharing. Thus, this study lays the foundation for future research to build upon and extend IT governance theories to the more complex context of multiparticipant network relationships.

## Practical implications

This study also offers some prescriptive insights into managing inter-firm IT relationships. First of all, it shows that there can be stable and sustainable inter-firm IT relationships, even under strong power-imbalanced conditions. Business and IT managers in the weaker party of a dyad have to accept the PI in the inter-firm relationship as a normal situation, and they can and should work with their counterparts in the stronger party to improve interfirm collaborations. The discussion on relational norms and inter-firm trust should provide normative guidance to the thinking and behavior of the managers involved.

Second, in order for the stronger party to share more information, the weaker party must improve its own IT capability and business processes. The evidence suggests that information security of the weaker party, especially the security capabilities for confidential production and business information, is the primary concern of the stronger party when considering more data sharing within the dyad. This is clearly a legitimate concern, since information security is only as strong as the weakest link in the system. A weak partner with unpatched security holes could easily expose the entire system to internal or external competitive or criminal elements, thus causing significant damage to both parties.

Finally, managers in both parties of a dyad, especially the stronger party, must understand that the other party’s reluctance to demand information does not necessarily indicate that the information is unimportant to the efficient and effective operations of one or both parties. Often, the operations of the weaker party could significantly improve with more information from the other party, which could then reduce the total cost of operation in the dyad. Managers should take a more proactive approach to sharing operational and possibly strategic information, which will facilitate greater business process integration and thus benefit all parties involved. To do this, the stronger party might have to assist and invest in the weaker party to improve its IT capability and business processes, to protect the entire dyad from interference and maximize operational efficiency and effectiveness.

## Limitations

While we believe our study makes significant contributions to the current theory and practice of IT governance, we must qualify our findings with known limitations. The most significant is that all five dyads we studied are similar in terms of the power distribution pattern – buyer dominance. The lack of variety in power distribution, while providing strong support for the unilateral inter-firm IT governance concept, limits its broader application. Future research is needed to focus on the dyads that have relatively balanced power distribution or reversed power distribution in order to develop a comprehensive understanding of inter-firm IT governance. The second limitation lies in the fact that all five dyads are in the manufacturing sector of their respective industries. Future research could enrich the findings by including dyads in other sectors, such as service, financial, and retail. Third, we are limited by the number of firms that were willing to participate in this study and the number of managers who were available for interviews when we visited the sites, therefore selfselection bias could exist. Empirical studies with much broader participation and larger sample size are certainly needed to test and validate the findings and propositions of this study. Finally, some of our interviews were conducted in group settings. Even though we believe such interview format has merits in the context of this study, it may limit the ability of individuals to provide more thoughtful and enriched perspectives on the issues of concern.
