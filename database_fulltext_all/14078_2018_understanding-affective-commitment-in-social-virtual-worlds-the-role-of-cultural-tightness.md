## Abstract

Understanding how cultural differences affect the formation of users’ affective commitment (ACO) to social virtual world (SVW) services is a critical but understudied issue. Using a novel, norm-based cultural approach, we introduce cultural tightness (CT) to capture cultural differences in users’ collective environment and postulate a model that investigates the contingent effects of CT on the relationships between ACO and its drivers. A two-level analysis of survey data gathered from 400 field users of an influential SVW service and secondary data of CT reveals that the effects of satisfaction, calculative commitment, and subjective norm on ACO are contingent on CT.

Keywords

Affective Commitment; Cultural Tightness; Social Virtual World

## 1. Introduction

Originated as environments for pleasure, social virtual world (SVW) services have nowadays attained legitimacy in educational, business, and government settings because of their large user bases and extensive applications in global distributed collaboration, project management, and real-time simulation [1]. Users’ sustainable participation and commitment in SVW services has been recognized as crucial to the sustainability of SVW services, and failure to maintain existing relationship will seriously jeopardize the viability and sustainability of SVW services [2, 3]. Thus, it is critical to understand why users are willing to continuously engage in a particular SVW service. A pivotal construct in this context is affective commitment (ACO) which has shown substantial effects on a wide range of important individual behaviors and outcomes [4-6], such as willingness to pay [3]. Particularly, among various dimensions of commitment, ACO has been repeatedly found to be the most influential in predicting users’ continuance intention in SVWs [6, 7]. As such, ACO is deemed to be the core of users’ commitment to SVW services [8] and the most critical predictor of a set of post-adoption behavioral outcomes.

Although the significance of ACO has been widely acknowledged, much remains unknown about how individuals develop their ACO to SVW services and what contextual conditions will influence the development of ACO [9, 10]. In line with the dual model of relationship maintenance in consumer behavior domain that focuses on intrapersonal factors [3, 11], previous SVW studies suggest that users derive a certain level of ACO to SVW services from not only net benefits (e.g., satisfaction) but also constraint factors (e.g., calculative commitment (CCO)) [6, 10]. In addition to the intrapersonal factors in the dual model, social influence as an interpersonal factor is also suggested to affect users’ behaviors and their relationships with IT applications [12] because SVWs maintain deep ties to the offline world [13]. Therefore, on the basis of the dedication-constraint framework, we explain users’ ACO by integrating the concept of subjective norm to represent social influence, with satisfaction and CCO to represent two distinct intrapersonal mechanisms.

Furthermore, the challenges induced by the globalization of SVW services and diversity within the distribution of users make it crucial to take cultural differences into consideration. Unlike other online

#

communities that contain predefined “closed culture,” SVWs are not clearly goal-oriented and tend to be more easily influenced by users’ offline cultures [13]. In other words, users’ practices will not be constrained by any predefined themes in SVWs, but affected by their offline cultural environments [14]. A review of previous research on the impact of cultural factors in SVW (see Appendix A) revealed that value-based approach is dominant but the findings are mixed. While some studies indicate no significant differences between users from different cultures [14], others demonstrate culture as a vital factor impacting how people interact with each other [15] and manage their depth and breadth of self-disclosure [16] and self-representation [17] in SVWs. Although those studies tap into the impact of cultural factors in SVW, they seem to skirt the issue of the development of users’ ACO. For a worldwide virtual world with users across different cultures like Second Life, culture incongruence is suggested to play a vital role in thoroughly understanding its users’ development of ACO [18-20]. Also, previous SVW studies indicate the need for cross-cultural research to empirically deliberate on the impact of cultural differences in virtual worlds [15, 21-23]. Thus, an examination of the role culture plays in the development of users’ ACO in SVWs across a large variety of cultural backgrounds could shed light on this understudied area and provide insightful implications for practitioners to better design SVW and tailor their services to foster users’ ACO toward it.

Moreover, the existing literature lacks consensus on the influences and implications of cultural differences for ACO on both theoretical and empirical levels [24]. Theoretically, competing (even contradicting) arguments exist regarding how cultural values influence ACO. For instance, some scholars posit that in collectivist cultures, the level of ACO should be higher as a result of the significance of socia ties (e.g., [25]), whereas others argue that ACO should be greater in individualist cultures because of the facilitation of individual-focused attachment (e.g., [26]). Empirically, there are notable inconsistent findings regarding the relations between ACO and cultural values. Although some studies reveal that the level of ACO varies across different national cultural values (such as power distance and individualism [27, 28]), others find insignificant correlation between these cultural values and ACO [24]. This lack of consensus is regrettable, because understanding how individuals in different cultural environments form

their favorable (or unfavorable) perceptions of SVW services is crucial for practitioners to manage the services.

The inconsistencies of previous studies imply that cultural differences may not exert direct effects on ACO in SVWs, but rather have indirect impacts by moderating the effects of other relevant predictors of focuses on one facet of culture (i.e., value-based) and neglects the fact that users’ practices in SVWs do not occur in a cultural void, but depend on the collective cultural environment wherein users acquire their fundamental norms that shape their thoughts, beliefs, and behaviors [14, 30-32]. Hence, another crossculture research approach allowing for the overall contextual cultural differences of users is urgently needed to further the cross-cultural research in SVWs domain. Drawing upon the norm-based approach, we introduce a specific dimension of culture—cultural tightness, which refers to “the strength of social norms and the degree of sanctioning within societies” [31, p.1226]. Unlike the value-based approach, our norm-based approach emphasizes on how shared norms within an individual’s collective environments guide and shape his/her attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors in SVWs [33]. In consideration of contextual norms, a more comprehensive understanding of ACO may be attained from a theoretical framework that explores the interaction between cultural tightness and other predictors. Therefore, in an attempt to extend the existing literature of ACO and cross-culture research in SVW domain, this paper aims to address the following research question (RQ):

RQ: To what extent does cultural tightness moderate the influence of satisfaction, calculative commitment, and subjective norm on user’s affective commitment?

To probe into this question, we postulate a model of users’ ACO in SVWs and investigate the interactions between its drivers and cultural tightness, as well as its outcome. Specifically, we follow the norm-based approach of culture by investigating the moderating role of cultural tightness in the dedication-based, constraint-based, and social influence mechanisms leading to ACO, which subsequently promotes usage behavior. We collect empirical data globally from 400 field users of an influential SVW (Second Life), along with secondary data on cultural tightness, to test the model at both international and

regional levels.

As such, contributions of this study lie in the enrichment of cross-cultural SVW research and commitment literature. Firstly, we comprehensively appropriate the norm-based approach to investigate the effect of cultural differences in SVWs and is among the first to offer a theoretical account of the (SVWs in particular). Secondly, this study extends our current understanding of cultural tightness pertaining to IT applications in general and online service usage in particular. Thirdly, while previous studies stressed the impact of ACO, we extend the commitment literature by integrating the intrapersonal factors (i.e., satisfaction and CCO) with interpersonal factor (i.e., subjective norm), as well as cultural tightness as a moderator, to constitute a comprehensive knowledge of the development of ACO. Lastly, we open the way for exploring the impact of cultural tightness at different levels by conducting analyses across over 20 nations at both international and regional levels. This is a notable extension to the crossculture literature as scholars in this area have called for going beyond the comparison of two or three countries [20], in order to improve the validity and generalizability of research findings.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. To begin with, we review the existing literature explaining users’ ACO to online services and discussing the factors that contribute to its development (Section 2). Then, we discuss the existing research on cultural factors in the context of SVWs; specifically, by reviewing the existing cross-culture literature on user behavior, we introduce the construct of cultural tightness to the context of online services (Section 3). Subsequently, we develop our research model and hypotheses in Section 4. We describe the research method in detail in Section 5 and present a thorough report of results in Section 6. Finally, we discuss this study’s implications, limitations, and opportunities for future research (Section 7), before concluding the paper (Section 8).

## 2. Theoretical Background: ACO to Online Services

Commonly applied in the organizational behavior literature, ACO refers to an individual’s emotional attachment which is represented as a psychological identification with and strong affective ties to an organization [34], that is, the “emotional attachment to, identification with, and involvement in the

organization” [35, p.1]. It was originally proposed to interpret why volunteers in nonprofit organizations demonstrate different levels of dedication [36], which is similar to individuals’ voluntary behavior in SVWs. ACO represents a desire to take a certain course of action when an individual becomes involved in, derives his/her identity from, and is emotionally attached to the pursuit of certain course of action or component that enables an individual to follow a certain course of action because of favorable perception and attitudes [4, 37]. ACO, which has been identified as the most critical commitment process and underlying psychological outcome of individual’s perceptions, was indeed proven to be correlated to a great deal of measures for “outcome” [4].

Recently, based on relationship marketing research [11] and organizational behavior literature [4], researchers in IS domain have also recognized the significance of ACO in the usage of IT applications and online services [6, 7]. ACO can independently influence user behavior, even in the absence of positive attitude and motivation or with the presence of conflicting attitudes or motives [4, 7]. To encourage users’ post-adoptive usage, the providers of SVW services have to find a way to improve users’ ACO. Despite its great importance, there is still a lack of thorough understanding of the development of users’ ACO to SVWs, while the few exceptions (e.g., [10]) only focused on users’ perception of various benefits.

According to the dual model of relationship maintenance in consumer behavior literature [3, 11], users are motivated to maintain relationships with an SVW and devote a high level of ACO to it either because they genuinely want to (a dedication-based mechanism) or they simply feel locked in (a constraint-based mechanism) [3, 11]. It has long been established in the existing literature that both dedication- and constraint-based mechanisms should be considered in developing a comprehensive understanding of relationship maintenance. The dual-motivation theory (dedication-constraint framework) is useful in analyzing long-term relationship in both online and offline contexts, such as employee–firm relationships [11] and online consumer–firm relationships [3]. Given the complexity of users’ relationship with SVW service, the dedication-constraint framework provides a theoretical basis for us to investigate users’ ACO to SVW service from an intrapersonal perspective. Based on this conceptual framework, two distinct factors, satisfaction and CCO, are proposed to characterize users’ relationship maintenance with SVW services, specifically their ACO to SVW.

Dedication-based mechanism occurs when users desire to maintain or improve the relationship because of net benefits. It centers on the concept of satisfaction, which is defined as the extent of users’ positive attitude and belief concerning SVW services [3]. The positive impact of satisfaction on users’ relationship maintenance, such as loyalty [3] and ACO [6, 38], has been confirmed in numerous studies [3, 6]. Organizational commitment research has also demonstrated that satisfaction (e.g., job satisfaction and pay satisfaction) is strongly correlated with ACO [39].

Constraint-based mechanism occurs when users believe they cannot get out of the relationship with SVW services due to social, economic, or psychological costs and they have no other choice. The central concept in the constraint-based mechanism is CCO, which is defined as the extent to which an individual perceives that she/he is locked in the relationship with SVW given the potential cost of leaving it [6, 35, 37]. According to the commitment literature, CCO develops when an individual realizes the costs of failing to follow a certain course of action [35] and/or perceives that there is lack of alternatives other than to do so [4]. On account of a psychological process of self-justification [11], users who are “locked in” a relationship tend to justify the rationality to themselves that they have committed to the relationship out of desire by investing additional resources and enhancing their ACO to a certain course of action [3, 6, 40]. Such an escalation of ACO arises from a kind of “retrospective rationality,” whereby previous investments or costs are regarded as relevant to the decision to engage in SVW services [41]. Hence, a user’s CCO to the SVW service represents the “lock-in” effect associated with previous investment and, consequently, is a key factor in the constraint-based mechanism that contributes to ACO.

In addition to the intrapersonal predictors in the dual model, social influence derived from subjective norm is also demonstrated to be a crucial interpersonal factor that influences users’ behaviors and their relationship with IT applications [12]. Considering the correspondence of social influence as well as individual behaviors between SVW and real world, experimental research is set out to investigate whether

#

and to what extent individual behaviors in SVWs mirror those in the real world [1, 42, 43]. Subjective norm, which is defined as “a person's perception that most people who are important to him think he should or should not perform the behavior in question” [44], is suggested to play a critical role in users choice, creation, and personalization of avatars [45], and even their moral lives [43] in SVWs as in real variables of subjective norms, such as employee’s organizational support [47], have been proven to influence individuals’ ACO, the influence of subjective norm on ACO has received little attention [48], especially in online service settings. Here, subjective norm is taken into consideration as the manifestation of social influence mechanism and considered to be a key factor that will influence users’ ACO to SVW services [49].

## 3. Theoretical Background: Norm-Based Cultural Approach

The rapid development of globalization has provoked a wide range of research on how different cultures influence the user behavior of IT application, especially their commitment. Previous research adopted a cultural value-based approach to demonstrate that culture has a bearing on individuals’ social cognitions and behaviors not only in offline world but also in virtual world [17], implying that the differences in users’ attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors across offline cultures can also be observed in SVWs [23]. For instance, users’ culture backgrounds of collectivism–individualism and high–low power distance are found to play a vital role in determining their attitudes toward design feature in virtual worlds [20]. Cross-national cultural differences are also evident in people’s knowledge-sharing activities in virtual worlds [50], and in the time spent, importance and motives to social networking site use [21]. However, Kim, Sohn and Choi [14] find no significant cultural differences between the motivation for using social networking sites in US and Korea. The inconsistencies of previous studies imply that offline cultures might exert an indirect, rather than direct, effect on individual’s attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors in SVWs [7]. Besides, while those studies tap into the impact of cultural factors in SVW, there seems to be little, if any, research on the issue of the development of users’ ACO.

In organizational commitment research, previous studies mostly focused on cultural values (i.e.,

#

cross-situational principles that guide individual’s life [51]) to examine the effect of culture on ACO. Studies adopting the value-based approach usually involve value-based constructs such as uncertainty avoidance, collectivism and individualism, and power distance (e.g., [52, 53]). For example, ACO was found to be positively correlated with collectivism for employees in Turkey [54], and a similar significant correlation was also found between ACO and power distance [55].

Recent cross-culture research in IS domain has also paid attention to ACO in online services context. These studies investigate the impact of cultural differences on how ACO is developed [10] and how it leads to behavioral outcomes, such as continuance intention [6]. For instance, based on Hofstede’s culture value data, Zhou et al. [10] suggested that users with low individualism showed a stronger impact of ACO on continuance intention, than users with high individualism. These findings not only highlight the significance of cultural differences to the behavioral outcomes of ACO but also imply the importance of taking cultural differences into consideration when exploring the development of ACO itself.

Despite extensive studies using the value-based approach, there is no consensus on the influences and significance of cultural differences on ACO [24]. There exist competing arguments of how cultural values influence ACO. For instance, some studies suggested that individuals will develop a higher level of ACO in collectivist cultures owing to the significance of social ties (e.g., [25]), while others suggested that ACO should be greater in individualist cultures on account of the facilitation of individual-focused attachment (e.g., [26]). Besides, inconsistent empirical findings of the relations between ACO and cultural values cannot be overlooked. For instance, Clugston et al. [52] did not find a significant relation between collectivism and ACO among American government workers. Similarly, both Fischer et al. [26] and Meyer et al. [24] found a non-significant correlation between Hofstede’s measures of cultural value and ACO. On the contrary, Meyer et al. [24] found a significant and positive relation between ACO and institutional collectivist value, which implicitly suggests that potential influences arise from the collective environments of cultures.

Apart from the neglect of potential indirect effects of cultural differences, the competing findings in previous studies may stem from an inadvertent focus solely on one aspect of culture—cultural values

#

while neglecting other aspects of cultural differences. To the best of our knowledge, only value-based approach was adopted in previous research on the impact of cultural factors in SVW. While the valuebased approach emphasizes relatively internally ingrained values, recent research suggests that culture also exists in norms and constraints that shape behavior and thought [30-32], thus representing a normindividual’s collective environments guide and shape his/her attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors. Although differences in cultural values may not directly influence users’ development of ACO, differences in shared norms will largely influence how users interpret their perceptions, shape their psychological outcomes, and eventually develop different level of ACO. However, as a crucial component of societal normative context, the strength of culture-embedded norm on IT application’s usage behavior has not been investigated yet. Unlike the most commonly applied value-based approach, the importance of norm-based approach has not been recognized in the IS domain. We believe that a norm-based approach will yield additional insights to the cross-cultural IS research to the extent that culture is regarded as a society’s own commitment to enforcing norms and constraints rather than nothing but values [31].

Compared with traditional online services, social presence is even more facilitated in SVW services. Therefore, whether and how norms and behaviors transfer from real word to virtual world is highly relevant to the context of SVWs, and individuals’ behaviors in SVWs are suggested to be regulated by social norms as in the physical world [42, 56]. In line with the extant literature, norms are expected to play a clear and significant role in users’ engagement with SVWs. Hence, understanding whether and how the shared norms within users’ real-life collective environments will influence their participation in SVWs becomes critical. To do so, we introduce the construct of cultural tightness into this study by following the norm-based approach of culture.

Cultural tightness reflects “the strength of social norms, or how clear and pervasive norms are within societies, and the strength of sanctioning, or how much tolerance there is for deviance from norms within societies” [31, p.1226]. It has been proven to be related to but distinct from cultural value dimensions, such as collectivism–individualism, power distance, and conservatism [32]. Ecological challenges such as

#

resource scarcity, high population density, and environmental threats will lead to an increase in cultural tightness as the need for strong norms and strict sanctions of deviation behaviors is increased [32]. Although the discussion of cultural tightness can be traced back to 1960s, it has not been systematically studied until recently. Gelfand and colleagues (2011) conducted a multinational study and found that the related to various organizational outcomes including leadership [57], organizational innovativeness [58], and CEO discretion [59].

It is noteworthy that cultural tightness is embodied in institutional practice, which will essentially affect individual-level cognition, motivation, and behavior [31]. Therefore, the way individuals are socialized is guided and shaped by cultural tightness, which in turn influences individuals’ thoughts, attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors [30]. In tight-culture societies, social norms are distinct and strictly enforced with severe sanctions [30], and there are situational constraints that reflect a vast variety of proper behaviors in everyday life. Hence, tight cultures are generally more predictable, orderly, and efficient [32]. On the contrary, social norms in loose cultures are usually unclear, and loose cultures tend to be more tolerant of deviation behaviors from norms. Over time, cultural tightness will shape individual-level cognitive style and motivation, which eventually influence individuals’ beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors [30]. For instance, Toh and Leonardelli [60] posited that cultural tightness would lead to a reluctance to change the existing course of actions and practices and thus impede the emergence of female leaders in tight cultures, while Chua et al. [30] suggested that cultural tightness would influence individuals’ creativity engagement and success. Therefore, in this study, we propose that cultural tightness has an impact on the development of users’ ACO as it will shape and guide individuals’ attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors.

## 4. Theory Development and Research Hypotheses

Based on the relevant literature presented earlier, we develop a model that helps explain how users develop their ACO to SVW, which eventually leads to their actual usage of SVW, as shown in Figure 1. The concept of cultural tightness indicates the extent to which the norms are distinct, pervasive, and

strictly enforced with severe sanction, and how much deviated behavior is tolerated in a given society [31]. It is proposed to impact the development of ACO as it is embodied in a society’s institutional practices and the way individuals form their cognition, motivation, and behaviors [30]. As mentioned earlier, ACO here refers to users’ emotional and affective attachment to SVWs [6, 7, 35] and is influenced by satisfaction as a dedication-based mechanism and CCO as a constraint-based mechanism.

## 4.1. Cultural Tightness and Dedication-Based Mechanism

It is widely accepted in both tight and loose cultures that people tend to devote more efforts and derive emotional attachment when they hold a positive attitude and a favorable feeling toward a given subject like SVW service. Besides, in tight cultures, there also exist situational constraints that restrict certain range and degree of cognitive processes and behaviors as appropriate in everyday situations [32]. For instance, investing resources for favorable things is usually considered to be appropriate in tight cultures. Such situational constraints guide individuals’ beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors in tight cultures as implicit norms, which consequently reinforce the cognitive process of developing a certain level of identification and attachment of a given subject from the perceived benefit and satisfaction. Hence, users in tight cultures are generally more willing to dedicate efforts and derive attachment when they possess a favorable feeling toward SVW services. However, in loose cultures, such cognitive process is less

#

transparent, as there exist greater tolerance and flexibility in dealing with positive attitudes and favorable feelings. Because there is no institutionally reinforced way to deal with positive attitudes and favorable feelings, users will not necessarily develop as much attachment or identification out of conformation to implicit norms as in tight cultures. Therefore, in loose cultures, users’ behaviors toward a given object when they have positive attitudes and emotions are not as predictive and orderly as in tight cultures [30]. They will be less likely affected by the implicit norms than their counterparts in tight cultures. Following this reasoning, users in tight cultures tend to develop a higher level of ACO when they hold positive attitudes and favorable feelings toward SVW service, as compared to those in loose cultures. Accordingly, we propose the first hypothesis as follows:

H1: The positive influence of users’ satisfaction on their ACO is stronger for high-tightness users than for low-tightness users.

## 4.2. Cultural Tightness and Constraint-Based Mechanism

As there exist minimal tolerance and strict sanction for deviated behaviors, situational constraints that regulate a vast variety of proper behaviors widely exist in tight cultures. For instance, escalating time and efforts for a costly cause will be considered as inappropriate and be accused of wasting resources in tight cultures. Owing to those situational constraints, individuals in tight cultures tend to avoid any deviated behaviors, including deviations from norms and situational constraints [30, 31]. Even though users would act on their self-interest by trying to justify their past investments as the manifestations of their emotional attachment and identification to the SVW [40], such escalation of commitment in tight cultures would be countered by the potential deviations from the situational constraints. As regulated by situational constraints in tight culture, the appropriate way for individuals to manage their previous investment is to avoid risk and further costs rather than to escalate commitment and invest additional resources. For instance, users in tight cultures will tend to end their business in SVW services if they find that it requires a regular input of time and efforts since they would like to avoid additional costs in the future. Users in tight cultures will find it hard in rationalizing their behavior by self-justification as it deviates from the situational constraints. On the contrary, users in a loose culture are less bounded by

norms and situational constraints and would be more inclined to escalate their ACO primarily based on their self-interest. Consequently, we expect that the positive influence of users’ CCO on their ACO will be stronger in loose cultures and propose the hypothesis as follows:

H2: The positive influence of users’ CCO on their ACO is weaker for high-tightness users than for low-tightness users.

## 4.3. Social Influence and Cultural Tightness

It has been long established that social influences, such as subjective norm, have a positive impact on user behavior [49]. Hence, in addition to intrapersonal factors in the dedication-constraint framework, we propose subjective norm as an interpersonal factor that will also contribute to users’ development of ACO. An SVW user’s subjective norm is defined here as the degree to which he/she perceives that others want him/her to use SVW services [46]. It reflects the influence of significant others’ opinions on the user’s behavior and emphasizes the value of conformity in the user’s behaviors. Users tend to guide their attitudes and form their beliefs toward the stimuli of others’ opinions and have the tendency to conform to others’ opinions due to the need for social companionship [46, 61]. If an SVW service is recommended by those people who are important to a user, he/she will likely comply with their opinions and endorse such social influence with ACO; even the user himself/herself has not formed attachment to and identification with the services per se. Such a feeling to follow and conform important others may lead to the development of ACO as a result of the internalization of the ideas or identification with the influencing parties [48]. Therefore, we propose our third hypothesis.

H3: Users’ subjective norm will positively influence their ACO to the SVW service.

The effect of subjective norm reflects users’ value of conformity and the extent to which users guide their attitudes and form beliefs toward the stimuli of important others’ opinion. People in different cultures differ in their responses to social influences and thus foster their affective commitment to variant extent. In tight cultures, norms are pervasive and explicit, and stringently enforced. There exist a minimal tolerance and strict sanction for deviation from norms [62, 63]. Moreover, various situational constraints in tight cultures also restrict certain range and degree of appropriate cognitive processes and behaviors

#

[32]. Hence, there exists a certain level of homogeneity in the population of tight cultures [60] wherein people’s values, norms, and behaviors tend to be similar to each other. Besides, institutional practices and situational constraints in tight cultures also facilitate psychological adaptations at individual level, such as self-monitoring and self-regulation [30]. As deviation behaviors in tight cultures will be quickly detected others’ opinions and behaviors to prevent potential deviations. Therefore, users in tight cultures exhibit a strong tendency to comply with important others’ opinions and regulate themselves according to subjective norm when considering ACO. On the contrary, users in loose cultures usually care less about the endorsement of their important others because they are less bounded by norms and situational constraints. Given that there exist greater tolerance and flexibility for deviated and unusual behaviors in loose cultures, users generally place little value in conformity to others and tend to behave less orderly on account of others’ perspectives rather than their own interests. Hence, we posit the hypothesis as follows:

H4: The positive influence of users’ subjective norm on their ACO is stronger for hightightness users than for low-tightness users.

## 4.4. ACO and Actual Usage

Previous literature suggested that affective commitment will enable individuals to continue in certain relationships out of positive attitudes, emotions, and perceptions which are derived from their own experiences [37]. In the context of SVWs, ACO was found to be a key predictor of users’ continuance intention [6], a surrogate of continued usage. Consistent with this logic, we expect that ACO will exert a positive impact on user’s actual usage behavior as well. Thus, we propose that:

H5: Users’ ACO to an SVW service will positively influence their actual usage of the service.

## 5. Methodology

The research site of this study is Second Life (www.secondlife.com), a typical SVW service. As one of the largest Internet-based user-created virtual world communities, Second Life has attracted millions of active users from around the world [64]. The large user base of Second Life and their wide distribution

across countries make it an ideal context for this research.

Data on cultural tightness were obtained from secondary sources (as detailed below), while primary data on the other constructs in the model (and the subjects’ personal characteristics) were collected via an online survey. In addition to the high generalizability of survey studies [65], online survey was chosen comparable validity, reliability, and quality, over traditional paper-based survey [66].

## 5.1. Measures of the Main Constructs in the Survey

To ensure validity, measurements for all constructs (except for cultural tightness) are adapted from the previous literature (Appendix B). Specifically, the measurements for affective and calculative commitment were adapted from Allen et al. [35] and Kim et al. [3]; those for satisfaction and subjective norm were adapted from Bhattacherjee [2] and Venkatesh et al. [12], respectively. In particular, we draw “perceptual” self-reported measure of the actual usage from Venkatesh et al. [67], which is considered to be appropriate as relative measures [68]. We followed the process suggested by Moore et al. [69] and conducted a simplified card sorting procedure (seven doctoral students were requested to independently sort the mixed-up cards with items) to further ensure construct validity. As well, we conducted a pilot study among students and research staff in a large university before the questionnaire was finalized.

Satisfaction was measured by a 7-point semantic differential scale [2] ranging from 1 (extremely dissatisfied) to 7 (extremely satisfied), while the actual usage were measured by two 7-point scale items (i.e., duration of use and frequency of use) ranging from 1 (0–10 minutes) to 7 (over 5 hours) and from 1 (less than per month) to 7 (several times per day), respectively. All the other items in questionnaire were measured by a 7-point Likert scale in a range from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

## 5.2. Primary Data Collection

We posted the finalized questionnaire on a popular servicing website for online survey and activated it for global access in a time period of six weeks. By verifying the submission hyperlinks and Internet Protocol addresses, repetitive submissions from the same respondent were forbidden.

#

To enhance the representativeness of the data sample, invitation messages were distributed through multiple channels to access the users of Second Life. We distributed the invitations mainly through four channels: (1) instant messages (IM) in Second Life, (2) posts on the forum operated by Second Life (www.secondlifeforum.com), (3) group emails to the mailing lists related to Second Life, and (4) posts on social media (Facebook and Twitter sites). In addition, the timing and virtual “places” of the invitation distributed through IM were carefully arranged to ensure that our distribution reached users from diverse geographical sectors in Second Life and different time zones in the real world.

To solicit more responses, we sent several rounds of reminders and, meanwhile, provided nominal economic incentives. In all, 400 valid responses were received (see Appendix C for the respondents’ profile). A wide variety and a well balance across demographic features appear in our sample, with a large portion of experienced and heavy users who are likely to possess well-formed beliefs about Second Life [70]. Given the reasonable level of usage experiences, our sample largely fits the research objective. The respondents come from 22 different countries. Most of them are US citizens (55%) followed by members from the UK (7%) and Hong Kong (6%). These figures are rather representative for Second Life as it is a platform hosted and mainly promoted in the US [71].

As a measure of non-response bias, the 175 early respondents (who answered the survey in the first three weeks) were compared with the 225 late respondents (who answered the survey in the last three weeks) in their key characteristics.<sup>1</sup> Our chi-square tests suggested that these two groups of respondents are not significantly different in their demographics (gender, age, education, and occupation in real life) and geographic distribution. Because late respondents are more analogous to non-respondents than are early ones [72], the testing results indicate that our respondents were similar to those non-respondents and our sample appeared to reflect the actual distribution across nations.

## 5.3. Sample Splitting Based on Secondary Data

#

Because individual behavior is influenced by culture at different layers ranging from regional, national, to group level [73], here we utilized secondary data to measure cultural tightness and validate our model at both international level and regional level, to alleviate common method bias and advance cross-culture research. Specifically, we adopted the scale of cultural tightness at both national and on a national-level cultural tightness and, hence, is adopted as a data source for our current study. With data from 6823 respondents across 33 nations, these authors measured cultural tightness with a six-item scale, assessing the number and clarity of norms, the extent of tolerance for deviations, and overall compliance with norms in each culture. Second, given the large proportion of US citizens (55%) in our sample and ample empirical evidence of significant cultural differences across the US regions [74], it is reasonable to also take the differences in regional cultural tightness into consideration to further categorize the US respondents and validate our conceptual model at the regional level. Based on a composite index of nine items which reflect punishment strength, latitude/permissiveness, institutions that reinforce moral order, and situational constraints and foreign population proportion, Harrington et al. [74] came up with a valid and reliable cultural tightness–looseness index across the 50 US states. Detailed scores of cultural tightness at national and regional levels are reported in Appendix D.

To validate our model at different levels (and particularly for testing the moderating effects of cultural tightness), we firstly extract US respondents $( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { U S } } { = } 2 2 0 )$ (hereafter, referred to as US sample) from the overall sample (N=400) for the analysis at the regional level and conduct international-level analysis with the rest of the respondents $( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { I N } } { = } 1 8 0 )$ (hereafter referred to as international sample).<sup>2</sup> We sourced cultural tightness scores at the national level to classify the international sample $( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { I N } } { = } 1 8 0 )$ as a pair of comparable subsamples, i.e., tight (N<sub>1</sub>=93) vs. loose culture $( \mathrm { N } _ { 2 } { = } 8 7 )$ ), and cultural tightness score at the regional level to classify the US sample $( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { U S } } { = } 2 2 0 )$ as a pair of comparable subsamples, i.e., tight

#

(N<sub>3</sub>=87) vs. loose culture (N<sub>4</sub>=133). To ensure the objectivity of the cultural tightness scale, we use the mean scores of cultural tightness—6.5 at the worldwide national level [32] and 50.14 at the US regional level [74]—as the thresholds for categorizing the sample at the national and regional level, respectively. Specifically, for the international-level analysis, the tight-culture subsample contained non-US respondents scoring 6.5 and above on cultural tightness, while the loose-culture subsample included those respondents with scores below 6.5 on the national cultural tightness dimension. Similarly, for the regional-level analysis, the tight-culture subsample contained US respondents scoring 50.14 and above on cultural tightness, while the loose-culture subsample included those respondents with scores below 50.14 on the regional cultural tightness dimension.

## 6. Data analysis and results

This study used partial least squares (PLSs) to examine the research model on account of its advantage in exploratory research [75]. Moreover, component-based structural equation modeling techniques performed by PLS are appropriate for between-group comparison with small sample size and small number of items [76, 77]. Specifically, the software package of SmartPLS 2.0 was used.

Meanwhile, the direct impact of subjective norm on ACO is not supported, which also highlights the contingent effect of cultural tightness. In addition, the influence of ACO on the actual usage is supported across all the overall samples and subsamples.

## 7. Discussion

Considering the vital role of affective commitment in predicting user’s behavior in SVWs, this study examines the factors that impact users’ ACO in SVW services from a cross-culture perspective. Particularly, we explore the contingent effects of cultural tightness on the influences of intrapersonal (i.e., satisfaction and CCO) and interpersonal (i.e., subjective norm) factors on ACO. As the results of international- and regional-level analyses are not quite the same, a set of interesting findings are revealed.

Firstly, with regard to intrapersonal factors, the effect of dedication-based mechanism (as represented by satisfaction) is found to be moderated by cultural tightness in predicting user’s ACO to SVWs in the international-level analysis but not in the regional-level analysis, while the effect of constraint-based mechanism (as reflected in CCO) in predicting user’s ACO to SVWs is found to be moderated by cultural tightness in both the international- and regional-level analyses. Specifically, satisfaction exerts a stronger effect on ACO in the tight-culture model than in the loose-culture model in the international-level analysis, while that of CCO is much stronger in the loose-culture model than in the tight-culture model in both the international- and regional-level analyses. Interestingly, the results of the international-level analysis with the overall sample including US respondents (N=400) and the international sample excluding US respondents $( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { I N } } { = } 1 8 0 )$ are consistent, suggesting that US as a whole country is not unique. However, different patterns emerge when we investigate the moderating role of cultural tightness on the relation between satisfaction and ACO at the regional level within US.

#

One possible interpretation is that the national- and regional-level tightness scores emphasize and capture different aspects of cultural tightness, as these studies use different methods to measure tightness score [74]. National-level tightness score is assessed directly via a six-item scale which lies emphasis on the extent to which norms are clearly defined and strictly enforced [32], while regional-level cultural tightness score is based on archival data and relates heavily to the degree of constraint and punishment [74]. That is, the extent to which situational constraints in tight cultures are explicitly defined and the extent to which they restrict and regulate individual’s cognitive process and behavior is more reflected in the national-level tightness score. Hence, the cognitive process of developing certain level of identification and attachment of SVW services from the perceived benefit and satisfaction tends to be captured more by the national- rather than the regional-level tightness score, which is probably why the regional-level analysis did not yield the similar result about the moderating effect of cultural tightness on the relation between satisfaction and ACO.

Secondly and importantly, intriguing findings have been revealed for how ACO derives from social influences (subjective norm in particular). In this study, the path from subjective norm to ACO is insignificant for the overall sample (H3 not supported), although social influence (subjective norm) was suggested to have a positive impact on user behavior in the previous literature [49]. This insignificant path is surprising at the first sight, but our regional-level analysis result regarding H4 provides a plausible explanation of it. That is, subjective norm may influence users’ ACO significantly only in tight cultures in the regional-level analysis where there exists strong pressure to comply with others and strictly enforced punishment as the deterrence to avoid potential deviations. As such, users in tight cultures will be more likely to regulate themselves according to the opinions of their significant others and behave accordingly. On the contrary, users in loose cultures are less affected by social influences. Given that there exist less clear norms and greater tolerance for deviated behaviors in loose cultures, users in loose cultures care less about others’ opinions and tend to behave heterogeneously and less orderly.

Inconsistent findings exist regarding subjective norm between international- and regional-level analyses. One possible explanation for this is that the measures of national- and regional-level tightness scores have different focuses. As mentioned earlier, regional-level tightness score focuses more on the punishment and restriction aspects of cultural tightness while national-level tightness score only captures little about them. As such, regional-level tightness score is more relevant to the extent to which deviation behavior will be quickly detected and strictly sanctioned; therefore, the moderating effect of cultural on subjective norm in the international-level analysis might be minimal because national-level tightness score focuses more on defined norms rather than sanctions of deviation behaviors. Taken together, subjective norm provides additional explanatory power of ACO’s variation in the tight-culture model; in other words, it accounts for more variation of ACO in the tight-culture model as compared to the looseculture model.

Thirdly, based on the post hoc analysis, this study also puts forward a latent mechanism that bridges users’ satisfaction, CCO, and subjective norm to their usage behaviors in the context of SVW services, wherein ACO is found to be an effective linkage. That is, in addition to being a key predictor of user’s post-adoptive behavior, ACO also exerts its role as the mediator between user’s attitude, perception, belief, and their actual behavior.

Finally, two-level analysis of cultural tightness provides nuanced insights for us to interpret the competing findings about the influence of cultural differences on ACO. Based on our results, cultural tightness strengthens the influence of the dedication-based mechanism (as represented by satisfaction) in the international-level analysis and social influence (subjective norm) in the regional-level analysis, but weakens the influence of the constraint-based mechanism (as reflected in CCO) at both the internationaland regional level analyses. Thus, the level of ACO is highly relevant to but not solely dependent on cultures; rather, it is determined by a combination of culture and a set of other factors that are either intraor interpersonal.

## 7.1. Theoretical Implications

First and foremost, although most prior research on cultural factors in SVWs adopts the value-based approach [17, 20, 21], this study comprehensively appropriates the norm-based approach to investigate the effect of cultural differences. This norm-based approach identifies a critical dimension of culture that has been relatively overlooked in the previous literature (i.e., cultural tightness) and provides understanding about how the shared norms within users’ real-life collective environments influence their development of ACO in SVWs. In addition, as existing literature focuses on the direct impact of culture on users’ behavior in SVWs, this study makes us aware of the contingent effects of cultural tightness from a norm-based approach. Thus, this study moves a step forward in the investigation of whether cultural factor matters in SVWs. Furthermore, our study, to the best of our knowledge, is among the first to examine the impact of culture on the development of ACO in SVWs, arguably one of the most influential factors driving SVW usage.

Second, while existing research on cultural tightness mainly investigates its impact on individual personality and organizational behavior in the offline settings [30, 60], the current study extends the applicable domain of cultural tightness to the context of SVW services. The results highlight the importance of cultural tightness in understanding the development of ACO of SVW services.

Third, this study also advances the commitment literature and enriches our current understanding of the formation of ACO by integrating intrapersonal factors (i.e., satisfaction and CCO in the dedicationconstraint framework) with interpersonal factor (i.e., subjective norm as the manifestation of social influence). While prior studies usually considered ACO as a result of users perceiving benefits (e.g., satisfaction) from SVWs [6], we extend the dedication-constrained framework by also incorporating subjective norm (representing a social-influence mechanism) to the explanatory model. Specifically, the relationship between subjective norm and ACO has been established in the tight-culture model in the regional-level analysis. More importantly, from the norm-based perspective of cultural differences, the results of this study shed some light on how ACO is developed under various levels of cultural tightness. Furthermore, our findings fill a gap in the IS literature, that is, the lack of empirical examinations of the mediating roles of affective commitment in the relationships between user’s attitude, belief, and perception and their actual behaviors.

Finally, our two-level analysis extends cross-cultural SVW research by exploring the impact of cultural tightness across a variety of cultural backgrounds at both international and regional levels. Responding to the call for a comprehensive examination of cultural factor in SVW context, this study goes beyond the comparison of users from two or three nations [20] and explores users’ development of international, regional, or organizational level, we conduct an investigation at both the international and regional levels, enriching our knowledge about how a variety of offline cultural differences across nations and regions affect users’ commitment in SVWs. Our findings further reveal the subtle differences between cultural tightness scores at two different levels, thus opening the venue for future research to examine the theoretical distinctions between these different cultural tightness measures.

## 7.2. Practical Implications

This study provides profound insights for practitioners into how to improve users’ ACO and consequently their actual usage of SVW services, which will largely enhance the viability of SVWs. Especially, this study provides a reference for how to localize their SVW services and apply different strategies to improve users’ ACO and subsequent usage, as it is crucial to take individuals’ culture differences into consideration and approach individuals differently according to their offline cultural background. Knowledge of the contingent effects of cultural tightness on users’ affective commitment to SVW services opens up favorable opportunities for culturally customized SVW services. SVW service providers could leverage on our research findings and tailor their services to diverse needs of their users from different cultures. Specifically, they are encouraged to place more emphasis on the dedication-based mechanism and social influences for users in tight cultures but focus more on the constraint-based mechanism for those in loose cultures. For instance, practitioners could manage the dedication-based mechanism and social influences by incorporating functions that allow users to get bonus by sharing their “work” in SVWs to others in tight cultures. In this case, users who get bonus will develop a sense of attachment out of satisfaction; their audiences will be encouraged to engage in SVW services as well.

## 7.3. Limitations and Future Research Directions

#

Just like all research, this study has limitations. First, as we gathered the survey data from a single SVW service, the generalizability of the findings to other SVW contexts cannot be assumed [85]. Although Second Life, as one of the most influential SVW services in the world, shares a lot of features in common with others, replicating this study in other SVW services would still improve the support for the contingent effects of cultural tightness on users’ development of ACO in SVWs, we urge future research to examine these effects under other conditions to advance the knowledge of cultural tightness.

While we have adopted the scale of cultural tightness at both national and regional levels, we encourage future research to further clarify the subtle differences between the two measures and replicate the investigation of cultural tightness at both the national and regional levels. Specifically, the moderating effect of cultural tightness on the relationship between subjective norm and ACO is only supported in the regional-level analysis in this study. We urge future research to take a closer look at the roles of subjective norm and cultural tightness in the development of ACO in SVWs. Also, future research can possibly measure cultural tightness at the regional level in non-US countries and investigate how the development of ACO is contingent on these regional differences in cultural tightness. Further, we would like to encourage future research to introduce IT design features to the current nomological framework and further extend it.

## 8. Conclusion

This study developed a research model for investigating the influence of cultural tightness on the development of users’ ACO in SVW services, by considering three key drivers identified in the literature: satisfaction (manifesting the dedication-based mechanism), CCO (manifesting the constraint-based mechanism), and subjective norm (manifesting the social-influence mechanism). While both satisfaction and CCO have salient direct effects on ACO, their relative importance is contingent on cultural tightness. Satisfaction has a stronger effect on ACO in tight cultures than in loose cultures in the international-level analysis, whereas the impact of CCO on ACO is much stronger in loose cultures than in tight cultures at

#

both the international and regional levels. Moreover, the path from subjective norm to ACO is also contingent on cultural tightness in the sense that it is significant only for tight-culture users at the regional level. Our findings enrich the cross-cultural research in SVW domain and commitment literature and provide a nuanced understanding for researchers and practitioners of the development of users’ ACO toward SVWs under various cultural circumstances.

## Appendix F: Multigroup SmartPLS Analysis

Multigroup SmartPLS Analysis is performed by making a parametric assumption, taking the standard errors for the structural paths and comparing the corresponding paths across different groups (tight and loose culture, in this study) by performing t-tests on their path coefficients, with the pooled standard error, as follows:

$$
S _ {p o o l e d} = \sqrt {\left\{\left[ (N _ {1} - 1) ^ {2} / (N _ {1} + N _ {2} - 2) \right] \times S E _ {1} ^ {2} + \left[ (N _ {2} - 1) ^ {2} / (N _ {1} + N _ {2} - 2) \right] \times S E _ {2} ^ {2} \right\}}
$$

$$
t _ {s p o o l e d} = (P C 1 _ {1} - P C _ {2}) / \left[ S _ {p o o l e d} \times \sqrt {(1 / N _ {1} + 1 / N _ {2})} \right]
$$

where

$S _ { p o o l e d }$ is the pooled estimator for the variance

?? refers to the t-statistic with $( N _ { 1 } + N _ { 2 } - 2 )$ degrees of freedom

$N _ { i }$ is the sample size of dataset for subsample i

$S E _ { i }$ is the standard error of path in the structural model of subsample i

$P C _ { i }$ is the path coefficient in the structural model of subsample i
