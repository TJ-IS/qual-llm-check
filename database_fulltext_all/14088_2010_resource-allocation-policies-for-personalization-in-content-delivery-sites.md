## 1. Introduction

One of the distinctive features of websites is their ability to garner enormous amounts of information about their visitors. Website owners can use this information to enhance subsequent visitor experience by providing targeted information or recommendations. This process of utilizing an individual’s information to deliver a targeted product is known as personalization. With the proliferation of Internet-based applications in recent years, firms are increasingly providing personalized services and products. For example, Amazon.com uses personalization techniques to recommend books and gifts to their customers. Double Click uses visitor profiles to target appropriate banner advertisements on their clients’ sites. Sites such as techrepublic.com analyze user clicks and articles read to determine which new articles to display to their readers. According to research and consulting firm

Strategy Analytics, the estimated global revenue for total personalization contents in 2006 was \$12 billion, with expected revenues of \$18 billion by 2010.

Personalization can reduce customer search costs and enhance customer loyalty, which translates into increased cash inflows and enhanced profitability (Ansari and Mela 2003). Häubl and Trifts (2000) have shown that in electronic shopping environments, personalized product recommendation enables customers to identify superior products with less search effort. Tam and Ho (2003), studying a website designed for music file downloads, find that consumers who receive personalized information typically download more music files. These works have demonstrated that personalization can be an effective tool for firms. Our focus here is on developing optimal policies for allocating personalization server resources in real time for providing such services on the Internet. Although such policies can be beneficial for both content delivery sites (e.g., The New York Times, Yahoo-News) and commerce sites (e.g., Amazon, eBay), we concentrate on the former. Personalization of content helps such sites enhance their customer base and increase revenues that are (at least partially) driven by online advertisements (Ihlstrom and Palmer 2002, Mermigas 2001). In addition, our policies are applicable to ad-server operations; such servers provide targeted advertisements on pages requested by customers.

To perform personalization, content delivery sites collect information about their visitors to develop user profiles. Profiles are formed based on a users’ clickstream data, and typically stored as word-vectors or session-vectors (Yan et al. 1996, Anderson et al. 2001). Given a user profile, the personalization engine tailors the contents of the page requested by the visitor, as well as the links to provide with that page (i.e., a personalized navigation space). Several techniques have been developed to provide real-time personalization by performing dynamic link generation when a user requests a new page. For instance, Yan et al. (1996) use session-vectors whose elements are pages in the website and the weight of each element is the frequency of browsing a certain page. When the user makes a new url request, the session-vector gets updated, and the personalization process proceeds by identifying other similar sessions. Anderson et al. (2001) use word-vectors to capture user profiles, and provide a method to compute the utility of the served page and embedded links by considering both the intrinsic utility of the page contents and the extrinsic utility of documents reachable by the embedded links.

For content-delivery sites, the goal is to provide personalized content and navigation space to each visitor by identifying and targeting their individual needs, an approach that is often termed one-to-one marketing (Peppers and Rogers 1997). Although current technologies provide an opportunity to deliver such service, the actual task of identifying the best possible personalized page and navigation space for each interaction with a user can be computationally intensive, thereby affecting a site’s performance (Chiu 2001). Sites typically use shallow pattern-matching techniques for efficiency considerations, which often leads to poorly matched recommendations; deep models (such as semantic networks) with good expressive power require significantly more computational resources (Gong 2004). Traditional techniques such as collaborative filtering do not scale easily because of the high computational cost of determining user-to-user or item-to-item correlation for a large number of items (e.g., pages) and users (Yan et al. 1996, Mobasher et al. 2000). The computational requirements can be further exacerbated when additional contextual information, such as time and location, need to be considered using more sophisticated approaches (Adomavicius et al. 2005). Although precomputing during off-peak times can help to some extent, it cannot provide perfectly personalized content because a visitor typically makes multiple requests within a session, most of which cannot be anticipated in advance. Similarly, computing the extrinsic utility of a page may be a difficult process, because estimating the expected utility of each reachable page involves, in turn, examining links on that page (Anderson et al. 2001). Consequently, it often becomes necessary to provide less-than-perfect personalization to a visitor (i.e., without exploring all possible solutions), and to provide a page that is suboptimal in terms of content and embedded links. A content-delivery website is therefore faced with the following trade-off: It can deliver a superior personalized version of the content to the visitor, possibly with a long delay because of the computational effort needed, or it can deliver an inferior version of the content more quickly.

The problem of allocating either less or more computing resources to provide personalized content to a request is further complicated when several requests are waiting for service. Now, the website needs to also consider the negative externalities associated with higher waiting costs for all other visitors who have requests pending. One approach to this problem is to generate an optimal schedule (sequence and amount of time allocated) for all requests waiting for service, and then repeat the process whenever a new request is received by the site. Obtaining an optimal schedule for a set of existing requests is a difficult problem. On top of that, when many requests arrive in a short amount of time (typically tens or hundreds of requests per second), this problem has to be solved a very large number of times in a short duration. This frequent revision of the schedule can by itself lead to server overload. As Chiu (2001) succinctly states: “The real question is not whether to personalize, but how and how much, and how to implement personalization while maximizing performance, which can be as important as the business effectiveness of the technique you choose.”

Although this resource allocation problem is a difficult one in general, we show that it can be considerably simplified by considering a deterministic policy<sup>1</sup> based on batching. The policy identifies the set of requests to be served within a fixed time threshold (i.e., a batch), and determines the best possible schedule for these requests. A preprocessing procedure examines each request as it arrives, and determines whether it should be kept in a queue for possible service in the next batch or provided default content, based on the potential revenues associated with that request and those already in the queue. The procedure optimally determines which requests to queue, and which to provide default content with little or no delay. We show that the policy is optimal for any predetermined batch length, and can be very efficiently implemented in practice. We then present an experimental approach to determine the optimal batch length based on other known parameters. We demonstrate that the policy dominates the universally used first-in-first-out queueing policy for personalization services, and compares favorably with more sophisticated queueing approaches that we develop specifically for this problem.

## 1.1. Related Literature and Our Contributions

Our work contributes to the body of research on personalization by formally modeling and analyzing the allocation of information technology resources to maximize revenues for content delivery sites. Moreover, this work also contributes to the literature on resource allocation and online scheduling. We provide a brief literature review of related research domains, and discuss our specific contributions.

Research on personalization has addressed a wide variety of issues. One body of research focuses on developing personalization and recommendation techniques with the objective of identifying the optimal content or product to recommend to a user (e.g.,

Breese et al. 1998, Adomavicius et al. 2005). Another set of issues studied relates to pricing of personalized services and the strategic outcome of such pricing (Chen and Iyer 2002, Dewan et al. 2003). A good summary of research in these areas is provided by Murthi and Sarkar (2003). Our work adds another dimension to the extant literature on personalization and recommendation issues. Importantly, the resource allocation problem we study is deeply intertwined with optimizing the personalization techniques themselves. To achieve the best overall performance, a firm should vary the personalization effort based on the server load. When servers are heavily loaded, the amount of personalization effort feasible for each request could be small, whereas at lesser loads, better personalization may be possible, leading to a more targeted effort. Therefore, the ability to optimally match content to a user would depend on the solution of the resource allocation problem.

The literature on user profiling is quite pertinent to our work. As mentioned earlier, user profiles can be formed by analyzing users’ clickstream data (Yan et al. 1996, Anderson et al. 2001). Mobasher et al. (1999) propose techniques for combining a user’s profile with the user’s ongoing Web activity to perform real-time personalization. Padmanabhan et al. (2001) show that compared with sitecentric data (i.e., clickstream data collected at one website), usercentric data (i.e., users’ browsing history data across multiple sites) could be used to build more accurate user profiles, which can result in better prediction of customers’ potential purchases. Liechty et al. (2001) propose a menu-based conjoint analysis approach with which firms can learn customers’ price sensitivity and preferences for customized options, and thereby achieve improved customization. Kramer (2007) argues that a firm’s preference elicitation process can help customers better understand and identify their own preferences. We assume that user profiles have been obtained a priori with one or more of the methods discussed in these works. Our work complements earlier work by showing how user profiles play an important role for optimizing allocation policies of personalization resources.

The literature on resource allocation has typically examined optimal allocation of a scarce resource across competing tasks (Bretthauer and Shetty 1995), often with additional constraints such as prerequisites for assigning resources to specific tasks (Mjelde 1983). However, the existing literature has not considered situations where the sequence of allocating resources is part of the decision problem. Our work explicitly examines delay externalities when determining the optimal sequence for providing service to different requests.

Much of the prior work in scheduling has focused on offline problems (Lawler et al. 1993, Brucker 1997, Pinedo 2002), and research on online scheduling has begun quite recently. Objectives include the minimizing of average completion time (Hall et al. 1997), minimizing total weighted completion time (Anderson and Potts 2004), and providing lead-time quotations (Kescinakok et al. 2001), among others. An important aspect of our work is that it considers service time for each request as a decision variable, something not studied in prior work on scheduling. This is an outcome of the impacts of novel information technologies (in this case the ability to provide differential levels of service by personalization systems) that need to be incorporated in scheduling environments. Moreover, in our batching policy we determine the optimal batch length for different arrival rates, an issue that has also not been addressed in previous research.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We formulate the personalization process as one of revenue maximization for a site and describe the batching framework in §2. In §3, we identify an optimal resource allocation policy for a batch. Section 4 presents the preprocessing procedure that identifies which requests should receive personalized content in the next batch, and which ones should get default content. We examine queueing approaches for the problem in §5, and discuss how to estimate parameters needed for implementation of the different approaches in §6. Section 7 shows how the optimal batch and queue lengths can be estimated in practice, and compares the various approaches. Section 8 discusses implications of our policy and presents directions for future research.

## 2. The Personalization Framework

Typical architectures for Web personalization use specialized servers that are distinct from the Web/ e-Commerce server to handle the personalization aspect of content or product recommendations (Datta et al. 2001). In such architectures, the Web server receives requests from visitors, and forwards them to the personalization server. The personalization server determines the target content and links based on the visitors’ available profile information, and sends it to the Web server. The Web server then adds its own services (e.g., composing the final page, conversion to HTML format, etc.) and forwards the personalized page to the visitor.

In order for a content-delivery site to effectively provide one-to-one personalization to a large number of visitors, it must determine the best sequence to provide personalization service and the time allocated for personalizing the response to each request. These activities can be carried out by the Web server itself, or by an ancillary load scheduler. When the personalization server is too busy to service a new request, the Web server directly serves the visitor the desired page without providing any personalization at all (i.e., a default version of the page is provided).

## 2.1. The Batching Framework

The degree of personalization, $P _ { i } ,$ for a request from visitor i (alternatively referred to as request i), is a function of the personalization technique being used (the technique is assumed to be fixed for our problem), the quality of this visitor’s available profile $( \gamma _ { i } )$ and how much effort (time x ) the personalization server spends on searching for optimally personalized content. In our problem, the arrival time and profile quality of each request is not known a priori. As discussed by Jaillet and Stafford (2001), such problems are very difficult to solve optimally. We use a strategy that divides the planning horizon into a number of intervals. Jobs arriving in one interval are batched as candidate jobs to be served during the next interval, as depicted in Figure 1a.

A schedule that includes the time allocated for each request and the sequence of providing personalization service is formed at time instance kT for the requests to be served in the interval $[ k T , ( k + 1 ) T )$ . ## 2.3. Revenue Maximization when Serving a Single Request

We present the profit maximization problem for the website when personalizing a single visitor’s request (say, request i) and no other requests are waiting. We assume that if a visitor’s request is not personalized at all (i.e., dropped by the personalization server), the incremental profit to the site is zero. Defining $\Pi _ { i }$ as the profit from personalizing request $i ,$ the profit maximization problem is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Max } \Pi_ {i} = r _ {i} \gamma_ {i} \bigg (\frac {x _ {i}}{h _ {i}} \bigg) - \rho x _ {i} - b x _ {i} = \bigg (\frac {r _ {i} \gamma_ {i}}{h _ {i}} - \rho - b \bigg) x _ {i}, \\ \text { s.t. } 0 \leq x _ {i} \leq h _ {i}, x _ {i} \leq T _ {p}. \end{array}
$$

The term $r _ { i } \gamma _ { i } x _ { i } / h _ { i }$ is the expected added revenue, $\rho x _ { i }$ is the delay cost, and $b x _ { i }$ is the resource cost. In this case, the server should provide the minimum of $h _ { i }$ or $T _ { p }$ units of personalization effort when the coefficient $( r _ { i } \gamma _ { i } / h _ { i } - \rho - b )$ for $x _ { i }$ is positive, and zero unit otherwise.

## 3. Characterizing the Optimal Schedule in a Batching Policy

We analyze the problem of the optimal scheduling policy for each batch of requests, and identify important characteristics of such a policy in Lemma 1 and Proposition 1 shown below. Based on these characteristics, we determine a batching policy that can be obtained in a very efficient manner. We then show in Proposition 2 that the policy is optimal. We consider the situation where n requests are to be scheduled in a batch.

Lemma 1. If a solution contains noncontiguous service time allocations to a request, then the solution can be improved by making it contiguous. (All proofs are provided in the appendix).

We illustrate using Figure 3 the noncontiguous and contiguous service time scenarios, where the total time allocated to a request i is divided into two parts (total amount $x _ { i }$ in parts $x _ { i } ^ { a }$ and $x _ { i } ^ { b } )$ and service is provided to another request j (amount $x _ { j } )$ in between these two parts. The proof follows from the fact that the total personalization effort to each request and the completion time for request i are the same in both cases, whereas the completion time for request j is delayed in the noncontiguous case (as compared to the contiguous case). Lemma 1 shows that time sharing is not desirable for a personalization server.

Proposition 1. (a) Personalization service should not be provided to a request with a revenue factor lower than another eligible request that is not being provided any personalization service.

(b) For requests being provided with nonzero amounts of personalization service, in the optimal solution, the sequence of service is based on a nondecreasing order of revenue factors.

Proposition 1a follows from the fact that if personalization service is provided to a request with revenue factor lower than another “dropped” request (i.e., a request not being provided any personalization service), then the firm will be better off by reallocating the former request’s assigned personalization time to the “dropped” one.

The intuition behind Proposition 1b is that if there exist two adjacent requests in the schedule with a decreasing order of revenue factors, then the firm can always be made better off by swapping the service sequence of these requests, and by reassigning service times to each of them while keeping the sum of their service times unchanged. Figure 4 illustrates the proof, which consists of two parts. In this figure, request j is assumed to have a higher revenue factor than request i, but request $j$ is served prior to request i. In Case 1, where $x _ { i } + x _ { j } \leq h _ { j } ,$ by reallocating the entire amount of time $x _ { i } + x _ { j }$ to serve request j, the firm increases revenues because of request $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ higher revenue factor, and also reduces the overall delay costs because request i no longer receives any service and therefore encounters no delay. In Case 2, where $x _ { i } + x _ { j } > h _ { j } ,$ a better solution is again obtained by swapping the sequence, allocating $h _ { j }$ amount of service time to request j, and by allocating the remaining service time $( x _ { i } + x _ { j } - h _ { j } )$ to request i. This ensures that the revenues generated from serving the two requests cannot decrease, and the total delay incurred by the two requests is always reduced.

Thus, in the optimal batching schedule, the service sequence is based on a nondecreasing order of revenue factor. If multiple requests have the same revenue factors, there may exist multiple optimal sequences. An interesting finding is that by instituting a batching framework, we can optimally sequence requests in a manner that is similar to that of the shortest processing time (SPT) sequence for certain single-machine scheduling problems in the classical scheduling theory (Pinedo 2002). We note that unlike the classical scheduling problems, the processing time is a decision variable in our problem. The proof above uses the concept of “job interchange argument” similar to the one used in classical scheduling theory. However, a key difference in our case is that the service times of the interchanged requests have to be determined simultaneously with the sequence, and all requests are not guaranteed to receive personalization service, to arrive at the result in Proposition 1(b). The joint determination of request selection, sequencing, and service times makes our problem significantly different from classical scheduling problems.

## Scheduling Policy SCH:

Based on Lemma 1 and Proposition 1, we propose the scheduling policy SCH. We denote the ith-largest revenue factor as $( r \gamma ) _ { [ i ] } , i \in \{ 1 , . . . , n \}$

1. Sort the requests in nonincreasing order of their revenue factors, i.e., $( r \gamma ) _ { [ 1 ] } \geq ( r \gamma ) _ { [ 2 ] } \geq \dots \geq ( r \gamma ) _ { [ n - 1 ] } \geq$ $( r \gamma ) _ { [ n ] } .$ . In the case of ties, sort requests in the sequence of their arrival times.

2. Find Q, where $Q = \arg \operatorname* { m a x } _ { k } \{ C _ { [ k ] } > 0 \} , \ C _ { [ k ] } =$ $( r \gamma ) _ { [ k ] } / h _ { [ k ] } - k \rho - b ,$ , where k represents the index of a request in the sorted list.

3. Find R, where $R = \operatorname { a r g m a x } _ { m } \{ \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { m } h _ { [ k ] } < T \} + 1$

4. Serve the p highest revenue factor requests in a nondecreasing order of their revenue factors in the sorted list, where $p = \operatorname* { m i n } \{ Q , R \}$ , and provide default content to the other requests.

5. The processing time allocated to request with index $j , j \leq p$ , is the minimum of $\begin{array} { r } { \{ h _ { [ j ] } , T - \bar { \sum } _ { k = 1 } ^ { j - 1 } h _ { [ k ] } \} } \end{array}$

6. If $\begin{array} { r } { T > \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { p } h _ { [ k ] } , } \end{array}$ , then the batch length is adjusted to $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { p } h _ { [ k ] } . } \end{array}$

If no requests are served in a batch, the next batch begins on the arrival of the first profitable request. The term $C _ { [ k ] }$ is the contribution coefficient (revenue less delay cost) for the request with the kth-largest revenue factor, given the ordering specified in step 2. Q is the number of requests that have positive contribution coefficients, given the ordering. R is the maximum number of requests that can be served (completely or partially) in a batch when the requests with highest revenue factors are provided with personalization service.

## Proposition 2. Schedule SCH is optimal.

Schedule SCH defines both the service sequence and the service times assigned to each request. We know from Proposition 1 that in an optimal solution, the sequence of service is based on a nondecreasing order of revenue factors of requests. To show that the service times assigned to each request are optimal, the proof considers two scenarios, $Q \geq R$ and $Q < R ,$ and shows by contradiction that in each scenario the service time determined by the schedule SCH is optimal. The intuitions behind the proofs for the two scenarios are illustrated in Figures 5 and 6, and briefly explained below.

## 4. Preprocessing Procedure for Batching Approach

We have shown that given a fixed batch length and a list of candidate requests waiting at the starting time for a batch, our proposed scheduling policy SCH guarantees an optimal solution for that batch. We have implicitly assumed that all requests arriving in the interval <sub>−</sub>T  0 wait until 0 before the best $p =$ minR Q requests are scheduled for personalization, and the rest are provided with default content. This would impose waiting costs to all requests arriving in an interval before a new batch starts, regardless of whether they receive personalized service. We refer to such costs as sunk costs; hereafter, delay cost refers to the cost of waiting during the processing of a batch, and total waiting cost refers to the sum of the sunk cost and the delay cost. Based on the arrival time of a request, its associated revenue factor, and revenue factors for other pending requests, it is often possible to determine before the start of a batch whether the request has any chance of receiving personalization service. If the request has no chance, then the sunk cost can be reduced or eliminated by providing default content to the request as early as possible.

We consider a preprocessing procedure for those requests that will be served in the next batch. The preprocessing procedure ensures that our policy maximizes the overall profit for the site, not only taking into account the profit obtained from personalized service in a batch, but also considering the sunk cost that is incurred prior to the start of that batch. We then show that the procedure maintains a list of requests that is optimal at any instant in time, given the realized requests up to that point.

The preprocessing procedure identifies and eliminates from consideration as early as possible those requests whose associated revenue factors are dominated by the revenue factors for enough other pending requests. Because only a limited number of requests can receive personalized service in a batch, this procedure maintains a list of the highest profitable requests in nonincreasing order of their revenue factors, and provides default service to requests that do not make it into this list. This list is updated based on arrivals of new requests. Assume that there are $M ( M \geq 0 )$ requests in the list when a new request arrives. Let $( r \gamma ) _ { [ i ] } , i = 1 , \ldots , M$ , be the revenue factor of the request in the ith position of the list with $( r \gamma ) _ { [ 1 ] } \geq ( r \gamma ) _ { [ 2 ] } \geq \dots \geq ( r \gamma ) _ { [ M ] }$ . Before describing the procedure, we state the following result.

Lemma 2. If a new request has a higher revenue factor than at least one request in the current list, then the new request should be inserted in the list.

The proof follows from the fact that the firm can improve its total profit by reallocating the assigned service time from a lower revenue factor request to the new request.

## Preprocessing Procedure:

(a) Compare the new request with existing requests, and find $w ,$ the position to insert the new request in the sorted list (w <sub>=</sub> 1 means that the new request has the highest revenue factor). In the case of ties, the new request is placed below all existing requests with the same revenue factor.

(b) If $w \leq M , \ ( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , at least one existing request has a lower revenue factor than the new request), all requests with lower revenue factors are moved downward by one position, and the service times are recalculated for all $i \geq w$ as $x _ { [ i ] } = \operatorname* { m i n } \{ h _ { [ i ] } ,$ , maxT <sub>−</sub> $\begin{array} { r }  \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { i - 1 } h _ { [ j ] } , 0 \} \} \end{array}$ . M is updated as $M = \mathrm { { a r g } m a x } _ { i } \{ T \ -$ $\textstyle \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { i - 1 } h _ { [ j ] } > 0 , i \leq M + 1 \}$ . Some requests at the bottom of the list might be dropped because of the batch length limit.

(c) If $w = M + 1$ , the new request is inserted at the end of the list if the available service time is nonzero. In that case, the number of requests in the list, M, is incremented by one.

(d) Starting from the Mth request, check if $( ( r \gamma ) _ { [ l ] } / h _ { [ l ] } - b ) * x _ { [ l ] } > ( l * x _ { [ l ] } + g _ { [ l ] } ) \rho$ for $l = M ,$ $M - 1 , \dots , w + 1$ , where $( l * x _ { [ l ] } ) \rho$ is the externality cost imposed by the lth request, and $g _ { [ l ] } \rho$ is the sunk cost. If this condition is not satisfied for the lth request, the request is provided with default service, M is updated, and checking continues for the l 1th request. If it is satisfied, there is no need to check any further.

So, on the arrival of a new request, the procedure determines where it should be inserted based on its revenue factor, pushing downward by one position all requests with lower revenue factors. The externality cost associated with each demoted request increases. Starting from the lowest request in the list, we examine each demoted request for net profitability based on the revised externality costs. If this net profitability remains positive, then this request stays in its new position in the list; the procedure stops and the value of M is revised. Otherwise, it is removed from the list and provided with default content at this time; the procedure to examine net profitability continues with the next-lowest revenue factor request. In situations where $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { M } h _ { [ i ] } < T } \end{array}$ , the length of the batch is reduced to $\textstyle \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { M } h _ { [ i ] }$ . If there are no requests in a batch, the next batch begins with the arrival of the first profitable request.

Proposition 3 shows that the profit associated with the list maintained by our procedure at any instant in time dominates the profit associated with all other feasible lists at that instant.

Proposition 3. Given the realized arrivals, the list maintained by the preprocessing procedure is optimal at any instant in time, and therefore provides the optimal list at the start of the next batch.

The main intuition behind this result is that by checking for time available in a batch and net profitability associated with a request, the procedure ensures that if a list is optimal at the time of arrival of a new request, it remains optimal after the list is updated (dropping from the list requests with lower revenue factors, if necessary).

The preprocessing procedure is computationally very efficient. For each new request, the server requires at most OlogU  operations to find the insertion location, and at most OU  operations to update the service times for all requests in the list, where $U = \lceil T / h _ { \mathrm { m i n } } \rceil$ and $h _ { \operatorname* { m i n } } = \operatorname* { m i n } \{ h _ { i } , i = 1 , \dots , Q \}$

## 5. Three Queueing Approaches

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the batching policy, we compare its performance with three queueing approaches. One is the first-in-first-out queueing policy, which is also the baseline approach because of its widespread usage. The proposed batching approach turns out to be far superior to the baseline approach. We also develop two more sophisticated and nonstandard queueing approaches tailored to this particular application. These approaches, which are variants of priority queueing approaches, reorder the queue based on the revenue factors of requests awaiting service, so that higher profits may be generated. To analyze these approaches, we introduce some additional notation in Table 2.

## 5.1. First-In-First-Out (FIFO)

This approach maintains a queue of size at most N requests sorted in the order of their arrival time. If the queue is full, new arrivals get default service immediately. If the queue is not full, a new request must satisfy two conditions before it is admitted to the queue; if either condition is not met, then the request receives default content. The first is whether the request will begin receiving service before its impatience constraint is violated if it is held in the queue, i.e., $\begin{array} { r l } { ~ } & { { } T _ { p } - ( \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { M } x _ { [ j ] } + } \end{array}$ $t _ { f } - t _ { n e w } ^ { a } ) > 0$ , where M is the number of requests in the queue when the new request arrives. The second is the following profitability constraint: given the revenue factor of the new request, whether it is profitable to provide personalization service to it. To decide this, it is first necessary to determine the maximum service time that can be assigned to this new request, which is: $x _ { i } = \mathrm { m i n } \{ h _ { i } , T _ { p } - ( \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { M } x _ { [ j ] } + t _ { f } - t _ { n e w } ^ { a } ) \}$ . Given $x _ { i } ,$ if $\begin{array} { r } { ( r _ { i } \gamma _ { i } / h _ { i } - b ) * x _ { i } > \rho * ( \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { M } x _ { [ j ] } + x _ { i } + t _ { f } - t _ { n e w } ^ { a } ) , } \end{array}$ , the request will be profitable. Note that once a request is admitted to the queue, it is guaranteed to receive personalization service because it will remain profitable to do so. The FIFO policy is computationally very efficient, requiring at most O1 operations to update the queue.

## 5.2. Maximum Revenue Factor Selected for Next Personalization Service (MaxRevFirst)

We consider two variations of an approach that reorders the queue based on revenue factor—preemptive and nonpreemptive. Both variants are considered, because it is not apparent a priori which one will perform better for the problem we are studying. The basic approaches are similar for both; we provide details of the nonpreemptive policy first. This approach maintains a queue of at most N highest revenue factor requests in a nonincreasing order of their revenue factor. An important difference between this approach and the FIFO policy is that a new request may be inserted in the middle of the current queue, and therefore its impatience constraint and the profitability constraint have to be evaluated accordingly. Furthermore, inserting a new request in the middle of the queue increases the delay for requests that get demoted. Each demoted request has to be reexamined to verify if it still satisfies its impatience constraint and its profitability constraint; if either one is not met, then the request is removed from the queue and receives default service at that time.

The impatience constraint for a request in the wth position in the queue is met if it satisfies the condition $\begin{array} { r } { T _ { p } - ( \sum _ { j < w } x _ { [ j ] } + t _ { f } - t _ { n e w } ^ { a } ) > 0 . } \end{array}$ . The maximum available personalization time for that request is: $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { [ w ] } = \mathrm { m i n } \{ h _ { [ w ] } , T _ { p } - ( \sum _ { i < w } x _ { [ i ] } + t _ { f } - t _ { [ w ] } ^ { a } ) \} . \mathrm { I f } \ ( ( r \gamma ) _ { [ w ] } / } \end{array}$ $\begin{array} { r } { h _ { [ w ] } - b ) * x _ { [ w ] } > \rho * ( \sum _ { k < w } x _ { [ k ] } + \dot { x _ { [ w ] } } + t _ { f } - t _ { n e w } ^ { a } ) . } \end{array}$ serving this request is profitable. Using these conditions, a queue is maintained that guarantees each request either receives personalized service or receives default service within its impatience constraint. We present some desirable properties about this queueing approach in Lemmas 3 and $^ { 4 , }$ and then provide details of the queue update procedure. Proofs of these lemmas are provided in the appendix.

Lemma 3. If a new request has a higher revenue factor than at least one request in the current queue, then it is profitable to include the new request in the queue.

Lemma 4. If a new request is inserted in the queue at the wth position, providing it more service time than that assigned to the request previously in the wth position still satisfies the profitability constraint.

Lemma 3 is shown by observing that the profit can be improved by providing default content to a lower revenue factor request and allocating the freed-up service time to the new request. The preemptive MaxRevFirst policy has only one difference with the nonpreemptive policy. If a new request i has a revenue factor higher than the request currently being served (say j), the server stops the service for request j and request i enters service immediately.

## 6. Estimation of Parameters

The results presented in §§3–5 can enable websites that face a large amount of traffic to provide personalized service to selected visitor requests, either by batching or using queues. To implement these approaches, a site would need to obtain estimates for the parameters $\gamma _ { i } , h _ { i } , r _ { i } , \rho ,$ and b. Word-vectors are a common profiling approach for personalization in content-delivery sites because they can be generated based on the content viewed by a user, and these vectors can be easily compared to words in a document (Pazzani and Billsus 1997, Anderson et al. 2001). The site content is represented by a word-by-document matrix whose entries represent the frequency of occurrence of each word in a document. Personalization algorithms compare a user profile with the word-by-document matrix to determine which pages to recommend.

As mentioned in §2, one way to estimate $\gamma _ { i }$ for a visitor is to use the proportion of her usage data used to build her word-vector profile, as compared to the amount of data that would enable perfect, or close to perfect, personalization. Although the estimate may be somewhat coarse, it would be very easy to implement.<sup>2</sup> A more refined approach would be to estimate the personalization $P _ { i }$ for a visitor by determining the proportion of recommended links traversed by the visitor (as against the use of a search engine, $^ { \mathrm { o r , } }$ backtracking, by the visitor), when ample time is spent on personalization such that the recommendations cannot be improved on given the profile, i.e., $x _ { i } / h _ { i } \approx 1$

Then, $P _ { i }$ would serve as a reliable estimate for $\gamma _ { i } .$ Some sophisticated personalization approaches consider multiple contextual dimensions such as time or location (Adomavicius et al. 2005), and may estimate a customer’s profile separately for each context (e.g., weekend versus weekday) so that the personalization technique can take advantage of the appropriate dimension when making recommendations. In such cases, the quality of the profile dimension appropriate for the current context should be considered.

The parameter $h _ { i }$ depends on the technology used for personalization, and may be estimated by examining when the marginal value to added personalization effort becomes close to zero. Depending on the desired level of precision for this parameter, an appropriate number of experiments can be run for different values of $\gamma _ { i } ,$ and the average computed from these experiments. As indicated in $\ S 2 ,$ $r _ { i }$ is the expected additional revenue from the increased likelihood of a clickthrough when perfect personalization is provided for visitor i. To estimate $r _ { i } ,$ the firm could track for each visitor the increased incidence of clickthroughs that occur for perfectly personalized content pages as compared to when no personalization is conducted $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ when default pages are presented). A default (average) value for $r _ { i }$ can be used for new visitors. $\rho$ can be estimated by performing controlled experiments to examine the change in (i) the duration of stay of a visitor in terms of the number of pages requested, and in (ii) the probability of that visitor returning to the site in future, when the response time is reduced $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ ., from eight seconds to four seconds). The computational cost per unit time, b, is estimated based on hardware, software, and related expenses for the site.

## 7. Experimental Results

We have conducted two sets of experiments. The first set of experiments demonstrates how the optimal batch length can be obtained in practice, because it is not possible to derive closed-form solutions for it. These experiments also examine the impact of different request arrival rates and revenue factor distributions on the optimal batch length and enable us to derive additional insights about the choice of the batch length. ## 7.1. Determining the Optimal Batch Length

An important consideration in implementing the batching policy is the choice of the batch length T . This length must be carefully chosen after studying the behavior (impatience parameter) of visitors, as well as the latency delays (communication and other processing latencies) associated with the service.<sup>3</sup> Even if the delays associated with delivering the page to the visitor are negligible, T should be chosen less than half the impatience parameter $T _ { P } ,$ because in the worst-case scenario a visitor’s request may be served after 2T time units have elapsed $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ when the request arrives immediately after a batch has started, and is scheduled to receive service at the end of the next batch). Therefore, once the batch length is determined, it is no longer necessary to track the impatience parameter for each request when determining the schedule for a batch. When latency delays are significant, the feasible batch length would need to be reduced by explicitly factoring in such delays.

The batching policy can be easily used to experimentally determine the optimal batch length based on the estimates of the other parameters. Given these parameter values, the site owner can evaluate the performance (in terms of expected profits) of the policy for varying batch lengths. The batch length that leads to maximum profits could be used thereafter.

7.1.1. Effect of Revenue Factor, Server Utilization, and Waiting Cost. To better understand what underlying phenomena determine the optimal batch length, we study how the profit function is impacted by the batch length. We find that varying the batch length affects the profit in three ways. First, the average revenue factor of requests receiving personalized service increases with increasing batch length (we call this the revenue factor effect). As the batch length increases, the average number of requests arriving in one batch interval increases, and the number of candidates selected for personalized service in the next batch also increases. This reduces the chance of a high revenue factor request being eliminated because of its proximity to the arrival of other higher-revenue factor requests. Our experiments show that the revenue factor improves in a concave manner with increasing batch length. Second, the overall use of the server also increases with increasing batch length (the server utilization effect). When the request arrival rate is not very high compared to the server capacity, the server utilization level can become important. When the server utilization level is low and the batch length is small, it is possible that some of the requests with high revenue factor are provided default service during a batch while the server remains idle during other batches. Having a larger batch length reduces the likelihood of this happening. We find that the server utilization also increases in a concave manner with increasing batch length, until the server is fully used. Finally, increasing the batch length leads to a higher waiting time (the waiting cost effect). As the batch length increases, the average waiting cost incurred by requests selected for personalized service increases, because the selected requests wait longer, on average, before the service to the batch begins as well as during the processing of a batch. The first two effects contribute positively to the profit, whereas the third one has a negative impact. Prior to the inflection point, the marginal contributions from the first two effects dominate the third one; thereafter, the third effect starts to dominate, and the profit decreases.

## 8. Implications and Discussion

We have addressed the resource allocation problem associated with providing personalization services for content-delivery websites in this paper. This problem is a novel and hitherto poorly understood phenomenon with significant practical relevance. We have presented batching and queueing approaches that can enable sites facing a large amount of traffic to select and schedule requests that should receive personalized service. Our analysis trades-off maximizing service time for a fully personalized response with the negative externality of higher waiting costs for other requests in the system. An important and novel aspect of our work is that it considers service time for each request as a decision variable, something not studied in either personalization or in scheduling contexts. Although the problem is a difficult one in general, we show that it can be elegantly solved by considering a policy based on batching, and is therefore easy to implement. The batching approach outperforms the queueing approaches when request arrival rates are within a few multiples of the personalization server capacity. The MaxRevFirst with preemption does only marginally better than the batching approach for higher arrival rates up to a point, after which the batching approach is again superior.

Our work provides several interesting insights on the batching solution. An intuitive finding is that when requests arrive at rates higher than a personalization servers’ capacity, requests associated with low revenue factors are provided with default content, whereas high revenue factor requests get personalized pages. On the other hand, requests selected for personalization within a batch should be scheduled in reverse order of their revenue factors to provide the maximum possible effort in personalizing pages for requests with highest revenue factors.

From a practical point of view, the resource allocation issue is closely tied to the optimization of personalization techniques (i.e., optimally matching content to customer). To achieve the best performance, a firm should vary the personalization effort based on the server load. When servers are heavily loaded, the ability to optimally match content to a user would depend on the solution of the resource allocation problem. For instance, if our methodology suggests that less time should be allocated to a request, then a relatively coarse-grained personalization could be deployed. This may help determine, for instance, if the available resources are enough for the personalization technique to do a comprehensive search across all categories, or if only a restricted search within a smaller set of the most relevant categories is feasible. In summary, there is a strong relationship between the content-matching problem and the resource allocation problem: Both are very important, one is inseparable from the other, and ignoring either one will result in an inadequate personalization outcome.

Our policy is also applicable in situations where recommendation techniques precompute part of the personalization effort (e.g., calculate correlations or form segments), and then in real time provide more fine-tuned personalization (e.g., identify and sort links to present to the visitor). Depending on the amount of resource allocated to a request, the personalization server could provide either a default set of links, sort the default set, or determine the best set and provide them in a preferred sorted order.

A limitation of this work is that we have assumed the expected revenue from personalization to be a piecewise-linear function of personalization effort. Different functional forms that apply to different personalization scenarios (e.g., scalability of different personalization techniques) need to be identified in future research, and solutions obtained for them. A worthwhile extension is to consider the expected revenue from personalization to be a concave function of personalization effort. While preliminary work suggests that a policy similar to the proposed one may be applicable for profit functions based on such functional forms, such a formulation appears to be hard to solve analytically. We are currently working on developing efficient heuristics that can perform well in practice.

Our work has several other implications for personalization services. Our work shows that users can benefit in two distinct ways if they are willing to provide their preference information to firms: Improved recommendations can result not only from the better profile, but also the additional effort made by firms when better profiles are available. An interesting scenario to examine is where a firm allows users to request a specific level of personalization effort depending on their requirements, and price such services. Consequently, users need to carefully compare the benefits obtained from better recommendations with the associated higher prices. For such services to be effective, users would need to ensure that the firm has adequate profile information on them. Because users are often concerned about privacy violations when interacting with websites, future research should also examine ways in which a site can provide the best possible value to its users while ensuring that privacy concerns are mitigated. Finally, we have analyzed a scenario with only one firm. Although there exists some literature addressing personalization in a competitive environment (Syam et al. 2005, Choudhary et al. 2005, Chen and Zhang 2001), the resource allocation problem in a competitive context is another interesting question to examine.

Proof of Proposition 3. Consider arrivals in the interval $[ - T , 0 )$ . The list is empty at time T . A request enters the list when the revenue factor of that request is sufficiently high that serving the request provides more added revenue than the total cost associated with waiting (sunk plus delay) and processing the request. Not including this request in the list is suboptimal, because the firm would then forgo the net profit associated with serving that request. Furthermore, because none of the previous requests would have satisfied the profitability requirement, it would have been suboptimal to include any of them in the list. Therefore, the list is optimal at that instant of time.

We now show that our procedure ensures that if a list is optimal at the time of arrival of a new request (say request i), it remains optimal after the list is updated by following the preprocessing procedure. In some cases, the new request is inserted in the list without requiring the removal of any existing request. The decision rule associated with each such case ensures that the new request enters the list only if it adds to the profit for the site (accounting for externality costs, as well as its own total waiting cost); not including this request in the list is then suboptimal. In other cases, inserting the new request leads to the removal of some existing requests from the list. This occurs when one of the following two situations is encountered. The first situation is where $\begin{array} { r } { \bar { \sum } _ { l = 1 } ^ { M } h _ { [ l ] } \geq T } \end{array}$ and M is the updated number of requests in the list after the new insertion. The server can provide service to a limited number of requests in a given batch, and our procedure ensures that the list is optimal by dropping the lowest revenue factor request; dropping any other request leads to less value generated from serving a relatively lower revenue factor request. The second situation is where the increased externality cost from serving the lowest revenue factor request in the list now exceeds the benefit from serving that request. Not removing the lowest revenue factor request at that point in time is suboptimal, because the site is worse off in that case. There are cases where the new request does not enter the list at all, and receives default service immediately on arrival. This occurs when the new request has a revenue factor less than or equal to all requests in the list, and either the existing requests use the whole batch length, or the externality and other costs associated with serving the new request is more than the value of the service. It is clear that including the new request in the list in such cases is never optimal.

Finally, consider time periods that lie between the arrival of two requests. Because no new event occurs in this interval, and every request in the list leads to a net positive profit after accounting for its delay cost, it is suboptimal to drop any one of those requests during that time. <sup></sup>
