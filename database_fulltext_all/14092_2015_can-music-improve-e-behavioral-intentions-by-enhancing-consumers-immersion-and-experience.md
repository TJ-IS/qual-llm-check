# Can music improve e-behavioral intentions by enhancing consumers’ immersion and experience?

## Abstract

Websites must involve visitors in enjoyable and memorable experiences so as to entice people to revisit the website and to recommend it. This article investigates the impact of music, and seeks to explain e-behavioral intentions through two underlying processes: immersion and experience. 250 persons were surveyed for their intention to re-visit a virtual art gallery and to recommend it. The results reveal that music fosters e-behavioral intentions. They also underline that immersion and aesthetic experience (emotions and contemplation) mediate this effect. The findings, confirming the impact of music on immersion, can thus help managers design effective website.

Keywords: immersion, aesthetic experience, e-behavioral intentions, web design, music, virtual art gallery

## 1 Introduction

To be effective, web communication must offer liveliness and foster a rich consumer services. Memorable experiences [2] in turn grant marketers a competitive edge. If website designers can immerse visitors in a great experience, those consumers likely return and spread positive word of mouth. Yet despite extensive research into online effects, recommendations for how to design websites to optimize customer experiences remain lacking [3], including “whether and how” different web elements, attributes, and tools, in combination, impact consumer’s psychological and behavioral reactions [4]. In turn, several sources call for investigations of online “experiential intensity” [5].

In response to those calls, this article extends research on web interface efficiency and website behavioral intentions, as a proxy for e-loyalty intention, by studying the effect of music online, while also proposing a new conceptualization and operationalization of the notions of immersion and experience in the field of consumer research. Existing studies pinpoint some elements that influence users’ attitudes toward websites [6], such as content, design, and architecture. However, elements such as entertainment should exert influences too [7], especially on low involvement sites [8]. Very few studies include music, despite strong evidence that music influences product evaluation and buying intentions in a virtual shopping experience [9], or emotional responses to e-commerce sites [10]. Following the suggestion from Chebat & Dubé [11] “to link more clearly variations in specific parameters of the environment to their unique impact on various customer responses, while delineating the mechanisms under which each parameter operates” (p. 90), this study investigates how music improves both immersion and aesthetic experience, which in turns mediate the impact of music on user e-behavioral intentions (i.e. revisit and recommend).

#

Accordingly, this research provides several contributions. First, the empirical test features the effect of web atmosphere (music) on an under examined factor, e-behavioral intentions, as a proxy for e-loyalty (instead of purchase intentions), which has significant effects on direct marketing strategies [12,13,14,15]. Second, music represents an atmospheric element that can produce a cognitive, emotional and/or physiological answer which in turn may prompt positive consumer response. Third, this study focuses on an experiential website, an increasingly prevalent type of site that rarely appears in prior research (focus on online grocery stores). Fourth, the present research explicitly considers and measures two relatively poorly understood processes, immersion and experience, which may offer significant explanatory power [16].

This article adopts the following organization: Section 2 defines the conceptual model and outlines how immersion and experience mediate the effects of music on intentions to revisit and recommend the website. Section 3 presents the virtual art gallery experiment (with and without music), using the work of a real painter and sculptor. The results of the hypothesis tests appear in Section 4. Finally, specific recommendations for designing experiential websites to foster visitors’ intentions to revisit and to recommend constitute Section 5.

## 2 Background

This research adopts the stimulus–organism–response model [17,8], which “provides an integrative framework for synthesizing existing research on website experience” [18]. In the proposed model, both immersion and experience (organism) mediate the effects of music (stimulus) on e-behavioral intentions (response). For this study, the experience is aesthetic, conceptualized in terms of emotions (enjoyment and stimulation; [19] and contemplation [20,21]). Following Chebat and Dubé [11], the present study proposes that a web atmospheric stimulus (e.g. music) impacts visitors’ responses – intentions to revisit and recommend (ebehavioral intentions) – through two explanatory processes – immersion and experience.

## 2.1 Impact of music (Stimulus) on immersion (Organism)

Studies on sound design confirmed the complementary role of the auditory experience on the response of consumers towards a product [22]. Also, if the music is a congruent stimulus (that memorization of the advertising message [23], creates positive emotions [24], and participates in the whole evaluation of the point of sale [25,26].

Concerning consumer behavior on the Internet, the various atmospheric elements of a website, including auditory stimuli, affect all levels of consumer processes. Actually, Dailey [27] suggests extending atmospheric variables to websites, which can contribute to the development of virtual environments and create positive affect and/or cognitions in website visitors and thus prompt positive consumer responses, just as in the real world [28]. Web environments produce cognitive pleasure through mental imagery [29], which affects consumers’ experience assessments. Consistently with the PAD model from Mehrabian & Russel [30], environments also physiologically affect consumers through vividness and volume (arousing quality of stimuli), which translates into positive or negative responses (e.g., returning, or not, to the website; spending more or less time on the site [8]). Music is one of those internet atmospheric elements that can be used to produce a physiological (arousing), cognitive and emotional (positive or negative) effect in order to increase the probability of sale.

Indeed web atmospherics include both highly task-relevant cues that are critical to utilitarian goals and less task-relevant cues that support hedonic goals [31]. The former encompass navigational characteristics, informativeness, information content effectiveness, structure, and organization; the latter tend to entail entertainment [8]. Sautter, Hyman, and Lukosius [32] also add the operator environment and specific stimulus properties (e.g., vividness, interactivity, symbolism, social elements) to this categorization. Some cues are particularly relevant for an experiential website, in terms of “creating or not impeding the experience of surfers” and providing entertainment, such that the cues help address users’ “needs of escapism, diversion, aesthetic enjoyment or emotional release” [8]. Thus, music is worthwhile to investigate as a means to understand how navigating on an art website playing music leads to a particular consumption experience, through a connection to contextual elements that creates an immersion state.

Sensory stimuli, such as music, can encourage immersion [6]; when listening to music, people tend to forget themselves and become detached from everyday concerns. “Dreamy” is a common description of emotive responses to music [33]. People seem to move into a mental state in which self-interest and threats from the real world are less relevant. This type of disconnection might result from attention distraction, either cognitive, in the form of reduced attention capacity, or emotional, such that people engage in an emotional sensation [34]. Emotional engagement with the stimulus may create the strongest distraction [35,36].

Thus music offers a particularly fitting stimulus that captures and holds attention naturally, by building melodic and rhythmic tensions [37]. Theory about the psychology of music cites emotional factors beyond the music and highlights the emotional component of distraction, such that listening creates an associative connection with personal memories and meanings [33,38]. Disconnection from the real world and connection to a virtual environment (immersion) may result when the environment features music.

## 2.2 Immersion and aesthetic experience fostered by virtual art galleries

## 2.2.1 Immersion

Conceptualizations of immersion refer to either technology or the individual. The former research stream refers to “a technology’s ability to create a convincing, immersive environment with which the user can interact” [39], according to five characteristics: inclusiveness, extensiveness, surroundingness, vividness, and proprioceptive matching [40]. The latter stream defines immersion as “involvement and emotional engagement” [39],

referring to “a psychological state characterized by perceiving oneself to be enveloped by, included in, and interacting with an environment that provides a continuous stream of stimuli and experiences” [41]. Immersion based on technology, i.e. sensory immersion, is a matter of form, whereas immersion based on individual interactions with the environment, i.e. psychological immersion, is a matter of content [39].

This study adopts the second view, because the focus is on consumers’ reactions in an environment that is not technologically sophisticated: a simply designed website. Thus, immersion in this study is a psychological state, characterized by being connected with the world offered by the experiential context and disconnected from the real or ordinary world [16]. Immersion implies that the experiential context completely invades people’s perceptual and emotional systems and psychological processes, such that the immersed persons become involved, absorbed, and totally committed [42]. Forgetting about the reality of the outside world, these people forfeit a real self-image for a new image, perceived only through the experiential context.

Yet immersion is not linear and may not be a permanent state. Consumers involved in an aesthetic experience tend to sense a succession of moments of immersion [43]. The level of intensity relates to the personal, global perception of successive sequences of immersion states, felt during the overall experience. The perceived intensity of an immersion state in turn depends on the duration of each immersion period and the number of connections/ disconnections: more intense when immersion moments last longer and when there are few disconnections [44].

Note that immersion is often associated, if not confounded, with the concept of flow. However, while immersion is a broader process that can generate both positive and negative states as a consequence of the aesthetics experience (e.g. simply designed experiential website), flow is defined as “the process of optimal experience achieved when a sufficiently motivated user perceives a balance between his or her skills and the challenges of the

interaction, together with focused attention” [45]. Flow is characterized by concentration, attention focus, enjoyment, a sense of total control over the actions performed, loss of time, and reality-consciousness [46;47]. It has been studied in many contexts and especially in environments mediated by computers (for a review see, for example [46]), where skills are requested to navigate and face the renewed challenges proposed by the interface. Flow corresponds to an optimal consumption experience that is extremely enjoyable and depends on a state of immersion continuously felt [16,45]. In other words, flow is a particular type of experience that stems from immersion, although immersion does not always lead to flow.

## 2.2.2 Virtual gallery and immersion

Immersion results from people’s interaction with a specific environment and thus relies on the features of the environment. Immersion is highly likely in a virtual environment, which “effectively isolates users from their physical environment, thus depriving them of sensations provided by that environment ... all the more as people can perceive themselves as moving inside a simulated environment or directly interact with other entities in that environment” [41]. However, beyond sophisticated technological devices, immersion can result from consumption environments that display characteristics that are likely to foster people’s connection to the consumption world (virtual or not) and their disconnection from the everyday world [16] that is, an enclosed, secure, and thematic context [49]. “Enclosed” suggests that the consumer becomes immersed in a definite universe created by the consumption experience; “secure” indicates to be immersed, consumers need anchor points to develop a sense of nesting or “feeling more or less at home” [43]; and “thematic” implies that the consumer enters the specific universe created by the consumption experience.

Entertainment settings, such as classical music concerts, are immersive [49]. Pieces of art also have the power to immerse someone contemplating them, because “the contact between the subject and the object is absorbing and filing to satiety all the conscious activities of conscious being: sense, imagination, intelligence, emotions, tendencies, all the energies of the human converge and confound themselves in a kind of intuition and the soul is immersed in an incomparable rapture” [50]. Moreover pieces of art display two characteristics that play an important role in aesthetic theory regarding likelihood to immerse the audience:

defamiliarization (i.e. they ability to “make the familiar strange” to engage the audience) and artistic quality [48].

A virtual art gallery that features enclosed, secure, and thematic elements and displays pieces of art thus should induce visitors’ immersion.

## 2.2.3 Experience

Experiential marketing research refers to experience as a personal, subjective occurrence that affects consumer consciousness through “a variety of symbolic meanings, hedonic responses, and aesthetic criteria” [51]. Consumers seek to gain new experiences and reinvigorate their lives through multiple experiences that may be more or less extraordinary [44]. The “experience construct is holistic in nature and involves the customer’s cognitive, affective, emotional, social and physical responses” [52].

The present research focuses on an aesthetic experience that is selfless, disinterested, affective, and contemplative [53] and also “appears to us at the emotional level” [54]. Emotions include pleasure as “part of the essence of aesthetic experience” [55], through a specific kind of pleasure, described as “ravishment,” “contemplative enjoyment,” and “a love of pure contemplation” [53,56]. For some, this pleasure is calm and serene, without agitation [53], whereas for others, pleasure arouses senses and excites appetites [54]. Regardless of the context, emotions can be positive (e.g., enjoyment, fulfillment, optimism, stimulation, surprise) or negative (e.g., fear, sadness, shame, anxiety, loneliness, anger) [57]. However, the current study addresses only positive emotions, because emotions connected to aesthetic experience include delight, joy, and pleasure [50,53,56], in that “the beaux-arts are interested in producing, by the work which they make, joy and delight in the intellect through a sensible intuition” [58].

Aesthetic experience also results from aesthetic contemplation, which “occurs over time and involves the evolution of meanings as our feelings and intellect mull over perception” [59]. From perceptions, viewers derive interpretations of the meaning of the piece of art, bring their own history to the work, and project themselves into the work [60]. In other words, contemplation is a peculiar state in which people find themselves when viewing a work of art, resulting from the interaction of the intrinsic meaning of the art (the artist’s intention) and the viewers’ representations [53]. Therefore, the aesthetic experience encompasses both emotions (enjoyment and stimulation) and contemplation

## 2.2.4 Immersion and aesthetic experience

Previous research demonstrates that immersion offers privileged access to experience [49], affecting the production of cognitive, emotional, and physical reactions that relate directly to the relevant experiential environment [16]. Thus, a viewer immersed in aesthetic context experiences contemplation and emotions elicited by and related to the context. The level of immersion within the experiential environment then influences the characteristics of the consumer’s experience. Deeper, more intense immersion increases the likelihood of intense [48], unusual, or even extraordinary experiences [43]. In contrast, low levels of immersion result in common experiences [16,61]. In the peculiar case of immersion in an aesthetic environment, the more intense the immersion, the greater the aesthetic experience should be; people should feel more contemplative and more intense emotions (enjoyment and stimulation) when more immersed.

## 2.3 Impact of aesthetic experience (Organism) on e-behavioral intentions (Response)

## 2.3.1 e-Loyalty, e-loyalty intentions and e-behavioral intentions

Prior studies attempt to differentiate real from inertial loyalty using attitudinal, behavioral, and relational approaches. According to Oliver [62] loyalty is “a deeply held commitment to rebuy or repatronize a preferred product/service consistently in the future, thereby causing repetitive same brand or same brand set purchasing, despite situational influences and marketing efforts having the potential to cause switching behavior.” This definition supports investigations of consumer loyalty in real and virtual consumption environments. Adapted to a web environment, e-loyalty refers to a deeply held commitment marketing efforts that aim to induce switching behavior. Research dealing specifically with website usage further conceptualizes e-loyalty as repeat purchases, repeat visits, recommendations, and positive word of mouth [63,64]. Thus, the present paper investigates and measures e-behavioral intentions (i.e. revisit intentions and positive word-of-mouth) as a proxy for e-loyalty. Because the present study deals with an experiential website (namely a virtual art gallery), the focus is on revisit intentions, instead of repurchase intentions, which would pertain to only a tiny sample of visitors. In line with the suggestion that attitudes and behavioral intentions underpin e-loyalty [65], intentions to return and to recommend serve as proxies for actual e-loyalty. Furthermore, consistent with research in e-marketing, the measure of e-loyalty relies on intentions [66,67] and positive word of mouth [12,68,69].

## 2.3.2 Experience and e-behavioral intentions

Uses and gratifications theory posits that if consumption fulfills consumers’ needs, “users will repeat the experience” [6]. Entertainment also enhances return intentions [13]. If an experience is enjoyable, users want another similar experience [70], especially new visitors, [71]. Sensory stimuli can foster repeat visits [15] due to the “ability to fulfill the needs for diversion, aesthetic enjoyment, or emotional release” [6].

## 2.4 Mediating effects of immersion and experience (Organism)

Based on the above, 1. the presence of music facilitates immersion, 2. immersion is a privileged way to access experience, the level of immersion affects the characteristics of the aesthetic experience, and 3. aesthetic experience fosters e-behavioral intentions. The central hypothesis for this study is that on an experiential website, the effects of music on ebehavioral intentions result from a two-stage mediation: Music enhances immersion, which in turn increases aesthetic experience, and then aesthetic experience has a positive impact on ebehavioral intentions (Figure 1).

## 3 Method

## 3.1 Experimental study

The present research tests a conceptual model in the specific environment of virtual art galleries. These websites allow artists or museums to introduce artworks to the public in a lifelike setting that produces the aesthetic experience when visitors are exposed to the masterpieces. Besides bringing visibility to the artwork, these sites are aimed at fostering favorable word-of-mouth and may create desires to visit a real, offline gallery or buy the works displayed.

Developing a sophisticated personal website is too expensive for many artists, who often choose instead a virtual art gallery with two main pages: an artist’s biography and a page of works, perhaps with a link for comments. Basic in structure, these galleries are easy to create, require no sophisticated virtual reality components (e.g., headsets, gloves), and favor a simple design (i.e., easy to navigate). Successful virtual art galleries also introduce works in a pleasing and entertaining fashion that allows visitors to escape from the real world and be transfixed in a contemplative experience [72].

To simulate these conditions, the authors designed a virtual art gallery website that was easy to browse and that encompassed several pages displaying paintings by a real artist. The paintings are abstract, colorful, and vivid, dealing with life movement and energy. To isolate the effect of music, in one experimental condition, the site featured no music, whereas in a second condition, the same site featured music, which is unusual in artistic and professional contexts. The chosen music was flamenco, the music the artist listens to when painting. Because the aim of the gallery was to elicit positive experiences, and music that is perceived as congruent yields more positive reactions, a pretest confirmed that flamenco music was appropriate. This pretest confirmed the music’s congruence with the paintings;

#

among 123 pretest respondents, only 3.2% considered the music incompatible with the style of the paintings.

The main study used an e-mail invitation to visit the virtual art gallery (5 minutes) and complete a questionnaire (10 minutes), sent to a convenience sample. A random assignment sent respondents to either the condition with or without music.

## 3.2 Questionnaire development

The structured, undisguised questionnaire instrument included close-ended questions to measure respondents’ agreement with each item on five points Likert scales. The measures pertained to immersion, experience (contemplation and emotions), attitude toward the presented music, the music’s congruence with the paintings, and e-behavioral intentions after the site visit. Additional items measured socio-demographic characteristics (age and gender). The scales came from extant literature, adapted to the virtual art gallery context (see Appendix A). In line with this study’s definition of immersion, as reflecting a connected/disconnected state, this measure relied on self-reported items, answered immediately after the experience [16,42]. The aesthetic experience measure specified the different components, using items from prior research: contemplation [20,21], enjoyment, and stimulation [57]. For e-behavioral intentions, the measures focused on revisit intentions [66,67], and positive word-of-mouth [69]. Attitude toward the music (collative properties; [73]) relied on a single item measure. Existing measures [23] served to assess music congruence (ecological properties [73]).

## 4 Results

## 4.1 Sample description

The convenience sample was obtained through snowball sampling, starting with the researchers’ professional and personal networks. The sample featured a great variety of ages and occupations. The 250 participants were native French speakers, of 29 years of age on average (range = 19–89 years), and 62% were women. Among these respondents, 70.9%

declared their interest in art exhibits, and 81.8% said that they visit art galleries, mostly for pleasure. The final sample included 122 respondents in the experimental condition (with music) and 128 in the control condition (without music).

## 4.3 Structural model

The test of the two-stage mediation hypothesis followed Preacher and Hayes’s [79] suggestion to include all mediators in the same model, because “it is often more convenient, precise, and parsimonious.” A structural equation modeling approach was selected because it is recommended when examining mediation effects to control for the effects of measurements errors [80]. Iacobucci, Saldanha and Deng [81] demonstrated the superiority of structural equation modeling for testing mediation hypotheses. In addition, a maximum likelihood adjustment function was selected because it offers effective applications for small samples [77]. Following recommendations from simulation studies on mediation testing [79,80,82,83], the present study adopted the bias-corrected (BC) bootstrap procedure with 1000 samples, generating a 95% confidence interval (CI) of the point estimations. If the CI around the estimations of the indirect effects does not include zero, the results confirm a mediation effect. ## 5 Interpretation and discussion

This research, designed to validate that music impacts e-behavioral intentions through the two-stage mediation of immersion and experience, confirms the provided hypothesis. First, immersion and aesthetic experience fully mediate the relationship between music and ebehavioral intentions, such that music fosters users’ immersion, enabling visitors to live a more enjoyable and stimulating experience, which in turn increases intentions to revisit the website and positive word of mouth. The direct effects are not significant, such that the proposed model appears complete, in support of the effect of immersion and experience on website users’ behavioral intentions.

Second, music played in an experiential website encourages immersion. Music has the power to distract people from real world, to transport them [30,85], to engage them to immersion because of its aesthetic properties [48]. Music in this study is congruent with the website. It may have fostered immersion since a music perceived as congruent triggers memories, fantasies, visual imagery associated with the website [38] and thus facilitates its appropriation [49]. This effect is even stronger when the visitor likes the music since he/she feels even more relaxed, more open to transportation [43].

Third, the more intense the aesthetic experience, the stronger e-behavioral intentions. In a word, when a person connects more closely with a virtual world (immersion), he or she experiences stronger enjoyment and stimulation, as well as greater contemplation, which increases e-behavioral intentions. Those results are in line with the uses and gratifications theory [6] and with Phillips, E.F. McQuarrie [48] suggesting that “the intensity of brand

#

experience provides an alternative route [to persuasion]” (p. 370). Paralleling their findings on ad and brand experience, one can assume that visitors seek to “engage the [website] at length and deepen [their] experience of [the painter’s work.]” (p. 388). The more intense the experience, the stronger the engagement and thus e-behavioral intentions. Results stress the importance of fostering both immersion and aesthetic experiences for website users.

## 5.1Theoretical and managerial implications

The results provide a better understanding of art galleries website visitors’ behavior. This exploratory study proposes a model of consumer behavior that embeds previously unexplored relational paths, including the mediating role of immersion and experience on the relationship between music and e-behavioral intentions.

First, these results complement previous research that investigates movie consumption and immersion [16] by highlighting that in a virtual art gallery, immersion and experience also mediate the impact of stimuli on consumers’ responses. These findings underline the importance of immersion and experience in experiential consumption, as well as confirm that the concept of immersion and its measurement are robust across contexts.

Second, by demonstrating that immersion and experience mediate the relationship between music and e-behavioral intentions, the present study confirms that immersion and experience must be conceptualized and measured as two separate concepts and that immersion is as a process for accessing experience [43]. Immersion offers a broader perspective that does not require consideration of perceived control and skills (cf. flow [45]). Both immersion and experience thus appear relevant for investigating the relationship between an experiential website and e-behavioral intentions and have significant explanatory power [16].

Third, music that is perceived by the visitor to be congruent with the website can positively affect intentions to revisit an experiential website and elicit favorable word-ofmouth. These results contribute to the body of knowledge on music’s impact in the particular context of experiential websites [10] and more generally on the significance of aesthetics to online consumers and their loyalty behavior intentions [4].

Fourth, the findings confirm that aesthetic experience consists of emotions (enjoyment and stimulation) and contemplation. In accordance with Richard’s [8] suggestion that “the addition of emotions might have helped explain some paths found non-significant or through indirect paths,” the proposed model includes emotions. Two positive emotions, enjoyment and stimulation, together with contemplation elicited by musical accompaniment, led to greater e-behavioral intentions through greater immersion and an enhanced experience. These results extend the findings of previous studies focused on e-commerce [86] to an experiential, noncommercial website setting.

These results also offer significant potential managerial implications for experiential websites, especially those that convey an entertaining experience. Their objective should be to immerse their visitor so as to deepen his/her experience with the website so as he/she is more engaged and displays positive outcomes [12,48]. Moreover, creating an intense experience thanks to multiple sensory channels stimulations (like here, the auditory and visual channels) should also make visitors willing to repeat this experience and revisit [87]. To foster immersion and provide an aesthetic experience, designers of virtual art galleries should play music that matches the artwork, especially if the gallery aims to attract a large audience. Music offers a very appealing tool for designers (even nonprofessionals) who want to design an experiential website that can enhance e-behavioral intentions but lack the skills or resources to integrate sophisticated technologies into their sites (e.g., 3D effects, interactivity). As long as music is congruent, designers gain an easy, economical, effective means to leverage behavioral intentions to revisit and recommend. Moreover, because website interface complexity can decrease visitor pleasure [4] and satisfaction [88], using music to enhance consumer e-behavioral intentions appears more efficient, at a lower cost, than adding features that might create navigation difficulties for Internet users. But to ensure these positive effects, it is necessary to perform pre-test to ensure congruence of the displayed music.

Finally, the act of examining and buying art is fundamentally experiential, so virtual art galleries should try to exploit this enhancement of immersion and aesthetic experience by adding music, as well as strive to preserve experiential notions inherent to visits to a sales location, such as art lovers seek. This notion suggests the potential for a major market expansion for art in general, simply by creating conditions that can enhance purchasing behaviors (on- or offline) and encourage loyalty behaviors to both art and artists.

## 5.2 Limitations and further research

This cross-sectional study captures e-behavioral intentions, as a proxy for e-loyalty intentions, at the moment the experiment takes place. A longitudinal study might clarify the extent to which consumers who declare these intentions are really loyal. Further research should investigate real word-of-mouth and behavioral loyalty, perhaps by tracking cookies on participants’ computers to count returns to the virtual gallery or check whether they visit a brick-and-mortar gallery if invited by the artist.

The music selected represents only one type of option, as does a virtual art gallery. Further research should examine the robustness of the proposed model and its generalizability to other websites that differ in type (experiential vs. e-commerce websites or online games) or form from the studied version (simple vs. complex) or to other music. The music under investigation was congruent with the work of art displayed. More manipulation checks would have been needed to investigate further subjective perceptions assessing the paintings as abstract, colorful and vivid, and not only the congruency between the displayed music and paintings. Results show that this music fostered immersion and experience through appropriation facilitation [49]. This raises the question of the impact of incongruent music on immersion and experience. Would immersion be hindered since interaction with the environment and finally its appropriation would be more difficult? Would the enjoyment and

#

contemplation of the aesthetic component of the website be disrupted by negative feelings elicited by the inappropriateness of the music? In other words, would access to the “sublime” (the ultimate experience) [53] be impeded? Or on the contrary would incongruency between intense experience)? Due to irrelevancy and/or inappropriateness, elicited emotions might be negative and thus potentially lead to negative experience. However the outcomes might be positive (intention to return and to recommend) since what matters is not the valence of experience but its intensity [48] or the number of dimensions stimulated within the experience [87]. It might be that the outcomes resulting from the experience route are more robust (resistant to change) than those resulting from central or peripheral route to persuasion. This issue of congruency deserves further attention.

Also, this model assumes the mediating role of immersion and experience. Results stand for a simple experiential website. It might be that in other environments where control or skills / abilities are required rather than sensitivity to aesthetics (for example a gaming experience used to engage customers with a website or a brand) flow might be more relevant [45,46,89] mainly because of the associated notions of perceived control and intrinsic enjoyment [71].

In this research, enjoyment and stimulation have been investigated. Other positive emotions, such as calm or relaxation, in contrast with stimulation, pleasure or even delight that reflect the artist’s intention according to a classical view of art [50,53,56] could be explicitly tested. Moreover, further research could consider contemporary pieces designed to be provocative (e.g., create disgust, anger, fear). In these cases, the virtual art gallery visitor’s aesthetic experience likely includes negative emotions. Researchers could determine if these emotions appear, instead of or as complements to positive emotions, as well as investigate the impact on the proposed model. Negative emotions, provided that they induce engagement to immerse, should deepen the experience with the web atmospherics and ultimately elicit

#

positive outcomes such as e-behavioral intentions [48].

Results suggest that music appreciation is a possible moderator of the relationships between immersion and aesthetic experience. A non-appreciated music could increase immersion without favoring aesthetic experience, but perhaps other types of experience which were not considered in the current model. In this study, only 24 participants declared not having appreciated the music played during their visit on the website. The sample size of this group did not allow for specifically testing the proposed model. Further research should consider investigating such groups for their patterns in relationship with our doublemediation model and assess the robustness of the model in all cases.

Since immersion and experience depend on how individual interact with their environment, individual variables, such as the goal of the visit, motivations, optimal level of stimulation, sensitivity to aesthetic properties, propensity to immerse [90] or expertise might be influential too. Indeed, systematic control over the level of online navigation expertise might support a more precise analysis of the relations among music, immersion, experience, and behavioral intentions. Other studies demonstrated an influence of expertise on esatisfaction and e-loyalty [91]; expertise similarly might moderate the relations in this model. For example, more expert users who feel completely at ease online may be less distracted by music and therefore less immersed, preferring informational content to sensory or immersive content [92]. Perhaps a combination of music and virtual reality slides would appeal more to such users [45]. Novice users might adopt a recreational attitude and exhibit more sensitivity to the sensory elements of a website, such that immersion and experience might be more intense.

Finally, researchers should strive to identify other possible antecedents of immersion and aesthetic experiences, such as interactivity or control and the liveliness of the interface, everything that provides the viewer with the necessary impetus for immersion [48].

## 6 Conclusion

#

The objective of this research was to investigate the impact of music on intentions to return and positive word-of-mouth to an aesthetic website, specifically, a virtual art gallery. Music provides an atmospheric variable that is easy for website designers to control and use. The present study highlights mechanisms responsible for music efficiency: immersion and aesthetic experience.

Despite some limitations specific to the experimental method, this study thus unveils a specific lever that website designers can manipulate easily. In practice, designers control various parameters to foster users’ intentions to revisit; analyses of these parameters should extend beyond quality considerations to involve appreciation and aesthetics. Various objective parameters might enhance the immersion and aesthetic experience of users and foster e-behavioral intentions—including music. This study recommends that website designers add music that visitors appreciate and perceive as congruent with the site to intensify the immersion effect and aesthetic experience and ultimately to foster e-loyalty intentions.
