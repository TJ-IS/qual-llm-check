# Emotion in IT Investment Decision Making with A Real Options Perspective: The Intertwining of Cognition and Regret

EUN HEE PARK, BALASUBRAMANIAM RAMESH, AND LAN CAO

EUN HEE PARK (epark@odu.edu) is an assistant professor of information technology (IT) and decision sciences at Old Dominion University. She received her Ph.D. in computer information systems from Georgia State University. Her primary research interest is in the role of emotion in various information systems (IS) contexts, including IT projects, IT investment, social media, and IS security. She has also worked on sentiment analysis and emotion ontology. Her work has appeared in Information Technology and People, Information and Management, and several conference proceedings.

BALASUBRAMANIAM RAMESH (bramesh@gsu.edu; corresponding author) is chairman and Board of Advisors professor of computer information systems and professor of computer science at Georgia State University. His research interests include supporting complex organizational processes such as requirements management and traceability, agile software development, information technology project management, and knowledge management. His work has appeared in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Journal of the AIS, European Journal of Information Systems, and others, and has been funded by several grants from leading government and private industry sources, such as the National Science Foundation, Defense Advanced Research Products Agency, the Office of Naval Research, ARL, and Accenture.

LAN CAO (lcao@odu.edu) is an associate professor of information technology and decision sciences and is the holder of the E.V. Williams Research Fellowship at Old Dominion University. She received her Ph.D. in computer information systems from Georgia State University. Her major research interests are agile software development, information technology project management, and software process modeling and simulation. Her work has appeared in Journal of Management Information Systems, Information Systems Research, European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of the AIS, and others.

ABSTRACT: Real options thinking helps individuals cope with uncertainties in the information technology (IT) investment decision-making process. Our study examines the intertwining of cognition and a specific emotion, regret, in IT real options decision making using a multisite case study of IT investments. We develop a process model that explains the context in which regret is triggered, the strategies used to regulate regret, and how these strategies influence the valuation, creation, exercise, and revaluation of several types of IT real options. Our research highlights that while some regret regulation strategies may be productive, others are counterproductive. Our findings extend the studies on cognition-based models of IT real options by including emotion, which allows us to explore human behavior on a psychological level and offer a more fundamental understanding of IT real options thinking. Practitioners must recognize the presence of regret that could cloud cognition-based analysis and must be sensitive to its impact.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: emotion, IT investment decision, IT real options decision, regret, regret regulation strategy.

Information technology (IT) investment decisions are often made in highly uncertain environments [9, 21]. Real options thinking has been suggested as a tool for coping with uncertainties in the decision-making process [58, 59]. In contrast to an option on financial assets, real options refer to the right (without a commitment) to take disparate courses of action (such as growing, staging, deferring, or switching) when investing in real assets [8]. In IT investments, real options correspond to flexibility in the scope, the schedule, and the implementation approach [18]. Existing models of cognitive processes that examine the perception, judgment, and reasoning involved in IT real options decision making [58, 59] contribute significantly to the understanding of decisions made by decision makers [53]. However, Jones [30] observes that in addition to cognition, emotion simultaneously triggers or hinders goaldirected behaviors, such as IT real options decision making. While much of the prior research has focused only on cognition-based approaches, Lankton and Luft [34] suggest that emotion (specifically regret) also impacts IT real options decisions and behaviors. However, even Lankton and Luft [34] do not investigate how cognition and regret are intertwined to impact real options decisions. Our research addresses this literature gap and develops a nuanced understanding of the role of intertwined cognition and regret in IT real options decision making.

Emotion may play a more important role in IT investment decisions than in other fields because the unique characteristics of IT projects make cognition-based analysis of IT investment difficult. For example, the benefits of IT projects are difficult to quantify and measure for several reasons. First, strategic, tactical, operational, and financial considerations are involved [24, 50]. Second, some payoffs from IT projects occur indirectly through improved business processes and therefore cannot be readily evaluated. In addition, IT infrastructure investments provide long-term payoffs that are not easily measurable [6, 39].

Emotion has been shown to play a significant role in decisions made under uncertainty in contexts similar to IT real options decision making [3, 36]. Information systems (IS) managers often rely on gut feelings instead of on concrete evidence when making investment decisions about new IT projects [13]. Bazerman and Moore [2] emphasize the need to understand how emotion influences decision making because it can help decision makers improve the quality of their decisions.

We investigate a specific emotion, regret, which is defined as “an aversive, cognitively based emotion that people are motivated to regulate” [70, p. 15]. Of all emotions, regret is particularly relevant in investment decision making. The literature on regret provides a general utility model that explains its effects on choice valuation [27], regret measurements [52, 69], and regret regulation strategies [28, 70]. The role of regret in investment decisions in various domains has been previously examined. For example, Ghosh [22] examines managers’ decisions about retaining earnings for investments; Zeelenberg and Beattie [68] study investments in safe and risky financial investment options; Hoelzl and Loewenstein [26] examine the social takeover of investment decisions; and Lankton and Luft [34] find that regret influences decision makers’ valuation of IT real options. These studies suggest regret is an emotion that significantly influences IT real options decisions in uncertain environments.

The literature suggests that cognition and emotion are naturally intertwined [30, 35]. For example, the sensemaking literature suggests that emotion is an element of the sensemaking process [40, 64]. Weick [64] states that emotion is generated when an expectation is interrupted. Maitlis and Christianson [42] suggest a reciprocal relationship between cognition and emotion; not only do interpretations of situations elicit emotion but such emotion leads to interpretations and therefore actions. Prior studies observe that investigations of the cognition–emotion relationship are still in their infancy and urge further studies in team or organizational contexts [42, 64].

Our study is guided by the following research question: How are cognition and regret intertwined to impact the course of action taken by decision makers in IT real options decision making? We answer this using a multisite case study and by drawing from the literature on IT real options, sensemaking, and regret. Specifically, we study real options decision making in the context of public libraries’ investments in radio-frequency identification (RFID) technology. We develop a process model that explains the context in which regret is triggered, the strategies used to regulate regret, and how these strategies influence the valuation, creation, exercise, and revaluation of several types of real options. Our study is one of the first attempts at conceptualizing the impact of the intertwining of cognition and regret in IT real options decisions.

## Theoretical Background

## IT Real Options

IT real options exist when an IT project affords flexibility in selecting the applications and functions to implement and in deciding the timing of and approach to implementation [18]. Real options are associated with tangible assets (e.g., capital equipment) rather than financial assets. They help firms improve strategic flexibility and handle uncertainties in identifying and pursuing opportunities [14, 47, 62]. Traditional methods, such as net present value (NPV), used to compare quantifiable benefits over costs, neglect the value of the strategic flexibility that real options theory considers [59]. The option-adjusted NPV method embraces both traditional

NPV and the value of real options [59]. In technology projects, the price of IT options refers to the costs involved in technology development [43]. An asset is generated when the development is accomplished. Subsequent investments are required to exercise the technology options for further commercialization.

Regarding the flexibility available to decision makers in IT projects, prior research has identified six types of real options [18, p. 80]: (1) growth (an initial baseline investment unlocks a variety of potential opportunities for follow-on IT investments); (2) stage (a project can be divided into distinct stages, where the pursuit of each stage is contingent on a reassessment of costs and benefits when the preceding stage is completed); (3) change scale (the allocated resources to be contracted or expanded or the operational system enabled by a project can be scaled up or down); (4) switch (switch use, where an asset developed for one purpose can be redeployed to serve another purpose, or switch input, where a key technology supporting a project can be swapped for another); (5) defer (a project can be deferred for some period without imperiling the potential benefits); and (6) abandon (a project can be terminated midstream).

Prior research identifies two different approaches to real options decision making that are used in practice [34]. The first approach, broadly called formal real options analysis [5, 57], employs techniques typically used to value options in financial securities (such as the Black–Scholes or binominal models [57]). The second approach, broadly called “real options thinking,” is a heuristic approach involving decision makers’ intuitive judgment based on the principles of option analysis without formal valuation of options. [34]. While managers often gain insights from real options theory [18], their decision making does not correspond to the exact steps used in option pricing models [59]. Prior studies suggest such intuitive judgment is vulnerable to potential pitfalls and is likely to be associated with systematic biases in the valuation of real options [18]. Prior studies suggest signaling effects, framing effects, and anti-failure bias may be the reasons managers value certain options over others [58]. The bounded rationality of decision makers may also lead to bias [59].

The real options literature [59] emphasizes that real options are more valuable under greater uncertainty because they provide the needed flexibility for a project [59]. For example, Fichman et al. [17] report that decision makers create defer options under high uncertainty. However, increasing empirical evidence suggests that emotion also influences managerial judgment in uncertain environments [54]. Lankton and Luft [34] find that under high uncertainty, anticipated regret influences real options decisions. Decisions makers with high anticipated regret greatly value defer options. Prior research [34] suggests that exploring emotional responses, particularly regret, can elucidate the fundamental mechanisms governing the valuation of real options.

## Regret in Decision Making

## Decision-Making Process: Intertwining Cognition and Emotion

The intertwining of cognition and emotion in decision making is increasingly recognized in the literature [30, 35]. Different views on the cognition–emotion relationship have been proposed [37, 63], including studies suggesting that emotion precedes cognition [67] and that cognition precedes emotion [35]. The sensemaking literature provides a relevant theoretical frame to explore how cognition and emotion are intertwined [42, 64]. Sensemaking is considered a recurring cycle that consists of sequential events happening over time [40]. When decision makers experience interrupting events that differ from their predictions, the interruptions induce an emotional response that causes them to appraise the cues and develop interpretations. Based on the interpretations, they may take action or make a decision. They also update their understanding of the situation and their predictions about the future accordingly. Here, appraisal and interpretations are cognitive activities decision makers use to construct a mental model of how an event happens and how the environment is impacted [42]. Appraisal involves incessant evaluation of situations and events [20]. Interpretation involves an “acceptable and approximating translation” of a situation [64, p. 7]. To construct an interpretation of reality, decision makers may engage in retrospective and prospective thinking [42, 55]. Retrospective thinking refers to examining the differences between the reality and how it was expected to be. This involves efforts to interpret how the unexpected reality unfolded. Prospective thinking refers to forward-looking thinking by imagining a future state that is desirable even if not well-defined. While retrospective sensemaking has received much attention in the literature, how prospective sensemaking unfolds is underresearched [55].

## Regret

Regret is associated with a sense of self-blame regarding the negative outcomes of a certain decision or behavior. It is a powerful emotion resulting in a strong desire to either undo a current situation or change a future situation and is closely linked with decision making [10]. As decision makers are motivated to avoid regret, their experiences of regret shape their decisions and behaviors [38]. Regret can be classified as experienced regret and anticipated regret. Experienced regret occurs when people think retrospectively about their past experiences and realize the current situation could have been better had they made different choices [70]. In contrast, people experience anticipated regret when they think prospectively. They compare the possible outcomes of the most preferred alternative with those of another alternative and guess that the other alternative might be better [29]. Anticipated regret occurs when the situation is uncertain and a decision is important and difficult [70].

Regret is called a counterfactual emotion because its generation greatly depends on counterfactual thinking, a cognitive activity in which decision makers try to make sense of a negative event [70]. Prior studies recognize that engaging in counterfactual thinking triggers experienced regret [16, 70], whereas anticipating counterfactual thinking results in anticipated regret [25]. Counterfactual thinking [16] is generally expressed as a conditional proposition. The literal meaning of the term “counterfactual” is “contrary to the facts” [51, p. 133]. Engaging in counterfactual thinking involves mental simulation of aspects of a past event in which people compare the actual outcome with the possible outcome of a different alternative [68]. Here, counterfactuals are thoughts about alternatives to past outcomes [51]. People think about the actual outcome and imagine how it might have been different. Experienced regret is generated when the counterfactual outcome of a forgone alternative is better than the actual outcome of the chosen alternative [27]. However, anticipating counterfactual thinking involves a mental simulation of aspects of a future event in which people anticipate potential counterfactuals, which are counterfactual alternatives to future outcomes [25]. Before a decision is made, people compare possible outcomes with counterfactual alternatives [7, 25]. Anticipating counterfactual thinking has also been shown to influence an individual’s emotional reaction to possible predicted outcomes by generating anticipated regret [25].

The concept of regret has been applied to all kinds of decisions, including investment [34, 38], financial [22, 26, 61, 68], and project management decisions [65]. Regarding IT real options decision making, Lankton and Luft [34] find that anticipated regret influences decision makers to highly value defer options but not growth options as uncertainty increases, although uncertainty should normatively increase the value of both option types.

## Regret Regulation Strategies

Regulation strategies are actions decision makers take to manage their emotions through instrumental behaviors [28]. An instrumental behavior originates from an action tendency in an attempt to achieve desired changes or maintain desired states with a specific purpose. Instrumental behaviors can be generated as responses to experienced or anticipated negative outcomes or events [19]. A major function of instrumental behaviors is to cope with negative emotion-eliciting events [19, 36]. Because people are typically regret-averse, when they experience or anticipate regret they seek to overcome and regulate it through instrumental behaviors [29]. Zeelenberg and Pieters [70] suggest three types of instrumental behaviors to prevent future regret and manage current regret: (1) decision-focused strategies are centered on the decision process and its outcomes (e.g., increased decision quality and justifiability); (2) alternative-focused strategies are centered on the alternatives not chosen (e.g., reappraised alternatives); and (3) feeling-focused strategies are centered on reducing the experience of the regret feeling (e.g., denied regret).

In summary, our literature review suggests that a detailed understanding of the role of intertwined cognition and regret can provide meaningful explanations of why and how people choose a course of action [3, 46] in IT real options decision making.

## Research Methodology

Because our approach is exploratory rather than confirmatory, we use a case study design [66]. While building theory from case study research is commonly done in the early stages of research, Eisenhardt [15] suggests that it can be used to develop fresh perspectives even when a topic has already been researched and is appropriate for inductively developing a theory [15]. A multisite case permits replication logic [66], whereby the cases are treated as a series of independent experiments that confirm or disconfirm emerging conceptual insights and enhance generalizability [44].

The selection of the study sites was driven by purposeful, theoretical sampling [66], that is, the potential to investigate the role of intertwined cognition and regret in IT real options decision making. We selected public libraries as the study setting because the decision-making process in public organizations involves factors that make cognitive evaluation more difficult than in private organizations [41]. The outcome of an investment is usually not only measured in economic terms but also includes other considerations, such as serving the public interest, that are difficult to quantify economically. Consequently, the impact of emotion on decision making in public organizations may be more pronounced because studies suggest that in such organizations the decision makers are often emotionally invested in their decisions [41]. Further, decisions about investments in different RFID components are often made in different project stages. Two libraries invested in RFID at similar levels, adopting a phased approach. A third library invested in all the components, while another decided not to invest in RFID at all. Therefore, this context offers multiple decision points for studying IT real options decision making under uncertainty, thus providing a theoretically relevant organizational context for our study.

The unit of analysis in this study is the individual level; we investigate individuals decision-making processes and the actions taken in response to the emotions they experienced or anticipated. Prior studies suggest that individual-level emotion influences group or organizational decision-making processes [33]. For example, Kelly and Barsade [33] suggest that positive emotions lead to positive judgment in group decision making.

## Data Collection

Prior studies on emotion that employ qualitative research methods have established interviews as effective for extracting data on emotional experiences and identifying meaningful implications [49]. In this study, data were collected using semistructured interviews with key stakeholders involved in making decisions about investments in RFID technology. The stakeholders included directors, senior managers (public service managers and service department managers), library branch managers, IT staff, and system analysts. Each of these individuals was responsible for making real options decisions regarding some aspects of RFID projects in their respective organizations. Our study focused on understanding the role of cognition and regret in the decisions for which each individual was responsible. Internal documents, such as policy reports, were used as complementary sources of secondary data. We also observed how RFID was used in the libraries, for example, in the self-checkout process. Table 1 lists the roles of respondents and the number of interviews conducted.

Our data collection focused on understanding the role of cognition and regret in shaping IT real options thinking. We therefore employed the approach commonly used in the field of psychology. Specifically, we followed Roseman et al. [52] and Zeelenberg et al. [69] in capturing data on regret and regret regulation strategies retrospectively. Regret is expressed in terms of feelings, thoughts, and motivational goals. Regret regulation strategies include action tendencies and actions. To reduce recall bias, we included multiple respondents from each organization and used secondary data to triangulate the findings [3, 11]. We conducted forty-five interviews with four groups of respondents (see Table 1). The interviews were audio recorded and transcribed when feasible, or detailed notes were taken. Each interview lasted approximately ninety minutes. An excerpt from our interview guide is provided in Online Supplement A.

## Data Analysis

Data collection was tightly interwoven with data analysis. Indeed, both activities occurred simultaneously. As interviews were conducted, the transcripts were analyzed. Preliminary results helped to identify additional participants and refine the interview questions [15, 53]. The concepts identified from the cognition and regret literature (technological uncertainty, anticipated regret, experienced regret, and regret regulation strategies) and IT real options thinking served as seed concepts in our initial coding of data. Our data analysis helped refine and/or modify these concepts and identify the relationships among them, guiding the next round of data collection. Data collection and coding were done until theoretical saturation was reached [15], that is, when additional data did not add to the concepts and categories developed.

Interview transcripts constitute the primary data in this study. The data analysis focused on identifying key concepts, categories, and relationships. Our respondents were encouraged to articulate their experiences in the form of narratives or stories, allowing us to represent the concepts in a temporal sequence depicting the processes used in IT real options decision making. Initially, the researchers separately analyzed each case for evidence. This evidence was summarized and entered into a matrix created for intermediate qualitative data analysis (see Online Supplement B). The researchers also actively searched for any new concepts and relationships that emerged. The results were then debated and modified until the researchers reached agreement. The data analysis helped to discover categories, relationships, and patterns in the data, which form the basis of our model. The process model was presented to key informants at the sites, and minor adjustments were made to the terminology used in the model based on their feedback.

## Study Sites

RFID technology is a combination of radio frequency and microchip technologies. RFID technology may be implemented in systems for check-in, self-checkout, security gates, material handling/sorting, and automated conveyor and inventory control in libraries. Investment in these RFID components may be done in multiple stages. Therefore, the study sites described below provided multiple decision points for understanding IT real options decision making.

The VLibrary system provides free access to information and materials to the public in a city in the northeastern United States. RFID check-in and checkout and a security system were implemented in nine branches. In addition, seven branches installed self-checkout counters, but barcodes were still used to identify specific copies of an item. Other components, such as automatic check-in and material handling, were scheduled for adoption in future stages. The inventory control component was implemented but did not function as expected.

The SLibrary system is among the largest public library systems in the southern United States and has fourteen branches. It initially invested over \$1.2 million in RFID technology and had plans to roll out the technology in the entire system after evaluating this implementation. The initial implementation included self-checkout, check-in, inventory control, and a security system. SLibrary decided not to invest in automatic check-in and material handling components.

The TLibrary system serves an internationally recognized public university in the United States with nearly 20,000 students, academic faculty, research staff, and other professionals. While the library evaluated the potential use of RFID technology, it decided against investing in this technology for several reasons. Although TLibrary is wellknown for its technological leadership, its strategic plan called for a focus on digital rather than physical collections. Therefore, decision makers had concerns about the long-term impacts of RFID technology on library operations. They were also concerned about the immaturity of the technology at the time the investment decision was made.

The CLibrary system serves a newly created university formed by merging smaller educational institutions. The university moved to a new campus that included a modern library building. The library had the opportunity to include the RFID project in its budget for the new building. Further, it had the opportunity to create physical infrastructure that would facilitate the use of the RFID technology. Because it was purchasing nearly half of its collection, CLibrary had the rare opportunity to incorporate RFID in all aspects of its operations. It therefore adopted most of the available RFID technology when the new library building was inaugurated.

## Results

## A Process Model of IT Real Options Decision Making

We developed a process model of IT real options decision making (Figure 1) based on the analysis of data collected from the four sites described above. Our model focuses on the interplay between cognition and emotion and its impact on IT real options decision making. Our model includes the following events: anticipate or engage in counterfactual thinking; anticipate or experience regret; regulate anticipated or experienced regret; and value, create, or exercise IT real options. These events occur in the context of uncertainties that influence decision making. Subcategories of these concepts and the interactions among them emerged from our data. Our model is described in detail below and is briefly summarized as follows.

IT real options decisions are often made in uncertain technological and project contexts. Before making decisions, the anticipated regret of decision makers is triggered by their anticipating counterfactual thinking (denoted as t1a in Figure 1; at time t1, “a” for anticipate counterfactual thinking and anticipate regret). Anticipating (and engaging in) counterfactual thinking often triggers regret. They are closely intertwined so we mark them at the same time zones. Decision makers engage in sensemaking to develop interpretations of the situation and use regret regulation strategies to manage their anticipated regret (at t2a). These strategies lead to the valuation, creation, or exercise of IT real options (at t3a).

Figure 1. A Process Model of IT Real Options Decision Making

After a decision is made, decision makers engage in counterfactual thinking that elicits experienced regret if negative outcomes of their decision are realized (at t1e, “e” for engage in counterfactual thinking and experience regret). They engage in sensemaking and use regret regulation strategies to manage their experienced regret (at t2e), leading to the valuation, creation, or exercise of IT real options (at t3e). These real options might be revalued, and they influence the creation of either experienced or anticipated regret regarding future phases of the IT investment decision. While our discussion on anticipated and experienced regret is presented sequentially, it should be noted that some decision makers may experience only one type of regret, while others may experience both.

## Context: Uncertainties Influencing IT Investment Decision

## Technological Uncertainty

The use of RFID in libraries was not widespread when the focal libraries were making their investment decisions. A policy report from VLibrary recognized the strategic nature of the investment in RFID: “Self-service check-out and RFID inventory control are infant industries, and they represent an investment in the future.” It was considered a very novel technology by the decision makers and required investments in new hardware, software, and infrastructure. At the time of its evaluation by all the organizations, RFID technology was believed to be still evolving. The technological uncertainty itself was considered the most critical issue in the investment decision, as described in the VLibrary policy report: “The cost of this equipment will decrease, and its availability will increase dramatically in the near future.”

## IT Project Uncertainty

Uncertainty regarding benefits: Some decision makers believed RFID would provide relative advantages over the barcode technology. However, they were unsure about the extent of specific benefits provided by this technology. For example, while the VLibrary director was convinced about the long-term potential of the technology, she was less certain about immediate operational improvements, such as enhanced throughput, increased quality of service, and improved material handling efficiency. “Initially I thought that the major difference [with RFID] was just . . . with barcodes you still had to handle every individual item, with RFID, you didn’t have to handle the items, you put on the antenna and it grabbed them all” (VB). Similarly, the benefits of RFID technology in library operations were unclear to the SLibrary decision makers. RFID functions and their impact on operations performance were not well understood. “The library did not set very specific goals about critical things like the accuracy of alarms, the reliability of the checkout process. . . . I am not sure I could be more specific about these . . . metrics because I was not sure what the technology can deliver” (SB).

Uncertainty regarding institutional trends related to IT adoption: Library decision makers were influenced by the uncertainty of institutional trends related to RFID adoption in libraries. Self-service applications were being adopted widely in the industry. However, the potential negative consequences of RFID also influenced the investment decisions of libraries nationwide. “I know that there are some concerns on security, privacy, and connectivity to ILS” (VI). Another uncertainty relates to the digitalization of library resources. The TLibrary director doubted RFID would deliver significant value, as the library’s collections were becoming increasingly digital. “[The library was] moving in the direction of digital collections rather than physical collections. This was part of our strategic plan. So, I feel that investments in RFID type of technologies that are helpful with physical collections are not likely to yield benefits in the long term” (TD).

## Anticipate Counterfactual Thinking, Then Anticipate Regret

Operating under uncertainty, decision makers participated in anticipating counterfactual thinking, which triggered anticipated regret. Anticipating counterfactual thinking was a cognitive process and was expressed as conditional propositions that specified an antecedent (e.g., “If I chose A . . .”) and an anticipated negative outcome (e.g., “I would face . . .”). Anticipated regret was an emotion and was expressed in the various response types as identified in prior studies on regret [52, 69]. Some decision makers explicitly expressed their regret, while others who might have been reluctant to explicitly express their emotions expressed anticipated regret in the form of “thoughts” about a mistake or lost opportunity (e.g., “I have a feeling that I am relying too much on what the vendors say . . .”).

Decision makers forecasted the outcomes that would result from their choices and identified the possible outcomes for each; this led to anticipated counterfactual thinking. “If [the library] continues using barcodes at a time when the library marketplace is moving toward self-checkout and RFID, the library may be forced to purchase replacement security gates for an aging and outmoded security system” (VI). The VLibrary director compared the costs associated with each alternative. “If I chose self-checkout with barcodes, it would cost much less” (VD). While this cognitive process helped decision makers better understand the consequences of each alternative, it frequently triggered anticipated regret. For example, the above instance of anticipating counterfactual thinking about the possibility of decreasing costs of RFID tags triggered anticipated regret. “Later when the tags are much cheaper, I might regret that we spent so much on tags” (VI).

The SLibrary director expressed his anticipated regret about investing in RFID, stating that he had a “sinking feeling that I may be making the wrong decision even though RFID was a technology of the future, as RFID might not work as I expected” (SD). The anticipated regret expressed by the CLibrary director was based on his view that investing in RFID in the new library was a rare opportunity. “I didn’t want to wonder later that I missed the opportunity to invest when I had the choice” (CD). However, the potentially negative outcomes associated with continuing with barcodes (rather than investing in RFID) were also a concern for the decision makers. “Our primary goal was improved customer service. We are not achieving it if we keep the status quo and we may regret it” (SI). Here, decision makers either explicitly (VI, SI, and SD) or involuntarily (CD) expressed their anticipated regret in various response forms.

## Regulate Anticipated Regret, Then Value, Create, or Exercise IT Real Options

The anticipated regret triggered the decision makers to mitigate it with regulation strategies [28, 70]. These strategies were expressed in the forms of “action” and “action tendencies,” as identified in prior studies [52, 69]. The regulation process may involve appraising and interpreting the situation. The thinking in this stage is “prospective” instead of “retrospective,” as the decision makers appraised and interpreted the expected outcomes of RFID projects. They engaged in futureoriented sensemaking to construct interpretations of the imagined reality and the factors involved in IT investment decision making. The regulation strategies then led to the valuation, creation, or exercise of IT real options. Consequently, three libraries (VLibrary, SLibrary, and CLibrary) made the decision to invest in RFID, but TLibrary decided not to invest (see Online Supplement C for details).

## Reach Goal Incrementally Strategy: Stage Options and Strategic Growth Options

After the initial decision to invest in RFID technology was made at VLibrary and SLibrary, the intense anticipated regret discussed above triggered prospective sensemaking. The decision makers engaged in forward-looking thinking to appraise and interpret the technological factors and their possible influence on the project. The appraisal and interpretation focused on framing the possible negative outcomes resulting from various decisions involved in implementing RFID and the technological uncertainty in the environment. “I wish we could have transitioned all the check-out to self-checkout. I wasn’t ready to do it because we didn’t know for sure that the technology will work perfectly as advertised” (SB). To regulate the anticipated regret related to possible project failure, decision makers used the “reach goal incrementally” strategy. “I had a feeling that we may get burned if we implement all the functionalities now” (VD). Decision makers considered investing in various components of the RFID technology in different phases.

This strategy led two libraries to value stage options. The valuation process, even though mostly cognitive in nature, also included emotional elements. The decision makers paid close attention to the strong anticipated regret and tried to understand what caused it. “I had the uncomfortable feeling that the technology for automating material handling wasn’t as mature and I may regret going all out with the RFID project and felt it was best to get to it at a later stage” (SD). They further created and exercised the stage option by starting with two major elements, self-checkout and inventory control, while automated check-in and material handling were considered future elements.

This strategy also impacted the valuation of strategic growth options for RFID infrastructure components in both VLibrary and SLibrary. The infrastructure, which included tagging equipment, readers, tags, and software, provided the foundation for capabilities such as self-check-in and material handling. Similar to the above example, cognition and emotion were intertwined in the valuation process. The anticipated regret related to missing opportunities and to system malfunction were very intense. To regulate both, the decision makers used the “reaching goal incrementally” strategy. “I was worried that we may miss out on the opportunity to take advantage of the new technology, but I was equally worried about going all out. I felt setting up the infrastructure first and then building on top of it . . . will minimize my worries” (SB). Strategic growth options were then created and exercised. While the infrastructure for RFID was built initially, this option was revalued in later phases.

## Delay Decision Strategy: Defer Options

In TLibrary, the intense regret anticipated by some decision makers was regulated differently. The TLibrary director decided to delay the investment decision without engaging in sufficient cognitive appraisal and interpretation of the investment environment to mitigate the anticipated regret. He was distracted from appraising the cues and emphasized only the negative outcomes that might be realized. He focused on the cues related to the appropriateness of RFID without paying adequate attention to other cues, such as potential benefits. “I was extremely concerned that I may be making the investment at that wrong time with the technology changing fast . . .” (TD). “Our patrons are looking for capabilities for any place any time access [digital library] . . . and this technology is not designed for this purpose” (TM).

In this case, the intense anticipated regret clearly distorted sensemaking instead of motivating it. This strategy to delay the decision led to the valuation of the defer option. “We wanted to wait until the technology is more stable before committing to a significant investment in the technology” (TD). After the valuation, the defer option was created and exercised. As TLibrary did not invest in the RFID technology, its patrons had to use the traditional checkout process, which sometimes caused delays at the checkout counter. A librarian recognized this negative consequence of the decision. “We are supposed to be a high tech institution. But we are using the age old technology and sometimes it causes delays and frustration for our users” (TL).

## Increase Decision Quality: Defer Options

When considering investing in the inventory control function even though it failed in a prior stage, the VLibrary and SLibrary decision makers were concerned about the possibility of it failing again: “I felt that there [were] too many unanswered questions about the technology and I felt I would regret going forward if I first didn’t review all the data from other libraries that have already implemented the technology” (SI). Anticipated regret triggered the decision makers to regulate it with careful appraisal and interpretation of the situation. This led to the strategy of increasing the quality of future decisions by following well-established procedures. For example, CLibrary decision makers noticed their initial request for proposal (RFP) contained ambiguities that made it difficult to evaluate the responses of potential vendors. Therefore, a revised RFP was issued to ensure that the investment decision could be made with better information. “The very thought of the possible fallout from implementing a technology that was not well understood made me uncomfortable and I decided to revise the RFP rather than go ahead” (CD).

With this strategy, defer options were valued and created in the three focal organizations. VLibrary and SLibrary then decided to exercise the option to defer the implementation of inventory control components. They planned to visit more libraries and test the various components of the technology thoroughly before implementation. CLibrary decided to exercise the option to defer the project. A revised RFP was sent out to collect more detailed information.

## Ensure Decision Reversibility: Switch Options

The anticipated regret about the possible RFID failure triggered the VLibrary and SLibrary decision makers to appraise and interpret the impact of system malfunction on their operations to regulate the regret. The appraisal and interpretation triggered by emotion focused on understanding the future outcomes of the project and led to actions to mitigate the regret. The decision makers adopted the ensure decision reversibility strategy—retaining the barcode system in parallel so they could revert back to it if necessary. “I wanted to make sure that we could continue the operations without much interruption even if the RFID didn’t work as anticipated. This [the barcodes] helped us go back to our old set up if and when necessary” (SI).

This strategy provided the decision makers the ability to value and create switch options in self-checkout and check-in components. Specifically, the two libraries prepared for switching back to the barcode technology due to the possibility that RFID might not perform as expected. However, this option was never exercised.

## Engage in Counterfactual Thinking, Then Experience Regret

After realizing negative outcome from a decision, decision makers engaged in counterfactual thinking, which triggered experienced regret. Similar to the anticipated regret, the counterfactual thinking after making decisions was a cognitive process and was expressed as conditional propositions with an antecedent (e.g., “If we had chosen not to replace the security gate . . .”) and an alternative outcome (e.g., “we wouldn’t have this issue . . .”). Experienced regret was expressed in the form of response types identified in prior studies [52, 69]. While the “feeling” of experienced regret was explicitly expressed by some decision makers (e.g., “I regretted . . .”), others who might have been reluctant to explicitly or involuntarily express their emotions expressed it as “thoughts” (e.g., “I was instrumental in making this major mistake”) or “motivational goals,” wishing for a second chance (e.g., “I wish I had a chance to reevaluate the decision to invest in this . . .”).

The cognitive process of counterfactual thinking evaluated the forgone alternatives and triggered regret. A senior SLibrary manager engaged in counterfactual thinking about the security gate by considering what would have been if they had not replaced the gate. He expressed regret over the problems caused by the frequent false alarms produced by the security gates. “If we had chosen not to replace the security gate [with RFID], we wouldn’t have this issue [of false alarms]” (SI). The director and a manager at SLibrary further elaborated:

It was one of the low points of my career when I had to apologize to a prominent member of our community. The security gate malfunctioned and the alarm went off. Everyone in the area was looking at her as if she was stealing. She didn’t take that well and I really wish we had not put that security gate there. I really had a sinking feeling when this happened. (SM)

“I really wished that I had not championed that in our meetings” (SD). The manager and the director surely regretted the decision and their decision to pursue the entire RFID project because they recognized the severe consequences of such an incident for their careers. In fact, the IT director was later fired, largely because of this incident.

Branch managers in both VLibrary and SLibrary expressed regret because the expected savings in staff costs were not realized. RFID offered the potential to free the library staff (whose salaries accounted for the largest portion of operating expenditures) from routine tasks so they could provide value-added services. Based on this expectation, VLibrary developed a staffing procedure that enabled self-checkout in their new branches. Staff members who were freed up from check-in/checkout activities were expected to be assigned to value-added activities. However, the actual self-checkout rate varied from 11 percent to 83 percent in VLibrary, and the expected reduction in staff check-in/checkout time was not realized. “I haven’t seen the savings [in staff]. I am thinking that barcodes might be a better solution considering the cost [of RFID]” (VB).

## Regulate Experienced Regret, Then Value, Create, or Exercise IT Real Options

To regulate the experienced regret, the decision makers in three libraries tried different strategies. They engaged in sensemaking to construct an understanding of the implementation process and the outcomes and took actions to regulate the regret they experienced. The strategies were also expressed in the forms of “action” and “action tendencies,” as identified in prior studies [52, 69]. These strategies influenced the valuation, creation, exercise, and revaluation of real options, in addition to the cognition-based analysis adopted by the decision makers.

## Decrease Goal Level: Strategic Growth Options and Stage Options

As discussed above, the goal of reducing labor costs by using self-checkout was not fully met and the VLibrary director experienced regret so intense that the decision maker focused on regulating it before attempting to understand the reasons for and the impact of not meeting the initial goal. “We had to . . . keep the [high] commitment level. The adjustment [of goals] was necessary because we didn’t want to focus on the past, thinking the decision was a mistake” (VD). The decision makers believed the original goal was too ambitious and they needed much more time to achieve this goal. Therefore, they used the decrease goal level strategy to mitigate this regret. This shifting of unaccomplished goals to future stages to justify the investment decision largely mitigated the regret. “I don’t think it [RFID] is a failure even though I wish we got the saving on staff [by RFID], [but] I think we are in the transitional period. That is our hope that as we capture a higher percentage of folks using self-checkout, we will see a reduction of the technician positions that we need to fill or to keep” (VD).

Decreasing the goal level was another strategy to regulate the regret related to the inability to implement inventory control. The perceived criticality of inventory control was changed to a “nice to have” feature. A VLibrary service manager commented that while this component was important for collections management, it was not critical for his operations: “It would be great to have it [inventory control] but I am right now working with the assumption that we know what is going out and what is coming in and therefore what we should have” (VM).

This strategy successfully regulated the negative emotion (regret) and resulted in the decision makers’ belief that they had made the right investment decisions. By decreasing the initial goal level, the project outcomes were deemed satisfactory. Therefore, the decision makers ignored the failure of some elements, such as reducing labor costs by self-checkout, and believed the problems would eventually go away. “I think . . . the folks needed time to adjust, they needed time to learn. It [self-checkout] was very different to them and many were intimidated. Over time, more people will choose to use self-checkout” (VD). The intensity of regret was reduced; consequently, no effective sensemaking was triggered when revaluing the strategic growth options created and exercised previously in VLibrary and SLibrary. Therefore, VLibrary and SLibrary could continue the investments later.

This strategy may also result in appraisal and interpretations of the initial goals. The strategy also led to valuing and creating stage options in CLibrary. CLibrary initially planned to have all its acquisitions tagged with RFID tags by entering into supplier agreements. However, the availability of critical material was impacted when one supplier did not complete the task on time. The CLibrary director appraised and interpreted the problem and realized it was unrealistic to tag all materials before they arrived. Therefore, he decided to decrease the goal level and exercise the stage option to tag material in stages. He explained this decision:

I wish we had taken this possibility [of delay] into account. Some important textbooks were not available at the beginning of the term and this was a serious problem. I didn’t want to face this situation again and decided to do the tagging in the library itself in some cases. Some of the collections get tagged before entering the library and others later. (CD)

## Transfer Decision Responsibility: Abandon Options

An action taken to mitigate experienced regret often involved transferring decision responsibility. This response largely reduced the motivation to engage in effective sensemaking. SLibrary fired its IT director largely due to the bad publicity generated by false alarms at the security gates. The decision makers did not engage in careful appraisal and interpretation of the reasons for the outcome but used the abandon option strategy to mitigate the regret. “The fiasco at the security gate when an important patron was stopped in fact created quite a storm. Someone had to take the fall for this. It turned out to be the director of IT because he did not do due diligence while implementing the gates” (SM). Effective sensemaking was never triggered because the problem was believed to have been solved by the firing of the IT director. This dramatic action created a negative work atmosphere.

Another example of this strategy involved a senior SLibrary manager who was quick to point out that the development of solutions for some of the problems faced by the library staff was solely the vendor’s responsibility. This strategy, while mitigating his regret effectively, largely reduced the need to engage in careful appraisal and interpretation of the problem. She required the vendor to resolve any issues with new investments. SLibrary later asked the vendor to install and test the system onsite and maintain it for several days. “It reduces a lot of stress my staff had to face because the vendor is [now] responsible for fixing the problems” (SM).

Following this strategy of transferring decision responsibility, SLibrary valued the abandon option to deal with situations when some components of the technology did not perform as anticipated. For example, when security gates were installed in the new wing, the director appraised and interpreted the situation that resulted in the valuation of the option to abandon the RFID-enabled gates and reinstall traditional security gates. This option was later created, providing the decision maker with the flexibility to abandon the investment if the technology did not work as expected. However, the need to exercise this option never arose.

## Improve Decision Outcome: Strategic Growth Options

The experienced regret about project outcome triggered the VLibrary director to appraise and interpret the environment. “[Not achieving the self-checkout rate] bothered us a lot. Then we thought about . . . how do we make that [RFID] work?” (VD). The appraisal and interpretation involved the understanding of operational processes that would work well with the RFID technology and the processes that had to be changed to better harness its benefits. They adopted the strategy “improve decision outcomes” to mitigate regret. Decision makers carefully evaluated and changed the business policies and operational procedures to be well aligned with the new technology.

Following this strategy, VLibrary decision makers revalued the strategic growth option that had been created and exercised and decided to continue the investment in RFID. “That has nothing to do with RFID, that is our policy, our procedure” (VM).

In summary, the decision makers mitigated their regret with regulation strategies. The regulation process may involve careful appraisal and interpretation of the project environment. However, in some cases, regret was reduced to a level that did not trigger effective sensemaking. Our analysis also shows that some regret regulation strategies may cloud cognitive appraisal and interpretation and hinder effective sensemaking (see, for example, Table 2). In either case, the strategies adopted to regulate the experienced regret influenced the cycles of valuation, creation, exercise, and revaluation of real options. ## Discussion

Our study contributes to the IT real options literature by providing a fresh perspective [15]. Our findings extend the studies on cognition-based models of IT real options by including emotion, which allows us to explore human behavior on a psychological level and offers a more fundamental understanding of IT real options thinking [34]. We have developed a process model that explains the linkages among counterfactual thinking (cognition), regret (emotion), regret regulation strategies, and IT real options decisions.

IT Real Options Decision-Making Process: Intertwining Cognition and Regret

Our findings suggest IT real options decision making involves intertwined cognition and emotion (Figure 1). The relationships identified in the model are consistent with the sensemaking literature [42] in that an unexpected situation triggers emotion and emotion triggers interpretations and thus actions. Our findings reveal that when IT real options decision makers experience a disruption due to negative outcomes that are either experienced or anticipated, they try to make sense of the situation. This cognitive activity—counterfactual thinking—induces regret, which triggers subsequent cognitive activities (e.g., appraisal and interpretation of the situation) and regret regulation strategies.

Regret differs from other emotions such as confusion, worry, fear, and surprise that have been examined in prior studies on organizational sensemaking. Those emotions trigger the appraisal of cues to understand what happened [64]. The responses to regret, however, are more complex and varied [70]. One type of response is feelingfocused (e.g., psychological repair work, such as identifying silver linings, reducing dissonance, and denying emotion). This type of response actually does not necessarily motivate effective sensemaking. Decision makers use this type of strategy to reduce the intensity of regret. When regret is regulated to a level that no longer concerns the decision makers, it will not be noticed and therefore will not trigger effective sensemaking. For example, VLibrary redefined the initial goal to mitigate the experienced regret about not meeting it. Project outcomes were considered satisfactory, even though the goal of cost savings was not met. Therefore, this type of strategy to regulate regret may distort decision makers’ thinking about what happened and what might have been [70].

The other type of response is decision or alternative-focused. This type of strategy is similar to the appraisal of cues identified in the sensemaking literature [64]. Regret triggers appraisal and action, which regulate regret. For example, to regulate the anticipated regret about the possible RFID failure, VLibrary and SLibrary decision makers engaged in sensemaking and retained the barcode system in parallel so they could revert back to it if necessary. However, if the regret is too intense, it distracts the decision maker from effective sensemaking and thus from appropriate real options decisions. For example, the intense regret anticipated by TLibrary decision makers caused them to postpone their decisions. Our result is consistent with Maitlis and Christianson [42], who observe that the intensity of emotion may impact sensemaking adaptively or maladaptively.

The impact of regret on real options decision making can either align with or deviate from the cognition-focused approach (see Table 4). In valuing stage options, for instance, decision makers engaged in counterfactual thinking after a goal was not achieved in an initial stage. This triggered experienced regret about the decisions made. This process aligns well with the cognition-focused approach. The use of the cognition-focused approach alone for valuing defer options may result in not creating this option if the RFID project was evaluated as beneficial. However, the intertwining of cognition and regret led decision makers to create the defer option to regulate their anticipated regret about RFID investment without evaluating the benefits.

Prior studies identify three real options decisions: value, create, and exercise [17, 18, 59]. Our study extends the literature by exploring how the options are valued, created, exercised, and revalued (see Figure 1). Our results suggest that regret plays a role in transitioning an option in one stage to the next stage. For instance, VLibrary decision makers who had anticipated regret about the RFID investment decision valued stage options. The intense anticipated regret then led the decision makers to create and exercise this option. Later, after the first implementation phase, the VLibrary decision makers revalued the stage option to address the experienced regret. Prior studies recognize the reasons that decision makers engage in revaluing real options from a cognitive perspective, such as to achieve a better outcome under uncertainty [60]. However, our findings reveal that emotion, particularly regret, also plays a role in revaluating real options. To mitigate regret, decision makers may revalue real options after they are created or exercised (Figure 1).

Fichman et al. [18] suggest that some “must do” project elements and related decision making offer no flexibility, and the use of traditional analytic tools such as NPV and return on investment (ROI) are appropriate in their evaluation. However, they further suggest that other project elements and related decisions that fall under the “may do” category offer flexibility but generate value that can be evaluated only through real options thinking. They further identify several real options strategies that may be used to create value. One such strategy is to change a part of “must do” project elements to “may do” elements. Our findings suggest that the intertwining of cognition and emotion may influence the shifting of some “must do” elements to “may do” elements when decision makers revaluate options. For instance, in VLibrary, the inventory control element was changed from “must do” in the initial plan to “may do” in the second phase to mitigate the experienced regret. This shift led to revaluation of the strategic growth option.

## Impacts of Two Types of Regret on Real Options Decision Making

Our study further investigates how the two types of regret affect the real options decision making process. Our results suggest that the impacts of anticipated and experienced regret on IT real options decisions are different in the strategies used to regulate the regret and the decision maker’s goals. For example, both anticipated regret and experienced regret influence the creation of stage options. To mitigate the anticipated regret regarding future problems due to the immaturity of RFID technology, SLibrary and VLibrary decision makers used the reach goal incrementally strategy by investing in various components of the RFID system in phases. This strategy led to the creation of a stage option. The major goal was to take preventive actions to reduce project risks. In contrast, to regulate the experienced regret regarding potential delays in receiving critical materials from a supplier, the CLibrary director used the decrease goal level strategy, which led him to value and create a stage option. He decided to decrease the initial goal from tagging all materials before they were accepted into inventory to a lower level—tagging them incrementally. Here, the goal of the decision maker was to take corrective actions to solve an identified problem. Therefore, our findings reveal that the two types of regret, even though they lead to the same option, are regulated by different strategies to achieve different goals.

Productive and Counterproductive Regret Regulation Strategies and Biases

Our results suggest regret regulation strategies may be either productive or counterproductive regarding their influence on the valuation, creation, and exercise of real options (see Table 2). As discussed previously, some strategies may hinder effective sensemaking, while others may motivate sensemaking. Based on prior literature [12], we define productive regret regulation strategies as adaptive behaviors and counterproductive regret regulation strategies as maladaptive behaviors adopted by decision makers to regulate regret. Below, we discuss the implications of these strategies and the biases.

Productive regret regulation strategies: Regret regulation strategies offer some advantages in real options decision. Productive strategies use adaptive actions to minimize regret and motivate sensemaking, thus reducing adverse consequences [19]. Moreover, decision makers employing productive strategies learn from undesirable outcomes to improve future decision outcomes. Experienced regret regulation strategies, such as improve decision outcome and undo or reverse decision, and anticipated regret regulation strategies, such as reach goal incrementally and ensure decision reversibility, are examples of the productive strategies used to take preventive/corrective actions or enhance learning. In addition, our findings demonstrate the advantage of regulating regret regarding decision procedures and outcomes. For example, decision makers engage in more vigilant actions to improve the procedures used and the quality of their real options decisions.

Counterproductive regret regulation strategies and biases: Counterproductive strategies use maladaptive actions to minimize regret, thus increasing adverse consequences [19]. Some counterproductive regret regulation strategies mitigate regret to a level that effective sensemaking is not triggered. For example, to minimize regret and reduce discomfort, TLibrary decision makers used the delay decision counterproductive strategy. This behavior might have caused the decision makers to create defer options inappropriately. Prior studies on real options identify this phenomenon as one of the pitfalls of defer options [18]. Our study offers an explanation for this counterproductive behavior. When anticipating intense regret, decision makers tend to focus on regulating their emotions, and therefore their cognition-based analysis in sensemaking is hindered. They become overly regretaverse and do not engage in the appraisal and interpretation of cues.

We also find two types of biases influencing regret regulation strategies: misprediction bias [23] and omission bias [1]. Prior studies on real options thinking suggest that decision makers are prone to engage in cognitive biases, such as bounded rationality bias and anti-failure bias, in the valuation and creation of real options [58, 59]. These studies explain the reasons for the biases at the cognitive level. Our study suggests that in some instances, the regret regulation strategies used by decision makers are biased toward certain actions, and these biases lead to the valuation, creation, and/or exercise of real options that produce negative consequences.

Gilbert et al. [23] suggest that misprediction bias occurs when the anticipation of regret leads decision makers to overvalue or undervalue a target event or phenomenon (e.g., overpay for technology products, overvalue an ability). In adopting RFID technology, overvaluing its potential might compromise the decision makers’ capability to arrive at an appropriate decision [48]. Tiwana et al. [59] suggest that under high parametric uncertainty, decision makers with bounded rationality may undervalue real options when the quantifiable benefits (such as NPV) are significant. They also suggest that the “bounded rationality bias” of real options occurs in information assessment—in the search (i.e., decision makers search for information to support their decision making) and in satisficing (i.e., decision makers end their searches for information without considering further broad areas of available information to support their decision making) [59].

However, Simon [54] argues the need to consider the role of emotion in human rationality. Inspired by Simon’s view, prior studies suggest that emotion is a source of bounded rationality [32], and thus information assessment practices are influenced by emotion [45]. Kahneman [31] suggests that emotions such as regret may play a role in decision makers’ bounded rationality. Consistent with this observation, our findings suggest that decision makers with high anticipated or experienced regret may either overvalue or undervalue a specific option. This finding links the misprediction bias resulting from regret [23] with the bounded rationality bias by identifying regret as a source of bounded rationality [59]. Regret may play a role in influencing the search and satisficing practices by causing decision makers to mispredict the value of specific real options. Tiwana et al. [58] suggest that decision makers tend to undervalue specific types of real options (e.g., abandon option and stage option) when the perceived benefits of the project are high. Decision makers are more likely to terminate the search process, and real options are usually undervalued and less likely to be created. This is called “bounded rationality bias” because it is a systematic cognitive bias in real options valuation. Our study complements the explanation of cognitive bias by suggesting that such bias may occur due to high anticipated regret regarding an investment decision and may thus influence the search process. Here, the experience of high anticipated regret blinds decision makers to properly perceiving the value of available options. Therefore, the decision makers satisfy the requirements they themselves imposed without considering further information, thus ending the information search process [56]. For example, CLibrary decision makers were consumed by high anticipated regret about being left behind in technology use and terminated their information search without considering all available information in their initial RFP.

While the study of Tiwana et al. [58] reveals biases that result in undervaluing real options, our study observes that real options can also be overvalued when the anticipated regret is high. For instance, SLibrary decision makers experienced intense regret about investing in RFID after a serious incident involving a false alarm. This regret led to overvaluation of the abandon option. Decision makers terminated the search process without collecting all relevant information about the technology as a result of experiencing regret.

While prior real options literature [18, 58] suggests that decision makers are susceptible to anti-failure bias that causes them to perceive project abandonment as undesirable, our study finds that omission bias resulting from regret leads decision makers to consider project continuation undesirable. Regret theorists suggest that to regulate regret, people tend to have omission bias that results from their inclination to prefer harm resulting from omission rather than harm resulting from action [1]. Our study suggests that decision makers’ difficulty in terminating a technology may result from high anticipated regret of potential negative consequences. In the case of TLibrary, the high anticipated regret about the appropriateness of the RFID technology for their current situation led decision makers to prefer the continued use of the barcode system and to accept the associated inefficiencies. To avoid regret, decision makers may engage in counterproductive behaviors whereby they prefer to accept current losses resulting from the continued use of a technology rather than risk potential losses that might result from terminating its use.

## Implications for Practice

Our study has implications for IT real options decision makers. First, decision makers should understand that people are often reluctant to express negative emotions, such as regret, and consequently fail to recognize the impact of regret on their decision-making process. Decision makers must recognize the presence of regret and be sensitive to its impact as that impact might be negative and could therefore cloud cognition-based analysis. Second, decision makers should seek to maximize the use of productive strategies, avoid the use of counterproductive strategies to regulate regret, and be wary of biases influencing regret regulation strategies.

## Limitations and Future Work

Our findings may be specific to the four organizations studied. The generalizability of the findings is undermined by the particular context of the study, including the organizational culture, individual characteristics, and the nature of RFID technology. This suggests that future empirical studies must examine the validity of the findings in different contexts. While we employed a research design commonly used in the field of psychology for studying emotion retrospectively, it is also conceivable that our approach might create recall bias in the informants. However, this risk is mitigated by including multiple respondents from each organization and by the availability of public documents about the decision made, which provide the ability to triangulate the findings.

Our study offers the necessary groundwork for further quantitative research operationalizing the elements in our framework and examining the relationships among them. While the study focused on the role of intertwined cognition and regret in the decisions of individual decision makers, investigations of factors such as organizational culture, norms, and environmental uncertainties that can influence real options decisions and the influence of group members on individuals that triggers emotions (i.e., emotional contagion) may provide further nuanced insight about the phenomenon.

## Conclusion

Our findings describe the intertwining of cogitation and emotion in IT real options decision making. We investigate how a specific emotion, regret, intertwines with cogitation and influences IT investment decisions in RFID technology. We present a process model that is based on a multisite case study. The model describes how regrets are triggered and regulated and how the regulation strategies influence the valuation, creation, exercise, and revaluation of several types of IT real options. Our findings reveal that an emotional response, such as regret, is an important element of the sensemaking process and that the regret regulation strategies may be productive or counterproductive in that they may trigger or hinder effective sensemaking. Practitioners must recognize the presence of regret and be sensitive to its impact as this impact might be negative and could therefore cloud cognition-based analysis. They should also seek to maximize the use of productive strategies, avoid the use of counterproductive strategies to regulate regret, and be wary of biases influencing regret regulation strategies. Overall, our study represents an initial step in understanding the role of regret in IT real options decision making. We contribute to the research on IT investment by revealing the impact of emotion on the stakeholder’s decision-making process in this context.
