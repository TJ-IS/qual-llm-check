# Versioning and Information Dissemination: A New Perspective

Atanu Lahiri,<sup>a</sup> Debabrata Dey<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. That versioning can be efective for information goods is well known. In particular, related literature shows that when consumers underestimate a product, it is often better for the manufacturer to ofer a base version of the product along with the fully featured version. This is because the base version lets consumers discover their true valuations, allowing the manufacturer to set a high price for the full version. This finding is also consistent with broader literature in economics, which contends that informing consumers cannot be optimal unless doing so also improves their average willingness to pay. Absent any such upward revision, keeping consumers in the dark becomes profitwise equivalent to first-degree price discrimination. This equivalence, although correct, does not hold when a fraction of consumers are fully aware of the true valuation to begin with. Accordingly, we propose a new perspective and explain why a manufacturer should consider versioning even when consumers do not underestimate the product and their average willingness to pay does not change after exposure to it. The point of this paper is to show that the presence of an informed segment of consumers fundamentally changes the information structure of the market, elevating in the process the relative value of information dissemination.

Keywords: experience good • information good • consumer learning • versioning

## 1. Introduction

An information good is also an experience good, and, therefore, a consumer “must experience it to value it” (Shapiro and Varian 1999, p. 5). As has been shown in recent literature, this experience aspect has considerable implications for the product-line strategy of a manufacturer of an information good (e.g., Chellappa and Shivendu 2005). For example, an efective way of marketing such a product turns out to be letting consumers use a portion of it at a low cost, or even for free (Cheng and Liu 2012, Dey et al. 2013, Dey and Lahiri 2016, Dou et al. 2013, Niculescu and Wu 2014, Wei and Nault 2013). Doing so allows consumers to discover the true value of the product, which is often higher than their initial estimate. This prompts some consumers, if not all, to acquire the remainder of the product at a premium, leading to higher revenues for the manufacturer. Indeed, this line of thinking provides a plausible explanation as to why strategies such as “version-to-upgrade” (Wei and Nault 2013) and “freemium” (Niculescu and Wu 2014) are currently so commonplace in markets for information goods.

The previous argument is also supported by broader literature on experience goods in economics and marketing (Shapiro 1983). This literature discusses the case of physical goods that consumers purchase repeatedly. It shows that when consumers underestimate a product’s usefulness, ofering it at a low introductory price is often the optimal strategy. A lower introductory price entices more consumers to try the product, and once they find it more useful than initially believed, the demand for repeat purchase spikes, leading to a greater pricing power in subsequent periods. Thus, again, the key is that consumers’ beliefs about the usefulness are initially lower than their true values, and the manufacturer engages in providing product samples to promote consumer learning. Doing so allows the manufacturer to mitigate the impact of any underestimation on the part of consumers; absent such underestimation, facilitating consumer learning is not desirable for the manufacturer.

There is also significant literature on when to inform consumers about their true valuations, and the extent to which they should be informed (Lewis and Sappington 1994, Johnson and Myatt 2006). An interesting finding here is that, under very general conditions, informing consumers actually lowers the manufacturer’s profit if doing so increases only the heterogeneity in their valuations with no positive impact on their average willingness to pay (WTP). In other words, if all consumers have the same expectation to begin with and, after using, some revise up while others revise down their WTP in such a way that the average valuation still remains the same, it cannot be profitable to inform them (Johnson and Myatt 2006). Of course, if the average also moves up in the process— as is the case when consumers collectively underestimate a product—informing them can become optimal. It is immediate that versioning with the objective of promoting consumer learning can be profitable only if consumers underestimate suficiently, that is, at least to an extent that makes the benefit of informing them large enough to outweigh any cannibalization loss that a cheaper base version would inflict on the higher version. All in all, the message is still the same— versioning of experience goods is unlikely to be efective if consumers do not underestimate the product and their prior is consistent with what the manufacturer considers to be the true worth of the product.

In this paper, we show that, contrary to impressions one might get from existing literature, underestimation on the part of consumers is not the only reason—and certainly not a necessary condition—for a manufacturer to consider versioning. Specifically, even when consumer learning has no impact on the average WTP, versioning can be efective as long as there exists a fraction of consumers who are informed and know their actual WTP to begin with. In other words, even if a small portion of the market is aware of their true WTP, intuitions from prior research do not extend. In fact, as we will see, the mere presence of an informed segment of consumers, regardless of its size, can make versioning justifiable in a wide range of situations.

This fresh perspective also happens to be relevant, for it is too restrictive to think that the entire consumer market is structurally homogeneous in its knowledge of a product and that all consumers are completely ignorant—or completely knowledgeable, for that matter—about their true fit. Whether it is a software product or a video game, there have to be some consumers who have had a first-hand experience with the product or its prior generation. For instance, consumers often use a wide variety of software products in the workplace; they could bring that experience to bear on their purchases of such products for use at home. And, even if the same exact software has not been used before, familiarity with other products from the same manufacturer can often provide suficient indication about fit. For information goods such as video games, exposure through “gameplays” at expos and conferences, or experience playing a game at a friend’s place, may allow some consumers to gather adequate information about fit. As a result, it is easily conceivable that some consumers have a much higher “literacy level” and, from a learning perspective, experience with a lower version is simply not relevant to them.<sup>1</sup> In fact, with fewer people now needing information from a lower version, it may seem at first as though version-to-upgrade is an even worse strategy here. Such an impression would, however, be misleading. As we will see, the presence of an informed group actually bolsters the argument in favor of versioning, so much so that it becomes optimal even when consumers do not underestimate the product. It is precisely this type of counterintuitive finding that necessitates examining how the presence of informed consumers influences a manufacturer’s versioning strategy, an exercise we undertake in this paper.

There is another curious angle. Consider the fact that the suboptimality of versioning in the absence of an upward revision in the average WTP is known to be robust at both the extremes: (i) when consumers are fully informed (Bhargava and Choudhary 2001) and (ii) when consumers are fully uninformed (Lewis and Sappington 1994). Now, when the same behavior is encountered at both the extremes, it is customary to surmise that the region in between will likely behave in a similar fashion. However, such an interpolation turns out to be too simplistic in our context. Indeed, in our setup, versioning is suboptimal under perfect information, just as it is under no information, but this suboptimality completely breaks down in the presence of partial information—evidently, at every point in between, versioning can be optimal. Put diferently, prior research may be applicable at the two extremes, but ours sheds light on all the shades in the middle.

Specifically, we start with a simple model, similar to the one proposed by Lewis and Sappington (1994), where consumers are all unaware of their valuations but have prior beliefs that coincide with the true one in an expected sense. We find that, in such a situation, keeping consumers in the dark becomes profitwise equivalent to first-degree price discrimination, making any efort to inform them—through versioning or otherwise—suboptimal. When we allow a portion of consumers to be informed, however, the equivalence to first-degree discrimination breaks down. Essentially, when a fraction of consumers are informed of their true valuation to begin with, the information structure of the market fundamentally changes, elevating in the process the relative value of information dissemination. In situations where information dissemination is still suboptimal, trivially, versioning remains suboptimal too. However, when information dissemination becomes beneficial, the benefits must outweigh the cannibalization loss for versioning to be efective. Accordingly, we explain the conditions that make versioning desirable. Subsequently, we show that our result not only applies to versioning, but also extends naturally to the broader context of information dissemination (Johnson and Myatt 2006, Lewis and Sappington 1994). Finally, we provide an analysis of the market coverage and welfare to derive additional insights about the versioning problem.

## 2. Model Preliminaries

We normalize the total consumer mass to unity and assume that there are two types of consumers: an α fraction for whom the product is a ${ \mathrm { f i t } } , \kappa = F ,$ and a $( 1 - \alpha )$ fraction for whom it is not, $\kappa = N _ { \iota }$ where $\kappa \in$ $\{ F , N \}$ denotes the true fit for a consumer. A consumer with $\kappa = F$ ought to have a higher WTP compared to a consumer with $\kappa = N$ , so we assume the following:

Assumption 1. If truly $a f i t ,$ a consumer’s WTP for a product of quality or feature set φ is $\phi ( 1 + \delta )$ , where $\delta \in ( 0 , 1 )$ On the other hand, if the product is a nonfit, the WTP is $\phi ( 1 - \delta )$ .

Initially, all consumers are uninformed about whether the product truly fits them, so they simply attach a probability of α to the event that they would find it a fit, and $( 1 - \alpha )$ to the event of nonfit. Consequently, all consumers initially have the same expected WTP for quality φ:

$$
v _ {0} (\phi) = \phi (\alpha (1 + \delta) + (1 - \alpha) (1 - \delta)).\tag{1}
$$

On the other hand, once consumers have experienced the entire product, they are sure whether or not the product fits—an α fraction gets a fit signal and hence also knows for sure that the product fits, and the remaining $( 1 - \alpha )$ fraction gets a nonfit signal and hence is confident that the product does not fit.

## 2.1. Consumer Learning

We now consider how a consumer’s perception of fit changes when he experiences only a part of the product—a lower or a base version—as would be the case in a version-to-upgrade context. For now, we normalize the quality level of the full product to one and let consumers experience in the lower version only a fraction of the features, denoted by $\theta \in ( 0 , 1 )$ <sup>)</sup>. ## 2.2. Versioning Decision

Can the manufacturer make a higher profit by ofering two versions? We answer this question for the standard setting of a zero marginal cost good, in which the manufacturer can costlessly create a lower version by deactivating a few features of the fully featured one.

First, as a benchmark, let us consider the case where the seller does not use versioning at all and ofers only the full product. In that case, consumers have only one decision to make, to purchase it or not. Consumer learning is not relevant, as there are no decisions to make after experiencing the product. According to (1), each consumer would be willing to pay up to $v _ { 0 } ( 1 )$

Obviously, it is optimal for the manufacturer to set the price of the full version at $p _ { 0 } = v _ { 0 } ( 1 )$ . This leads to a total revenue of $R _ { 0 } { \mathrm { : } }$

$$
R _ {0} = v _ {0} (1) \times 1 = \alpha (1 + \delta) + (1 - \alpha) (1 - \delta).\tag{6}
$$

Now, suppose that the seller also ofers a base version of quality θ at a price r, with the expectation that consumers will first buy this base version before deciding whether to pay an additional $( p - r )$ to upgrade to the full version. This kind of versionto-upgrade approach is indeed common for information goods (Wei and Nault 2013). One reason such an approach is popular is that it is quite straightforward to implement—premium features can be easily included in the base version in a “locked” form and, then, the license key necessary for unlocking them can be sold online.<sup>2</sup> The main idea behind selling features in this piecemeal way is that consumers who get a fit signal upon using the base product will be willing to pay a relatively higher price for the additional features. The question is whether this higher WTP can also translate to a higher revenue.

A consumer will purchase the base version of quality θ if and only if the following individual rationality (IR) constraint is satisfied:

$$
v _ {0} (\theta) \geq r,\tag{IR}
$$

where $v _ { 0 } ( \cdot )$ is as given by (1). It is easy to see that the manufacturer will set the price of the base version at $r = v _ { 0 } ( \theta )$ —any lower, the price drop does not expand the market, and any higher, nobody buys. The consumer subsequently buys the upgrade of size $( 1 - \theta )$ if and only if the following incentive compatibility constraint (IC) is satisfied:

$$
v _ {U} (1 - \theta , \theta , s) \geq p - r,\tag{IC}
$$

where $v _ { U } ( \cdot , \cdot , \cdot )$ is as specified in (4).

The manufacturer can set the upgrade price, $( p - r ) $ either to $v _ { U } ( 1 - \theta , \theta , F )$ or to ${ v _ { U } } ( 1 - \theta , \theta , N )$ . It is clear from (IC) that setting the price to ${ v _ { U } } ( 1 - \theta , \theta , N )$ would propel every consumer to upgrade, whereas setting it to $\hat { v _ { U } } ( 1 - \theta , \mathbf { \hat { \theta } } , F )$ would mean that only those with a fit signal would upgrade. Although $p - r = v _ { U } ( 1 - \theta , \theta , N )$ brings in more upgrades, selling the upgrade at a discount vis-à-vis the prior valuation may hurt the overall revenue. On the contrary, selling the upgrade for $p - r = v _ { U } ( 1 - \theta , \theta , F )$ leads to fewer upgrades, with each upgrade occurring at a higher price. In the end, the manufacturer will choose a price that brings in a higher revenue:

$$
\begin{array}{r} R _ {U} = \max \{v _ {0} (\theta) + v _ {U} (1 - \theta , \theta , N), \\ v _ {0} (\theta) + \alpha v _ {U} (1 - \theta , \theta , F) \}. \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

To obtain the optimal $\theta ,$ we can now maximize the previous expression for $R _ { U }$ . Curiously, this maximization problem has no interior solution.

Proposition 1. In the aforementioned market, selling to uninformed consumers is as good as first-degree price discrimination, and informing consumers through versioning is suboptimal.

To see this result clearly, suppose that consumers are all informed of their valuations and the manufacturer can practice first-degree discrimination. In this case, the manufacturer will collect $( 1 + \delta )$ from every consumer whom the product fits and $( 1 - \delta )$ from each whom it does not, leading to a revenue of $R _ { 0 } ,$ , which is exactly what the manufacturer makes when consumers are uninformed. Thus, keeping consumers in the dark is simply as good as practicing first-degree discrimination, which no other form of discrimination can top, let alone versioning.

Proposition 1 echoes broader economics literature in the context of information dissemination to consumers. When imparting information to consumers makes them more heterogeneous but does not impact their average WTP, doing so has been found to be counterproductive (Johnson and Myatt 2006, Lewis and Sappington 1994). In fact, this is exactly what we observe in Proposition 1. In the absence of informative signals, all our consumers have the same exact WTP, $v _ { 0 } ( 1 )$ , which simply depends on the parameters α and δ. In the presence of informative signals, however, depending on the signal, they get split into two groups with two diferent valuations, which adds heterogeneity without shifting the average.

Now, although our versioning problem has similarities with the information dissemination literature, there is an important distinction as well. Since, in addition to providing product information, versioning also involves selling a portion of the product at a relatively cheaper price—actually, at a cheaper price-to-quality ratio—the lower version inflicts a cannibalization loss on the full product; consumers who would have purchased the full product otherwise may now be satisfied with just the base version and not upgrade. Such a cost, though, is not relevant in the information dissemination literature. What is interesting here is that, in Proposition 1, versioning is suboptimal, with or without considering this cannibalization loss. This is because it is not just versioning that is suboptimal here; any form of information dissemination is suboptimal in itself.

Let us now see how Proposition 1 relates to the information systems literature, which in the contexts of version-to-upgrade and freemium has routinely found that ofering a base version, for a price or as a free trial, can indeed be optimal in certain situations (Cheng and Liu 2012, Dey et al. 2013, Niculescu and Wu 2014, Wei and Nault 2013, among others). Why do we then find a diferent result? The answer lies in how the experience with the base version manifests itself in revisions of a consumer’s belief and his WTP. Our belief revision is traditional in the sense of Bayesian consistency—upon experience, the consumer may revise his WTP up or down but, in the end, his prior WTP must be consistent with the posterior; see (5). In other words, there is no persistent bias in the consumer’s prior WTP, in terms of over- or underestimation of the product’s usefulness. In contrast, whenever recent research has made the case for product sampling or versioning, it has done so by considering misinformed consumers for whom learning may lead to an eventual positive impact on the average ${ \cal W } { \mathrm { T P } } ^ { 3 }$ In other words, only by including an overall underestimation of a product has the previous literature found versioning to be optimal. Absent such underestimation, versioning cannot be optimal:

Proposition 2. In a market with misinformed consumers, versioning can be optimal only $i f ,$ on average, there is an underestimation of the product.

## 3. New Perspective

The prevalent wisdom that informing consumers is efective only when they underestimate the product is not wrong, and Proposition 2 does corroborate its merits. Unfortunately, however, such thinking does not apply to situations where a fraction of consumers are potentially informed and already know whether the product fits. To make our point, we allow a $\beta > 0$ fraction of consumers to be fully informed—from the get-go, this group knows for sure whether or not the product fits. Among them, an α fraction should duly recognize the product to be a fit, and the rest of them, nonfit. Thus, now, an $\alpha \beta$ fraction is already willing to pay $\phi ( 1 + \delta )$ for quality φ, whereas a $\beta ( 1 - { \dot { \alpha } } )$ fraction is willing to pay only $\phi ( 1 - \delta )$ . The remaining $( 1 - \beta )$ segment is still uninformed—their prior and revised WTP are as characterized in Section 2. Notably, there are no misinformed consumers in this setup, so the following results cannot be attributed to underestimation on the part of consumers or, for that matter, inconsistent priors of any kind.

This revised setting actually does away with the implicit assumption that learning has the same efect, qualitatively, on every consumer. As mentioned in Section 1, some consumers are likely to have ample familiarity with the product, with prior generations of the same product, or with other similar products from the same manufacturer. It is also conceivable that—by virtue of their prior experience, access to outside expertise, exposure to the product at the workplace, or their ability to acquire product information from sources such as trade shows—they already have a much higher literacy level and do not need additional information to assess the true fit. A model with such informed consumers is, therefore, not only realistic, but also a more general representation of the setting in which consumers have consistent priors and learning has no impact on the average WTP.

As before, we first consider the no-versioning case as the benchmark. Now, even when selling just one version, the manufacturer has several choices—it can set the price, ${ p } _ { 0 } ,$ to $( 1 - \delta )$ , to $v _ { 0 } ( 1 )$ , or even to the other extreme of $( 1 + \delta ) ;$ the corresponding demand would be 1, $\alpha \beta + ( 1 - \beta )$ , or $\alpha \beta ,$ respectively. Accordingly, the revenue in the absence of versioning would be

$$
R _ {0} = \max \{(1 - \delta), v _ {0} (1) (\alpha \beta + (1 - \beta)), \alpha \beta (1 + \delta) \},
$$

where $v _ { 0 } ( 1 ) = \alpha ( 1 + \delta ) + ( 1 - \delta ) ( 1 - \alpha ) .$ , from (1).

Let us now revisit the versioning decision for the manufacturer. When ofering two versions, the manufacturer has to first choose $r ,$ the price of the base version. Of course, the manufacturer cannot set r to anything more than $v _ { 0 } ( \theta )$ , because doing so would attract the only $\alpha \beta$ fraction, who already know the product to be a fit. Selling the upgrade subsequently at $p - r =$ $( 1 + \delta ) ( 1 - \theta )$ cannot be any better than selling only the full product for $p _ { 0 } = 1 + \delta$ . In other words, the manufacturer has just two choices for $r \colon \theta ( 1 - \delta )$ or $v _ { 0 } ( \theta )$ , with a corresponding demand of 1 or $\alpha \beta + ( 1 - \beta )$ , respectively. The manufacturer will, of course, choose the one that results in better revenue.

As far as the price for the upgrade is concerned, again, the manufacturer has several choices. In addition to setting $( p \mathrm { ~ - ~ } r )$ to either ${ v _ { U } } ( 1 - \theta , \theta , F )$ or ${ v _ { U } } ( 1 - \theta , \theta , N )$ as before, there is also a third choice now: $p - r = ( 1 + \delta ) ( 1 - \theta ) . ^ { 4 }$ Given all these choices, the versioning problem here is somewhat complex. So, for ease of exposition, we break it down into two separate cases.

Case A. $v _ { 0 } ( 1 ) ( \alpha \beta + ( 1 - \beta ) ) \geq 1 - \delta$ . In this case, the benchmark (no-versioning) revenue is

$$
R _ {A 0} = \max \{v _ {0} (1) (\alpha \beta + (1 - \beta)), \alpha \beta (1 + \delta) \}.\tag{8}
$$

Furthermore, any versioning strategy that sets r to $\theta ( 1 \textrm { -- } \delta )$ is strictly dominated, since raising r to $v _ { 0 } ( \theta )$ increases the revenue from the base version— specifically, from $\theta ( 1 - \delta )$ to $v _ { 0 } ( \theta ) ( \alpha \beta + ( 1 - \beta ) ) =$ $\bar { \theta \boldsymbol { v } } _ { 0 } ( 1 ) ( \alpha \bar { \beta _ { \mathrm { ~ + ~ } } } ( 1 - \beta ) ) \geq \theta ( 1 - \delta )$ —without negatively impacting the revenue from the premium component. Hence, the only meaningful choice here is $r = v _ { 0 } ( \theta )$ , with a demand of $\alpha \beta + \bar { ( 1 - \beta ) }$ . Thus, in Case $\scriptstyle \mathrm { A , }$ a $\beta ( 1 - \alpha )$ part of the market remains uncovered in the equilibrium.

We now consider how the manufacturer should price the premium features, that is, how it should set $( p - r )$ . If the manufacturer sets $p - r = ( 1 + \delta ) ( 1 - \theta )$ , it will get a total revenue of

$$
v _ {0} (\theta) (\alpha \beta + (1 - \beta)) + \alpha \beta (1 + \delta) (1 - \theta),
$$

as only those who are fully sure of a fit are willing to pay up that price for the premium features. This expression is linear in $\theta ,$ attaining maximum at either $\theta \dot { = } 0 \ \mathrm { o r } \ \theta = 1$ , and the maximum is the same as $R _ { A 0 } ,$ essentially making this pricing strategy inferior (or equivalent) to no versioning. Similarly, if the manufacturer sets $p - r = v _ { U } ( 1 - \theta , \bar { \theta } , N )$ , every consumer who purchases the base version will upgrade, resulting in a total revenue of

$$
(v _ {0} (\theta) + v _ {U} (1 - \theta , \theta , N)) (\alpha \beta + (1 - \beta)),
$$

which can be shown to be less than (or equal to) $R _ { A 0 } ,$ making this pricing strategy inferior (or equivalent) to no versioning as well. Hence, with versioning, the only meaningful strategy would be to set $p - r =$ $v _ { U } ( 1 - \stackrel { \cdot } { \theta } , \theta , F )$ , in which case the α fraction that gets the fit signal will upgrade, leading to a total revenue of

$$
R _ {A U} = v _ {0} (\theta) (\alpha \beta + (1 - \beta)) + \alpha v _ {U} (1 - \theta , \theta , F).\tag{9}
$$

This $R _ { A U }$ needs to be maximized and compared to $R _ { A 0 }$ in (8) to verify whether versioning is optimal.

Case B. $v _ { 0 } ( 1 ) ( \alpha \beta + ( 1 - \beta ) ) < 1 - \delta$ . The reasoning is quite similar in this case. The benchmark revenue now is

$$
R _ {B 0} = \max \{(1 - \delta), \alpha \beta (1 + \delta) \},\tag{10}
$$

and the only meaningful choice for r is $\theta ( 1 - \delta )$ , which renders the market fully covered. For the premium version, $p - r = v _ { U } ( 1 - \theta , \theta , F )$ continues to be the only relevant choice as the revenues from the other two possible alternatives can never be better than $R _ { B 0 }$ . Hence, the revenue of interest is

$$

$$

the maximum of which should be compared to $R _ { B 0 }$ to determine when versioning is optimal. Maximizing (9) and (11), and comparing the optimal revenues respectively with (8) and (10), we now obtain the following result.

The implications of Theorem 1 are obvious. Contrary to findings in prior research, it shows that informing consumers can be optimal even when doing so does not result in an increase in the average WTP. In fact, the average WTP for the entire product remains $v _ { 0 } ( 1 )$ both before and after consumers learn about the product. Furthermore, informed consumers do not revise their WTP, whereas uninformed ones revise theirs, up or down, contributing to the overall heterogeneity. Yet, as duly shown by this theorem, the manufacturer may still find it optimal to inform consumers using a suitable versioning strategy.

The intuition behind this new finding is as follows. Although the optimal no-versioning price for Case A is identical to what we have seen in Section 2, the market is no longer covered at this price. In Case B, in contrast, the market gets covered at the no-versioning price, but the price is now lower and clearly below the average WTP. Evidently, keeping consumers in the dark is no longer the same as first-degree discrimination. Contrast this with the result in Proposition 1. There, when everybody was in the dark, the case of no versioning was indeed equivalent to first-degree discrimination. When a few consumers are already informed, though, that equivalence no longer applies, and the benchmark against which versioning is to be judged shifts accordingly. This is precisely why there is now an incentive to version unless, of course, δ is small. When δ is small, all consumers—whether informed or not—are clustered around the average WTP very tightly, and it is still possible to use just one version to efectively tap the relatively inelastic demand.

## 4. Value of Information Dissemination

To explicitly isolate the value of informing consumers from the cannibalization loss, we ask, how would the manufacturer behave in situations where the cannibalization loss is not even a concern? When would the manufacturer decide to inform consumers about the product’s true fit, and how would its decision relate to the versioning problem we study? More specifically, if the manufacturer could provide uninformed consumers informative signals of accuracy $\psi \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ in a costless manner—perhaps through means other than versioning—when would it do so, and what ψ would it actually choose?

Answering these questions is not only important in understanding the versioning problem itself, but also relevant for connecting to the broader economics literature that has studied this very problem, but without the informed segment of consumers (e.g., Johnson and Myatt 2006, Lewis and Sappington 1994). Indeed, manufacturers can and do use other approaches to inform consumers about their products, and, in doing so, they must contend with the fact that the literacy levels of all consumers are not always the same.

To find the optimal $\psi ,$ we need to recognize that the revised WTP of an uninformed consumer upon receiving a positive signal is

$$
v _ {U} (1, \psi , F) = (\alpha + \psi (1 - \alpha)) (1 + \delta) + (1 - \alpha) (1 - \psi) (1 - \delta),
$$

and when the price is set to this value, a fraction α buys the product. The resulting revenue is $\alpha v _ { u } ( 1 , \psi , F )$ Similarly, the revised WTP of an uninformed consumer upon receiving a negative signal is

$$
v _ {U} (1, \psi , N) = \alpha (1 - \psi) (1 + \delta) + ((1 - \alpha) + \alpha \psi) (1 - \delta);
$$

when the price is set to this value, a fraction $( \alpha \beta +$ $\left( 1 - \beta \right) )$ buys the product, resulting in a total revenue of $( \alpha \beta + ( 1 - \beta ) ) v _ { U } ( 1 , \psi , N )$ . Finally, the manufacturer also has the option of not informing consumers at all. In that case, the manufacturer has three choices: (i) it can charge $p _ { 0 } = 1 + \delta$ and sell it to the $\alpha \beta$ fraction; (ii) it can charge $p _ { 0 } = v _ { 0 } ( 1 )$ <sup>)</sup> and sell it to the $( \alpha \beta + ( 1 - \beta ) )$ fraction; or (iii) it can sell it to everybody by setting $p _ { 0 } = 1 - \delta$ . Of course, the manufacturer would choose the option that gives it the maximum revenue:

$$
\begin{array}{r} R _ {I} = \max \{\alpha v _ {U} (1, \psi , F), (\alpha \beta + (1 - \beta)) v _ {U} (1, \psi , N), \\ \alpha \beta (1 + \delta), (\alpha \beta + (1 - \beta)) v _ {0} (1), (1 - \delta) \}. \end{array}\tag{12}
$$

As it turns out, this maximization problem has no interior solution. The following result is immediate:

Theorem 2. When consumers can be informed in a costless manner, it is optimal to either fully inform them or not inform them at all; that is, $\psi ^ { * }$ is either 1 or 0. Full information is optimal, and $\psi ^ { * } = 1$ , when δ is greater than both $h _ { 2 } ( \alpha )$ and $h _ { 3 } ( \alpha )$ . Otherwise, it is optimal to not inform and choose $\psi ^ { * } = 0$

Clearly, there is a contrast between Theorem 2 and the findings in prior research; yet our result is also consistent with prior findings when $\beta = 0$ . To understand, note that when costs are assumed away, the only consideration is the benefit from informing consumers. It is well known that informing consumers is not usually beneficial because, when consumers have consistent priors, information leads only to a greater heterogeneity among consumers, or a more elastic demand curve (Johnson and Myatt 2006). The argument is that consumers are no longer concentrated at a common prior, which makes it impossible for the manufacturer to charge one price and extract all their surplus. Indeed, this insight from prior research would have extended to our work, had we simply assumed $\beta$ to be zero—the conditions in Theorem 2 imply that no δ fits the bill when $\beta = 0$ and $h _ { 2 } ( \alpha ) = 1$ , essentially echoing the finding in Proposition 1. When informing consumers is itself not beneficial, what is the point in allowing cannibalization to set in, to facilitate dissemination of that information?

What is interesting, though, is that this intuition from prior research does not extend to the case of $\beta > 0$ To be clear, when $\beta > 0 ,$ , information dissemination still has exactly the same impact on consumer demand—it still increases consumer heterogeneity without adding to the average WTP. Moreover, since a portion of consumers are already informed to begin with, disseminating information is expected to be of even lesser value. However, as Theorem 2 shows, information dissemination surprisingly turns out be to beneficial when $\delta$ is suficiently large. This happens because when the manufacturer decides to conceal the information about fit, it is forced to set the price at either $p _ { 0 } = 1 - \delta \mathrm { o r } p _ { 0 } =$ $v _ { 0 } ( 1 )$ , both lower than <sup>(</sup>1 <sup>+</sup> δ<sup>)</sup>. This low price allows an $\alpha \beta$ fraction of consumers, who are not only informed but also find the product to be a fit, to walk away with the full product at a price that is well below their WTP of $( 1 + \delta )$ . Indeed, this becomes a rather serious problem when δ is large. This is why, at a large δ, the manufacturer prefers to inform consumers and sell the product at a premium. We thus enrich existing literature by establishing that when consumers are heterogeneous in terms of prior information, that is, not everyone has the same information to begin with, the efect of information dissemination is qualitatively diferent—unlike the case where all consumers are uninformed to begin with, informing consumers can be surprisingly beneficial despite its unwelcome efect on consumer heterogeneity.

As shown in Theorem 2, information dissemination becomes beneficial only if δ is suficiently large; that is, if $\delta$ is below $h _ { 2 } ( \alpha )$ or $h _ { 3 } ( \alpha )$ , it is actually better to keep consumers in the dark. The reemergence of the boundaries $h _ { 2 } ( \alpha )$ and $h _ { 3 } ( \alpha )$ in Theorem 2 is somewhat curious, but can now be easily explained. These boundaries, as defined in Theorem 1, were obtained by equating the size of the signal—the quality of the base version—to one. In the case of costless dissemination of information, where there is no interior solution and $\psi ^ { * }$ is either 1 or $0 ,$ it is only natural that those boundaries would still be applicable.

However, all other boundaries arising from the tension between cannibalization and information dissemination disappear now, as cannibalization is no longer an issue. In other words, if consumers can be informed in a costless manner, it becomes optimal to provide them with full information when $\delta$ is large. In Figure 1, this includes the regions marked $^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { V e r - }$ sioning in Case $\mathrm { A , \Omega ^ { \prime \prime } }$ “Versioning in Case $\mathrm { B } , { } ^ { \prime \prime }$ and $^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { N o }$ Versioning” in the top portion of panel (b). This tells us that the two no-versioning regions in Figure 1, the one at the bottom and the other at the top, are qualitatively diferent. The bottom no-versioning region happens because it is simply not optimal to inform consumers there, regardless of the cost of doing so. Clearly, cannibalization is not an issue of significance there. In contrast, the top no-versioning region occurs simply because the cost of informing consumers— cannibalization loss from versioning—is so great that it outweighs the benefits of information dissemination. To summarize, the bottom no-versioning region merely represents the case in which informing consumers is not a good strategy, whereas the top noversioning region stands for the one where informing consumers is beneficial but the benefits are simply not large enough to ofset the burden imposed by cannibalization.

How would the results in Theorem 2 change if we were to include a cost associated with information dissemination? Of course, if this cost were also linear in $\psi ,$ we would continue to get a boundary solution. However, if this cost were convex, interior solutions would certainly be possible. In fact, that is exactly what happens with versioning, which carries an associated cost of cannibalization. It turns out that this cost is actually quadratic in the size of the signal, and an interior solution does become possible, making versioning an optimal strategy under certain circumstances.

The implication of Theorem 3 is immediate. Consumers may gain or they may lose from the monopolist’s decision to use versioning. This is not entirely surprising, as the impact of price discrimination on welfare is known to be mixed (cf. Tirole 1988, chapter 3). The correlation between the market coverage and consumer welfare is also intuitive—only when a manufacturer has to reduce the price of the base version to expand coverage to the lower end of the market does it need to concede a surplus to those at the upper end. Interestingly, though, versioning does not expand the market coverage across the board. This is in stark contrast with the common wisdom that the ability to segment the market and ofer multiple versions allows a monopolist to cover a greater portion of the market (cf. Tirole 1988, chapter 3). In fact, only in Cases A1 and B1 does versioning work as expected and expand the market coverage. In Cases A2 and B2, however, the efective market coverage shrinks, for, although the total number of consumers using at least one version does not change, the number of consumers using the full version, $\breve { \lambda } ,$ drops when compared to the benchmark case of no versioning, resulting in an efective shrinkage of the market for premium features. Evidently, in Cases A2 and B2, the main purpose of versioning happens to be extracting a higher surplus from an efectively shrinking market—only consumers who are sure that the product fits or those who have received a fit signal from the base version are targeted for the premium component. This, in essence, is a reflection of the curious double role that the base version plays in our context as a product sample, in addition to playing its traditional role as an imperfect substitute for the fully featured version.

Moving on to social welfare, the impact of versioning is rather obvious in Cases A1 and B1—a higher consumer surplus also means greater social welfare in those cases, since the manufacturer uses versioning only when it leads to higher profits. However, Cases A2 and B2 are not as straightforward, as the manufacturer’s gains in those cases come at the expense of consumers. The following result summarizes the net impact on social welfare:

Proposition 3. In Cases A1 and B1, social welfare rises as a result of versioning. In contrast, in Cases A2 and B2, versioning reduces social welfare.

Proposition 3 shows that, much as the efect on consumer welfare, the efect of versioning on social welfare is also dependent on the parameter values. If the parameter values are such that market coverage expands as a result of versioning, social welfare too expands with it. In contrast, when the market coverage decreases, so does social welfare. This is because, for a zero marginal cost good, fewer people using it invariably means less welfare for the society. The profit of the manufacturer simply represents a portion of the surplus generated from usage—specifically, the part that gets handed over from consumers to the manufacturer through the pricing mechanism in place. This is why the profit, by itself, cannot ofset the detrimental efect of a lower market coverage.

## 6.1. Asymmetric and Nonlinear Signals

So far, we have taken the strength of the signal obtained from a feature set θ to also be θ, the implicit assumption being that the signal strength linearly increases with the feature set of the lower version. Another limitation of the linear assumption is that the strength of the signal is symmetric in both positive (fit) and negative (nonfit) directions. ## 6.2. Overall Product-Line Strategy

In practice, versioning is only a part of the overall product-line strategy, which can be viewed as consisting of two stages. In Stage 1, the manufacturer decides on the overall quality level and incurs the fixed cost of development. In Stage 2, the manufacturer sells the product as is or creates a lower version to sell to consumers along with the higher version. Prior literature on versioning traditionally focuses on the second stage and assumes that the quality level of the higher version is exogenous. This is done because, as it turns out, the versioning decision itself does not depend on the overall quality level of the product, which typically is normalized to one. That, however, does not mean that the overall quality level is independent of the versioning decision. To obtain a complete picture of the product-line strategy, we now examine the manufacturer’s decision at both the stages.

We denote the overall quality level of the product by Θ. As before, if a lower version is used, it contains a $\theta$ fraction of the overall features; that is, the quality level of the lower version is θΘ. It is easy to see that, when the overall quality level is Θ, the corresponding revenue from Section 3 simply gets scaled by a factor of Θ. ## 6.3. Independent Product Modules

One could also conceive of a situation where the overall product is essentially made up of two independent modules, each of which can be separated into a product in its own right. The overall product then becomes a bundle of these two modules. If, in such a situation, experience with one of the modules provides consumers a signal about fit with the other, should the manufacturer communicate that information by selling the two modules separately, or should it suppress that information by bundling the two modules? Can our modeling experiment with noisy signals extend to such a scenario and provide insights about the manufacturer’s bundling strategy?

Certainly, there are many practical situations where such an abstraction makes sense. TV episodes are often bundled into a series, but are also sold separately. Likewise, console games are often bundled into units containing two or three separate games, and music albums bundle songs that can also be purchased separately as singles. In each case, a reasonable idea about fit with the constituent modules can be developed by experiencing one of them.

There are similarities between the versioning and bundling problems. Both, for example, contend with the issue of whether two distinct sets of features— denoted θ and θ<sup>¯</sup> <sup></sup> 1 <sup>−</sup> θ henceforth—should be sold together or separately, and in both cases, an experience with one set reveals information about fit with the other. There are also a few diferences. First, in bundling, there is no concept of premium features or upgrades. If the two modules are unbundled, a consumer can, at least in principle, buy either one and not buy the other. In versioning, in contrast, the consumer cannot purchase the premium features in the θ<sup>¯</sup> portion without first buying the base version, θ. Second, in bundling, the demarcation between the two modules θ and θ<sup>¯</sup> is set at the time of product design, making θ extraneous to the bundling problem. In versioning, however, θ is fully endogenized.

Despite these diferences, it turns out that our earlier analysis can help with the aforementioned questions. First, Proposition 1 still holds, implying that, as before, when all consumers are uninformed, keeping them that way continues to be equivalent to first-degree price discrimination, making information dissemination suboptimal in this context as well. Therefore, in a market with only uninformed consumers, the manufacturer would simply sell the bundle and not ofer the two modules separately.

Let us now consider the case where a β fraction of consumers is already aware of their fit with the two modules. If the manufacturer sells the modules separately, in its efort to squeeze as much surplus from the consumers receiving a fit signal as possible, it will price one module low and the other one high.<sup>5</sup> Without loss of generality, we can label the θ module as the low-priced one, implying that $( p - r ) / ( 1 - \theta ) > r / \theta$ . It is then clear that consumers who buy the θ<sup>¯</sup> module will also find buying the θ module worthwhile, as the latter is priced even lower. In other words, now some consumers will purchase both the modules, some will buy only θ, and the rest will buy neither. Thus, under no circumstances will we end up with consumers who own just the premium part, θ<sup>¯</sup>. Moreover, those who buy and consume θ will make a further decision about whether or not to buy θ<sup>¯</sup>, based on their experience with θ. Taken together, it becomes immediate that the resulting number of purchases for either part will be the same as before, and the bundling problem is logically equivalent to our earlier versioning problem.

The implication is clear. There might indeed be situations where the manufacturer may wish to communicate fit information by selling the modules separately, making bundling suboptimal. More specifically, for the given θ, if $R _ { A U } > R _ { A 0 }$ and $\delta \geq h _ { 1 } ( \alpha ) ^ { - }$ , or if $R _ { B U } > R _ { B 0 }$ and $\delta < h _ { 1 } ( \alpha )$ , the manufacturer may unbundle the products; recall that $R _ { A 0 } , R _ { A U } , R _ { B 0 } ,$ , and $R _ { B U }$ are given in (8)–(11), respectively. Of course, since θ is exogenous here and is no longer a part of the decision problem, a nonbundling outcome would be less likely in optimality—only when θ is close to the optimal value stated in Theorem 1 would we find bundling to be suboptimal.

## 7. Conclusion

Information goods are known to have interesting properties, such as that they are experience goods and they are easily reproduced at a zero marginal cost. In fact, both properties have significant implications for the product-line strategy of their manufacturers. On one hand, when all versions have the same zero marginal cost, versioning is usually not efective (Bhargava and Choudhary 2001). On the other, so long as the experience aspect plays a significant role, a version-toupgrade strategy can be very useful (Wei and Nault 2013). Such a versioning strategy essentially involves luring a large number of users to the product with a cheap base version and then charging a substantial price premium for an upgrade to the full version, primarily to profit from those who have had a positive experience with the base version. Although prior research has shed some light on such versioning strategies, in this work, we look at them once more in a completely new light.

More specifically, we show that the usefulness of version-to-upgrade has been understated. This is because prior literature has placed much of its emphasis on how positive personal experiences with a base version might lead to a greater willingness to pay on the part of consumers, creating possibilities for the manufacturer to jack up the price for the upgrade. And broader economics literature concurs that, absent an upward revision in the average WTP, informing consumers of the true usefulness of a product is often counterproductive. Taken together, existing literature leaves us with an impression that version-toupgrade can be efective only when the base version significantly lifts the average WTP for the upgrade.

We show that this insight, though not wrong, has its limitations—it is entirely possible that consumers have consistent priors and there is no upward revision in their average WTP, but such versioning still leads to higher profits for the manufacturer.

To make our point, we first use a setup similar to that in prior research and show that versioning is ineffective when all consumers are uninformed but have consistent priors. We explain why not informing consumers through a base version and keeping them in the dark essentially amounts to first-degree price discrimination, which a manufacturer ought to prefer to any other strategy. This is also consistent with broader economic theories that suggest that a mean-preserving revision in the WTP distribution, which invariably exacerbates consumer heterogeneity, is detrimental to the manufacturer’s interests. Of course, if one injects inconsistent priors into the model and treats consumers as misinformed, versioning could easily become efective, because the upward revision in the average WTP may now allow the manufacturer to significantly raise its price for the upgrade. After putting existing research into perspective this way, we move on to explaining why version-to-upgrade can be efective in additional contexts. To do this, we propose a new model with two types of consumers, informed and uninformed, all having consistent priors, and challenge the wisdom that informing consumers is efective only when doing so leads to a substantially higher average WTP overall. If our results are any indication, an increase in the average WTP is not even a precondition for versioning to be optimal.

The new model with informed and uninformed segments is not just more realistic but also provides us a direct way to explore how the optimal versioning strategy of the manufacturer depends on the relative sizes of the two segments. What we find is quite interesting. The wisdom that versioning is inefective when consumers have consistent priors is good only at the two extremes: one where all consumers are uninformed $( \beta = 0 )$ and the other where all are informed $( \beta = 1 )$ . Everywhere in between $( 0 < \beta < 1 )$ , this wisdom is brittle, and versioning can indeed be optimal at every interior $\beta .$ To elaborate, there are two other model parameters—the size of the consumer segment that would truly like the product when fully informed, α, and the extent of individual revisions in percentage terms, δ—whose sizes determine whether or not versioning is optimal at a given value of $\beta \in ( 0 , 1 )$ When $\beta$ is small, versioning is optimal when either α or $\delta$ is large. When $\beta$ becomes larger, the picture gets somewhat complicated, with versioning turning out to be optimal only at moderate values of $\delta$ for a range of α values. We discuss this apparent anomaly in detail as well as why it happens—while the reason for not versioning at a low δ is simply that informing consumers is not optimal then, that for not versioning at a high δ is that informing becomes too costly when versioning entails excessive cannibalization. All these results provide new insights into why and when versioning of experience information goods works the best in a market where consumers are potentially uninformed but have consistent priors.

We also provide an analysis of consumer and social welfare and find that the impact of versioning on welfare is critically linked to whether or not the market coverage expands with versioning. Interestingly, the coverage may not expand in some cases, a stark reminder that versioning to inform consumers is very diferent from traditional versioning strategies that seek to segment the market. As a result, both consumer and social welfare can dwindle with versioning even as the manufacturer’s profit spikes. Fortunately, however, in some other situations, the market coverage does expand as anticipated, leading to higher consumer and social welfare.

Although we provide a new perspective into the version-to-upgrade strategy and establish its usefulness beyond the previously analyzed contexts, we do abstract away a number of related issues. Such issues include piracy and network efects, which are common for information goods. Additionally, complementarity between the features of the base version and the premium one could be explored (Niculescu and Wu 2014)—it is entirely possible that a manufacturer might seek to enhance or limit this complementarity when adopting the product-line strategy recommended in this work. Addressing none of these issues is trivial, however. Further research is clearly necessary.

Likewise, our modeling setup is not suitable for exploring the value of free trials. For example, a feature-limited trial can certainly be viewed as a special case of versioning where the base version is given out for free. In our setup, though, a free base version trivially turns out to be an inferior choice to versioning, as the manufacturer would not want to leave a penny on the table by selling the base version for a price any lower than $\mathbf { \dot { \theta } } ( 1 - \delta ) > 0$ . In essence, it is difficult in our setup to make a fair comparison between versioning and freemium; for, in an attempt to keep our model parsimonious, we ignore a variety of issues that are critical in the freemium literature. Such factors include word of mouth, network efects, complementarity between the trial version and features not included in the trial, among several others. A more complete discussion of these issues is available in a comprehensive work by Niculescu and Wu (2014), as well as in works by a few others (e.g., Cheng and Liu 2012, Dey et al. 2013, Dou et al. 2013). Since we do not model any of these factors, our results cannot be extrapolated to the broader context of product sampling strategies, which includes time- and featurelimited trials as well as seeding. In fact, the only insight that carries over to an extent is the one with respect to efectively informing consumers. Specifically, Theorem 1 establishes that informing consumers can work even when doing so does not improve the average WTP, a result likely to have fallouts in contexts beyond versioning. However, further studies employing more sophisticated models are needed to clearly delineate these fallouts.

Another issue not considered in this work is that of competition. In fact, it is dificult to predict exactly how our results would be impacted by competition and whether the versioning regions would expand or contract. Competition, on one hand, could be viewed through the lens of a diminishing pricing power. If competition caps a manufacturer’s ability to increase prices, it ought to dampen the motive to ofer premium content, making versioning less appealing. On the other hand, in the absence of information, the competing products may look the same to consumers, leading to a less diferentiated market and intensifying competition in the process. Therefore, when consumers get fit information, the products may actually start looking diferent to them, easing price competition and restoring the pricing power somewhat (Hahn 2005). Put diferently, by informing consumers through versioning, it may actually become possible to relax competition, and when that happens, a manufacturer would be more likely to adopt versioning in the presence of competition. In short, the net efect of competition remains elusive and requires additional scrutiny.
