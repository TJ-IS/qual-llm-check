# Interoperability of E-Government Information Systems: Issues of Identification and Data Sharing

BENOÎT OTJACQUES, PATRIK HITZELBERGER, AND FERNAND FELTZ

BENOÎT OTJACQUES is a Deputy Scientific Director of the Informatics, Systems, and Collaboration Department at the Centre de Recherche Public–Gabriel Lippmann in Luxembourg. His research themes include information systems modeling, computer-supported cooperative work, human–computer interaction, and information visualization.

PATRIK HITZELBERGER is a Scientific Project Manager at the Centre de Recherche Public–Gabriel Lippmann in Luxembourg. His research interests include interoperability of IT systems, one-stop government, and business process modeling for information systems in the public sector.

FERNAND FELTZ is a Scientific Director of the Research Department of Informatics, Systems and Collaboration at the Centre de Recherche Public–Gabriel Lippmann in Luxembourg. He is responsible for various R&D projects in the domains of e-government, e-business, computer-supported cooperative work, knowledge management, and virtual organizations.

ABSTRACT: In business and government organizations, information systems often handle sensitive data about individuals and other organizations, using various kinds of identifiers. The growing cooperation of organizations results in the need to share and exchange such data. This collection and sharing, however, is affected by privacy concerns, and organizational and technical issues have to be solved and taken into account. This paper describes the results of an exploratory study in the government sector, focusing on the way public organizations manage identity-related data and the sharing of such data, either with other public agencies or with private organizations. Despite significant progress in harmonizing the legal and administrative provisions and technical standards in the European Union, there are still considerable cross-country differences regarding this subject. These differences—together with the growing mobility of goods, persons, and related data within the European Union—cause particular challenges for information systems in digital government in this region. After discussing and defining the key notions and methodology of the study, we present the status quo in 18 out of 25 EU member states and compare it to the results of a prior study by the same network done in 2001. Finally, we draw conclusions about identity management in cross-border contexts.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: data sharing, e-government, identifier, identity management, information sharing, interoperability, interorganizational information systems, privacy.

THE COLLECTION, STORAGE, AND MANAGEMENT of data about individuals and enterprises in information systems are routine tasks in virtually all public or private organizations. Businesses, public agencies, and nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) deal with customers, suppliers, employees, partners, and so on, as well as with the data related to these customers, citizens, individuals, or legal entities.

The potential of electronic information systems, however, and their opening to the Internet have invoked more and more concerns and questions regarding privacy during the last decades. Hiller and Belanger [13] point out the following issues related to the “online world”: faster and easier data collection, cross-referencing (aggregation), and hidden data collection. We describe these issues in more depth below:

• Faster and easier data collection. More and more, data is generated or derived in an electronic form that allows for easy loading and processing into databases.

• Cross-referencing (aggregation). Data about the same individual or business is handled in a number of independent information systems. This data can be shared and cross-referenced, or even merged, as might happen after organizational mergers, for example. More often, the exchange and sharing of data between organizations (within the private and public sector, or crossing this border) is necessary in order to provide seamless, integrated business or governmental services.

• Hidden data collection. The collection of data without consent is technically easy whenever this data is collected in electronic form, particularly on the Internet, where many users are normally not familiar with technologies such as cookies or the storage of connection parameters. Even if there is formal consent from the user, typically by default approval of standard terms and conditions, the user either does not thoroughly understand the consequences or has no alternative other than to not use the service at all.

One could add several more issues regarding information systems that deal with sensitive person-related data:

• Speed, (nearly) unlimited space, and advanced analytic technologies. Due to the continuing price decline of computing power and storage capacity, and the existence of advanced analytic processing technologies, such as data warehouse systems [16], new and more complex evaluations and profiling of person-related data can be carried out. The new quantity of data (both in terms of facts collected and records stored) brings a new quality of privacy issues along with it. It is technically feasible now, for example, to store and analyze connection data of complete populations—and to relate it to other information sources.

• Long-term durability of easily accessible data. Despite the fact that paper-based records theoretically still have a longer lifetime that is probably superior to current electronic media, it is possible to store information on digital media in an easily accessible form and for a long period. Special procedures to handle the access, deletion, and updating of such data are necessary.

• Sensitivity of data. Given the ubiquitous use of electronic information systems in all kinds of organizations and for all kinds of purposes, the data collected with or without the knowledge of the individuals concerned can be exceedingly sensitive. This is obvious for medical records, financial transactions, and so on. Sensitivity, however, depends on the context. The recent political debate about the sharing of airline passenger data between the European Union and the United States [5] illustrates this.

• Security of data. Due to the growing usage of identity-related data in information systems, the consequences of loss, theft, and errors of such data can be enormous. Recent examples, such as the mass theft of credit card data, are well-known for this [21]. “Federated identity management” solutions in which one system accepts the identity and authentication mechanisms of another are “sometimes criticized as exacerbating the problem of online identity theft” [24].

Despite these numerous issues regarding privacy, the collection and processing of person-related data in information systems is indispensable for the efficient deployment of these systems and has undisputed benefits—independent of the sector in which such systems are used. In the following, we focus on the subject of identification and sharing of identity-related data in the area of government information systems. In this field, data sharing and exchange is driven (among other things) by the fact that public agencies want to offer seamless, integrated services, following an analogous trend in business, as Lee et al. point out: “e-government initiatives aim at enabling government agencies to more efficiently work together and provide one-stop service to citizens and businesses. Such practices are somewhat analogous to supply chain management (SCM) in the business world, which stresses coordination and collaboration among supply chain partners” [22] (compare, e.g., [27] for an earlier work on interorganizational information systems [IOS] in business).

To enable this collaboration, agreements and common understandings on identity management, identification, and the exchange of data between the participating organizations are crucial. The most basic question regarding these agreements is how organizations identify individuals and businesses. More precisely: What are the technical issues, and which organizational and legal constraints exist for the management of identities? What is a digital identity?

## Background of the Study: The European Union and Identity Management in the Public Sector

SINCE THE ACCESSION OF 10 NEW STATES in May 2004, the European Union now consists of 25 member states. Moreover, there are four so-called candidate countries. According to current EU contracts, each of the member countries takes turn in offering a representative to act as president of the European Union for a six-month period. Besides administrative tasks, the principal responsibility of the presidency is to achieve a set of political and other objectives for the ongoing development of the European Union. The presiding country organizes meetings and activities of the numerous formal and semiformal institutions and groups in the Union. The European Public Administration Network (EPAN) is one of these. It consists of representatives from the national ministries and administrations responsible for managing the public sector of the member states, and it comprises a working group on e-government.

During Luxembourg’s presidency in the first half of 2005, this working group ordered a study (the complete study is available at www.eupan.org) to investigate the identification of citizens and enterprises and related data protection issues in the European Union—a subject that is becoming of more and more interest for many digital government initiatives and research (e.g., [1, 8, 15, 26]). The study was partly longitudinal because it was based on a similar one carried out by the same working group under the Belgian presidency in 2001—hence, before the accession of the 10 new member states to the European Union in 2004. About two-thirds of the data revealed was already asked in 2001. The objective was to gain an insight into the evolution of the subject over the past four years—evidently only for the 15 “old” members.

The explorative study analyzes an issue that can be placed in the general domain of the interoperability of information systems, applied to such systems in the public sector. Interoperability and cooperation can be regarded as enablers of the integration of e-government applications [18]. A prerequisite (or, according to Scholl [28], the “ultimate goal”) for any integrated, collaborating system and organization is the sharing of information or data. Such sharing is only possible when the identification of the entities the data describes is well understood.

There are numerous other aspects of interoperability and data and information sharing (e.g., [6, 12, 28]), but the study focalized on the identification and related data protection as enabler or hindering factor for this interoperability. It should be noted that we have not investigated the different national initiatives dealing with electronic identification (such as digital ID cards, digital signatures, and so on), but the existing systems for identification are still often paper-based and do or will serve as the base for the electronic systems as well.

## Identity, Identification, and Identifiers

WHAT IS “IDENTITY”? THIS QUESTION CAN BE regarded from, for example, a philosophical, a societal, or a psychological point of view, which leads to a certain lack of clarity and overload of the concept, illustrated by the existence of a lot of different identity notions such as gender identity, national identity, corporate identity, and digital iden tity, to name just a few. The Directive 95/46/EC [10] on data protection, which will be discussed below, enumerates “physical, physiological, mental, economic, cultural or social identity” [10, Article 2].

In this study, we limit ourselves to a more pragmatic approach. Nevertheless, what can be obtained from the existence of so many notions of identity is the fact that human beings most obviously play different roles in different societal contexts—an observation that has consequences for our research field as well. Without oversimplifying, and leaving a more philosophical discussion to the social sciences, we can come back to a generic term as a starting point for our discussion of identity: information systems involve data about entities. Chen’s definition of entity is: “An entity is a ‘thing’ which can be distinctly identified” [3]. We focus here on natural persons and legal entities. Thus, for our purposes, it is thus sufficient to define identity as a synonym for an individual particular human being or a particular enterprise.

The definition of the term identification can be derived consequentially. Clarke defines it for human beings (applicable for legal entities as well): “Human identification is the association of data with a particular human being” [4]. Camp [2] uses a similar approach. Based on her definition of attributes (“an attribute is a characteristic associated with an entity, such as an individual”), she defines identity as “that set of permanent or long-lived temporal attributes associated with an entity,” and identification as “the association of a personal identifier with an individual presenting attributes” [2].

There are numerous candidate attributes that allow for making such an association. In daily life, we normally just use a person’s name and associate it to an individual. While this generally works fine, and we even have means to cope with the fact that we normally know more than one “Joe” (we simply add more attributes: surnames, appearance, behavior, profession, or biometric, appearance characteristics such as “the one with the blond hair,” and so on), this concept is not suitable for the mass administration of natural and legal entities. Given that it is common that there is more than one attribute used (see results below), we can consider an identifier as a data set. It can consist of one or several attributes, be it “natural” (such as a person’s name, date of birth) or artificial (such as the value-added tax [VAT] number for legal entities).

According to our definition, we limit the scope of identifiers to specific data sets bound to people. Figure 1 illustrates that these data sets represent a small subset of the real-world characteristics of an actual—in this case natural—person. For identification purposes, the fact that someone plays violin, for instance, is irrelevant in most contexts. Please note that the database in Figure 1 is a conceptual term—the database is not necessarily an electronic one and may be a paper-based register in reality.

As pointed out above, the fact that people play different roles in different areas leads normally to the existence of more than one identifier for the same individual. It is worth mentioning that a person can (and, in most, cases actually does) have more than one identifier (or identifier data sets)—depending on the specific roles he or she or it plays in societal, economic, technical, and public contexts. We are used to having a name, perhaps a nickname, but also a member number in sports clubs, a social security number, and more and more identities in the digital, virtual world (e.g., nicknames in instant messaging systems).

Therefore, it is also justified to talk of more than one identity that the same person bears. This multiplicity makes it more and more necessary to define technical, organizational, and legal measures and processes to handle it, or in one term, identity management (called, more precisely, “identities management” by Koch and Möslein [19]). There is a strong technical bias in the research and development of identity management–related concepts. Notions such as digital signatures, authentication, smart cards, identity federation [24], and so forth, require and encompass advanced technical approaches to handle digital identities [7]. Nevertheless, paper-based identifiers for identifying natural persons and legal entities have existed for decades. The U.S. social security number, for example, which is used as a universal national identifier and authentication token, was created in 1936 [29].

Figure 1. Identity and Identification

All countries we investigated use such “traditional” identifiers for natural persons and legal entities. The most fundamental difference in these systems is the number of different identifiers used by public administrations in different countries. There might be a single, national, and “all-purpose” identifier (which we will call single identification number [SIN]), used by all administrations, and possibly by private organizations. Or there might be several sector-specific identifiers which are independent of each other—or actually a combination of these, although this could be regarded as being contradictory. The coexistence of so-called single and further identifiers could be explained nonetheless either as a transitional phenomenon, because the implementation of a national identifier takes time, or as a definition problem, meaning that “single” in this case means “almost” single, and further identifiers exist only in niches.

Independent from their realization, the national systems had and will have a strong impact on any technical solutions, especially for cross-border e-government applications. Besides the technical and organizational aspect of identifiers, data sharing applications are highly affected by data protection and privacy concerns.

## Data Protection

PRIVACY OF DATA BECOMES AN ISSUE whenever data is processed that can or must be related to people. This is why identification and data protection and sharing are strongly interdependent subjects. In order to harmonize the data protection legislation in its member states, the European Union promulgated its Directive 95/46/EC (hereafter “Directive”) in 1995 [10]. This Directive was closely related to the creation of the “internal market” of the European Union—a market that entailed a high degree of mobility both of goods and information. Although there are further important international guidelines and provisions regarding data protection, for the European Union, this Directive represents the overarching legal basis and the minimal requirements applicable on national and EU levels. (For a discussion of the federal policy and practice in the United States, see [14].)

The EU member states are obliged to implement the general provisions of the European Union as national applicable law. This process is more complex than a simple translation, because directives do not take into account national particularities and remain necessarily general. Consequentially, the national implementation is prone to generate a certain cross-country divergence.

It is outside the scope of this paper to explain the Directive in detail. Within the study, we evaluated the national laws and provisions brought into force chiefly regard ing the following aspects:

• Implementation of Article 8.7, which explicitly asks the member states to “determine the conditions under which a national identification number or any other identifier of general application may be processed.”

• Implementation of the specific rights the Directive guarantees to the “data subjects” (the person whose data is processed or recorded)—namely, the information and notification right, the access right, and the right to object.

• Provisions regarding the obligation to notify the so-called supervisory authorities before carrying out any processing operation of personal data, especially related to identifiers.

• The sharing of databases using identifiers.

• The usage of the identifiers by private bodies and citizens.

## The Objectives of the Study

THE STUDY AIMED (1) to gather data about the current situation in the EU member states and the four candidate countries regarding the way these states identify citizens (and other subgroups of natural persons) and legal entities. In particular, we were interested in the procedures and technical details of all national systems that use an SIN for this purpose. Furthermore, we wanted (2) to investigate the laws and provisions relevant to the processing of identifiers and related data. In other words, the objective was to examine the national legal implementations of the EU Directive as mentioned above.

In the European Union, as elsewhere, the political discussion about identifying individuals has changed significantly since 2001. To name just one example, the UK “Identity Cards Bill,” which is supposed to change completely the UK national system of identification, has sparked a lively debate in terms of its impact on the society and civil rights [25]. Despite this, we have confined ourselves to evaluating the European status quo of identity management and data and privacy protection application in this area, and do not make any political statement.

## Methodology

THIS EXPLORATIVE STUDY IS BASED on a partly longitudinal, iterative expert survey. This approach resembles a Delphi design in terms of the repeated interrogations of experts. However, we were not particularly interested in the experts’ judgments and opinions regarding the future situation of identity management but in evaluating the status quo in every EU country. The repeated questioning aimed to augment the comprehension of the answers where necessary, and was not thought to come to a common consensus in the expert group or to provide forecasting. Furthermore, due to the composition of the expert panel, the anonymity of the experts was neither possible nor desirable. Consequently, the Delphi design has not been completely realized.

The experts for the relevant domains of data protection and identity management were systematically chosen by EPAN representatives from their respective countries. In order to collect the necessary basic data, a questionnaire consisting of 17 mostly open-ended questions focused on four issues: existence of an SIN, technical aspects, organizational aspects, and legal aspects. As one of the objectives of the study was to reveal the developments and changes since the 2001 Belgian study regarding the SIN, the first three parts were retaken from the 2001 Belgian study. It is important to point out that all questions were designed with the perspective of developing a possible network of national identification systems within the European Union. The legal aspects part was composed (and evaluated) in collaboration with experts from Luxembourg’s National Data Protection Commission (Commission Nationale pour la Protection des Données [CNPD]).

In the beginning of 2005, the questionnaire was sent by e-mail to the representatives of each EPAN member and then forwarded to the national experts. Eighteen of the 25 EU countries contacted answered, which equals a 62 percent response rate. (When disregarding the candidate countries, the rate was 68 percent.) In 2001, all 15 members replied. Some countries that did not answer the questionnaire in 2005 informed us that their status has not changed significantly since 2001. This confirms the results and trends which we will illustrate below.

After the first deadline, all answers were shortened and edited in order to harmonize terms and vocabulary used by the respondents. Then, synoptical tables with the results from all the countries that answered were redistributed to the experts, allowing them to verify the editing process, and to be “inspired” by the answers that other countries had given for completing or modifying their original answers. Moreover, in cases of unclear or missing information, the experts were contacted directly by the authors in order to clarify, amend, or complete the answers. After this second phase, a preliminary report was compiled and presented during an EPAN working group meeting in May 2005. A last round of verification followed and led to some final clarifications and enhancements.

## Results of the Study

## Existence of a Single Identifier

IN TERMS OF THE IDENTIFICATION OF PEOPLE IN GENERAL, the data shows a tendency toward the usage of national single identification numbers. Regarding natural persons, in 2001, nine out of 15 (60 percent) countries had a national SIN, and one country (Austria) envisaged the installation of such a number. In 2005, 14 out of 18 countries (78 percent) used an SIN, and two countries (Ireland and the United Kingdom) had ongoing projects to realize it in the future. Another two countries (Germany and Hungary) were explicitly against the installation of such numbers, and gave data protection concerns as the reason (see Figure 2).

Regarding legal entities, the tendency toward unification is similar: In 2001, nine of the 15 member states had an SIN (60 percent), and two planned to install it. In 2005, 15 out of 18 had an SIN for legal entities (83 percent). No country indicated plans here (see Figure 3).

## Technical and Organizational Differences

Although the notion “single” might be relatively clear at first glance, the usage of a truly single national identifier in all public and economic areas is still rather rare—especially for natural persons. The official label “single” for an identifier does not mean that, in practice, there are no further administrative databases that use different, sector-specific identifiers. Only a few countries have established a real homogeneous system along with the organizational and legal framework for it, such as, for example, Sweden and Luxembourg. Nevertheless, among the countries where there are further identifiers besides the “official” single one, some indicate that this SIN will gradually replace the sector-specific and parallel systems.

## Construction of the SIN and Linked Data

Again, the possible interoperability of identification systems was the main reason for examining the construction of the single identifier (where existent) and the actual data set directly linked to it. We focus first on natural persons here.

As regards the construction of the number, a minority of countries, three out of 14, have identifiers composed of purely random figures (Cyprus, the Netherlands, Spain) where such a number already exists. The other 11 countries use codes that bear a certain semantic. Typically, the code contains the date of birth and a consecutive number, sometimes sex, a code for the place of birth, and other informational bits. Figure 4 illustrates the typical structure of the SIN. Note that a few countries embed nationality information within the SIN. In Cyprus, a prefix is used to distinguish Cypriots from non-Cypriots. Malta uses a more radical approach, as the structure of the SIN for Maltese citizens and foreigners is completely different. The Czech Republic reports that there are ongoing domestic discussions about the coding of the date of birth and sex in the identifier. The Austrian system differs significantly in general and will be illustrated below.

As regards the data set that is linked directly to the identifier (see Figure 1 for the concept), the replies show a wide variety in the European Union. The “minimalist” approach limits the fields to the ones that are absolutely necessary to reliably identify individuals. This core set consists of surname, first name, sex, date of birth, and the place of birth (Lithuania is the only exception to this rule and does not register the place of birth).

Apart from this minimum, we found more than 30 further possible identity attributes. We have grouped them into different sets. A first group includes the marital status, the ID/name of a spouse, the name of household members, the parents’ names/IDs, and the children’s, which we called “household information.” The second set regroups data related to authentication, namely signature, public key digital signature, and photograph. We also found a group of data that makes a link to other identification systems: electoral booklet number, social insurance number, driver’s license number, refugee booklet number, military number, birth document number, and identity card number. Another data set is composed of information related to the judicial history of the person—that is, imprisonment information, placement under guardianship, deprivation of legal capacity, and restriction of residency. The data about the death of the person (date, place, and cause of death) must also be specifically grouped. The next set of data regroups potentially highly sensitive information, such as nationality, community, religion, education, and legal basis for stay. Considering the potential threat that this data may cause to privacy, it is clear that some countries would probably never accept linking such data to an identification number. We grouped the remaining data into a group called “various data.” It includes the former names, academic or other titles, and the classic “remarks/other” field.

## Organizational and Other Differences

In this subsection, we briefly illuminate further areas where significant divergence regarding the processing and definition of identifiers could be found. As regards the groups of (natural) persons covered by the national identification numbers, we can sum up that besides the expected “citizens,” the experts also mentioned, for example, residents, people born in the country, foreign workers, migrants, refugees, or taxpayers. Different definitions, restrictions, and provisions apply to these groups. The fact that, for example, migrant workers also normally obtain a national SIN reflects the situation that there is no Union-wide ID that could be used, at minimum, to identify a citizen of the European Union.

There is more consensus in terms of the public authority that should be responsible for the management of the identifier database or register for natural persons: in most countries, the national Ministry of Interior is charged with that. In Sweden, however, this role is taken by the “Swedish Tax Agency,” which is a government authority under the Ministry of Finance. The operating authorities are either dedicated registers, such as Lithuania’s People Register, or technical bodies, such as the Luxembourg State Centre for Informatics, which has many other tasks.

Investigating possible cross-border interoperability, it seemed interesting to us to examine the already existing domestic approaches of networking different national databases. Here, Lithuania, for example, reported an exhaustive list of registers that access the central identifier database—in this case, supported by a technical replication solution provided by a database manufacturer. A similar list was given by Denmark and Malta, but those countries just “use” the SIN in other registers—there is no technical and well-defined propagation process. Cyprus uses its single identifier as a primary key in sector-specific databases. As regards legal entities, many countries have already installed online accessible public registers of the data.

In sum, most countries allow either direct access or distribute the identifiers and the related data to other registers—which should not be surprising, because the usage of the SIN in such registers follows the basic idea of such an identifier. However, the technical realization of access differs a lot.

As regards the national documents that comprise the SIN, there is a more or less common subset that normally includes identity cards and passports, and very often sector-specific documents such as social security or health system cards. Beyond this, some countries, such as Cyprus, Spain, and Denmark, for example, use the SIN as a multipurpose identifying characteristic on nearly all documents with personalrelated data, and also allow its usage for private purposes. This reminds us of the usage of employee numbers in companies, where a pragmatic and demand-driven view prevails.

## Legal Issues

In this section, again, we can only exemplify some areas of divergence in the European Union regarding data protection and identifiers. Furthermore, we limit the presentation of the results to natural persons here.

## Processing of Personal Data

Obligation to notify the authority. The Directive states that a supervisory authority must be notified beforehand of any processing of personal data. In fact, we found that in most countries, a general notification to the supervisory authority is required before processing personal data. For instance, in Austria, as a matter of principle, the Austrian Data Processing Register has to be notified of each data application.

Regarding the processing of identifiers, the general approach seems to be to consider the identifier analogous to other personal data and therefore not to submit it to special rules under the obligation of notifying the supervisory authority. For instance, in Denmark, no specific notification is required on the sole basis that the processing concerns civil registration numbers.

The Directive also allows for exemptions to the notification rule. According to the answers received, only Denmark and Ireland offer no possibilities for exemptions.

In all other countries, exemptions are possible. These exemptions fall into several categories:

• appointment of a personal data protection official;

• specific data processing explicitly listed in legislation;

• processing of data with political, philosophical, religious, or trade union aims; and

• processing of data with national or public security concerns.

For special cases, the personal data processing is subject to prior checks (Article 20).

Yet, again, different attitudes were encountered:

• In some countries, no prior check is required.

• Other countries impose a prior check in some cases, depending on the nature of the data processing or on its context.

• Some countries also rely on the initiative of the supervisory authority. For instance, in the Czech Republic, if the Office for Personal Data Protection, after having been notified of a personal data processing, has a justified concern about the processing, it shall initiate proceedings.

## Rights of the Data Subject

The Directive guarantees the data subject several rights—namely, an information and notification right, an access right, and a right to object. We strived to find out how these rights are realized in the national legislations. From a general viewpoint, all these rights are granted in the national legislations. It is useful, however, to discuss in detail each of these rights to point out some nuances in their implementation.

In all countries, the data subject is granted the information and notification right. Nevertheless, the information that is communicated to the data subject, the time when it is communicated, and the process of communication may vary. The set of data that the data subject is notified about includes, at the very least, the purpose of the data process, as well as the name and address of the controller. In many cases, it is requested that complementary data be communicated, such as the recipient, or the categories of recipients of the data, or the rights of the data subject concerning the processing of his or her personal data.

The answers show that some countries provide certain exemptions to the information and notification right. The most common exemptions are summarized in the (nonexhaustive) list below:

• processing of personal data obtained with the consent of the data subject;

• personal data processing imposed by an act or a law;

• personal data processing performed for statistical, historical, or scientific purposes;

• personal data processing carried out for national security reasons;

• personal data processing in the context of detection of criminal offenses, or related investigation; and

• processing of lawfully published personal data.

In all countries, the data subject has an access right. The data subject usually has access to the following information:

• the confirmation that some data concerning him or her is or is not being processed;

• the processed data;

• the available purpose of the use of data; and

• the recipients of the data.

In some countries, the data subject has access to complementary information, such as, for example, the legal basis for the processing. In Cyprus, there is a right to access information concerning the progress of the processing. Included with the access right, the data subject is given the right to obtain the correction of any incorrect personal data that concerns him or her. Some countries mention that the correction must be offered free of charge to the data subject (e.g., Belgium).

Finally, it must be noted that some countries provide exceptions to the access right, as the following example illustrates: In Sweden, access must not be provided in some exceptional situations where the Secrecy Act prescribes that information may not be disclosed to the data subject. The way to access the personal data is normally not specified by law. The type of access given to the data subject depends on the specific context. Several approaches may be identified from the cases reported in the answers:

• In Belgium, due to the existence of an electronic identity card, online access is allowed and authentication is achieved via the identification certificate stored on the electronic identity card.

• Some countries offer online access but only for some registers.

• In the United Kingdom, since January 2005, the information may be sent by e-mail to the person making the request.

• In the Netherlands, individuals in general do not have online access to their data.

In all countries, the data subject has a right to object to the processing of his or her personal data. We did not find any significant differences in the national implementations of this right.

## Data Sharing Between Administrations

We evaluated the provisions regarding the transfer, sharing, interconnection, and exchange of personal data between public agencies or administrative authorities using an identifier. We asked whether those operations have to be authorized explicitly by a specific law or any other provision; in particular, if the public interest pursued and the purpose for which the data is intended by the various administrations are different. For general data sharing between administrations, it appears that, in most of the cases, the processing has to be authorized by specific laws. Nevertheless, we have been made aware of other approaches. In Belgium, for instance, the authorization is given by the supervisory authority. In Cyprus, the provisions of the general Data Protection Law cover the cases related to this issue.

For the data sharing between administrations using an identifier, the collected answers show that many countries have no specific authorization or provision that would apply in this specific case. For instance, Austria mentions that “regardless of the constitutional principle that every usage of data by a public authority requires the legal form of an act, no additional authorization is needed due to the use of new identifiers.” Nevertheless, some exceptions are reported. In Ireland, for instance, the sharing of data using a single identifier has to be allowed by law.

We also evaluated if the advice of the supervisory authority has to be asked before giving such an authorization. We found several attitudes:

• some countries do not require the comment of the supervisory authority at all;

• some countries mention that the supervisory authority has to be consulted in some specific circumstances;

• some countries do not require that the supervisory authority be asked for comments, but, in practice, there is often some consultation;

• some countries report that the supervisory authority is consulted during the legislative process; and

• some countries require the supervisory authority to be involved.

A further question treated the data sharing at the international level and asked whether it is submitted to specific provisions. The survey indicates that most of the countries impose some specific and possibly cumulative provisions, such as

• the transfer to a foreign country is allowed only if this country assures an adequate level of personal data protection;

• the transfer demands a specific legal basis in order for it to take place;

• the transfer is permitted if the data subject has provided his or her consent;

• the transfer is necessary for the prevention or investigation of criminal offenses; and

• the transfer is necessary for the conclusion or performance of a contract with specific requirements.

## Shared Databases

The questionnaire also investigated the issue of shared databases; in particular, when these databases are deployed by public authorities and agencies and when the data stored includes at the same time common entities recorded or accessed by all organizations, and entities restricted to the organization(s) for which these entities are relevant. The responding countries have adopted two main attitudes concerning this point. On one hand, most of the countries allow the presence of shared databases, but only in some specific circumstances and under strict conditions. First, the operations relative to shared databases must obviously meet the principles stated in the Personal Data Protection legislation. Second, some countries impose some specific and sometimes cumulative conditions, such as

• obtaining prior checks and authorization by the supervisory authority;

• being authorized by a specific law;

• being kept up-to-date and not being considered as a reference data source;

• taking appropriate measures to ensure that the admissibility of individual access can be monitored at all times; and

• appointing a suitable operator for the shared database.

On the other hand, some countries, such as the Czech Republic and Hungary, do not allow shared databases. Yet some mechanisms to exchange data, in particular, for synchronizing purposes, are permitted.

## Allowed Use of the Single Identification Number

The first question of this subsection of the survey concerned the use of the SIN by private bodies for their internal needs. The answers show that very divergent national provisions exist:

• some countries prohibit the use of the SIN in this case;

• some countries allow the use of the SIN but enforce strict conditions, such as (1) explicit consent of the data subject, (2) use follows from law or regulation, and (3) processing is carried out for scientific or statistical purposes; and

• some countries, such as Spain and Bulgaria, allow using the SIN in this case.

Another question focused on the use of the SIN in contacts between private bodies and public administrations. Several countries permit the use of the SIN for such contacts (e.g., Bulgaria, Lithuania). In some cases, this type of use is allowed while the preceding one is prohibited (e.g., Belgium). The other countries limit the circumstances in which the use of the SIN is allowed (e.g., in France, where the use is limited to contacts with social security organizations) or add conditions similar to those required for the two previous cases (e.g., explicit consent of the data subject).

## The Special Case of the Austrian Identification System

In 2004, Austria put in place a new technology-based identification system (for a detailed description, see [11] for the legal base and [23] for a technical discussion). The system, on one hand, takes into account the strong, history-related data protection concerns that prevail in the country. On the other hand, it provides an efficient way of identification management and serves as an enabler for e-government solutions, also treating authentication-related issues (digital signature, public key infrastructure [PKI], etc.). All Austrian citizens are registered in the national Central Register of Residents (CRR) (this was already the case before the new system) and have a registration number. From this number, a “source identification number” (sPIN) is derived, using a strong encryption algorithm. This number is stored on the new electronic citizen card. When used in contacts with public administrations, the sPIN serves together with a sectorspecific tag as input for the encryption algorithm for “sector-specific PINs” (ssPINs) for exclusive usage in the corresponding sector (e.g., tax administration). This means that, in practice, each sector must use different identifiers, hence the combination of the central identity management and its advantages with a strong “silo” approach making uncontrolled data sharing outside well-defined sectors impossible. ## Conclusions

BEFORE SUMMING UP THE CONCLUSIONS OF THE STUDY, two principal limitations regarding its scope and its significance need to be pointed out. First, as a horizontal restriction, not all of the member states and candidate countries have answered the questionnaire. Hence, all conclusions drawn are—strictly speaking—just valid for the member states and candidate countries of the European Union that have answered to the survey. For the reason of readability, we simply speak of Europe or “all countries.” Second, as a vertical restriction, the study could only highlight its subjects, because identity management, data protection, and data sharing are very complex and emerging topics, and because the scope of the answers differed—depending on the question and the country.

Nevertheless, the results obtained and the comparison with the study of the Belgian presidency of 2001 have allowed for some interesting conclusions that might help us to gain a sound insight into the treated issues. Moreover, when it is appropriate, we will try to draw some comparisons with general identification number and data protection issues in the private sector.

As a further general remark, it is worth mentioning that there is a clear difference between identifiers for natural persons and legal entities. For natural persons, we found concerns and, more often, formal rules to protect their data usage—even if the degree of attentiveness in this area varies. Generally, it seems that identifiers for legal entities, however, are handled pragmatically, and that most countries regard their identification as a technical issue that has no or just a “slight” link to data protection issues. There has been feedback from some experts that this approach might be problematic, at least for cases in which the enterprise is identical to the person who runs it. If the distinction between a natural person and legal entity is obvious in the e-government context, it appears much more subtle in the business context. Indeed, the temptation may be great for private companies to consider their customers equally, whatever their nature (other companies or individuals). Therefore, the fact that different rules apply to these categories may raise significant issues for the companies handling data such as customer profiles. Simply reducing the individual customer to an economic agent is not acceptable in complying with the regulation. Considering its importance in our study, we focus on natural persons in this section hereafter.

As a last important general remark, we must mention that the results show that there is no single, “optimal” solution for the identification issue in e-government that can be regarded as appropriate for every country. National culture, legal aspects, technical feasibility, costs, and many more factors have to be taken into account when installing and deploying such solutions, and for good reasons—the divergence of this framework results in distinct, specific applications. One might conclude that this divergence is an obstacle. A couple of countries explicitly pointed out, nevertheless, that legal “obstacles” are desired obstacles. In this context, it may be useful to restate that the principal objective of Directive 95/46/EC was not to facilitate data sharing—it provided regulations in terms of the “protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data.” The study did not evaluate the quality of national approaches—it tried to describe the differences between them.

This diversity in legal provisions concerning personal data protection may raise challenging issues for multinational companies, as each of their subsidiaries must potentially satisfy different rules. The recent Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication (SWIFT) affair [9], where an EU company with a U.S. subsidiary faced major problems because it had to comply with both EU and U.S. legislation, clearly illustrates such a case at an international level. From a business point of view, the lack of harmonized legislation may potentially hinder or forbid the compilation of customer data collected in different countries.

Our study also shows a tendency toward the acceptance of single, multipurpose identifiers in the European Union. Since the Belgian study in 2001, three countries have either realized former plans to introduce an SIN for natural persons, or plans are under way or are likely to be realized in the near future. Germany is now the only country of the 15 original EU members that does not have and does not want to install a single identifier in the future. Hungary, a new member, shares this attitude.

Regarding the legal aspect that has also been investigated, all countries have brought into force national laws and regulations that conform to Directive 95/46/EC. Hence, on a very general level, one might assume that the legal framework for the essential question of pan-European data sharing of person-related data using identifiers can be regarded as a “piece of cake”: There is a single identifier nearly everywhere, and all members refer to the same directive for regulating data protection. In reality, however, the definition of the SIN is not clear. The identifier is not “really” single in most of the countries that have indicated that they have such an SIN. The majority of the respondents have reported the existence of further identification systems in their countries. Sometimes, these numbers are totally independent from each other or the “single” identifier; sometimes there are common keys or other mechanisms of synchronization between these sector-specific numbers and the general, central identifier. The degree of centralism depends on technical issues or historical evolution. For most of the countries, however, the fact that parallel and sector-specific IDs exist is a clear obstacle when sharing data on a domestic level—and might cause even more trouble when such an exchange is realized in a cross-border administrative process.

Besides the denomination problem, the construction of the identifier is based chiefly on two different philosophies. The first and bigger group of countries use a semantic approach in terms of coding, usually the date of birth and sex but seldom any further information. The second group uses random numbers, sometimes with data protection concerns as a reason for this. Again, these diverging attitudes are potential hindrances when sharing data. In business, this distinction is less relevant. Indeed, in most cases, random (often sequential) numbers are given to the identified entities (e.g., customers, suppliers, etc.). If a semantic approach is used, it rarely creates data protection issues either because the data is public (e.g., creation date of a supplier company) or not sensible (e.g., customer VAT number).

In our study, we observed that further remarkable discrepancies exist in terms of the handling of the SIN regarding

• the amount of data attributes linked to the identifiers;

• the person subgroups obtaining an SIN;

• the documents that comprise identifying numbers;

• the legislative and organizational framework set up to rule the use of identification numbers; and

• the supervisory authority mentioned in the Directive may play various roles concerning the protection of personal data.

This result may also raise questions when transferred to a business context. Previous research has shown the importance of customer relationship management for consumer satisfaction (e.g., [17, 20]), but less focus has been put on the fact that customer profiles that link purchasing habits, accounting data, family composition, and so forth, to customer identifiers may create some problems in terms of privacy, especially with regard to who can access the data.

We observed that the three basic rights mentioned in the Directive (information and notification right, access right, right to object) are granted to the individuals, but the details of the provisions differ. The extent to which these rules are given to identify entities in the private companies is worth investigating. More precisely, the evaluation should focus on the real means provided on the field to implement them rather than on the formal process theoretically available for the identified entities.

This paper could only highlight areas where we found some differences in the implementation of these rules in the European public sector. As a general conclusion, we can state that the reality regarding single identification numbers and the related data protection legislation and provisions in the European Union is very heterogeneous. This heterogeneity results obviously from the different national legal, political, and historical frameworks, on one hand, and the actual chosen solutions for identity management, on the other hand. Despite this heterogeneity, the majority of countries are concerned with data protection–related issues when dealing with identifiers and related data. As we have seen, most of the countries affirm the installation of an SIN. Nevertheless, the countries that decided not to put such a system in place must and do use different mechanisms for identification. The evaluation criteria used depend heavily on national particularities, politics, and historical developments. Irrespective of the basic decision of whether or not to install an SIN, the concrete realization of the national solution consists not only of the implementation of a mere number but also of a complete package of different measures and procedures. Taking a formal approach, this package can be seen as a triplet of organizational, technical, and legal measures and provisions. The study shows the high discrepancy of these national triplets. It is evident that this discrepancy can hamper cross-border data sharing. But, as was already pointed out above, this is—technically seen—a neutral statement, because the hindrances might be volitional, from a political point of view. Even technically advanced systems, such as the Austrian and Belgian ones that could be designed according to recent e-government and data protection and privacy needs and insights, are not necessarily compatible, because the triplet “behind” is perhaps too divergent.

On the contrary, the divergence of the triplets does not totally exclude cross-border data sharing. The report has shown that there are still a lot of similarities; for instance, the tendency to affirm the necessity of an SIN or the data protection Directive as a common base for national data protection legislation. Any e-government solution that realizes pan-European data sharing should nevertheless scrutinize the concerned national triplets in its analysis phase and strive to find compatible solutions. Although this study investigated the situation in Europe, we hope that the results and the preliminary conclusions we have drawn might be of interest for areas outside this continent as well.
