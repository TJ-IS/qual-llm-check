# A Strategic Analysis of Multi-Channel Expert Services

M. TOLGA AKÇURA AND ZAFER D. OZDEMIR

M. TOLGA AKÇURA (tolga.akcura@ozyegin.edu.tr) is a professor of marketing at Ozyegin University in Istanbul, Turkey, and founder of a high-tech startup, eBrandValue.com. Previously, he taught at Purdue University and Carnegie Mellon University, from where he also holds a Ph.D. His research has appeared in Management Science, Marketing Science, Decision Sciences, Decision Support Systems, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, and others.

ZAFER D. OZDEMIR (ozdemir@miamioh.edu; corresponding author) is a professor of information systems at the Farmer School of Business, Miami University. He holds a Ph.D. from Purdue University. His research focuses on economics of e-commerce and e-health and has appeared in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Decision Sciences, Decision Support Systems, and others.

ABSTRACT: Using stylized models, we investigate when and how expert service providers should offer their services online, and whether they should charge separate prices for face-to-face and online services or provide the online service as a free supplement. Interestingly, consumer surplus can rise when a monopolist charges different prices for face-to-face and online services, and it may drop when the monopolist starts offering the online service as a free add-on to its face-to-face service. We find that a market-wide adoption of the online channel by competing experts in a duopoly setting intensifies price competition and thereby reduces overall profits. Furthermore, the rate of adoption is highest when the online service is moderately effective, whereas one of the experts refuses to offer the service when it is highly effective. These results provide theoretical support for the viability of online expert services as well as practical guidance on pricing strategies.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: channel management, expert services, expertise pricing, game theory, online expertise, pricing.

Think of a local technology support company considering supporting its clients remotely online. Given that some technology issues are hardware-related and cannot be handled online, should they even try it? If they do, should they charge separate fees for online and offline services, or should they bundle them and offer a single price for both types of services? How does the channel decision of the other main competitor in town affect the answers to these questions? The purpose of this study is to present simple game-theoretic models and use them to explore possible courses of action that can be taken by expert service providers who find themselves in a similar situation.

Consumers often seek the help of professional service providers such as lawyers, doctors, designers, financial advisers, tax consultants, and accountants. While these services have traditionally been provided via face-to-face channels (such as a doctor’s office), recent advances in and widespread adoption of digital and e-commerce technologies have made the Internet a viable alternative. Broadband Internet access has become the norm, and the share of e-commerce among all retail commerce transactions has been rising steadily, from 2.8 percent in 2006 to over 6 percent in 2015 according to the U.S. Department of Commerce. This overall rising trend in business-to-consumer e-commerce has also been observed in expert service markets, albeit at a slower pace than retail, as many providers have started to offer online services.

Health care is a notable example. Many doctors now offer online consultations and treatments through websites such as American Well, TelaDoc, SwiftMD, MDlive, and Doctor on Demand. In fact, online doctor visits are reportedly one of the biggest trends in health care these days.<sup>1</sup> Companies and their employees benefit because virtual visits reduce work hours lost due to unnecessary office visits for minor medical issues as well as emergency room visits that can be avoided with timely access to a physician. Providers benefit because virtual visits allow them to connect with their customers in an efficient way and leverage downtime. A typical virtual consultation costs \$40 to \$50 which is less than visiting an emergency room, an urgent care facility, or a doctor’s office [26]. The American Telemedicine Association estimates that about 450,000 patients will see a doctor through the Internet this year for a primary care consultation, and many more are expected to receive specialty care online [36]. Walgreens and major insurers (such as UnitedHealthcare and Anthem) have recently announced initiatives to facilitate and encourage virtual visits.

From the consumer’s perspective, the Internet offers potentially low prices as well as the convenience of interacting with an expert online without the hassle of physically going to an office. For example, a consumer seeking psychological therapy can use VirtualTherapyConnect.com to identify online experts and schedule appointments. On average, a traditional office visit costs \$150, while an online session is available for under \$50 [25]. An initial virtual visit can be followed up by a face-to-face visit if the problem cannot be solved online. Online expert services are not exclusive to medicine; similar services are also offered by lawyers (e.g., rocketlawyer.com), tax consultants (hrblock.com), tutors (e.g., tutor.com), and computer support professionals (support.com). Also becoming popular are portals that bring together consumers and experts from a variety of disciplines and professional areas (e.g., prestoexperts.com, justanswers.com, elance.com, guru.com, and odesk. com). Again, these online options are typically cheaper for consumers than their face-to-face counterparts, as in the case of H&R Block, which offers face-to-face tax solutions for over \$100 and online services that cost between \$40 and \$75.

In this context, we maintain that market structure and the effectiveness of technology in facilitating the expert–client interaction are two main factors that can predict adoption. Online visits would not be appropriate for situations when either the technology is poor or the expert-client interaction requires the physical presence of the client (e.g., in order to collect blood samples for medical diagnosis). One would, however, expect a high level of adoption of the online channel beyond a certain level of effectiveness or quality. Similarly, provider incentives associated with market structure can play a significant role in adoption, and a more competitive landscape in which providers feel a need to attract clients could lead to a higher level of adoption of the online channel than would be the case in a business environment with less competition.

While the emergence of the Internet and the technological feasibility of providing complex expert solutions online have led many expert service providers to adopt their own online channels, many others have not followed suit. There are also differences in the pricing strategies among the adopters because some providers offer plans that cover online and face-to-face visits (e.g., Geek Squad provides technical support services at a fixed price that covers both online and face-to-face visits), compared with the typical practice of charging different prices for online and offline services. These intriguing differences in firm strategies lead us to ask the following questions: What drives the difference in channel strategies? When should firms offer online expert services, and how should they price them? How does the intensity of competition affect channel adoption and pricing decisions of firms? How will technology-related improvements in online service quality affect the availability of online services? And how will the availability of online expert services affect consumer and social surplus?

To our knowledge, there is limited research that addresses these issues. While previous research did examine some of these issues in the context of retail products, their results do not apply in the context of expert services where service quality can vary substantially across channels and consumers engage in multiple visits to obtain a satisfactory outcome.

We develop a game-theoretic model to investigate these issues. In the model, consumers search for a solution to a specific problem and may acquire multiple services for a satisfactory resolution. They can obtain either a face-to-face or an online service assuming both are offered. A consumer who is not satisfied online may then consider a second, face-to-face visit. In this context, we first investigate whether a monopolist will offer online expert services and then examine how duopolistic competition affects the optimal strategies, while paying close attention to the implications for firm profits, consumer surplus, and social welfare.

It turns out that the decision to offer online services is not trivial for expert service providers. We find that a monopolist would benefit from adopting the online channel since doing so reduces its costs. We also find that the monopolist would charge a single price for both services and offer the online service as an add-on to its traditional offering when the online service can be offered with a reasonable quality. Interestingly, consumers can be worse off when the monopolist offers the online service as an add-on to the traditional service. We also show that the rate of adoption of the online channel in a duopoly setting does not monotonically increase with the quality of the online service. In particular, there is a threshold quality level below which neither firm will offer an online service. Both firms offer online services when the quality is above that threshold (but not by too much), while only one of them adopts the online channel when the service quality is close to that of a face-to-face visit. In other words, the adoption of the online channel by expert firms first increases and then decreases with the quality of the online service.

Our analytical analysis of firms’ incentives to offer online expert services provides insights on the interaction of these incentives with the prevailing market structure. The interplay between online service quality and market structure as well as the corresponding pricing strategies that we characterize here can guide future empirical investigations in this domain with their hypothesis development. If empirically confirmed, these results can guide firms regarding when and how to offer online services in monopolistic and duopolistic markets. Given our finding that the availability of online services should increase social welfare, there may be room for public policy making in terms of facilitating the introduction of these services online. In that light, subject to empirical confirmation, firms may need to be incentivized not just when the quality is slightly deficient but also when the quality becomes sufficiently high. We elaborate on potential empirical results that would verify our analytical results in the last section of the study.

## Related Literature

This research is related to the literature on how the presence of online channels impacts firm behavior and price competition [6, 13, 31, 40, 48, 49]. Evidence suggests that the Internet can lead to lower prices [8, 12] and a higher consumer surplus [11]. Furthermore, it has been shown that online direct channels can be used by a manufacturer to discipline its retailers [17]. Also, a multichannel strategy can increase customer loyalty [44]. In situations where price consistency across channels matters, retailers can deter online entry by not adopting the online channel [33]. This research is also related to work that examines the impact of new information technologies on consumer welfare. Specifically, past research has shown that investments in information technology improve consumer surplus [9], and that information technology positively impacts the production of services in the health-care industry [34].

While the above research findings are on the impact of business-to-consumer e-commerce and information technology on firm strategies in general, we focus on the expert services market and contribute to the research in information systems on experts service providers [2, 32, 37]. There are significant differences between selling products and offering expert services online. The first important difference is related to the interaction between product/service quality and the delivery channel. In traditional retail, product quality typically does not change with the channel. A book purchased online from Amazon is the same book purchased offline from

Barnes & Noble. To the extent that product attributes are observable (as in homogeneous goods), customer search will focus on price and availability rather than product quality in online retail [10, 16, 18]. In contrast, an expert’s ability to provide advice and offer solutions may very well depend on the channel in which the service is being rendered. For example, the online channel may not be as effective a medium of service delivery as the brick-and-mortar channel for complex consumer problems that require a rich information exchange that can only be facilitated through a faceto-face interaction with the expert.

The second important difference is related to the nonrefundable nature of expert services. In traditional retail, once the consumer purchases a product from one of the channels, she can typically return it for a full refund should the product fail to satisfy the customer with respect to its basic functionality. This is typically not the case with expert services. Because of reasons such as information asymmetry between the expert and the client as well as the prevailing business norms, services rendered in expert markets cannot be returned, even when the customer feels completely dissatisfied with the experience, regardless of whether the dissatisfaction is felt immediately or later.<sup>2</sup> The next course of action in such a case is typically to seek a second consultation or service from a different expert or from the same expert through a different channel. The likelihood of an unsatisfactory outcome is an important concern that consumers need to take into account when they make their decisions about which expert to see and through which channel. As discussed further in the next section, these differences make our analysis substantially different from those for traditional retail settings.

There is yet another difference between expert services and traditional retail goods. Due to the information asymmetry in expert markets, even when the success of performing the service is observable, customers often cannot determine the extent of the service that was needed and how much was actually performed [46]. A limited but growing literature in economics examines these issues. Some of these studies posit that consumers cannot observe service quality, which results in the possibility that experts will defraud their customers by misrepresenting a low-cost service as a costly one [4, 5, 39, 46]. In some other studies, consumers observe the actual service quality but cannot judge whether the costly service was really needed [19, 20, 21, 35, 43]. In addition, the diagnostic effort and the success of the expert may not be observable or verifiable [38]. Also, information asymmetries and consumer search for multiple opinions can give rise to an equilibrium in which some experts specialize in different levels of service [46]. Interestingly, specialization through second-opinion services and appropriate pricing strategies can lead to a reduction in competition through temporal differentiation [41]. In many expert markets, however, reputation, trust, and government regulations discipline firms to such an extent that they have little or no incentive to mislead consumers (see, e.g., [28]). Accordingly, we abstract away the issues associated with fraud in this research (for a similar approach, see Bouckaert and Degryse [7]) and instead focus on the effects of service quality, market structure, and channel accessibility on the optimal pricing strategies and channel choices for experts.

## A Model of Expert Services

The market consists of consumers each facing a problem and experts who can provide a solution to consumers’ problems. Consumers can visit an expert of their choice either face-to-face or online.

The Face-to-Face Channel. An expert who uses this channel solves consumer problems by interacting with them and providing personalized solutions. For example, a visit to a doctor’s office entails a doctor who diagnoses the patient’s problem and provides specific treatments. The marginal cost of serving a customer is $c ,$ where $0 < c < 1$ , and the value of a satisfactory solution for the consumer with the index i is given by $\nu _ { i } \mathbf { : }$

$$
v _ {i} = v - \theta_ {i},\tag{1}
$$

where v is the base valuation and $\theta _ { i }$ is the idiosyncratic degree of “lack of $\mathrm { f i t } ^ { \dag }$ between the provider’s face-to-face service and the consumer. This channel fit (or lack thereof) may depend on the characteristics of both the consumer and the expert. Since the expert provides a personalized service face-to-face, aspects such as personal rapport can affect the degree of satisfaction that a consumer receives from the service. The degree of fit could also depend on the consumer’s physical distance from the expert. In order to incorporate consumer heterogeneity in this channel fit, we assume that $\theta _ { i }$ is distributed uniformly across 0; 1 .

The consumers also incur a fixed cost (denoted by $k _ { f } )$ in order to obtain the services of the expert face-to-face. For example, a consumer who visits a lawyer’s office must organize and submit copies of the relevant documents, establish a professional relationship with the lawyer, and fill out a contract in accordance with the issue he is facing. If the expert charges the price $p ^ { f }$ , then the utility of consumer i who purchases a service from the expert is given by:

$$
U _ {i} ^ {f} = v - \theta_ {i} - k _ {f} - p ^ {f} = v _ {f} - \theta_ {i} - p ^ {f},\tag{2}
$$

where $\nu _ { f } = \nu - k _ { f }$ . We assume that $\nu _ { f }$ is sufficiently high $( \nu _ { f } > 1 )$ such that at least some consumers will seek service in equilibrium.

The Online Channel. Previous research has shown that privacy concerns may limit the amount of information exchange online [3]. Face-to-face interactions in private office locations can provide the intimate environment and sense of confidentiality that consumers need to freely disclose all relevant information. In addition, such an environment physically facilitates the exchange of information through the use of senses, equipment, and testing. These factors are all likely to decrease the expert’s diagnosis accuracy, but as technology matures over time, we conjecture that the loss in diagnosis accuracy will be less pronounced. Accordingly, we assume that consumers will receive a satisfactory service online with probability $\alpha ,$ where $0 < a < \bar { a } \le 1$ (in contrast, consumers always receive a satisfactory service faceto-face), where α represents the technological upper limit.

There are inherent cost efficiencies associated with offering services online [24], and we capture these efficiencies by normalizing the marginal cost of the online service to zero. Accordingly, the parameter c represents the cost differential associated with serving face-to-face versus online, where a large value for c implies correspondingly large cost savings to be achieved by adopting the online channel.

If the consumer receives an online service that successfully solves her problem, she receives the utility $\nu - k _ { o } - p ^ { o }$ , where v is the base utility from receiving a successful service, $k _ { o }$ is the fixed cost required for availing the service, and $p ^ { o }$ is the price charged. It is possible that $k _ { o } > k _ { f }$ because it might take more effort for the consumer to transmit the necessary information online than face-to-face. However, if the online service fails to solve the customer’s problem, she will then consider a subsequent face-to-face visit. The expected utility for consumer i is thus:

$$
U _ {i} ^ {o} = \alpha v - k _ {o} - p ^ {o} + (1 - \alpha) (v _ {f} - \theta_ {i} - p ^ {f}),\tag{3}
$$

$$
= (a v - k _ {o}) + (1 - a) v _ {f} - p ^ {o} - (1 - a) (\theta_ {i} + p ^ {f}).\tag{4}
$$

Define:

$$
v _ {o} = a v - k _ {o}.\tag{5}
$$

Then Equation (4) becomes:

$$
U _ {i} ^ {o} = v _ {o} + (1 - \alpha) v _ {f} - p ^ {o} - (1 - \alpha) (\theta_ {i} + p ^ {f}).\tag{6}
$$

Note that this framework captures several important aspects associated with the decision to offer online expert services. First, it incorporates the cost efficiencies inherent in offering online services [24]. Second, it incorporates the quality trade-off and the associated consumer uncertainty on whether the online service will be as effective as its face-to-face counterpart. Third, it captures the fact that the expert can reach more consumers by serving online [22]. And fourth, it allows for second visits by unsatisfied consumers, which is a common feature in expert markets. An important implication of this last feature is that the total number of services rendered is endogenous to the model despite the constant number of consumers in a covered market. As we will see, an important consequence is that consumer welfare always increases in our framework with the adoption of the online channel, while in a retail setting this is not always the case. We discuss this issue in detail later in this section and in Online Appendix A.

## Monopoly

We first consider the benchmark case of a monopolist expert with the following sequence of decisions.<sup>4</sup> First, the expert decides through which channel(s) to make its services available. The expert then sets its price for each service being offered.

Finally, the consumers decide which service(s) to use. There are three possible equilibrium outcomes in terms of the expert’s channel adoption: face-to-face only, online only, and both face-to-face and online. We analyze each of these cases separately.

Offering an online service may require an investment, which may affect the expert’s adoption decision. However, these costs have come down quite substantially in the recent past, and we expect them to come down even further. Also, since we are interested in understanding the strategic issues involved and we do not consider the fixed costs as being strategic, we normalize them to zero. As we will see, in some situations the experts will not want to offer online services even in the absence of any fixed costs.

Without loss of generality, we assume in this subsection that the market is not covered $\left( \nu _ { f } - 2 < c \right)$ , meaning that some consumers in the market will decide not to seek the expert’s service(s) in equilibrium. While this assumption allows us to capture the online channels’ reach, we acknowledge that in some cases (such as serious health conditions) customer valuation $( \nu _ { f } )$ may be significantly high such that $\nu _ { f } > > c .$ In these cases, consumers will seek a satisfactory solution until they get one, and hence the market will be covered. We provide an analysis of such a case in Online Appendix A and show that our main results still hold.

Consider the case when the firm has only a face-to-face offering. The indifferent customer location is given as $\nu _ { f } - p ^ { f }$ ; which is equivalent to total demand. The profit is thus $\Pi _ { m } ^ { f } = ( p ^ { f } - c ) ( \nu _ { f } - p ^ { f } )$ . Accordingly, the firm charges the price:

$$
p ^ {f *} = \frac {v _ {f} + c}{2}.\tag{7}
$$

Total demand equals:

$$
\frac {v _ {f} - c}{2}.\tag{8}
$$

The profit for the firm is:

$$
\Pi_ {m} ^ {f *} = \frac {(v _ {f} - c) ^ {2}}{4}.\tag{9}
$$

The consumer surplus in this case is given by:

$$
C S _ {m} ^ {f *} = \int_ {0} ^ {\frac {v _ {f} - c}{2}} \left[ (v _ {f} - \theta) - \frac {v _ {f} + c}{2} \right] d \theta = \frac {(v _ {f} - c) ^ {2}}{8}.\tag{10}
$$

Now consider the case when the firm decides to offer expert services online. The consumer has the following options: (1) he can directly seek a face-to-face service, (2) he can obtain an online service first and, if unsatisfied, then obtain a face-to-face service, or (3) he can obtain an online service and then terminate his search even if unsatisfied. All these possibilities exist in equilibrium as long as $\alpha / ( 1 - \alpha ) { < } \nu _ { o } < \alpha \nu _ { f }$ (see Online Appendix A).

First, consider the consumers located away from the expert who prefer their inital visit to be online. Upon an unsatisfactory online service experience, customers who are located farther away than:

$$
\theta_ {0} = v _ {f} - p ^ {f},\tag{11}
$$

will find it too costly to do a subsequent face-to-face visit, while those who are closer will go for it. Among the consumers closer to the expert, the consumer who is indifferent between a face-to-face and online (first) visit is located at $\theta _ { 1 }$ . Solving Equations (2) and (4) for $\theta _ { 1 }$ , we have:

$$
\theta_ {1} = \frac {p ^ {o} - \alpha p ^ {f} + \alpha v _ {f} - v _ {o}}{\alpha}.\tag{12}
$$

The profit function for the firm is thus:

$$
\Pi_ {m} ^ {o} = \theta_ {1} (p ^ {f} - c) + (1 - \theta_ {1}) p ^ {o} + (1 - \alpha) (\theta_ {0} - \theta_ {1}) (p ^ {f} - c),\tag{13}
$$

where the first, second, and third terms are for the profits from face-to-face (first) visits, online visits, and face-to-face (second) visits, respectively. The optimal equilibrium prices are:

$$
p ^ {f *} = \frac {v _ {f} + c}{2} + \frac {\alpha}{2 (1 - \alpha)},\tag{14}
$$

$$
p ^ {o *} = \frac {v _ {o}}{2} + \frac {\alpha}{2 (1 - \alpha)}.\tag{15}
$$

A comparison of Equations (7) and (14) reveals that the expert increases the price for the face-to-face service upon the introduction of the online service, while offering the online service as a cheaper alternative. It follows from the comparison of Equations (14) and (15) that the price difference for the two services increases with the cost advantage of the online service, and more consumers are served online as a result. We thus have:

$$
\theta_ {0} = \frac {v _ {f} - c}{2} - \frac {\alpha}{2 (1 - \alpha)},\tag{16}
$$

$$
\theta_ {1} = \frac {v _ {f} - c}{2} - \frac {v _ {o} - \alpha}{2 \alpha}.\tag{17}
$$

The total demand for the face-to-face service equals:

$$
\theta_ {1} + (1 - \alpha) (\theta_ {0} - \theta_ {1}) = \frac {v _ {f} - c - v _ {o}}{2},\tag{18}
$$

and the optimal profit for the firm is:

$$
\Pi_ {m} ^ {o *} = \frac {1}{4} \left(2 v _ {o} (1 - v _ {f} + c) + \frac {v _ {o} ^ {2}}{a} + \frac {a}{1 - a}\right) + \frac {(v _ {f} - c) ^ {2}}{4}.\tag{19}
$$

A comparison of Eequations (9) and (19) shows that it is profitable for the monopolist expert to offer online services. Furthermore, the profitability increases as the value for the online service $\left( \nu _ { o } \right)$ and the cost efficiency improve. Providing the online service is profitable for two reasons: first, it offers a cheaper alternative to the potential customers who do not find a face-to-face visit worthwhile, increasing the aggregate demand. Second, the online service reduces the average cost of operation for the expert. As long as the online service is moderately effective in solving the consumer’s problem, the monopolist expert always benefits from offering the online service.

The impact of adoption on consumer surplus is not as clear as that on the expert’s profit. On the positive side, fewer consumers are now served face-to-face (the higher-priced channel), and more of them get a satisfactory solution to their problems compared with the benchmark case, especially when c is high, per Equations (8) and $\left( 1 8 \right) . ^ { 5 }$ On the negative side, the price for the face-to-face service is higher now compared with the benchmark case. In addition, the consumers located at the far end of the market used to receive no utility, but in the presence of the online service, they may end up with a negative utility upon an unsuccessful online visit. These consumers will ex post regret their decision to seek the online service. The net effect of adoption on consumer surplus is positive when the cost differential c is sufficiently high. The intuition is that in such a case the online service offers a compelling value added that substantially boosts the overall number of satisfactory outcomes for the consumers when compared with the benchmark case. We thus have the following result:

Proposition 1: The monopolist expert always introduces an online service. The consumer surplus increases due to adoption when c is relatively high $\left( c > \nu _ { f } - 1 \right)$ 6

The proposition shows that online expert services can be a win–win situation for all parties when cost (c) is an important consideration. As mentioned earlier, the online service reduces the monopolist expert’s costs (cost-efficiency effect) and allows it to serve more customers (market reach effect). Furthermore, simultaneously offering two services enables the expert to price discriminate among consumers who vary in terms of channel preference (market segmentation effect).

So far, we have only considered the case where the market is uncovered when served face-to-face only and when the relative face-to-face cost is sufficiently high $( c > \nu _ { f } - 2 )$ . However, as discussed earlier, for some expert services customers will be motivated to find a solution irrespective of the costs involved. In Online Appendix A, we show in the context of a covered market, just as in the context of an uncovered market, that the introduction of the online service increases the profit of the monopolist expert as well as consumer surplus.

We would like to contrast how the results presented here differ from those for the retail context. Consider a situation where a monopolist firm needs to decide whether to introduce an online retail channel that exhibits lower valuations for the consumers (αv due to factors such as the lack of instant gratification and the inability to inspect the physical characteristics of a product (and thereby misjudging product fit), but at a cheaper cost and price point. This formulation is similar to the one presented here, except that in the expert services context the outcome of the first visit can lead some consumers to demand a second visit, since an unsuccessful online visit may precede a successful face-to-face visit (which provides the expert with additional information to solve the customer’s problem) with the same expert. Whereas in the context of standardized retail products whose effectiveness does not vary by channel, customers who are dissatisfied with an online transaction would typically not be interested in purchasing the same item from the same vendor offline. We find that the results are substantially different for the two contexts in the case of a covered market.<sup>7</sup> The details of this analysis are available in Online Appendix A.

In the retail context, a monopolist will adopt the online channel only if customer valuations are close to those for the offline channel (i.e.,αis close to one). This is unlike the expert services context where the monopolist will introduce the online service even for relatively small α. In the retail context, consumers choose strictly between the high value offline channel (subject to a transportation cost) versus the lower value online channel. In the expert services context, however, a consumer who chooses the online service may also visit the expert face-to-face with probability 1 α, mitigating the effect of lower valuations for the online service on the expert’s profit, and thereby rendering the adoption of the online channel profitable even for low α. Furthermore, we find that the adoption of the online channel in a retail context by a monopolist reduces consumer surplus. In contrast, the case is reversed in the expert services context, as the possibility of second visits limits the ability of the monopolist to extract all the surplus. Later in this section, we make similar comparisons for a duopoly setting and again find that the results for the two contexts are different.

Interestingly, online retail started with the sale of homogeneous products (such as books, CDs, and flight tickets) for which α is close or equal to its upper theoretical limit, and then expanded to other product categories with potentially lower α (such as clothing). One possible explanation for online expert services’ not being as prevalent as retail goods is their lower but certainly rising quality levels with respect to their offline counterparts (captured by the parameter α in our model) in these markets.

## Duopoly

We next consider duopolistic competition between two symmetric firms with the same level of expertise and cost structures. Expert 1 is located at point 0, while the other expert is located at point 1 on the standard Hotelling line. As in the monopoly case, consumers are uniformly distributed over the market of unit length. The parameter θ in this setting thus represents consumer preferences for expert 1’s face-to-face service relative to that of expert 2. Note that consumer valuations for the service need to be sufficiently high for all consumers to seek a service from one of the experts (i.e., there exists a segment of consumers who value both services positively), leading to competition for consumers by the experts. Otherwise, consumers away from both experts on the Hotelling line would not demand a service from either expert, and the experts effectively become monopolists (i.e., no competition).

First, consider the benchmark case with no online services. The consumer located at θ receives the utility:

$$
U _ {f} = v _ {f} - p _ {1} ^ {f} - \theta ,\tag{20}
$$

for receiving the service from expert 1, where $p _ { 1 } ^ { f }$ is the price charged by this expert. The consumer who is indifferent in regard to buying the service from either expert is indexed by $\theta _ { 2 } ,$ , where:

$$
\theta_ {2} = \frac {1 - p _ {1} ^ {f} + p _ {2} ^ {f}}{2}.\tag{21}
$$

The consumers located between 0 and $\theta _ { 2 }$ visit expert 1, and the rest visit firm 2. The profit function for expert 1 is given by:

$$
\Pi_ {1} ^ {f} = \theta_ {2} (p _ {1} ^ {f} - c).\tag{22}
$$

The experts simultaneously set their prices. The Nash equilibrium in prices is given by:

$$
p _ {1} ^ {f *} = p _ {2} ^ {f *} = 1 + c.\tag{23}
$$

The corresponding profit for each expert equals $1 / 2$

Now, consider the case when the experts have the option to introduce an online service.<sup>8</sup> In what follows, we explore whether either of the experts has the incentive to offer an online service. Suppose that one of the experts (say, expert 1) decides to do so. The purchase pattern of consumers in this case is illustrated in Figure 1.

We know that a consumer who receives the online service will not get a satisfactory solution with probability $( 1 - \alpha )$ . This consumer will then seek a face-to-face service. The consumer who is indifferent in regard to visiting experts 1 and 2 for the second service is indexed by $\theta _ { 2 }$ as defined in Equation (21). A consumer also needs to decide whether to first demand the online or face-to-face service. If it is the latter, the consumer indexed by θ obtains the utility:

$$
U _ {f} ^ {o} = v _ {f} - p _ {1 f} ^ {o} - \theta .\tag{24}
$$

However, if the consumer receives the online service first and face-to-face service next, then the expected utility is given by:

$$

$$

where the first and second utilities are for the cases where the consumer obtains the second service from expert 1 $\left( \theta < \theta _ { 2 } \right)$ and expert $2 \ ( \theta > \theta _ { 2 } )$ , respectively. Let $\theta _ { 3 }$ denote the consumer who is indifferent regarding obtaining an online or a face-toface (first) service from expert 1, where $\theta _ { 2 } > \theta _ { 3 }$ , meaning that such a consumer would prefer expert 1’s face-to-face service to that of expert 2 upon an unsuccessful online visit. Accordingly,

$$
\theta_ {3} = \frac {\alpha v _ {f} - v _ {o} + p _ {1 o} ^ {o} - \alpha p _ {1 f} ^ {o}}{\alpha}.\tag{26}
$$

Similarly, let $\theta _ { 4 }$ denote the consumer who is indifferent regarding obtaining a faceto-face (first) service from expert 2 or obtaining an online service from expert 1 followed by (if necessary) a face-to-face service from expert 2. We have:

$$
\theta_ {4} = \frac {\alpha (p _ {2} ^ {o} + 1) - p _ {1 o} ^ {o} - (\alpha v _ {f} - v _ {o})}{\alpha}.\tag{27}
$$

For these purchase patterns to be indeed the optimal behavior for the consumers in equilibrium, we require: $0 \leq \theta _ { 3 } \leq \theta _ { 2 } \leq \theta _ { 4 } \leq 1$

The profit function for expert 1 is given by:

$$
\Pi_ {1} ^ {o} = (p _ {1 f} ^ {o} - c) [ \theta_ {3} + (1 - \alpha) (\theta_ {2} - \theta_ {3}) ] + (\theta_ {4} - \theta_ {3}) p _ {1 o} ^ {o}.\tag{28}
$$

The first term in Equation (28) represents the profit from the face-to-face channel that results from both direct and second visits. The second term represents the profit from the online channel. Similarly, the profit function for expert 2 is given by:

$$
\Pi_ {2} ^ {o} = (p _ {2} ^ {o} - c) [ 1 - \theta_ {4} + (1 - \alpha) (\theta_ {4} - \theta_ {2}) ].\tag{29}
$$

We consider the situations where face-to-face and online services can coexist in a pure strategy equilibrium. A sufficient condition to ensure this is that the cost advantage of the online channel is not too large, and in particular $c < 4 \alpha / ( 3 + \alpha ) . ^ { 9 }$ Also, in order to manage the mathematical tractability of the analysis as well as for the purpose of ease of exposition, we henceforth set $k _ { o } = \alpha k _ { f }$ such that $\nu _ { o } = \alpha \nu _ { f }$ . Solving for the Nash equilibrium, we find the following result.

Proposition 2: Neither expert has the incentive to offer the online service $i f .$

$$
\alpha <   \alpha_ {1} ^ {*} = \frac {3 (2 - 8 c - 3 c ^ {2})}{(2 + 8 c - c ^ {2})}.\tag{30}
$$

This result shows that even though the introduction of the online service is costless, neither expert will offer it unless the service is of sufficiently high quality. Also, we find that $a _ { 1 } ^ { * }$ is decreasing in $c ,$ so the proposition can be equivalently stated as saying that neither firm will offer the online service unless there are sufficient cost efficiency gains.

The incentives of the experts in the competitive setting are notably different from those in the monopoly setting (i.e., they are less enthusiastic in adopting the online channel). The intuition can be understood by recognizing that an online service offered by expert 1 changes the pricing behavior of expert 2. Prior to the introduction of the online service, expert $2 \mathrm { { : } s }$ price for the face-to-face service was $p _ { 2 } ^ { f * } = 1 + c$ . However, after expert 1 introduces the online service, a segment of expert ${ 2 \mathrm { { \dot { s } } } }$ customers try firm 1’s online service and end up with a satisfactory outcome. In response, expert 2 lowers its equilibrium price to:

$$
p _ {2 o} ^ {f *} = (1 + c) - \frac {2 \alpha (2 + c)}{3 (1 + \alpha)}.\tag{31}
$$

In other words, expert 2 retaliates with a price reduction. Since prices are strategic complements, expert 1 also reduces its face-to-face price (albeit in a smaller degree) to:

$$
p _ {1 o} ^ {f *} = (1 + c) - \frac {\alpha (2 + c)}{3 (1 + \alpha)}.\tag{32}
$$

It is easy to see that the introduction of the online service by one of the experts intensifies price competition. The nonadopting expert is compelled to reduce the price more than the adopter and is worse off as a result. The adopting expert may or may not be better off, since the loss of profit because of the intensified competition may dominate the additional revenue from the online service. For low values of c and $^ { d , }$ the competitive effect indeed dominates the market reach effect, and the adopting expert does not find it beneficial to introduce the online service.

Next we characterize the case discussed above as an equilibrium outcome. This follows from the observation that it is not profitable for the experts to simultaneously offer undifferentiated online services as part of an equilibrium strategy.<sup>10</sup> We have the following result:

Proposition 3: For $a > a _ { 1 } ^ { * } ,$ only one expert (say expert 1) introduces the online service. In equilibrium:

a. Expert 1 charges a low online price and a face-to-face price that is higher than that of expert 2 but lower than the price level in the benchmark case.

b. Expert 2 becomes the market leader in the face-to-face segment. Expert 2’s lead diminishes with the online channel’s cost advantage c .

c. Expert 1 raises its profit (at the expense of expert 2) by adopting the online channel.

There are several interesting properties of this equilibrium. First, consistent with casual observation, the online service is cheaper than the face-to-face service. Expert 1 benefits from customers who receive its online service before any face-to-face visit, because such customer behavior increases total demand and aggregate revenue and reduces the average cost of operation for the expert. Expert 2, however, aggressively competes in price with expert 1’s online service and serves first visits as well as more than half of the second visits. Following this focused strategy allows expert 2 to emerge as the dominant player in the face-to-face market. While the intensified price competition impacts expert 2’s profit negatively, it still lacks the incentive to offer its own online service. This proposition clearly illustrates why firms should be reluctant to mimic the introduction of online services by their competitors. Even though such a move by a rival takes away market share and hurts firm profits, the strategy of matching the competitor would lead to an even worse outcome for future adopters.

We now turn our attention to the impact of the online service on total profits, consumer surplus, and social welfare. Recall that, in the monopoly setting, the impact was a net positive for the expert as well as the consumers (win–win scenario), causing social welfare to rise. The next proposition formalizes the impact of the online service adoption for the duopoly setting.

Proposition 4: In the asymmetric equilibrium, total industry profits are lower and consumer and social welfare are higher compared to the benchmark case of no online service adoption.

Proposition 4 establishes that, while expert 1 benefits from the online channel adoption, the cumulative net effect on total profits is negative mainly because of the intense price competition. On the flip side, the online channel benefits all consumers in the form of reduced prices and some consumers in the form of reduced transaction costs. Social welfare also increases since the online channel is more efficient than its face-to-face counterpart for both the experts and the consumers, even though some online expert–consumer interactions end up being wasteful ex post.

The results so far show that the introduction of the online channel is always profitable in the monopoly setting but may not be so in the competitive setting. In the latter setting, the quality of online service has to exceed a threshold for it to be introduced by one of the experts. The results also show that, in equilibrium, one of the experts will choose to specialize in face-to-face delivery and will end up dominating that segment of the market.

We previously discussed that the results for the retail context were substantially different from those for the expert services context in a covered market. Interestingly, in a duopoly (retail) setting, we find that the online channel is not introduced at all as the firms try to avoid intense price competition. In contrast, there are conditions under which an expert introduces the online service while continuing to offer a face-to-face service. The reason is again tied to the fact that the online channel increases the total market size in the expert services context. Some consumers end up paying for multiple visits, potentially rendering the online channel profitable for the adopter due to this market expansion effect.

## Model Extensions

In this section, we consider two variants of the base model. First, we investigate the optimality of bundling face-to-face and online services and offering them at a combined lump-sum price. Since reputable online services are typically introduced by incumbent experts that already have existing face-to-face channels, this can be viewed as the strategy of offering the online service as a “free” add-on.<sup>11</sup> Second, we consider the situation where a segment of the market will simply not use the online channel, possibly due to technological constraints (such as a lack of Internet access with sufficient bandwidth) or because of the personal conviction that the expert would not be able to solve their problems online.

## Online Service as a “Free” Add-On

There can be situations where experts may find it difficult to charge separate prices for their online and face-to-face services. In particular, fairness considerations can play an important role, in that a consumer may resent having to pay for a face-toface service after having paid for an unsuccessful online visit. One way to counter these concerns strategically is to bundle the two kinds of services such that any consumer who receives an unsatisfactory online service can visit the same expert face-to-face for free. As we discussed earlier, this strategy is commonly implemented by firms such as Dell.

Note that the bundling strategy we discuss here is different from what has been studied in the bundling literature for retail settings (see, e.g., [1] and [15]). In a typical bundling problem, firms decide whether to offer a single price for a bundle of potentially complementary products. In our context, however, what is included in the bundle is a guaranteed first service and an option to receive a second service from the expert through a different channel in case needed.

In this subsection, we investigate whether firms should offer online expert services as add-ons and how this would affect channel adoption choices in equilibrium. We explore these issues in a monopoly setting first and a competitive setting next.

## Monopoly

The monopolist expert charges $p _ { b }$ for its online/face-to-face service bundle. If a consumer is not satisfied with an online visit, she can then visit the expert face-toface without having to pay any additional fees. Accordingly, the consumer’s utility from directly visiting the expert face-to-face is:

$$
U ^ {f} = v _ {f} - p _ {b} - \theta ,\tag{33}
$$

whereas the expected utility of visiting the expert online and then (if necessary) faceto-face is:

$$
U ^ {b} = v _ {o} - p _ {b} + (1 - \alpha) (v _ {f} - \theta).\tag{34}
$$

We can now characterize the profit implications of online channel adoption and bundling for the expert. We find the following result.

Proposition 5: The monopolist expert will offer an online service as a free supplement to its existing face-to-face service when $c < 4 a - 1$ , which always holds for relatively high online service quality $( \alpha > 1 / 2 )$ . Consumer surplus decreases with the introduction of the online service when $\nu _ { f } > c + 2 \sqrt { 1 - \alpha } .$

Interestingly, Proposition 5 shows that offering the online service for free can actually be detrimental to consumers. This result is quite intriguing given that consumers would always be better off if the expert were to charge separate prices for the online and face-to-face services (recall Proposition 1).

The intuition can be explained as follows. When the two services are priced separately, the total price that the customers of the online service pay depends on the outcome of this visit, which is not known a priori. The resolution of this uncertainty leads to the ex post (temporal) segmentation of consumers: those who need the additional face-to-face visit and those who do not. Obviously, the latter segment avoids paying for the face-to-face service. Now, when the two services are bundled, all customers of the monopolist expert essentially pay for both services up front. Considering that the consumers are homogeneous (aside from their location preferences) in terms of their valuations for the expert’s services prior to their first visit, this homogeneity may allow the expert to extract more of the consumer surplus by selling both services at the same single price.<sup>12</sup>

## Duopoly

Consider the competition of two experts located at the two ends of a standard Hotelling line as discussed previously. This time, however, the experts have the additional option of selling their online and face-to-face services as a bundle at a single price. The sequence of decisions is as follows. First, the experts decide whether to offer their services online. The experts then decide on a pricing strategy of whether to charge separate prices or a single price for the bundle. Finally, the experts set their prices in line with their chosen pricing strategies. This particular sequence of decisions reflects the fact that channel adoption decisions are harder to change and more difficult to deviate from than pricing decisions. Solving the game as usual for the subgame-perfect equilibria, we find the following results.

Proposition 6: The optimal channel adoption and pricing strategies are as follows:

a. If $a < a _ { 1 } ^ { * }$ , then neither expert offers the online service.<sup>13</sup>

b. $H \alpha _ { 1 } ^ { * } \leq a \leq \alpha _ { 3 } ^ { * }$ , then both experts offer online services as free add-ons, where:

$$
\alpha_ {3} ^ {*} = \max \left(\alpha_ {1} ^ {*}, \frac {3 (2 c + 1)}{(2 c ^ {2} + 2 c + 5)}\right).\tag{35}
$$

c. If $a > a _ { 3 } ^ { * }$ , then only one expert offers the online service and prices it separately from the face-to-face service.

Note that we have the same equilibrium outcome as in Proposition 2 (no online channel adoption) when the online service quality is relatively low $( \boldsymbol { \alpha } < \boldsymbol { \alpha } _ { 1 } ^ { * } )$ and the same outcome as in Proposition 3 (online channel adoption by one expert) when the online service quality is relatively high $\left( \boldsymbol { a } > \boldsymbol { a } _ { 3 } ^ { * } \right)$ . For intermediate values $( \alpha _ { 1 } ^ { * } \leq \alpha \leq \alpha _ { 3 } ^ { * } )$ , however, both experts now offer the online service as an add-on, as opposed to adoption by only one of the experts as in Proposition 3. The online services of the experts are not differentiated at all from the perspective of the consumers when they are priced separately from the face-to-face service, and the potential for an extreme form of price competition prevents the experts from simultaneous adoption. However, when they are provided as an add-on, consumers evaluate the value of the bundle, which is still differentiated for the consumers (albeit on a diminished scale compared with just the face-to-face service), and this gives the experts an incentive to offer the online service simultaneously. Nevertheless, such an outcome inevitably intensifies price competition and reduces expert profits. The experts would actually be better off if they could agree to not offer any online services, since unlike the case in Proposition 3, as soon as one adopts the online channel, the other expert is also tempted to adopt. The result is a classic prisoner’s dilemma where both experts end up being worse off.

Interestingly, for relatively high values of online service quality, the level of differentiation for the service bundles becomes so diminished and the corresponding price competition so intense that the experts prefer that outcome to the inferior position outlined in Proposition 3 (i.e., focusing on becoming the market leader in the provision of the face-to-face service). Furthermore, when one of the experts withdraws from the online market, the other expert no longer offers the online service as an add-on and prices it separately from the face-to-face service. This separation allows the expert to increase the level of differentiation for its face-to-face service and to expand the reach of its online service.

To sum up, while offering the online service as an add-on improves market reach and improves profits in a monopoly setting, it intensifies price competition and reduces profits in a competitive setting. Thus, contrary to common sense, the adoption of expert services may as well decline in competitive markets as the quality of these services improves beyond a certain threshold. This result highlights the importance of considering competitive factors in offering expert services online.

The next proposition characterizes the welfare implications of equilibrium outcomes presented in Proposition 6.

Proposition 7: Consumer surplus and social welfare always rise when the experts have the option to offer the online service as a free add-on to the face-to-face service. However, total expert profits decrease with improvements in the online service quality when both experts offer online services (i.e., when $a _ { 1 } ^ { * } \leq a \leq a _ { 3 } ^ { * } )$

The simultaneous adoption of the online channel makes both experts worse off and consumers better off.<sup>14</sup> In other words, any efficiency gains from adoption is passed on to the consumers as additional surplus, and, again, this is the exact opposite of what we have found for the monopoly setting. In both monopoly and competitive settings, however, the adoption of the online channel increases social welfare because of the reduction in costs or increase in operational efficiency. Interestingly, total expert profits rise as the online service quality exceeds a certain threshold, simply because only then the experts have the proper incentives to reduce price competition by making different channel choices.

## Variations in Channel Preferences

So far, we assumed that all consumers would rationally be willing to use the online service given the proper incentives. While Internet access has reached a saturation point in developed economies and is rapidly rising in developing economies, there still exists a significant segment of consumers who do not feel comfortable getting advice over the Internet. This kind of behavior can be manifested because of privacy concerns, as some ultra-privacy-concerned consumers may simply refuse to engage online (see the discussion of privacy fundamentalists in Lee et al. [30]).<sup>15</sup> It could also be due to the incorrect belief that the nature of the problem they are experiencing requires a face-to-face encounter (even though it can actually be handled online).

We can modify the model to account for the presence of such a segment of consumers. Specifically, let β denote the fraction of consumers who have the technological means and are willing to try the online service. The remaining fraction $( 1 - \beta )$ will only consider receiving face-to-face service. Note that this model reduces to the base model for $\beta = 1$ . We find that our main results continue to hold for $\beta < 1$ (see Online Appendix B for details) as summarized in the next proposition.

Proposition 8: As β increases, the experts are more likely to offer online services. In particular, the online service is offered in equilibrium $i f .$

$$
\alpha > \alpha_ {1 \beta} ^ {*} = \frac {3 (2 - 8 c - 3 c ^ {2})}{\beta (2 + 8 c - c ^ {2})}.\tag{36}
$$

When an expert offers the online service, prices and total profits decline with $\beta .$ In contrast, consumer surplus and social welfare increase with $\beta .$

Interest in online services increases with $\beta ,$ so it is only logical for the experts to consider offering the service when there is enough demand. The value added for this proposition is that it characterizes the relationship between the level of interest in online services and the minimum level of service quality for the experts to even consider the adoption of the online channel. As expected, the higher the interest of the public, the less restriction the experts place on service quality to deem the online channel profitable. Consequently, improvements in Internet access and general acceptance of the online channel as a valid form of expert service delivery should lead to the introduction of these services at progressively lower levels of quality (as opposed to not being offered at all), although one would expect the quality levels to increase over time due to technological advances.

An increase in $\beta ,$ however, diminishes the differentiation between the services of competing experts and intensifies the competition in prices. This can be seen in the optimal prices as a function of $\beta$ (when expert 1 offers the online service):

$$
p _ {2 o} ^ {f *} = (1 + c) - \frac {2 \beta \alpha (2 + c)}{3 (1 + \beta \alpha)},\tag{37}
$$

$$
p _ {1 o} ^ {f *} = (1 + c) - \frac {\beta \alpha (2 + c)}{3 (1 + \beta \alpha)}.\tag{38}
$$

As $\beta$ increases, prices and total expert profits decline (since the the adoption of the online channel becomes more detrimental to the nonadopting expert), while consumer surplus as well as social welfare increases. This pits the welfare of firms against the welfare of the society. Clearly, experts will not be very enthused about the improvements in Internet access and the public’s perception of the efficacy of online services going forward. They will much rather prefer a world in which this new form of service delivery did not exist or was less accepted by the public or of lower quality, although they will still engage in it out of strategic concerns. On the flip side, such changes will be a net benefit to the society, and therefore we see a potential benefit for policymaking in this context.

The main insights of Proposition $^ 6$ continue to hold for $\beta < 1$ , but the region defined by $( a _ { 1 } ^ { * } , a _ { 3 } ^ { * } )$ , where both experts offer the online service disappears for small values of $\beta .$ This implies that a large swath of consumers should become potential customers for online services to be offered as an add-on to the face-face-service.

## Conclusion

In this study, we analytically investigate the impact of online services on expert profits, consumer surplus, and social welfare, and provide a number of insights on the potential implications of offering these services. We should emphasize that our contributions to the literature are mainly theoretical and the insights we list below apply directly only to situations that fit the restrictive assumptions made in the model. Nevertheless, we believe that they are still valuable in guiding future empirical research as well as allowing managers to think in a formal and structured way about our research questions that they may find relevant.

1. Should experts offer online services? Experts that enjoy a monopoly position in their markets should indeed seriously consider adoption. However, the answer for a competitive duopoly setting is not as clear-cut because in such a setting, offering the online service is likely to induce price competition. The quality of the service should be sufficiently high for adoption, but beyond a certain level the negative effect of intensive price competition induced by adoption may dominate the positive effect of operational efficiency, forcing one of the experts to focus on the face-to-face service only. Thus, one may expect the rate of adoption of an online service by experts to first increase and then decrease as the quality of the service improves. In other words, some experts may require additional incentives to offer online services as online visits become less and less distinguishable from face-to-face visits through advances in information technology.

2. Should experts offer online services as a free add-on to face-to-face services, or should they charge separate prices? Among other things, the answer depends on the market structure and the level of service quality. According to our findings, a monopolist should offer the online service as a free add-on assuming the service quality is sufficiently high. In a duopoly setting, when the online service quality is moderately high, both experts offer the service as a free add-on and both are worse off compared to the benchmark case of no adoption (a prisoner’s dilemma). When the online service quality is sufficiently high, only one expert introduces the service and charges separate prices for the online and face-to-face services.

3. How does the provision of the online service affect consumer surplus and social welfare? Regarding consumer surplus, the market structure again plays a significant role. In particular, consumer surplus can decline when a monopolist offers the online service as a free add-on to its face-to-face service. In a competitive setting, consumers generally benefit from the price competition induced by the online channel adoption, even though many of them end up having to pay for multiple services. Furthermore, one consistent finding is that social welfare always increases with the provision of the online service in a competitive setting. This is because the online service improves experts operational efficiency and reduces consumers’ transaction costs. While there is some welfare loss due to multiple transaction costs incurred by the consumers with the online service, this is typically more than made up by the other gains.

4. How does an increase in the number of potential online customers affect the adoption of online services? As expected, experts are more likely to offer their services online as the number of potential online customers increases. We also find that as their number increases, experts are more likely to offer their online services as a free “add-on” rather than charging separate prices for online and face-to-face services.

Our model captures the key aspects of online expert services, including the cost efficiencies inherent in offering online services, the quality trade-off and the associated consumer uncertainty regarding the effectiveness of online services, the provider’s ability to reach more customers through the online channel, and the possibility of second visits. In addition to these key aspects, some of our results lend further credibility to our model. Specifically, as is observed in actual practice, not all providers offer the online service in equilibrium (see Propositions 2 and 3), and when they do, they price it lower than its face-to-face counterpart (see Proposition 3). In addition, Propositions 3 and 6 show the opportunities that are available to incumbent expert firms. This winner-take-all nature of online markets is well-known and well-appreciated by Silicon Valley investors, and there appears to be plenty of investment appetite to bring expert services online across many diverse fields such as security, medicine, insurance, and beaut y.<sup>16</sup>

As is the case in any stylized game-theoretic analysis, we made several restrictive assumptions in developing the model. For example, we have considered symmetric experts in our analysis, when in fact they are often differentiated by reputation and trust in their respective markets. Future analytical research can examine how asymmetries in skills and reputation can affect the decision to adopt the online channel.

## Implications for Future Research

This study offers several avenues for future research in expert services, especially from an empirical perspective. For example, the model we present here assumes that consumers receive a certain value from consultations with the experts. That value can be measured by asking consumers their highest willingness to pay for the ideal services. Past research that measures consumer’s willingness to pay can provide guidance here (see, e.g., [29] and [45]). As such, the base valuations, the impact of any cost associated with online and offline consultations $( k _ { o } / k _ { f } )$ as well as the fit (θ ) can all be measured, potentially yielding the empirical distribution of the willingness to pay for the target population $( \nu - k _ { f } - \theta _ { i } )$

In the monopoly setting, Proposition 1 states that the monopolist expert introduces the online service. This proposition can be verified by studying the rate of adoption of online services among experts that command some level of monopoly power (such as those in rural areas and/or with a high level of specialization). Verification would require that in cases and markets where customers consider online services as viable, monopolist experts have a meaningful web presence with an option for online service delivery.

Propositions 2 and 3 state that in competitive markets and where online service quality is high, only some of the experts would provide online services. To the extent these markets can be identified, this result can be verified empirically by comparing the level of adoption in these markets to that in monopolistic settings and confirming a higher level for the latter.

Our results on the pricing of expert services may also be verified empirically. Again, subject to proper identification of markets where online services are viable, empirical research should confirm a higher likelihood for the provision of online expert services as an add-on to face-to-face services, as opposed to charging separate prices for these services.

What we have discussed so far can be analyzed using cross-sectional data. The role of improvements in information technology on the quality of online services and corresponding optimal market outcomes can be investigated using longitudinal data.
