# Managing culture creep: Toward a strategic model of user IT culture

Isabelle Walsh <sup>a,</sup>⇑, Hajer Kefi <sup>b</sup>, Richard Baskerville <sup>c</sup>

Keywords: IT culture IT users taxonomy IT strategy IT requirements IT needs IT motivation

## a b s t r a c t

This article describes a framework of IT user culture that has implications for organizational IT strategy. The research was conducted in multiple settings with a grounded theory approach. The resulting framework is anchored to nine archetypal IT user profiles and encompasses their inter-group dynamics. By adopting a cultural perspective on IT usage, the framework can inform IT adoption and usage strategy with possible cultural antecedents and determinants of usage constructs common in IS research. The proposed framework suggests how management can influence the migration of IT user culture (culture creep). This framework can also enrich other acceptance models in order to more fully consider the human factor during IT implementation and adoption. The results underscore the importance of culture-customizing organizational IT socialization, training and evolution programs.

## 1. Introduction

Many Information System (IS) implementation failures are attributed to human/organizational issues rather than technical problems (Au et al., 2008). The concept of Information Technology (IT) usage is a multi-level construct consisting of individual level, group level, and organizational level (Burton-Jones and Gallivan, 2007). It involves the user, the system, and the task (Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006). Because it involves the user, it necessarily also involves the user’s culture. Culture is an important factor in the acceptance and effective use of information technologies (Gallivan and Srite, 2005; Gallupe and Tan 1999; Kappos and Rivard, 2008; Leidner and Kayworth, 2006; Straub et al., 2002).

Our concern for culture arises most obviously in the globalization, internationalization and offshore outsourcing that have become everyday elements of many organizations. IS has played an essential role as catalyst in this phenomenon, enabling and accelerating the cross-border processes. As a consequence, many IT adoption and diffusion settings are also cross-cultural. IS cultural studies often center on national or organizational culture (Gallivan and Srite, 2005: Leidner and Kayworth 2006) and disregard other cultural dimensions important in IT usage, such as religious culture, or ethnic culture.

The pervasiveness of IT in users’ everyday life blends IT with social practice and brings forward the cultural dimension in IT usage. The concept of a specific IT culture construct has been emerging as an important aspect in IS research (Kaarst-Brown and Robey, 1999: Leidner and Kayworth, 2006: Massit-Folléa, 2002: etc.). While such studies are deeply descriptive and insightful, there is little investigation of the more pragmatic notion of how IT culture might respond to management activity (Ford et al., 2003). The existing research helps us to identify and assess IT cultural profiles, but even if we conceptualize a cultural goal for IT projects, we know little about any instrumental strategy that might change an IT culture.

Our research into cultural strategies evolved from a consequentialist perspective: How do IT cultures emerge from IT usage? If we could understand how cultures emerge, it could lead to models with more strategic purposes. In order to study such ‘cultures-in-motion’, we studied and tracked IT user cultural profiles over a period of 24 months. We investigated possible differences between users expressed by their IT usage by employing known user culture profiles together with new profiles found in the data. Because we followed a grounded theory methodology, the research was conducted iteratively: alternating and combining data collection, data analysis, and comparison between existing theory and emerging concepts. For the purpose of this paper, however, the work is presented sequentially in order to ease reading and promote comprehension

## 2. Culture and IT culture

Defining culture is a challenge in itself. There is a wide range of viewpoints on this concept and even a lack of consensus as to its definition: Kroeber and Kluckohn had already identified 164 definitions of the concept of culture in 1952. Passeron reminds us that ‘‘culture is the most protean of sociological concepts. More than other polymorphous concepts [. . .] culture is the term which leads us to the most vertiginous maze of a Babelian library”.<sup>1</sup> Culture is not a distinct, harmonious and stable entity with well defined boundaries. Cultures are plural and always interpreted and reinterpreted. Accordingly, it has been argued that the term ‘‘cultural aspects” should be preferred to ‘‘culture” because of the fragmented, composite, emergent and pluralistic nature of this concept (Martin, 1992; Fleury, 2006). Hence the concept of culture is often studied in the literature within some of its many dimensions and this leads to fragmentary perspectives. In the organizational and IS fields, existing intercultural literature mainly investigates the national and organizational dimensions of culture and does not take into consideration the other various cultural layers which make up the construct of culture (Gallivan and Srite, 2005).

Through Social Identity Theory and the virtual onion model, Straub et al. (2002) open the way to other conceptualizations of culture at the individual level. Using a virtual onion metaphor, they question the categorization which assumes that individuals belong to a single national culture. They show that culture is made up of a set of sub-cultures which constitute the layers of the virtual onion; these layers result from the individual’s various group affiliations. Each person is thus the unique product of various interacting identity layers. These layers are not static but evolving and intermingling. They are of varying importance depending on the individual’s situations. Works of this school of thought (Gallivan and Srite, 2005; Karahanna et al., 2005, 2006; Srite and Karahanna, 2006) contribute to a cumulative tradition.

Enrolling in this school of thought, we conceptualize culture at the individual level as resulting, in a systemic way, from the user’s affiliation to different social groups: national, religious, organizational, professional, ethnic, etc. Culture is thus understood as a recursive phenomenon: individual culture and group sub-cultures shape and inform each other (Walsh and Kefi, 2008). This conceptualization allows us to take into consideration the three complementary perspectives on culture underlined by Kappos and Rivard (2008): integration, fragmentation, and differentiation. These perspectives provide a framework for delineating cultural features in human settings. Integration means each sub-cultural layer results from group memberships; hence it can be understood as interpretations ‘‘shared by members of a collective” (p. 602). Fragmentation means our conceptualization of culture takes into consideration the various sub-cultures within the individual global culture and their possible conflicting values. Differentiation means that an individual culture results from a unique combination of group cultures. It takes into consideration the individual’s idiosyncrasies, which are the witnesses of human beings’ cultural diversity, and allows multiple interpretation of a given cultural manifestation. The main implication of the chosen approach is methodological. In order to assess a sub-culture at group level, we first assess it at the individual level, and then identify possible central average tendencies. Results concerning these average central tendencies in one given cultural sub-layer can only be globally unbiased if all other cultural layers and contexts are diversified as much as possible. Otherwise one would be studying a specific sub-group of a given cultural group.

We propose to include an IT culture layer in the virtual onion model of Straub et al. (2002). This IT sub-cultural layer results from the individual’s membership to a given users group. The concept of an IT culture appears in IS literature. In 2006 Leidner and Kayworth had identified only two previous works that used this concept at the organizational level: Kaarst-Brown and Robey (1999) and Kaarst-Brown (2004). These works investigate how cultural aspects of IT interfere with IS management and governance. The term IT culture has also been used in some works to denote the culture of the people involved with IT in corporate settings (e,g.. Nord et al., 2007: Guzman et al., 2007). This conceptualization is different from the notion of the culture of a users group. In contrast, Lamb and Kling (2003) effectively reject this cultural boundary between IT developers and IT users as artificial. Other researchers have studied concepts close to the concept of IT culture while naming them differently. Technoculture was explored in 1999 by Robins and Webster through the social and cultural meaning of IT. Proulx (2001) conceptualized numerical culture as the set of skills linked to the mastering of IT knowledge. Serres (2007) investi gated informational culture that includes practical mastery of IT and intellectual mastery of information.

In organizational studies, Schein (1985, 1991) defines culture as ‘‘the sum total of all shared, taken for granted, assumptions that a group has learned throughout its history” (Schein, 1985, p. 29). He proposes a three-level model of culture which includes basic assumptions, values, and audible or visible behaviors expressed through artifacts. Behaviors are the expression of underlying values, and basic assumptions are sedimented values which have become tacitly accepted (Schein, 1991). Thus, all three levels brought forward by Schein relate to human values. We extend Schein’s (1991) work beyond the realm of organizational culture and define the concept of IT culture at group level as the set of IT related visible or audible behaviors, IT related values, and IT related underlying assumptions shared by a group. IT related behaviors and IT related assumptions lead to IT related values (Walsh and Kefi, 2008). At the individual level, we propose that IT culture results from the individual’s affiliation to an IT users group, given that the effects of this IT affiliation interact with the individual’s other group affiliations. This extended model provides us with a set of cultural features that informed the data analysis described below.

## 3. Culture, needs and motivation

Rokeach (1973) indicates that needs have to be processed through group norms in order to be cognitively represented and transformed into values. Norms prescribe or proscribe some behaviors in specific situations whereas values transcend specific situations. According to Rokeach, norms bring an attribute called oughtness (i.e. what an individual ought to do in order to follow group norms) that acts upon individual needs, leading to values. Values possess the oughtness attribute, but needs do not. Needs are thus conceptualized as being antecedents to values and one may investigate culture through the concept of human needs (Walsh and Kefi, 2008).

Consistent with Rokeach’s work, needs and motivation emerged as the embodiment of culture in our research. Even though the concept of needs has been implicitly or explicitly called upon in most IS research, needs theory is rarely used Needs theory holds that an unsatisfied need will lead an individual to act in such a way as to satisfy it (Au et al., 2008). Needs represent an internal force that guides behaviors (Maslow, 1954). Primary needs relate to one’s biological nature and secondary needs relate to one’s personality. Beyond primary needs, Winter (1996) proposes that only three secondary human needs are fundamental: self-accomplishment, power, and affiliation. Self-accomplishment needs regard overcoming obstacles in order to actualize one’s gifts. Power needs regard the prestige that influences other people’s actions and well being. Affiliation needs regard social exchanges with other people (Steel and König, 2006). We propose that, in some instances, these secondary needs may be satisfied through, and therefore may guide, IT usage. It is also possible that primary needs apply. For example, IT usage can become an addiction for some users, thus close to a biological, primary need.

Needs theory is linked to motivation. In the social psychology literature it is accepted that fundamental needs lead to motivation (Maslow, 1943) but that all motivation does not result from fundamental needs e.g. Vallerand (1997) details three levels of motivation: situational (state), contextual (life domain) and global (personality) that do not all results from fundamental needs. Furthermore there appears to be no consensus in the literature as to which human needs are fundamental, and no consensual theoretical proposal appears to be available in the literature to explain fully the relationship between needs and motivation (Dalmas. 2007). We do not try to reconcile the various divergent perspectives on the concepts brought forward in our theoretical framework; instead we accept that, as agreed by all researchers of the social psychology field, the concepts of needs and motivation are closely inter-related and that their relationships are multiple and complex. Concretely, motivation concerns the active directing of behavior towards some preferential categories of situations or objects.<sup>2</sup> In motivation theory, motivation is a psychological process that results from the interaction between an individual and the environment, i.e., between the needs of the individual and the needs of the environment (Latham and Pinder, 2005). Motivation theory was previously applied in IS research in IT adoption and usage (Davis et al., 1992; Venkatesh and Speier, 1999).

Self-determination Theory (Deci and Ryan, 1985; Ryan and Deci, 2000) embraces three types of human motivation: intrinsic, extrinsic, and amotivation. Intrinsic motivation is ‘‘doing an activity for its inherent satisfaction rather than for some separable consequence” (Ryan and Deci, 2000 p.56). Extrinsic motivation is ‘‘that pertains whenever an activity is done in order to attain separable outcome” (Ryan and Deci, 2000, p. 60). Amotivation is the state of ‘‘lacking an intention to act” (Vallerand, 1997, p. 279).

There are three types of intrinsic motivation: to know, to accomplish, and to experience stimulation (Vallerand, 1997, 2001). Intrinsic motivation to know leads to behavior that is performed in order to learn. Intrinsic motivation to accomplish leads to behavior that is performed in order to create something, to surpass oneself. Intrinsic motivation to experience stimulation leads to behavior mainly associated with the satisfaction of one’s senses.

Extrinsic motivation has differing degrees of autonomy or self-determination from the least autonomous to the most autonomous. The lowest autonomy means behavior is externally regulated, with an ‘‘external locus of causality” (Ryan and Deci, 2000, p. 59). Introjected regulation is of a higher degree of autonomy where behavior is adopted to attain pride and/or ego-enhancements. A higher form is identified regulation where behavior is accepted because outcome is perceived as important to achieve one’s goal. The highest form is integrated regulation, where behavior is assimilated to the self, congruent with one’s needs and values. Ryan and Deci (2000), Gagné and Deci (2005), show that the self-determination continuum goes from amotivation to intrinsic motivation, through extrinsic motivation. They find the state of amotivation is totally lacking self-determination; they also show that intrinsic motivation is invariably self-determined.

Pelletier et al. (1997) work specifically on the concept of amotivation and identify a four-factor model of amotivation due to capacity-ability beliefs, strategy beliefs, capacity-effort beliefs and helplessness beliefs.

## 4. Methodology

We used a grounded theory approach (Glaser and Strauss, 1967; Glaser, 1978; Strauss and Corbin, 1990, 1994) involving an interpretive and hermeneutic orientation (Klein and Myers, 1999). This exploratory, adductive mode (Peirce, 1974) resulted in a taxonomy arising from the continuous interplay between the collection, the analysis and comparison of data, as well as the consultation of relevant literature to improve our theoretical sensitivity (Glaser, 1978).

## 4.1. Research settings

The research was conducted in two different settings over a period of approximately 24 months. The first was a corporate setting consisting of eight diverse small-to-medium enterprises (SMEs) (see Appendix A). All of these firms had recently overhauled their information systems affecting most employees’ day-to-day work lives. Six firms were part of a local government project that financed strategic computerization in SMEs. Consultants appointed by the local government body assisted these firms in the strategic alignment of their information systems. The second setting was a society composed of diverse professionals, teachers, students, retired workers, etc.

The research emerged as four phases. Because this is grounded theory research, these phases were not pre-planned, but evolved as the study results arose. The first phase of the research involved a survey and analysis of social identity theory and IT culture. The study of experience is the starting point of any social investigation (Clandinin and Connelly, 1994). Therefore, at the beginning of this phase, an exploratory post-analysis was conducted. In ethnomethodology, post-analysis is a method suitable to re-examine past experiences through the light of new elements intervening in the meantime, or with new criteria In fact, the first author was an experienced project manager in corporate settings who had kept observational notes relating to the implementation of new computerized IS. These notes included unexpected user profiles. As these elements were summarized in the post-analysis, the theory evolution process began. Further literature reviews and analysis continued throughout the four phases. Phase two was carried out in the corporate setting. During the third phase, the second phase results were confirmed, informed, or refined by our results in the society setting. The research also continued in the corporate setting during the third phase. The final phase involved interviews only in the society setting (see Fig. 1).

We approached IT usage as a cultural phenomenon in a holistic perspective of culture (Gallivan and Srite. 2005). The diversified settings and participants helped us identify central tendencies in IT culture emerging through IT usage that are more likely to be valid across diverse organizations and technologies.

## 4.2. Data collection, coding and analysis

Prior to collecting data in the corporate settings, we conducted a preliminary focus group with the IS consultants who were involved with the subsidized SMEs.<sup>3</sup> The purpose of this focus group was to eliminate any bias in our approach that might have resulted from our previous knowledge of the corporate settings.

Because the sampling is theoretical, the number of interviews and subjects could not be pre-planned in grounded theory. 54 interviews (29 in the corporate setting and 25 in the societal setting: details of interviewees are available from the authors) and one focus group were conducted. The focus group and 35 interviews were recorded and transcribed (13 corporate and 22 societal). There were 19 interviews that could not be recorded and were memorialized in notes and memos. In total, there were 350 pages of transcripts and notes.

The interviews began with selective sampling, followed by theoretical sampling. Theoretical sampling is ‘‘the process of data collection for generating theory whereby the analyst jointly collects, codes, and analyses his [sic] data and decides what data to collect next and where to find them, in order to develop his [sic] theory as it emerges” (Glaser and Strauss, 1967, p.45).

The first phase interviewing began with IS project managers. Subsequent interviewees were selected through the ‘‘snowball” method (Patton, 2002) in which interviewees gave the names of other people to interview. Project managers were asked to identify users who appeared as pre-implementation or adoption facilitators (voluntarily or through being appointed to do so). We then interviewed both facilitators and non-facilitators. As our framework for user IT culture emerged, subsequent sampling became theoretical. This theoretical sampling drove interviewee selection during the second phase.

Data was collected and analyzed simultaneously through a constant comparative method (Glaser and Strauss. 1967: Glaser, 1998). The theoretical framework emerged from the data and was constantly verified, modified, or completed when new data were collected and analyzed. A semi-structured interview guide was used for the first eight interviews. Subsequently, this structure was adapted according to the evolving theoretical construction. We also used available documents such as technical software documentation, and consultant reports) and attended several meetings with consultants and project managers.

Open coding of data helped us describe what was ‘‘happening” and explore similarities and differences (Goulding, 1999). We grouped and labeled similar data as concepts which were then categorized. The coding process involved the software NVIVO. The double coding procedure was conducted using EXCEL as the paragraph was the unit of analysis (Huberman and Miles, 1991) and this option was not available in QSR NVIVO 8.

Axial coding provided the investigation of possible dynamic inter-relationships between concepts (Glaser and Strauss, 1967; Goulding, 1999). The software enabled the study of cross concepts, categories, or data codes in matrices<sup>4</sup> to reveal possible relationships between ‘‘substantive codes” developed during open coding and develop theoretical codes (Glaser, 1978; Kelle, 2005).

Finally selective coding: ‘‘means that the researcher reviews the collected data by checking out whether the newly developed categories remain constant when the data is analyzed specifically for these categories” (Elliott and Lazenbatt, 2005). Other axes of analysis that emerged from the data, but were not confirmed through selective coding, were eliminated. When the results of data collection and analysis stabilized, we reached ‘‘theoretical saturation” (Glaser, 1978, 2004; Strauss, 1987; Strauss and Corbin, 1990) and stopped the interviews. The final three main axes of analysis were (1) fundamental needs satisfied through IT, (2) perceived IT needs, and (3) IT motivation along the self-determination continuum. The last two axes of analysis are reported in more detail below.

All interviews were coded by the first author of the present article, To check for an undue bias, a second coder (with a considerably different background than the author) also coded five interviews. Because this study is interpretive, this process did not aim to verify the objective reading of the data. It aimed at verifying the fit between the proposed reading grid and the data, as well as its understandability (Glaser and Strauss, 1967; Douglas, 2003). Accordingly the second coder used 43 de fined codes. The fit is convincing with a 92% agreement level (Huberman and Miles, 1991).

While objectivity in observations is less important in interpretive or grounded theory studies, we could not resist returning to some of the informants during the course of the research and informally presenting them with the results. Appraisals were uniformly positive and confirmatory. One participant even thanked us for understanding her ‘‘predicament” so well.

## 4.3. IT needs and IT motivation

The concepts presented in this section are grounded in, and emerged from, our data. For grounded theory reports, a theoretical preview can improve a reader’s comprehension of the results (Suddaby, 2006).

Beyond users’ fundamental needs satisfied through IT, we investigated users’ IT needs and IT motivation as a means to assess the users’ IT culture/IT values. Table 2 details the different kinds of IT needs and IT motivation encountered during the study. We specifically adapted these concepts from needs and motivation theories to information systems. We used these to interpret and understand the user profiles that emerged in the data.

Perceived IT needs: Individuals who use IT develop perceptions of certain new needs that are beyond those fundamental to all human beings. These needs emerge on three levels: situational, contextual, and global. Situational needs are for specific IT in order to fulfill given tasks, e.g. the need for a new ERP needed to fulfill clients’ expectations. Contextual needs are for the use of IT globally in some context, e.g. the need for IT in order to meet the overall requirements of one’s work life. Global needs are for IT in all aspects of one’s life e.g. the implicit need for IT in one’s everyday life beyond any immediate practical need.

Intrinsic IT motivation: IT may be used for its own sake, for the satisfaction inherent to its usage, Three forms of intrinsic IT motivation emerge: to know IT, to accomplish through IT, and to experience stimulation through IT. To know IT means the individual is motivated to use IT in order to discover, understand, and learn about IT. To accomplish through IT means the individual is motivated to use IT to better one’s self through IT usage and adequately master one’s IT tools. To experience stimulation while using IT means IT usage corresponds to the search for pleasant sensations, for sensory satisfaction.

Extrinsic IT motivation: IT may be used as a means to an end in order to attain a distinctive outcome. We adapted the self determination continuum to explain different kinds of extrinsic IT motivation. Integrated regulation means IT usage is congruent with one’s global goals and fundamental values. Identified regulation means the choice to use IT is self-determined. IT usage is important because one knows this usage will achieve purposes one considers important for the self. Introjected regulation means that the self does not regulate the choice to use IT but one imposes other people’s choice on oneself. IT usage is performed to enhance self-esteem, to valorize oneself with respect to people who are deemed important. External regulation means one uses IT to comply; one knows that one has to use IT e.g., to do one’s work or to communicate with one’s peer group. IT usage is not performed through one’s own free will, but compelled by supervisors, teachers, family, or an entourage

IT amotivation: Sometimes there is simply a lack of intention to use IT. We can characterize IT amotivation along several dimensions adapted from Pelletier et al. (1997). IT capacity/ability beliefs mean one lacks necessary competency to use IT adequately. IT strategy beliefs mean one will not obtain hoped-for results when using IT. IT capacity/efforts beliefs mean that using IT will require too much effort compared to the expected results. IT helplessness beliefs mean IT usage is beyond one’s overall capacity; one is not overall able to use IT.

## 5. Results

We found that the subjects in our study reflected a set of cultural features. However, this set of features was not common to every subject, but rather each subject reflected a subset of these features, and certain features more than others. This result is not surprising considering the complexity of culture. We can most easily report these results by first delineating the general cultural feature set. We will represent these features as ‘‘culture archetypes”, or idealizations of a pure cultural form that is unlikely to occur in nature. This set of archetypes will enable us to explore the ‘‘culture hybrids”: those cultural forms that are the more naturally occurring vitiations or hybrids of the archetypes. The culture of each subject in our study represents a culture hybrid. Through this understanding of culture archetypes and culture hybrids, we are able to develop and present a theory of ‘‘culture creep”, a process by which one culture archetype disperses throughout an organization by inhabiting more and more culture hybrids.

## 5.1.1. Pro-active users

In the corporate settings. Pro-active users participate voluntarily in new IT projects right from the pre-implementation IT needs assessment stages. In the societal setting, these users investigate new IT even where immediate practical needs are obscure. Most users of this group have a high degree of personal innovativeness in IT (‘‘PIIT ‘‘, Agarwal and Prasad, 1998). In both settings they also have global IT needs, a sense that they need IT for all aspects of their lives. Pro-active users include four archetypal profiles: passionate, dangerous, interested, and studious. 5.1.1.2. The dangerous users. These users are attracted by IT and will actively initiate their own participation in any IT project. Often their academic training or organizational position do not make them natural candidates for participation so there is an intrinsically motivated drive to self-accomplishment through IT usage. If management permits their voluntary involvement in a new IT project, they use IT as a means to improve their position in the hierarchy. The role of IT usage as a power symbol is well known (Jasperson et al., 2002; Jouët, 2000; etc.). The power aspect is present even in the societal setting, although more informal and less explicit. These users often accomplish tasks for which they are badly prepared and delude themselves and their entourage with supposed competences. Dangerous users often lack the natural disposition to go beyond the playful use of computers.

With other employees, his speech was ‘I can’t do these menial tasks anymore. I leave these to you. I don’t have time. I am into computers now’ . . . 5.1.1.3. The interested users. These users welcome any new IT tool with ease, or even pleasure, because they enjoy learning about IT. They have an intrinsic motivation to know, but they avoid becoming captivated by IT tools because these can absorb too much of their time. They need IT in all aspects of their lives, recognizing it as a necessity. While they have globally perceived IT needs, they tend to link IT to their professional career. For example, they leave the computer behind when going on holidays and vacations.

For these users, IT mostly fulfils their needs for affiliation with a work group or an informal peer group.

5.1.1.4. The studious users. Studious users are dedicated learners who like to study and learn in diverse domains, including IT. They are models to their direct entourage even when lacking good academic training. While not properly trained for some of their IT tools, their intrinsic motivation to know IT drives them to go deeper and deeper in the pursuit of understanding IT. They will even self-train in their free time to reach more significant levels of knowledge. Their self-accomplishment need is satisfied through IT usage and an IT intrinsic motivation to accomplish

And then, at that time, everybody was starting to talk about computers, and that’s when I started, on Saturdays, Sundays, night and day, to learn about computers. On my own . . . Computers fit with my personality which has always been organized and methodical . . .

I like to know, I like to find solutions for myself; it’s the same when I meet a problem on software.

– Societal setting I12-F22

This group approximates Rogers’ (2003) innovators. They are similar to innovators because of their strong need for learning. However, in our data, this group sometimes lack the high academic levels and the innovative tastes of Roger’s innovators.

## 5.1.2. Passive users

This attitudinal group of user archetypes must be compelled to use IT by their needs for affiliation and peer group conformity. Unless forced by urgent situational or contextual IT needs, they will only use the IT Tools they know to the minimum degree necessary. They are extrinsically motivated. Sometimes there may be amotivation factors where their involvement with IT is forced and mandatory. This group includes four archetypal IT cultural profiles: disciplined, frightened, disenchanted and constrained.

5.1.2.1. The disciplined users. These users are competent and well trained but avoid involving themselves voluntarily in IT implementation projects. They perceive the need for IT to improve their self-efficacy in their required tasks. This is an extrinsic IT motivation with integrated regulation. However, IT is just another tool without any particular or specific personal interest. However, they show no sign of amotivation toward IT. Once they have mastered the IT tools perceived as IT needs in their context, they can be very efficient in their use. They will not go beyond this mandatory or convenient usage of IT.

They do not like computers. They have only understood that computers are mandatory if companies are to develop and progress – Corporate setting F1-M45

(Talking about some of his employees)

I am not a computer expert; I just can use most computers and software, mostly for work purposes, but I don’t particularly like IT.

– Corporate setting D3-M23

5.1.2.2. The frightened users. For these users, the world of IT is an unknown and terrifying place. They feel inadequate when they use IT. Because of their ability and effort beliefs, they show signs of IT amotivation. They often avoid attempts to learn more, sometimes because of their fear of failure

Computers and software help me do my work on a day to day basis but they represent tools which frighten me as I do not master them . . . I feel like a failure.

– Corporate setting F2-F44

I was always afraid of making mistakes, to lose what I was working on if I struck the wrong key. . .For me computers are a big mystery.

– Societal setting I4-F62

These users satisfy IT contextual needs that emerge from their work context. In the corporate settings, the mandatory IT use justifies their need for self-efficacy in order to retain their job. But there is no personal commitment. This IT extrinsic motivation through identified regulation means they know that IT use improves the quality of their work, but they limit themselves to the mandatory use imposed by the corporate hierarchy or the society in which they live.

In our data, their latent anxiety for IT usage can be traced to a lack of IT mastery and training. The anxiety diminishes as their competence improves. IT anxiety and its consequences became known in the early 1970s, and have been thoroughly studied over the ensuing decades. (Cambre and Cook, 1985; Howard and Mendelow, 1991; Marcoulides, 1988; Mc Elroy et al., 2007).

We found some elements of this profile in most of the untrained or novice users. It may be a transitory state for some users. There are traces of it in the ‘‘digital immigrants” (Prensky, 2001) who have not yet reached sufficient IT mastery. Unex pectedly, some ‘‘digital natives” in our settings belonged to this group.

I feel like it’s too complicated; I am afraid and I don’t have sufficient basic know-how.

– Societal setting I3-F29

5.1.2.3. The disenchanted users. Most users in this group have previously encountered difficult and unsuccessful IT implementations in their work lives. Their high initial expectancies were unfulfilled. As a consequence they purposely minimize their perceived IT needs and use IT only when mandatory. This is extrinsic IT motivation with introjected regulation. They are disenchanted with IT and expect IT tools to fail. IT strategy beliefs show strong amotivation less attributed to IT itself than to IT service people who abused their ignorance.

We were not given all relevant information; the software providers delivered without giving us proper training which led to failure.

– Corporate setting G1-M42

I only experienced sabotages . . . Too many software companies . . . promised miracle solutions without even doing the bare minimum, hence my indifference for IT today.

– Societal setting F1-M45

I have had some very bad experiences with IT at work. We were sold software much too heavy, too complicated and which did not fit our needs.

– Societal setting I1-M56

This novel profile resembles known profiles of users who experienced cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957) related to IT and move from an initial acceptance and adoption toward refusal (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Bhattacherjee and Premkumar, 2004). Disenchanted profiles use IT for professional reasons and know that they need IT for some of their tasks but they resent this obligation. They often have difficulty in expressing their resentment and do not like talking about IT.

5.1.2.4. The constrained users. These users know they cannot be active members of an organization without IT, but believe IT does not improve the quality of their work. They only use IT when unavoidable. Their IT needs are situational with such elements of IT amotivation as capacity-ability beliefs, strategy beliefs, capacity-effort beliefs and helplessness beliefs (Pelletier et al., 1997).

It annoys me and makes me nervous, it’s so complicated. . .We have to wait on technicians, programmers. . .When I retired I was happy to leave all that behind me but, after a few months I had to buy a PC to be able to surf on the web and to stay in touch with family and friends everywhere in the world.

– Societal setting I4-F62

These profiles use IT to avoid punishment and mostly to stay in touch with their reference group. Their motivation is extrinsic and obtained through external regulation.

5.1.3.1. The dodgers. For dodgers, IT is an imposition to be avoided at all cost. They have no perceived IT needs and are completely IT amotivated. IT is a subject of such complete boredom and uselessness that our interviews had to be prompt, informal, and unrecorded. Even our descriptive quotes are taken from others although we sought to confirm these verbally with the person described.

## 5.2. Hybrids of culture archetypes

Table 3 summarizes idealized and simplified archetypes. This table provides a detailed representation of the distinct features found in each archetype. Most users demonstrated traits of two or more archetypal profiles but in most cases one was dominant. Some profiles seem mutually exclusive, such as the dodger and the studious profiles. Others seem mutually inclusive. For example, all identified passionate users appeared to include traits of either studious, dangerous or interested. Table 3 shows how the profiles of archetypes resemble each other, and how they differ. For example, the profiles of the studious and dangerous culture archetypes are quite similar. They differ only in two ways. One is the exact way fundamental needs are satisfied. A studious culture is satisfied with self-accomplishments, while a dangerous culture has a power need. These also differ in the exact motivation for usage. A dangerous culture is more motivated by stimulation than learning. Consequently these two culture archetypes inhabit each other in terms of the overlap in the features of their profiles. While a studious culture might be dominant, a dangerous culture lurks just beneath the surface. Only two features need change slightly to shift a culture from its dominance by one archetype to its dominance by another.

The archetypes are inter-related in progressive ways. Perceived IT needs tend to be progressive. Contextual IT needs build from situational IT needs. Global IT needs build from contextual IT needs. This building process involves socialization and cultural exchange with other IT users.

All users except the dodgers appear to satisfy affiliation needs through IT motivation. Studious users who satisfy selfaccomplishment needs do not satisfy power needs through IT. The self-determination axis follows the continuum described by Gagné and Deci (2005). In Fig. 3, this continuum of IT usage self-determination extends from IT amotivation to intrinsic IT motivation. There are varying degrees of self-determination at the extremes. Some amotivation factors occur together with extrinsic motivation factors in several profiles. The amotivation elements disappear as self-determination increases along the contiuum. We found that Pelletier et al.’s (1997) four dimensions of amotivation were negatively correlated with Dec and Ryan’s dimensions of extrinsic motivation. (These elements need to be investigated further through quantitative methods in order to be confirmed.) The self-determination continuum also appears to follow a user’s IT personal-voluntaryinvolvement continuum in the CIS context. At one extreme there is no personal involvement. They refuse to use IT. At the other extreme is personal commitment. They use IT voluntarily. In between these extremes is mandatory involvement. They use IT because they feel compelled.

The usage history varies without an obvious pattern. Some users had mandatory use of IT before voluntary use, others were immediately voluntary. Some passionate users made tremendous efforts to obtain some rumored new IT.

Culture interacts with an individual’s nature to determine needs and motivation. These do not result exclusively from culture in terms of context, training and socialization processes. Nor do these results exclusively from users’ natural dispositions. They result from the intricate inter-relationships of these two components. For example. two brothers (interviewees I8 and I14, see Appendix B) had exactly the same academic training and lived all their lives in the same cities and surroundings. They had incompatible IT cultural profiles (i.e. passionate/studious versus dodger/constrained). In contrast, a brother and a sister with different initial training, who lived for over 20 years in different countries (interviewees I1 and I4 see Appendix B) had exactly the same IT cultural profile (constrained).

Surprisingly, gender and academic training had no direct causal link with the user IT cultural profiles. Sometimes, well educated ‘‘digital natives” (Prensky, 2001) were dodgers or constrained. ‘‘Digital immigrants” (ibid.) with little academic back ground were sometimes identified as passionate and studious. These results contrast previous research that found age, gender, and academic level significant in relation to IT usage (e.g. Igbaria et al., 1990; Prensky, 2001; Venkatesh and Morris, 2000; etc.). Possible explanations include (1) IT user profiles have globally evolved, and (2) the studied populations were not sufficiently diversified.

These findings indicate that the profiles result from both an acquired cultural component and innate individual idiosyn: crasies. Because there is an acquired component, there is an opportunity for managers to influence culture. However, because there is an innate component there are limits to this influence. This means that managers and trainers may consider influencing IT user cultural change when designing information systems, but only within the limits imposed by individual idiosyncrasies.

## 5.3. Culture creep

An understanding of how an IT user culture changes will open possibilities for management to strategically influence the directions of this change. Many cultures were represented in the IS organizations in our study. This result is important because it highlights how the culture of an IS organization is not necessarily a simple, monotonic culture archetype. Rather, it is a culture junction where many different culture hybrids mix together in purposive ways. The result is a culture dynamic in which some culture hybrids may shift forms according to individual IS experiences and interactions with other individual culture hybrids. For example, an organization without a dominant culture archetype, but one with a large mix of studious users and dangerous users, could gradually shift to become an organization where one of these two profiles is dominant. This creeping cultural domination would proceed because these two are so closely related in terms of their features that each inhabits the other. In such a case, the culture creep might create a trend in the organization toward a more dangerous archetype or toward a more studious archetype.

Users’ IT needs and motivation provide a lens that allows us to assess IT culture and define archetypal profiles. We found user profiles dynamic because culture is acquired and evolving. The IT cultural evolution of two users was particularly striking. The project manager (and CEO) of corporation F evolved from dangerous to disenchanted. This evolution followed the failure of two successive IT projects under his leadership. Simultaneously, the CFO of the same company evolved from frightened to disciplined. She attributed this change to the present research process that afforded opportunities for expressing her feelings and socializing with others about her IT usage and past fear of IT.

I was frightened of IT . . ..I still need some specific know how and it still is not a passion for me. . .However, today I am able to understand, accept and try and fulfill my IT needs. . . I can ascertain that before, I used to prefer pen and paper and today I prefer screen and mouse when I have things to write. It’s a huge leap forward for me.

– Corporate setting F2-F44/2

Some users were extrinsically motivated to start using IT through external regulation (e.g. IT use necessary for work purposes). Afterwards their motivation evolved to intrinsic motivation through the pleasure, stimulation and satisfaction of fundamental needs

Originally it was mandatory, it was an obligation. . .Now I really need IT. . ..partly for mandatory, professional reasons but also for optional reasons. . .You have to use a computer to experience the pleasure of using it. . .

– Societal setting I20-M65

There were two important phenomena in the group dynamics between pro-active and passive groups. These phenomena appeared to encourage culture to creep from one group to the next. One was the emerging social identities that appear linked to attitudinal group membership.

The first phenomenon was described by a CFO as follows:

There really is a ‘‘split” between people who have an easy approach to IT, and people [. . .] like me [. . .], an intellectual ‘‘split” between the group of people who ‘speak’ computer language as if it were a usual language, and the others who do not have this easy approach to IT and who, in fact, do not understand really all that is said and done about IT.

– Corporate setting F2-F44/1

The ‘‘split” mentioned by this speaker is felt strongly by the individuals who belong to the pro-active group and also by those outside this group. This split the ‘‘good ones” or reference group from ‘‘the others”. Everyone perceived this other group as ‘‘less good” or inferior. The split was not absolute. Disciplined users, in some instances, appear to partake in both groups depending on their friendly appraisal of other users.

The second phenomenon is the organizational socialization process that centers on IT. A positive social identity appears in the pro-active group with a strong image attached to the membership of this group. These processes fit the social identity theory ‘‘We–They” phenomenon (Tajfel, 1978; Tajfel and Turner, 1979). The identity is not only felt within the group, but it is also noticeable outside the group. In corporate settings this positive image is may be informally linked to the reference group because the reference group is close to senior management. Some of the consultants among our subjects were very explicit about these inherent dynamics, perhaps because their external perspective was clearer than that of the main actors.

They (the people belonging to the emerging reference group) make themselves indispensable, let’s say. After a certain time . . . even if the project manager is not aware of it, he relies anyway on these people.

– Consultant, female, 42

The relationship between the project manager and this person changes, evolves; there is a trust, a special relationship, which implies that even if the person has no particular title, it makes this person closer to the boss.

– Consultant, male, 40

Somebody. . .who, at the start, is at a level which is not involved with managerial decision, will finally be at the core of managerial strategic decisions. because of his attendance at certain meetings during the start of the new IS implementation. Then suddenly, he becomes important He becomes important and detains a power which was not particularly always planned at the start.

– Consultant, female, 42

An individual in one of the inferior groups feels the subjective hope of belonging to the reference group as he anticipates the acquisition of a preferable social identity. The anticipatory socialization (Merton et al., 1965) by which ‘‘an individual acquires and internalizes the values of a reference group he wants to belong to”, helps him ‘‘haul himself up into the group”

(Dubar, 1998, p. 61). Belonging to the reference group is appropriate behavior because organizational socialization regards learning the appropriate value systems, norms, attitudes and behaviors (Lacaze, 2005; Schein, 1985).

IT allows you to go much further, especially in a world where people communicate more and more through IT.

– Corporate setting – F2-F44

The person, if she is from a low hierarchical position and if she is seen as belonging to the pro-active IT reference group, is then in a much improved socio-professional set up compared to what it was before.

– Consultant, male, 67

Possible evolutions in a user IT culture could be nurtured through socialization processes brought about, consciously or unconsciously, by the individual himself. These efforts aim to reach the social identity of the sought-after reference group.

IT for him? . . . He is not an executive, he has a job with middle/low responsibilities, but now he is involved in IT implementation, then in a way he stands in for the executives, the ‘white collars’.

– Corporate setting – F1-M45

Individuals may evolve and their behaviors/ attitudes may change. Globally evolving user IT cultural profiles depend on the socio-organizational context (e.g. IT projects constructed through participatory management versus prescribed/ imposed, organizational socialization centered on IT, etc.). This evolution may provide an opportunity for organizations to shape culture creep. Once you understand cultural profiles, they may be guided towards future cultural profiles that might fulfill organizational IT needs. Such a nurturing of cultural creep provides means of managing culture in meaningful ways without subscribing to extreme notions such as value engineering or cultural control as described by Meyerson and Martin (1987).

## 6. Discussion

The large body of work that seeks to meaningfully classify users is nearly omnipresent in the information systems literature. These works adopt very different levels of analysis and methodologies. Space limitations do not permit here a summary of this extensive literature; however, Appendix C summarizes in chronological order previous typologies which we found relevant to our work. This table does not pretend to be exhaustive. Our purpose was to focus on typologies that were anchored to cultural aspects, especially where related to the user IT culture. This theoretical lens has rarely been used in the existing literature

Though extremely useful and providing some grounding for our own work, these typologies leave a gap in the study of usage through a cultural lens. These typologies focus on specific technologies, specific populations of users, or globally use an innovation approach. This gap is filled by our culture-analytical framework compelled by our data.

In terms of innovation, most of the users we met in our research settings did not consider information technologies as new and considered most of the IT they used as having reached the status of ordinary objects blended in social practices (Jouët, 2000). In corporate settings, IT implementations were considered, in many instances, an issue of change management rather than innovation adoption. To study the specific IT culture layer, we had to choose settings and populations sufficiently diversified to account for the users’ other sub-cultural layers. Study populations have to be diversified. It is only under these conditions that we are able to identify average central tendencies6 in the IT culture layer.

Our results elaborate, and sometimes contradict, existing literature. Rogers’ innovation diffusion approach (1962, 1995, 2003), though valuable for rupture innovations, is not well refined for incremental innovations (i.e. innovations which are in the continuity of preceding innovations). We found in our settings that all proactive users appeared to be early adopters but all early adopters were not proactive. Disciplined users were also found to be early adopters but were not proactive. Jouët’s typology (1987) illuminates the quest for social identity through usage. She used a framework similar to ours, but hers could not accommodate the fast-paced IT represented in several of our profiles. Lawless and Price’s (1992) group ‘‘technological champion” encompasses both studious and dangerous users although it fails to distinguish between these very different groups. Similarly, we identified distinctive high PIIT groups that Agarwal and Prasad would classify only as proactive users (1998). Boullier’s typology (1997) is specifically tied to mobile technology and to a specific population (high mobility profiles). It does not extend to other IT and users profiles. Kellman’s (1958) categorization ‘‘conforms, identifies and internalizes” approaches the concepts of extrinsic and intrinsic motivation, but the concepts are subdued. For example, when Malhotra and Galetta (1999) used this framework to elaborate the TAM model (Davis, 1989), they did not attempt to identify the users groups. Prensky’s typology (2001) takes only age into consideration. Our research finds no correlation between the taxonomy and user’s age. Raz and Goldberg’s typology (2006) resulted in fairly low $R ^ { 2 }$ (explained variance). This could be because the work considers only expected usage within corporate settings and depends on the user’s position in the organizational hierarchy. Our results precisely indi cated that the user IT culture does not appear correlated to their hierarchical position.

The cultural dimension of usage has been somewhat neglected in the literature although some work (e.g. Lu et al., 2005; Lamb and Kling, 2003) might be considered inexplicit if pioneering. Other work (e.g. Kaarst-Brown and Robey, 1999) explicitly investigates IT culture but regards users only as an element of organizational culture. Our results further our understanding and conceptualization of usage by illuminating its cultural dimension.

The cultural dimension of usage helps open the ‘‘black boxes” of concepts like PU (Perceived Usefulness) and PEOU (Perceived Ease Of Use) that are used in models like the TAM and its derivates. ‘‘PU and PEOU have largely been treated as black boxes that very few have tried to open” (Benbasat and Barki, 2007, p. 212).

From a business perspective, our findings offer exciting instrumental possibilities for managing the direction of culture creep. Based on an understanding of the mechanics of IT user culture, and a survey of an organization’s constellation of IT user cultures, corporate IT training could be customized according to the user IT culture. For example, before implementing new IT in an organization, it would be productive to study prospective user IT culture. Organizations may be able to shape their future IT user culture by influencing the transitions of neighboring profiles from a less appropriate profile to a more appropriate profile. For example, it would be ideal to help migrate users who will approach any IT with a negative stance (due perhaps to unsuccessful previous IT experiences) or users with deeply ingrained fear of IT toward a user’s culture with fundamental needs that include the absolute and passionate need to use IT in everyday life. This could require a very differ ent kind of training for a new IT tool (see Table 4).

End-user involvement is often considered critical for successful strategic alignment (Burn and Szeto, 2000; Mohdzain and Ward, 2007; Reich and Benbasat, 2000; etc.). If the user IT culture could be assessed ex ante at pre-implementation stage, facilitating profiles could be identified and their voluntary involvement encouraged. Profiles in need of global as well as situational IT training could be identified. Organizational socialization processes between informal groups constituted around IT usage could also be nurtured between pro-active and passive groups. This socialization may allow intergroup emulation and encourage technological enculturation (Straub et al., 2000). Such nurturing is necessary. Pro-active users in our settings voluntarily socialized around the subiect of IT whereas passive users were very reluctant to do so without encouragement.

Our results show that IT usage may be mutating to include a true and intrinsic IT cultural dimension. If we can assess this dimension, we can identify unexpected user profiles: for example, untrained studious and passionate users at a low hierarchical level, or interested elderly employees with high computing competencies but neglected IT training. These profiles could prove valuable if encouraged with well targeted training and socialization. Such possibilities could prove extremely valuable in terms of strategic IT management, because these provide grounding for executable strategies. We can identify and assess the IT culture archetypes that are present. Depending on these archetypes, management activity can seek to influence the culture creep, providing a pragmatic strategy for achieving certain reasonable IT cultural goals.

## 7. Limitations and future research directions

Like all interpretive works, our results may contain interference from the cultural bias of the subjective researcher. It is a common issue with cultural research, ‘‘Because we are of our own culture, it is difficult for us, researchers and managers alike, to both live in our cultural context and to question it. It is difficult to engage in contextual, reflexive management and research, with the requirement of examination and critique of one's own assumptions and values." (Smircich. 1983. p. 355).

Culture is dynamic and evolving, especially with fast-moving IT developments. A taxonomy grounded empirically on the user IT culture may be contemporarily dependent on today’s available technology, tools, training, and basic academic curricula. The current process of mass computer literacy may modify the users’ IT needs, their IT culture and more widely their culture.

Future research is needed to confirm our findings, both methodologically and temporally. We advocate more field work, including additional interpretive work by other researchers and also work using objective positivist methods. We also advocate future work to study how IT user culture evolves in response to advances in IT and IT education.

The mutual inclusiveness of some archetypes (e.g. passionate and studious, or passionate and dangerous) should be investigated further as it could be important to explain mixed, contradictory results obtained in previous research. Agarwal and Karahanna (2000) underline that passionate profiles were studied for their negative aspects (Dern, 1996; Nash, 1997; Sinha, 1999); other works (Csikszentmihalayi, 1990; Ghani and Deshpande, 1994) however suggest that positive attitudes and exploratory usage of IT may result from this type of user profiles. It could be useful to investigate if these contradictory results may be explained by the users’ different fundamental needs satisfied through IT usage e.g. power needs or selfaccomplishment needs.

## 8. Conclusion

This research has mobilized needs, motivation and self-determination theories within the context of an interpretive case to develop an IT culture users’ taxonomy. This taxonomy identifies a new set of IT user cultural profiles that includes both original profiles and refinements of previously identified profiles from the literature. The results show how user IT culture evolves simultaneously with the emergence and development of their IT needs.

Humanity has migrated along its history from an oral culture to an oral and written culture, and more recently to an oral, written and numerical culture. From the perspective of these results, perhaps the last two decades have been less of an information or knowledge revolution, and more of a cultural revolution. Understanding how such revolutions relate to culture can help organizations improve the success rate of new IT implementations. By identifying IT user culture profiles, and how these profiles can transition, our work provides a foundation for analyzing and influencing IT usage culture. Certain users may be actualized to their greatest potential not only through a technical migration, but through a cultural migration. IT culture profiles help identify and understand how culture can creep along these migration paths. By carefully managing an organization’s culture creep, perhaps by training, perhaps by socialization, the shape of future IT culture profiles can be defined.
