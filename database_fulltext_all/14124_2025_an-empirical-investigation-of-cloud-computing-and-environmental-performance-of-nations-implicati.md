## Abstract

Cloud computing separates service users from carbon emission sources, obscuring IT’s ecological responsibility across national boundaries. This study dissects the digital responsibility of cloud computing, with a focus on obligations and accountability, to address its impact on the environment. To identify direct emitters and beneficiaries of pollution resulting from cloud computing and define the associated obligations, we estimate how cloud computing alters the energy and carbon intensities of IT capital and services in cloud region countries hosting data centers, as well as in neighboring countries. Using country-level data on IT capital stock and IT service expenditures for 51 countries from 1995 to 2016, combined with data on geographical proximity to cloud regions of global public cloud services, our results show that in cloud region countries, the environmental benefits of cloud-based IT services come at the expense of increased energy and carbon intensities from the IT capital providing those services. Conversely, neighboring countries seem to benefit from cloud services that generate pollution in cloud region countries, as evidenced by lower energy and carbon intensities in cloud-based IT services, without compromising their own environmental performance. Our findings suggest that both cloud service hosting and using countries share obligations for the environmental impacts of cloud computing. Building on our empirical findings, we discuss implications for accountability in cloud computing to facilitate the fulfillment of these obligations. We propose a shared responsibility model that allocates accountability between service providers and users, advancing the discourse on cloud computing responsibility and outlining directions for future research.

Keywords: Cloud Computing, IT Capital, IT Services, Sustainability, Carbon Intensity, Energy Intensity, Digital Responsibility, Shared Responsibility

## 1 Introduction

Reducing carbon and energy intensities of economic production, measured by carbon emissions and energy consumption per unit of economic output, has been emphasized as a promising pathway to addressing climate change while sustaining economic growth. A long-standing literature in the information systems (IS) discipline has accumulated empirical evidence showing that information technology (IT) capital makes a significant contribution to productivity growth at various levels, including countries (Dedrick et al., 2013; Dewan & Kraemer, 2000). In addition to its central role as a growth engine, IT has been cast as playing a role in the transition to sustainable economies by making physical processes more energy efficient, bringing it to the forefront of sustainable development (Dedrick, 2010; Melville, 2010; Watson et al., 2010).

In recent years, IT resources have rapidly moved to cloudbased systems and infrastructure (Frank et al., 2023). As a growing portion of IT is provided as a service on demand, cloud computing has become a basic utility for the digital economy (Brynjolfsson et al., 2010; Marston et al., 2011). However, cloud computing can be a doubleedged sword in pursuing environmental sustainability (Park et al., 2023). On the one hand, cloud computing could worsen environmental performance and elevate energy and carbon intensities in the overall economy, given that data centers that support cloud services consume a massive amount of electricity (Shehabi et al., 2016). With the rapid expansion of artificial intelligence and other computationally intensive applications, global electricity use by data centers and other IT uses is projected to grow from 460 TWh in 2022 (2% of global electricity demand) to over 1,000 TWh in 2026 under a worst-case scenario (IEA, 2024). Data centers alone could consume as much as 1,300 TWh by 2030, or 3.7% of global electricity demand (Ramachandran et al., 2024). On the other hand, cloud computing could allow for optimization of IT resource utilization and deployment of sophisticated IT applications, making production processes and operations across the economy more energy-efficient (Park et al., 2023).

Moreover, what makes the impacts of cloud computing more complicated is the fact that such dual effects of cloud computing on environmental sustainability can take place in different geographic locations (Mytton, 2020). In other words, cloud computing can separate service users from the carbon emissions caused by their use, making IT’s ecological responsibility obscure across national boundaries. For example, companies can employ cloudbased IT services that are offered from cloud regions (where cloud service providers locate resources, especially data centers) in neighboring countries. In this case, cloud-using companies and their home economy can benefit from cloud-based IT services without being held responsible for carbon emissions associated with those services outside their territories. By contrast, the countries that host cloud data centers would be given responsibility for carbon emissions from cloud-related infrastructure, even when users are outside their borders. Thus, accounting for responsibility in cloud computing from a broader perspective—by considering both service providers and users at the firm and country levels—is crucial to achieving sustainability in the digital economy, especially as more IT and IS move to the cloud.

To this end, this study dissects the digital responsibility of cloud computing, with a focus on obligations and accountability, to address its impact on the natural environment. To identify the direct emitters and beneficiaries of pollution resulting from cloud computing and define the associated obligations, we estimate how cloud computing alters the energy and carbon intensities of IT capital and services in cloud region countries hosting data centers, as well as in neighboring countries. For an empirical investigation, we developed a novel strategy by consolidating a unique country-level dataset on IT capital stock and IT services expenditures for 51 countries with geographical proximity to cloud regions of global public cloud service providers from 1995-2016. To proxy accessibility to cloud services, we created a proximity measure from the closest cloud region of global public cloud services and then interacted it with IT capital stock and IT services. Doing so allowed us to separate cloud infrastructure and cloud-based IT services from non-cloud-based infrastructure and services. Furthermore, we leveraged the cloud proximity measure to distinguish between cloud region countries and neighboring countries so that we could estimate the differential effects of cloud infrastructure and cloud-based IT services depending on the presence of cloud regions that host cloud data centers.

Our findings suggest that access to cloud services within the country makes IT services greener, reducing the energy and carbon intensities of nations while not significantly changing the contribution of IT capital to carbon emissions. More importantly, we reveal the crosscountry differences depending on the presence of cloud regions where cloud service providers operate data centers. In cloud region countries that host data centers, the environmental benefits of cloud-based IT services come at the expense of increased energy and carbon intensities from the IT capital providing those services. Conversely, neighboring countries seem to benefit from the cloud services that generate pollution in cloud region countries, as evidenced by lower energy and carbon intensities in cloud-based IT services, without compromising their own environmental performance. Taken together, our findings suggest that both cloud service providers and users share responsibility for the environmental impacts of cloud computing. Building on our empirical findings, we discuss the implications for accountability in cloud computing to facilitate the fulfillment of these obligations. To this end, we propose a shared responsibility model that allocates accountability between service providers and users, advancing the discourse on cloud computing responsibility and outlining directions for future research. This study makes scholarly contributions by synthesizing the green IT/IS and digital responsibility literatures within the context of cloud computing.

## 2.1 Green IT/IS: Environmental Impacts of Information Technology

Compelling evidence has accumulated that investments in IT capital can lead to increased economic output at the firm, industry, and country levels (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Dewan & Kraemer, 2000; Stiroh, 2002). However, it has remained unclear whether and how IT affects the environment by increasing or decreasing negative externalities such as pollution, energy use, and carbon emissions.

Extant literature has shown mixed evidence of the impacts of IT and telecommunication infrastructure— especially information and communication technology (ICT) penetration—on the environmental performance of a country. Some studies have found that higher ICT penetration is associated with greater electricity consumption in a specific region (Freidin & Burakov, 2018; Salahuddin & Alam, 2015) and across multiple countries (Sadorsky, 2012), possibly because a significant amount of electricity is needed to operate ICT infrastructure and equipment. Other research has found evidence of a negative relationship between ICT penetration and the use of electricity (Bernstein & Madlener, 2010; Hao et al., 2022).

Similarly, studies on the relationship between ICT and carbon emissions show mixed evidence. For example, studies found that higher ICT penetration was associated with higher carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions in 23 EU countries (Park et al., 2018), 14 South and Southeast Asian countries (Arshad et al., 2020), and 10 emerging markets (Tsaurai & Chimbo, 2019). By contrast, other studies found that ICT penetration was related to lower CO2 emissions in 20 developing countries (Ozcan & Apergis, 2018), 12 Asian countries (Lu, 2018), and 5 BRICS countries (Ulucak et al., 2020). Such inconclusive evidence may be attributable to timevarying effects and cross-country heterogeneities (Dinda, 2004). For instance, Raheem et al. (2020) found that internet and mobile use were positively related to CO2 emissions in the long run but negatively related in the short run in G7 countries.

When it comes to national IT capital stock, researchers have investigated its impact on the energy intensity of nations and industries. For example, in 10 OECD countries, Schulte et al. (2016) found that IT capital stock was negatively associated with total energy demand. Cho et al. (2007) suggested that IT stock in some manufacturing sectors appeared to contribute to the reduction of electricity consumption, whereas IT stock in the service sector and other manufacturing sectors seemed to increase electricity consumption in South Korea. Moreover, different types of IT play different roles in energy intensity; for example, Collard et al. (2005) found that computer and software capital stock decreased electricity intensity, but communication capital stock increased it in the French service sector.

## 2.2 Environmental Impacts of Cloud Computing

A particularly interesting IT trend is the widespread adoption of cloud computing, which offers IT resources from computing and storage to software and applications over an electronic network on an on-demand basis (Marston et al., 2011). Cloud computing represents one of the most significant innovations in IT in recent years, yet no studies we are aware of have scrutinized if and how the environmental impacts of IT have been affected by the shift to cloud computing. We are motivated to bridge this critical gap by exploring the environmental impacts of cloud computing at the country level and going deeper into the ecological responsibility of cloud computing in a global context.

Data centers consume vast amounts of electricity to operate and cool, and emit CO2 associated with energy consumption (which depends on the carbon emissions created at the source of that energy, e.g., a hydroelectric versus coal-fired power plant) (Shehabi et al., 2016). Serious concerns have been raised about the impacts of data center operations on carbon emissions with the rapid growth in computing in the economy associated with applications such as big data analytics, cryptocurrency mining, and AI (Kumar & Buyya, 2012). However, data centers have made large reductions in the need for energy to cool equipment through energy-efficient hardware design and data center layout and operations (Lei & Masanet, 2022). Much of the improved efficiency in data centers has come from the shift to cloud and hyperscale data centers, which have lower (better) power usage effectiveness ratings than older data centers.

Cloud computing can also contribute to energy-efficient IT operations by offering sophisticated cloud-based IT services and helping to optimize the utilization of IT resources through server virtualization and elastic scaling of cloud resources. Using data from US industries, Park et al. (2023) demonstrated that the use of cloud computing leads to better energy efficiency through (1) mitigating the energy consumption of internal IT infrastructure and (2) facilitating energy-efficient production and operations. As a result of these improvements, it has been estimated that the number of compute instances in data centers around the world grew six-fold, data center IP traffic grew 11-fold, and installed storage capacity grew 26-fold from 2010 to 2018, while energy use remained flat (Masanet et al., 2020; Lei & Masanet, 2022). A simulation study suggests that if all US businesses moved their applications running on their own servers to cloud service providers’ servers, their computing energy footprint could be reduced by 87% (Masanet et al., 2013).

In the IS literature, green IS/IT positions green IS as a solution for enabling sustainable business processes, whereas green IT presents a challenge due to the physical operations of IT, calling for strategies to enhance energy efficiency and optimize equipment utilization (Malhotra et al., 2013). In this context, the dual effects of cloud computing place it at the intersection of green IT and green IS, giving rise to new challenges for sustainability and digital responsibility. However, to the best of our knowledge, no empirical research has comprehensively examined the dual effects of cloud computing, likely due to a lack of access to relevant data, limiting our understanding of the environmental impacts associated with cloud computing. Thus, our study contributes to the green

IT/IS literature by highlighting the distinction between direct polluters providing IT services (relevant to green IT) and the beneficiaries of those services (relevant to green IS), a distinction that becomes more complex in the case of cloud computing. Additionally, it underscores that the location of IT resources and the parties they serve are key factors in determining the environmental impacts of digital technologies and clarifying the respective obligations of those involved. Building on this notion, this study introduces a novel empirical approach that allows us to identify the dual impacts of cloud computing within and across borders, paving the way for future research in this field.

## 2.3 Digital Responsibility and Cloud Computing

While the advent of digital technologies has brought many advantages to our lives, such as improved productivity through automation and greater access to education and healthcare via digital platforms, some studies have identified issues and challenges caused by the rapid spread of digital technologies into most aspects of modern life, from the individual level (Ayyagari et al., 2011) to the organizational (Watson et al., 2010) and societal (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2022) levels. In other words, the potential power and development of digital technologies often come at the cost of dysfunctional outcomes, which calls for the concept of digital responsibility. In this study, we adopt the definition of digital responsibility provided by Recker et al. (in press) as:

a multidimensional concept that includes the acceptance of accountability for both harmful and beneficial outcomes from the engagement with digital technology, the obligation to enable positive outcomes of digitalization while safeguarding against possible negative consequences of the same, and the promise to be dependable, that is, committed to maintaining accountability and obligation in a persistent, continuous manner.

This definition emphasizes the obligation and accountability associated with digital technologies.

To contribute to a better understanding of digital responsibility, this study aims to dissect and scrutinize the responsibility of cloud computing, focusing on obligation and accountability from a broader perspective that encompasses both the firm and country levels in order to address its impact on the environment. First, a critical question raised by the concept of digital responsibility is exactly who is responsible for addressing and mitigating these outcomes. In particular, locating the locus of responsibility has become more critical for networked IT services, which are delivered via electronic networks. This separation distances service users from carbon emission sources, both within and across national boundaries. Moreover, the dual effect of cloud computing—potentially reducing carbon intensity on the user side while increasing it on the provider side—makes this issue more pronounced. In this regard, this study provides empirical evidence to identify the direct emitters and beneficiaries of pollution caused by digital technology, which we believe is a critical first step in establishing the obligations tied to digital responsibility.

Second, to offer insights into how to facilitate the fulfillment of obligations faced by cloud service providers and users, we discuss accountability mechanisms within the context of cloud computing at both the country and firm levels. In the literature, accountability has been studied both as a virtue and a mechanism (Bovens, 2010). While existing research on digital responsibility emphasizes accountability as a virtue and a positive quality within organizations that enhances organizational legitimacy (e.g., Oduro et al., 2024), less attention has been given to accountability mechanisms that focus on social relations or processes that involve an obligation to explain and justify conduct ex post facto. Particularly in the context of cloud computing, the discrepancy between beneficiaries and sources of environmental degradation makes it challenging for rent-seeking firms alone to resolve this tension, especially when they are located in different countries. Thus, the digital responsibility of cloud computing necessitates relevant accountability mechanisms rooted in a relational perspective, aimed at motivating both cloud computing providers and users to fulfill their obligations.

## 3 Methodology

## 3.1 Empirical Strategy

For the environmental performance of nations, we focus on carbon and energy intensities of economic production—measured by CO2 emissions and electricity consumption per unit of economic output. However, empirical challenges remain in examining the dual effects of cloud computing on these outcomes. First, it is challenging to disentangle the dual role of cloud computing in environmental performance—namely, the increase in energy consumption and carbon emissions from cloud infrastructure versus the improvement in energy efficiency from cloud-based IT services. These effects need to be separated from those of traditional, non-cloud-based IT resources. Moreover, service areas of cloud computing can go beyond the boundary of a country, making it challenging to measure the extent to which the country utilizes cloud-based IT services.

To overcome these empirical challenges, we developed a novel empirical strategy by combining national IT capital stock and IT service expenditures with geographical proximity to cloud regions of global public cloud services. First, we distinguished between IT capital stock and IT service expenditures to measure IT assets endowed in a country as a stock and ongoing use of IT services as a flow, respectively. Prior work has considered IT capital stock to be a key production input to measure the extent to which an economy utilizes IT resources in assessing its role in national GDP growth (e.g., Dedrick et al., 2013; Dewan & Kraemer, 2000). In addition, IT services expenditures as an intermediate input have been used to proxy cloud services (e.g., Jin & McElheran, 2024). Given that not all IT services are based on cloud computing, Park et al. (2023) further separated cloud-based IT services from non-cloud IT services, based on specific service types. However, it is not possible to decompose IT service types across over 51 countries in our sample, which motivated an alternative approach based on the locations of global cloud service regions.

Second, we created a proximity measure from the closest cloud region, normalized in the range of 0 to 1. The cloud proximity measure allowed us to distinguish between the focal countries hosting cloud regions and data centers and neighboring countries; a proximity of 1 indicates that the cloud region country and neighboring country have a proximity measure of less than 1, depending on the distance to the nearest cloud region country. In doing so, we focused on the four biggest global public cloud service providers— Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, and Alibaba Cloud, which account for a majority of global market share in public cloud infrastructure.<sup>1</sup> This strategy relies on the premise that proximity to cloud data centers is important to achieving better cloud service performance, thereby leading to higher utilization of cloud services. Industry experts suggest that data center proximity can minimize network latency, reduce data replication issues, provide easier access to IT support, and achieve better compliance with national data protection and privacy laws.<sup>2</sup> Such benefits of data center proximity explain why cloud service providers keep expanding their data centers across the world. In keeping with this, Greenstein and Fang (2022) showed that customers’ preference for proximity is one of the factors that determines the supply of data centers. In addition, Jin (2022) used the size of cloud service vendor industries in the state to instrument firms’ cloud adoption.

Finally, we interacted the cloud proximity measure with IT capital stock and IT services. This enabled us to differentiate between traditional IT (captured in the main terms of IT variables) and cloud-based IT (captured in the interaction terms between cloud measures and IT variables). It also disentangled cloud infrastructure (e.g., data centers) (reflected in the interaction between cloud measures and IT capital) and cloud-based IT services (reflected in the interaction between cloud measures and IT services), considering the dual role of cloud computing in environmental performance. Furthermore, the cloud proximity measure was decomposed into cloud region and cloud neighbor variables to represent cloudhosting and cloud-neighboring countries, respectively. This enabled us to estimate the differential effects of cloud infrastructure and cloud-based IT services between focal countries hosting cloud regions and neighboring countries. In sum, Figure 1 illustrates the conceptual framework underpinning our empirical strategy.

## 3.2 Data

We consolidated a country-level panel dataset of national IT resources and environmental performance for 51 countries from 1995 to 2016 (see Table A1 in the Appendix for the list of countries in our dataset). Specifically, the compiled dataset consisted of a set of variables where the values for these variables existed in the given country and year. <sup>3</sup> Our sample was constrained by data availability from all the sources.<sup>4</sup> Table 2 reports descriptions and summary statistics of the variables used in our analysis.

First, the focal outcomes of interest are (electric) energy and carbon intensities at the country-year level, which are measured as the amount of electricity consumption and CO2 emissions per unit of GDP, respectively. We obtained data on electricity consumption and CO2 emissions from the US Energy Information Administration (EIA) and World Bank (WB), respectively. To represent the economic output and total capital endowed in a country, GDP and total capital stock were obtained from the Penn World Table, Version 9.1 (Feenstra et al., 2015).

Second, to measure a country’s IT resources, we obtained IT spending data on computer hardware, software, and services from the International Data Corporation (IDC), which has been used in cross-country studies in IS literature (Dedrick et al., 2013). Hardware spending mainly includes server/storage, enterprise networks and infrastructure, personal computers, workstations, and peripherals. The majority of software spending is related to packaged software such as application tools and solutions that are available for consumers and commercial purposes. IT services include IT consulting services, operations and management of IT resources, training, and support services.

To compare monetary values across different countries over time, it was necessary to transform all monetary units into common units. To this end, the monetary values across countries were converted into international dollars based on purchasing power parities (PPPs), obtained from the WB database, as consistent with prior work using cross-country comparisons (Dedrick et al., 2013; Dewan & Kraemer, 2000). In this way, we were able to compare the purchasing power of the same basket of goods and services between one country and other countries by addressing different price levels among these countries. We then converted current international dollars to constant 2011 dollars by applying price deflators. Doing so allowed us to directly compare the monetary values from one year to another, accounting for time-varying factors such as change in product quality. For example, comparing current values of PC in 1995 and those in 2011 would not be desirable, as the quality of computers has been significantly improved over time. Thus, we needed to apply a qualityadjusted deflator to express the current values of PCs in 1995 with respect to the values of PCs in 2011. Thus, for hardware and software, we adopted price deflators for the categories of “Computers and Peripheral Equipment” and “Software” obtained from the US Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) (2003), respectively. For IT services, we averaged deflators of output prices for two IT service industries taken from the BEA (Han et al., 2011): Data Processing, Hosting and Related Services (NAICS 5182) and Computer System Design and Related Services (NAICS 5415).

Additionally, we converted the flow series of IT assets to stock values. When constructing the stock values, one of the underlying logics is that the value of a product (e.g., PCs) in the current year lasts in the future years but at a discounted rate assuming a depreciation of the product. We applied different depreciation rates for different products and services. Specifically, we adopted 25% and 40% as depreciation rates for hardware and software capital, respectively.<sup>5</sup> We then employed the perpetual inventory method to derive stock values of hardware and software capital based on 10 and 4 years of service life, respectively. In so doing, we extrapolated hardware and software capital in early years traced back to the past 10 and 4 years of flow using nonlinear least-square fitting. Then, we combined both hardware and software capital to come up with IT capital, as common in the IT productivity literature (e.g., Dewan & Kraemer, 2000). Non-IT capital stock was calculated by subtracting IT capital stock from the values of total capital stock. It is noteworthy that, consistent with prior literature, we did not construct a stock measure for IT services considering its nature of flow (e.g., Jin & McElheran, 2024).

Third, availability and proximity to cloud services were measured at the country-year level, based on cloud regions of global Big 4 cloud service providers (i.e., Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, and Alibaba Cloud). We obtained this information from the cloud providers’ websites and news articles. Figure 2 depicts the global expansion of their cloud regions since

2006 (see Table A2 in the Appendix for the list of cloud regions of global Big 4 cloud service providers). For each country, we then calculated the closest distance from its centroid to the metro areas of cloud regions available in each year; note that a 0 distance means that a cloud region is available within the country. For ease of interpretation, we normalized the distance measure between 0 and 1 such that the cloud proximity measure increases when a country can be provided with cloud services from closer cloud regions; the measure reaches the maximum value when a country hosts a cloud region within the border. Specifically, the cloud proximity is constructed as $\frac { M a x ( \sqrt { d _ { i t } } ) - \sqrt { d _ { i t } } } { M a x ( \sqrt { d _ { i t } } ) } ,$ , where $d _ { i t }$ denotes the distance (in miles) from country i to the closest cloud region in year t, which ranges from 0 to 1; note that we consider the square root of distance to give greater weights to shorter distances (i.e., closer access to cloud services). By definition, focal countries with cloud regions will have the value of 1 for the cloud proximity measure; a cloud proximity value of 0 indicates that a country is farthest (relative to other countries) from any available cloud region, which means virtually no accessibility to cloud services. To distinguish between focal countries with cloud regions and neighboring countries, we decomposed the cloud proximity measure into the binary indicator for cloud regions with the proximity measure being 1 (cloud region) and the cloud neighboring distance less than 1 (cloud neighbor). It is worth noting that, consistent with prior work, we coded 0 for cloud proximity before 2006 when the first public cloud services (i.e., Amazon Web Services) were launched (Park et al., 2023).

Finally, we obtained other control variables from the WB database. Labor input was measured as total employment adjusted by the unemployment rate. For population and road densities, we used data on the number of people (in thousands) and length of roads (in kilometers) per square kilometer of land area.

## 3.3 Empirical Model

We modeled energy consumption and carbon emissions as a function of capital and labor, which are considered key production inputs in a production function. In particular, we separated IT capital from non-IT capital, which is common in the IT productivity literature (Kundisch et al., 2014). Moreover, we additionally considered IT services as an intermediate input (Park et al., 2023). To account for economic scales across 51 countries, we normalized all variables by the GDP and used the natural logarithm to correct their skewed distributions.

To estimate the extent to which each input factor contributes to environmental performance, we considered the following model specification:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {E P}{Y}\right) = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \ln \left(\frac {I T}{Y}\right) + \alpha_ {2} \ln \left(\frac {K}{Y}\right) + \alpha_ {3} \ln \left(\frac {L}{Y}\right) + \alpha_ {4} \ln \left(\frac {I S}{Y}\right),
$$

where EP is the environmental outcome of interest (electricity consumption and carbon emissions), Y is output (GDP, or gross value-added), and IT, $K , L ,$ and IS denote IT capital, non-IT capital, labor, and IT services, respectively. Each coefficient corresponds to the elasticity of each factor intensity with respect to the environmental outcome, representing the percentage change in energy or carbon intensity associated with a 1% increase in each factor’s intensity. In order to incorporate the role cloud computing plays in altering the elasticity of IT capital and IT services with respect to energy and carbon intensities, according to our empirical strategy, we interacted the cloud proximity measure with those IT variables; in some analyses, cloud proximity is decomposed into cloud region and cloud neighbor:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln \left(\frac {E P}{Y}\right) = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \ln \left(\frac {I T}{Y}\right) + \alpha_ {2} \ln \left(\frac {K}{Y}\right) + \alpha_ {3} \ln \left(\frac {L}{Y}\right) + \alpha_ {4} \ln \left(\frac {I S}{Y}\right) + \\ \beta_ {1} (C l o u d P r o x i m i t y) + \beta_ {2} \Big [ C l o u d P r o x i m i t y \times \ln \left(\frac {I T}{Y}\right) \Big ] + \\ \beta_ {3} \Big [ C l o u d P r o x i m i t y \times \ln \left(\frac {I S}{Y}\right) \Big ]. \end{array}
$$

Finally, given the longitudinal nature of our dataset, including 51 countries over 22 years, we accounted for country fixed effects $( \sigma _ { i } )$ and year fixed effects $\left( \tau _ { t } \right)$ to control for time-invariant country heterogeneities as well as global time trends in terms of carbon and energy intensities (e.g., Paris Agreement adopted in 2015). In addition, we further control for time-varying covariates $( C o n t r o l s _ { i t } )$ to reflect energy-consuming and carbonemitting economic activities other than production processes where IT and cloud computing play a role. Given that residential and transportation sectors are responsible for a significant portion of nationwide energy consumption and carbon emissions (Lamb et al., 2021), we controlled for population and road densities normalized by the total land area, which should affect the energy and carbon intensities of nations (Sun et al., 2022). Standard errors are clustered by country to correct for serial correlations within a country.

Taken together, our final empirical model for country i in year t is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\ln \left(\frac {E P}{Y}\right) _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \ln \left(\frac {I T}{Y}\right) _ {i t} + \alpha_ {2} \ln \left(\frac {K}{Y}\right) _ {i t} + \alpha_ {3} \ln \left(\frac {L}{Y}\right) _ {i t} +} \\ & {\alpha_ {4} \ln \left(\frac {I S}{Y}\right) _ {i t} + \beta_ {1} (C l o u d P r o x i m i t y) _ {i t} +} \\ & {\beta_ {2} \left[ C l o u d P r o x i m i t y _ {i t} \times \ln \left(\frac {I T}{Y}\right) _ {i t} \right] + \beta_ {3} \left[ C l o u d P r o x i m i t y _ {i t} \times \right.} \\ & {\left. \ln \left(\frac {I S}{Y}\right) _ {i t} \right] + C o n t r o l s _ {i t} + \sigma_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}.} \end{array}
$$

## 4.2 Comparison of Cloud Region Countries and Adjacent Countries

To offer deeper insights into the cross-border nature of cloud computing when it comes to the environmental performance of nations, we decomposed the cloud proximity measure into the binary indicator of cloud region-hosting countries (cloud region) and cloud neighboring distance for countries without cloud regions (cloud neighbor). Table 4 shows that the impacts of IT services on electricity and carbon intensities are still reduced by cloud region availability in the focal countries (indicated by cloud region), whereas it augments the positive contribution of IT capital intensity to electricity and carbon intensities.

By contrast, when we consider both focal countries of cloud regions and adjacent countries in Table 4, Columns 2 and 4, closer access to cloud regions does not necessarily increase the IT capital’s impacts on electricity and carbon intensities in neighboring countries without hosting cloud regions and associated data centers. These findings highlight that the adjacent countries (cloud neighbor) enjoy the benefits of reduced energy and carbon intensities from cloud-based IT services without the adverse environmental impacts of cloud-related IT capital, whereas the countries that host cloud regions (cloud region) experience increased energy and carbon intensities associated with cloud-related IT capital.

Overall, while the improved environmental efficiency from the cloud-based IT services complements the findings of Park et al. (2023) that cloud computing is associated with greater energy efficiency at the industrylevel, this study paints a more complete picture of the dual effects of cloud computing worldwide. Our findings imply that positive or negative environmental impacts do not come from a simple measure of cloud computing but rather come from different types of IT assets and locations of IT resources. In other words, the dual impact of cloud computing helps explain the presence of contrasting evidence on the environmental effects of cloud computing.

## 4.3 Robustness Checks

We conducted several robustness checks to enhance the credibility of our empirical findings by addressing potential endogeneity concerns, including reverse causality and selection bias due to confounding factors. In the interest of brevity, we report all tables for robustness tests in the Appendix.

To address this issue, we reran the main analysis by replacing our main cloud terms with the lagged cloud variables, in line with many previous studies (Hitt & Brynjolfsson, 1996; Mithas et al., 2012). In Table A3 (see Appendix), the results with lagged cloud variables remain consistent with our main findings, confirming that reverse causality is not a significant concern.

Second, we used a matching technique to ensure balance between cloud region countries and neighboring countries in terms of potential confounding factors before the onset of cloud computing, addressing selection bias. Specifically, we matched countries based on IT resources and environmental performance prior to the expansion of cloud computing, which represent a country’s preexisting IT and environmental conditions. To achieve this, we employed inverse probability weighting (IPW), which assigns weights to samples based on the inverse probability of receiving treatment (Li et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022). The probability of being treated, or propensity score, was calculated using IT capital and services and energy and carbon intensities just prior to the cloud computing expansion (i.e., in 2005) because they would have been unaffected by the rollout of cloud regions since 2006 (see Table A2). Table A4 shows the mean differences for the focal variables between cloud and non-cloud (neighboring) region countries. We found that while no significant mean differences in the focal variables were observed in the unweighted samples, the weighted samples also showed no significant differences. This indicates that the treatment and control groups were balanced not only in terms of environmental conditions but also in terms of IT intensities during the pre- and postcloud computing periods. In Table A5, the estimation results using the weighted samples remain consistent, further ruling out the likelihood of confounding effects on the relationship between cloud computing and environmental performance, particularly with regard to preexisting conditions prior to the onset of cloud computing.

Third, one might be concerned that our cloud accessibility measure is merely reflective of the development of ICT infrastructure, which has been shown to affect energy and carbon intensities, as reviewed above. To rule out the confounding effect of ICT infrastructure, we further tested whether it was internet penetration rather than cloud accessibility that altered the environmental performance of IT capital stock and IT services. For internet penetration, we obtained data on the number of people who used the internet from the WB database. In Table A6, the results show that the interaction terms of internet penetration with IT capital stock and IT services are not statistically significant, which thus does not change the impacts of proximity to cloud regions. These findings imply that cloud accessibility and cloud infrastructure play a distinct role from that of ICT infrastructure in shaping the environmental performance of nations, substantiating our main argument on the ecological responsibility of cloud computing.

Fourth, an alternative mechanism of reduced energy and carbon intensities in a country is a sectoral shift in the economic structure from high-carbon, energy-intensive manufacturing sectors to information-intensive service sectors (Stern, 2004), which is likely to coincide with IT investments (Shu, 2001). In addition to the sectoral shift within a country, international trade also contributes to the environmental performance of nations, in that shifts to a low-carbon service-based economy in some countries have largely been achieved by outsourcing high-carbon industrial and heavy manufacturing sectors to less-developed countries (Suri & Chapman, 1998). To account for the influence of sectoral shifts toward service sectors and international trade, on top of the role of IT and cloud computing, we leveraged World Bank data that represents a country’s economic structure in terms of three broad sectors of agriculture, manufacturing, and services. Thus, we considered the GDP share of service sectors as a control variable. In addition, we adopted the international trade variable, measured as the sum of imports and exports of goods and services as a percentage of GDP, available from the WB database. Table A7 demonstrates that our findings remain consistent after controlling for the service sector scale and international trade.

Lastly, one might argue that public awareness and perceptions of environmental issues would affect the environmental performance of countries. Although it is difficult to obtain these nuanced factors across countries over the years, we considered two proxy measures that could arguably represent public attitudes and awareness toward environmental issues: level of educational attainment and degree of exposure to extreme weather. Specifically, educational attainment is known to be associated with the extent of public awareness about environmental issues (Lee et al., 2015). Personal experiences with extreme weather may also shape climate change awareness and opinions (Howe, 2021). For educational attainment and exposure to extreme weather at the country-year level, we obtained data on the enrollment ratio in primary education from WB and data from OECD on exposure to climate-related hazards, including extreme temperature, extreme precipitation, and air pollution. As shown by Table A8, our main findings remain consistent after accounting for these proxy measures for public awareness about environmental issues. Overall, we conclude that our main results are robust, demonstrating a distinct role of cloud computing in the environment even after accounting for the notable potential confounding factors.

## 4.4 Post Hoc Analysis

Given that the primary goal of this work is to shed light on digital responsibility in cloud computing, with a focus on obligations and accountability, we turn our attention to how cloud computing stakeholders can be held accountable for fulfilling their obligations. In doing so, we begin by focusing on existing public policies and regulations, arguing that they may not be sufficient to fully address accountability related to the provision and use of cloud computing—a point that will be further discussed in the next section.

To address the environmental harms caused by firms’ activities, a common approach is the implementation of public policies and regulations that encourage and mandate firms to reduce energy consumption and carbon emissions. A notable example is carbon pricing mechanisms such as carbon taxes and emissions trading systems (ETS), which hold companies accountable for their emissions. Carbon pricing mechanisms have served as central instruments in incentivizing the reduction of carbon emissions across countries (Haites, 2018). Carbon taxes are directly applied to the amount of greenhouse gas emissions, whereas ETS restricts one’s capacity to emit pollution, providing emission allowances that are tradable between polluting entities based on a market-based price.

To explore the effectiveness of carbon pricing mechanisms in mitigating direct pollution from IT capital in data center-hosting countries, we conducted an additional post hoc analysis, reestimating the empirical model separately depending on the presence of carbon taxes or ETS. The main term of interest here is the interaction between cloud region and IT capital, which points to the direct pollution impacts of cloud data centers. We obtained data on the presence of carbon tax and ETS and their associated prices from the Our-World-in-Data database. The results suggest that while the mere presence of a carbon tax and ETS does not moderate the environmental impact of cloud data centers (Table 5), they appear to be effective in mitigating this adverse impact, especially for electricity intensity, when the carbon prices in ETS are sufficiently high to provide economic incentives to firms (Table 6). This early evidence highlights the role of public policies and regulations in addressing the environmental issues associated with cloud computing.

## 5 Discussion

## 5.1 Summary of Key Findings

Our findings reveal that, in general, access to cloud services within a country can make IT services greener, reducing the energy and carbon intensities of nations while not significantly changing the impacts of IT capital. Also, we reveal the cross-country differences in carbon emissions, depending on the presence of cloud regions where cloud service providers operate data centers. In cloud region countries that host data centers, the environmental benefits of cloud-based IT services may come at the expense of increased energy and carbon intensities from the IT capital providing these services. Conversely, neighboring countries seem to benefit from the cloud services that generate pollution in cloud region countries, as evidenced by lower energy and carbon intensities in cloud-based IT services, without compromising their own environmental performance.

It is worth noting that the results do not necessarily mean that investing in cloud-related IT capital, such as data centers (cloud-based IT services), will necessarily lead to increased (reduced) energy and carbon intensities. Instead, cloud computing may require complementary shifts in business activities and how firms leverage IT. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as broader evidence of how cloud computing technologies reshape the way that IT capital and services affect the natural environment, rather than as direct causal effects of cloud computing adoption. This perspective provides valuable insights into the locus of obligations associated with the provision and use of cloud computing, which is further elucidated below.

## 5.2 Implications for Accountability in Cloud Computing

Accountability involves processes and mechanisms that facilitate the fulfillment of obligations by emphasizing that the actors being held accountable are responsible for acting in accordance with accepted standards of behavior and will face sanctions for failing to meet these expectations (Grant & Keohane, 2005). Mashaw (2006) sets forth six fundamental building blocks of accountability regimes: (1) Who is being held to account? (2) To whom is accountability owed? (3) For what are they accountable? (4) What process or mechanism demonstrates accountability? (5) By what standards is accountable behavior to be judged? (6) What are the potential effects when those standards have not been met? In this section, we discuss the implications for accountability in cloud computing based on these building blocks.

First, defining the obligations associated with digital responsibility is an essential first step in addressing the first two questions—who holds responsibility and to whom it is owed. It necessitates identifying the direct emitters and the beneficiaries of pollution resulting from digital technologies. The key findings of this study provide empirical support for different obligations of cloud service providers and users: (1) cloud data center-hosting countries have a direct obligation to address increases in carbon and energy intensities of IT capital within their own territories, and (2) neighboring countries incur indirect obligations for their use of cloud services located in hosting countries, as they benefit from the pollution generated outside their own territories. Although environmental responsibility and obligations are often assigned to direct emitters of pollution, the beneficiaries of the services or activities that generate it, or both (Zhang et al., 2023), our findings support the notion of shared obligations between cloud service providers and users. While our findings are at the country level, we believe that the principles of distinct yet shared obligations between cloud service providers and users also apply at the firm level, given that cloud-using firms rely on third-party providers’ infrastructure and resources to reap benefits without directly assuming responsibility for the environmental harms associated with them.

For the third question—what cloud service providers and users are accountable for—our findings highlight that the increased energy consumption and carbon emissions associated with cloud data centers, as well as the improved environmental performance resulting from cloud-based IT services, provide a basis for attributing accountability at the country level. However, at the firm level, cloud service providers offer and maintain varying levels of cloud infrastructure and resources across different service models, which makes their specific accountability more ambiguous.

To further delineate the accountability between cloud service providers and users, we propose a shared responsibility model of cloud computing to highlight the joint responsibility of both parties to minimize the environmental impacts of cloud computing. The shared responsibility model adopts the principle of “common but differentiated responsibilities” (CDR), which has gained recognition in international laws and agreements (Stone, 2004) and was adopted in the Paris Agreement to emphasize that there is a duty for all countries to take climate action.<sup>6</sup> One of the most notable changes from the Kyoto Protocol to the Paris Agreement is the recognition that climate change is a shared problem, calling for shared responsibility across all countries to set emissions targets (Pan et al., 2017). In addition, attributing shared responsibility for emissions to both providers and users is crucial in order to minimize their joint production of carbon emissions along cross-border IT services supply chains (Caro et al., 2013; Gopalakrishnan, 2022; Gopalakrishnan et al., 2021).

To account for the unique characteristics of digital services and cloud computing when defining shared responsibility, we focus on a loosely coupled, multilayered architecture, which plays a role in enabling digital services (Yoo et al., 2010). We contend that responsibility can be distributed across the layers of digital technology architecture, considering that the underlying layers enable the materialization of digital objects in the upper layers (Yoo et al., 2010; Lyytinen, 2022). Specifically, service providers are responsible for the foundational underlying layers that support the digital services they offer, while service users are accountable for the layers they build on top of the provided layers. This concept of shared responsibility across architectural layers has been applied in cloud security, where it has been established as an industry standard.<sup>7</sup>

The concept then extends to cloud computing, which consists of multiple layers, including hardware, infrastructure, platform, and application layers (Zhang et al., 2010). Cloud service models—infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS), and Software as a Service (SaaS)—are defined by the specific layers of the cloud computing architecture being delivered (Marston et al., 2011). Accordingly, the division of responsibilities between the cloud service provider and the consumer typically falls along a spectrum, depending on the type of cloud service. Figure 3 illustrates how the shared responsibility model can be applied to cloud computing architecture. In the case of IaaS, the service provider manages and bears responsibility for the underlying hardware and infrastructure layers (e.g., computing, storage, and network hardware), while the service user assumes responsibility for everything built on top of them, such as databases and applications. Conversely, for SaaS offerings, the provider assumes responsibility not only for the application layer but also for all underlying layers that support the application. PaaS occupies a middle ground: the provider is responsible for the platform, infrastructure, and hardware layers, while the user takes responsibility for their application implementations built on top of them.

Therefore, we believe future studies can further explore these concepts and investigate strategies to better define and empirically validate shared responsibilities for both parties, ultimately promoting sustainable and responsible cloud computing practices.

## 5.3 Implications for Accountability Mechanisms

The last three questions of accountability regard mechanisms and processes to enforce obligations and accountability—specifically: What processes or mechanisms demonstrate accountability? By what standards is accountable behavior judged? And what are the potential consequences when those standards are not met? — are beyond the scope of this study to explore in depth. Keeping that in mind, we explore potential accountability mechanisms here to guide the discourse on digital responsibility in cloud computing.

Market accountability mechanisms: Cloud service vendors are held accountable to consumers and investors through market mechanisms (Grant & Keohane, 2005; Mashaw, 2006). Firms may be judged by consumers’ preference for eco-friendly products and services, as well as their preference for sustainable businesses. Institutional investors are increasingly factoring corporate social responsibility into their investment decisions. For cloud service markets, a market-based accountability mechanism grounded in competitive contracts can be applied between cloud service providers and users. When shared responsibility is explicitly addressed in service contracts, firms are more likely to select vendors who offer feasible methods for implementing this responsibility; conversely, noncompliance may result in contract termination or renegotiation within the market.

Despite the substantial potential of the market accountability mechanism, however, we argue that it is not yet as effective in the cloud computing context, as environmental issues related to cloud computing currently have low salience and are less likely to attract public attention, particularly regarding the responsibility of cloud service users. Mytton (2020) highlights a significant limitation in the current carbon reporting system regarding cloud computing’s carbon emissions by cautioning that “the emissions are aggregated into the global emissions reporting by the large cloud vendors, which can result in individual corporation’s emissions being hidden when they are moved to the cloud” (p. 701). Our study offers practical insights into the need for public scrutiny of the environmental responsibility of cloud computing, as well as the importance of carbon reporting systems that provide relevant information to investors and consumers, which are essential for enabling market accountability.

Regulations as accountability mechanisms: Regulations have garnered attention among IS scholars for their role in ensuring the effective use of emerging technologies (Butler et al., 2023). In the post hoc analysis, we demonstrated that carbon pricing regulations—where emission targets and associated prices serve as standards of appraisal, and polluters may forfeit profits in the event of noncompliance—can effectively mitigate environmental harms in countries that host cloud data centers. However, they primarily address the direct obligations of cloud service providers while largely overlooking the indirect responsibilities of cloud service users. Thus, more comprehensive regulations may be necessary as a legitimate accountability mechanism to ensure that the cloud computing value chain fully realizes its potential in sustainability and responsibility.

Given the cross-border nature of cloud computing, as highlighted in our empirical findings, international laws and agreements may be necessary to enforce the accountability of neighboring countries toward data center-hosting countries. In particular, as internet connectivity and data center access are interlinked across multiple countries, supranational institutions could play a key role in coordinating shared responsibility among nations, extending beyond bilateral agreements between individual countries. To the best of our knowledge, no major international laws or agreements addressed the environmental impacts of data centers and networked digital services during the years and countries in our study. The most relevant international agreement regarding environmental impacts along supply chains is the European Union’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), which came into effect for large European companies in 2024 (Primec & Belak, 2022). The CSRD mandates reporting on greenhouse gas emissions across all scopes, with direct emissions from cloud service providers classified as Scope 1 or 2, with indirect contributions from cloud service users falling under Scope 3. Although such directives enhance transparency by making the carbon footprints of both cloud service providers and users visible, they do not allocate responsibility between them. Instead, they result in “double counting,” as emissions classified as Scope 1 or 2 for providers are also reported as Scope 3 emissions for users (Caro et al., 2013).

In this regard, we believe that an effective accountability mechanism for digital responsibility in cloud computing should not only enhance carbon footprint transparency through regulations such as CSRD but also allocate responsibility along the cloud computing value chain. The proposed shared responsibility model complements existing regulations by providing a framework for this allocation. We anticipate that it can serve as a starting point for research focused on regulations as an accountability mechanism in the context of cloud computing, advancing our understanding of digital responsibility in an era where digital resources are increasingly moving to the cloud. Additionally, future research on digital responsibility could explore how the potential for sanctions resulting from noncompliance with accountability mechanisms can motivate organizations to adopt more innovative approaches to managing the environmental impacts of digital services and strategies (Bovens, 2010).

## 5.4 Contributions to the Literature

Our study makes significant scholarly contributions by synthesizing and thereby contributing to two key research streams in the IS literature: green IT/IS and digital responsibility. First, we contribute to the green IT/IS literature by illustrating and explaining how cloud computing impacts the relationship of IT to environmental performance across countries. In keeping with the strand of IS literature on the role of IT in national economies (Dedrick et al., 2013; Dewan & Kraemer, 2000) and societies (Ganju et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2018), our findings echo the potential role of cloud computing, especially cloud-based IT services, in reducing energy and carbon intensities of national economies. Park et al. (2023) found that cloud computing can lead to net energy savings in the US economy. Our cross-country evidence, including several measures of national environment and policies, complements and extends the scope of extant knowledge. We further suggest that the differential impacts of cloud-based IT capital and IT services may help reconcile the contrasting evidence from prior literature of IT impacts on the environment.

Second, this study offers new insights into the digital responsibility of networked IT services, with a particular focus on cloud computing. Our findings highlight unique issues regarding the ecological responsibility of cloud computing by disentangling its dual roles and distinguishing between harmful direct impacts in provider countries and benefits enjoyed in user countries. While a few studies have conceptually identified challenges in accounting for the environmental impacts of IT outsourcing (Babin & Nicholson, 2011) and cloud computing (Mytton, 2020), to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to provide empirical evidence demonstrating how the ecological responsibility of cloud computing can extend across national borders.

This study further advances the theory of digital responsibility, particularly its focus on accountability and obligation, within the context of cloud computing. Our findings indicate that both cloud-hosting and cloud-using countries share an obligation to maximize the beneficial outcomes of cloud computing while minimizing its potential harmful environmental effects. Informed by the empirical findings, we propose a shared responsibility model that allocates accountability between service providers and users. Some researchers have paid attention to the producers or service providers of technologies whose characteristics raise potential ethical challenges, arguing that they should be held responsible for the harmful outcomes of these technologies (Moore & Tambini, 2018). Others have emphasized the responsibility of users who decide how to use those technologies and to what ends (Benbunan-Fich et al., 2020). However, the attribution of responsibility to either party becomes more ambiguous in digital services, where both service providers and users contribute. The shared responsibility model takes into account the unique characteristics of cloud-based digital services, grounded in the multilayered architecture of digital technologies. Given that the standards and processes for demonstrating and assessing accountability are crucial for implementing effective accountability mechanisms, we believe this shared responsibility model can advance the discourse on cloud computing responsibility and highlight directions for future research. Overall, we believe that our study provides a robust theoretical foundation for researchers exploring digital responsibility in the context of environmental sustainability, particularly in the domain of cross-national networked IT services.

## 5.5 Limitations and Future Research Opportunities

There are several limitations in this study, which remain to be addressed in future research. First, we took advantage of geographical distances from cloud regions to proxy accessibility to global public cloud services, but our empirical strategy and available data did not allow us to zoom into international trades of cloud services between countries. Future research might scrutinize the ecological responsibility of cross-border cloud computing if bilateral trade data on cloud services between two countries are available.

Second, while different types of cloud services can be assumed to entail differential responsibility allocations between service providers and customers based on the shared responsibility model, this study could not empirically decompose these services to assess their varying environmental impacts due to data limitations. We recommend that future research build on the shared responsibility model and investigate the implications of different types of cloud services on environmental outcomes in both the focal and adjacent countries of cloud regions. As such, leveraging the shared responsibility model in this context should be regarded as a conceptual starting point for future research opportunities, rather than a definitive conclusion from our study.

Third, we suggest that the differential but shared obligations to cloud data center-hosting countries and adjacent countries, informed by our country-level empirical findings, can extend to the firm level. While it is consistent with the concept of “scale variance” (Rodrigues et al., 2006) to assume that the environmental performance of cloud computing at an aggregated organizational level would reflect that of a country, we could not account for other economic agents (e.g., the public sector and households) who also use cloud services and influence a country’s overall environmental footprint. As a result, our argument is limited by the simplified distinction between firm and country levels. Future research should incorporate a broader range of economic stakeholders across the entire economy. Finally, while a series of robustness tests helped to address potential concerns about reverse causality and selection bias related to environmental conditions and IT intensities, we cannot rule out all possibilities of endogeneity at the country level, as various factors (e.g., energy policies and macroeconomic conditions) likely influence the environmental performance of nations. Therefore, we recommend that future research explore moderating factors affecting the environmental performance of cloud computing on both the provider and user sides.

## 5.6 Concluding Remarks

Cloud computing has played a crucial role in digital transformation and increasingly serves as the backbone of the digital economy. At the same time, these dataintensive and cloud-based IT services build upon largescale data center facilities, which consume a huge amount of energy and take a growing toll on the planet. However, the borderless nature of cloud computing makes the environmental consequences of increasing use of cloud computing opaque. This study takes an initial step toward establishing the digital responsibility of cloud computing by highlighting the obligations and accountability of cloud computing stakeholders regarding environmental impacts.

In conclusion, this study asserts that responsible cloud computing has the potential to harmonize the value creation driven by leveraging cloud-powered digital technologies, including artificial intelligence, with the principles of environmental sustainability. Building on our empirical findings, which highlight the shared obligations between cloud service providers and users, we propose the shared responsibility model as a foundation for future research on accountable and responsible cloud computing. We hope that our research can provide a theoretical lens and serve as empirical evidence to understand the digital responsibility of networked IT services toward sustainability and spur further research on how to manage and harness cloud computing in tackling global climate issues in a digitally interconnected world.

## About the Authors

Jooho Kim is an assistant professor of computer information systems at Baruch College, City University of New York. His research interests include sharing economy, online community, and green IT/IS. Prior to joining Baruch College, he completed his PhD degree in information systems, The Paul Merage School of Business at University of California, Irvine. His work has been published in MIS Quarterly and Journal of the Association for Information Systems.

Jiyong Park is an assistant professor of management information systems at the Terry College of Business, University of Georgia. He earned his PhD from the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST). His research focuses on green IT/IS, the societal impacts of digital platforms, and IT value. His work has been published in leading journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, and Journal of the Association for Information Systems.

Jason Dedrick is professor emeritus at the School of Information Studies at Syracuse University. His research interests include adoption and impacts of smart grid technologies, cybersecurity and privacy issues associated with distributed energy markets, global value chains in the electronics industry, and the economic and organizational impacts of information technologies. His work has been supported by grants from the US National Science Foundation and the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation. He holds a PhD in management from the University of California, Irvine.
