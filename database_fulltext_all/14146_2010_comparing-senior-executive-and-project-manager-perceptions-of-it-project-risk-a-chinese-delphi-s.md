# Comparing senior executive and project manager perceptions of IT project risk: a Chinese Delphi study

Shan Liu,\* Jinlong Zhang,\* Mark Keil<sup>†</sup> & Tao Chen<sup>‡</sup>

\*School of Management, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, Hubei, China, email: shan.l.china@gmail.com; jlzhang@mail.hust.edu.cn, <sup>†</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, USA, email: mkeil@gsu.edu, and <sup>‡</sup>College of Public Administration, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, Hubei, China, email: chentao15@163.com

Abstract. The success rate for information technology (IT) projects continues to be low. With an increasing number of IT projects in developing countries such as China, it is important to understand the risks they are experiencing on IT projects. To date, there has been little research documenting Asian perceptions of IT project risk. In this research, we examine the risks identified by Chinese senior executives (SEs) and project managers (PMs), and compare these two groups. The importance of top management support in IT projects is well documented. Prior research has shown that from the perspective of IT PMs, lack of support from SEs is the number one risk in IT projects. Surprisingly, senior executives’ perceptions towards IT project risk have never been systematically examined. One reason why lack of support from senior executives continues to represent a major risk may be that senior executives themselves do not realize the critical role that they can play in helping to deliver successful projects. In this study, we use the Delphi method to compare the risk perceptions of senior executives and project managers. By comparing risk factors selected by each group, zones of concordance and discordance are identified. In terms of perceived importance ascribed to risk factors, PMs tend to focus on lower-level risks with particular emphasis on risks associated with requirements and user involvement, whereas SEs tend to focus on higher level risks such as those risks involving politics, organization structure, process, and culture. Finally, approaches for dealing with risk factors that are seen as important by both SEs and PMs are provided.

Keywords: Chinese Delphi study, IT project risk, project manager, risk perception, senior executive

## INTRODUCTION

Information technology (IT) continues to be of critical importance to modern business organizations and in 2001 the number of IT projects undertaken in the USA alone crossed the 500 000 threshold (Tesch et al., 2007). However, the success rate for such projects remains unacceptably low. The Standish Group’s 2006 ‘CHAOS’ study update, incorporating data from several thousand IT projects, reveals that only 35% of IT projects started in 2006 were categorized as successful, 19% were judged to be outright failures, and the remaining 46% were completed over-budget, behind schedule or failed in some way to meet user requirements (Rubinstein, 2007). A recent paper by Sauer et al. (2007), however, suggests that the failure rates may be lower than previously believed. While there is some uncertainty around the actual IT project failure rates in different parts of the world, there is general agreement that risk management can help to reduce the incidence of failure.

Risk management advocates suggest that the first step in the risk management process is to identify risk factors (Charette, 1989; Jones, 1994). Consistent with Schmidt et al. (2001), a risk factor is defined here as a condition that can present a serious threat to the successful completion of an IT project. Based on prior research (Keil et al., 2002), it is important to note that various stakeholders may hold different perceptions of risk. Previous attempts to identify IT project risk factors have typically been limited to a single stakeholder group, such as project managers (PMs) (Moynihan, 1997; Schmidt et al., 2001). Schmidt et al. (2001, p. 29) suggest the need for further research in this important area because ‘it is quite possible that different stakeholders will have divergent opinions regarding what the risk factors are, as well as their relative importance’. Surprisingly, key stakeholders such as senior executives (SEs) are rarely involved in assessing IT project risks. In some instances, this can give rise to serious problems. As Nolan & McFarlan (2005, p. 98) point out, ‘a lack of board oversight for IT activities is dangerous; it puts the firm at risk in the same way that failing to audit its books would’.

This paper builds on prior research by examining IT project risk from the SE perspective and comparing this to the PM perspective. We argue that understanding the SE perspective is critica because it is widely recognized that senior management commitment is a key ingredient to producing successful IT project outcomes. Further, comparing and contrasting the SE and PM perspectives on risk may yield insights into how PMs can forge better relationships with SEs. This would seem to be important, as the literature suggests that one of the ways in which PMs can improve project performance is to develop strong relationships with SEs (Simonsen, 2007).

Prior research has shown that the perception of IT project risks can also vary from one country to another (Schmidt et al., 2001). In this paper, we examine the risks identified by Chinese SEs and PMs and compare these two groups. In the case of China, IT plays an increasingly important role in the country’s economic growth. In terms of global outsourcing, the China offshore software market size reached CNY 3.927 billion in revenues for the first quarter of 2008, an increase of 25.3% compared with the first quarter of 2007 (The Analysis International Organization, 2008). Moreover, The Chinese Computer World Research Organization (2005) reported that there was CNY 286.5 billion of IT investments in China alone in 2004.

Unfortunately, little is known about the risks associated with undertaking IT projects in China. Compared with European and North American countries, China has a relatively short history of using information technology and managing IT projects. In addition, Chinese companies are operating in a more dynamic business environment. With respect to Hofstede’s (2001) five dimensions of culture, China is significantly different from European and North American countries in terms of power distance, individualism, and long-term orientation (LTO), as Chinese culture is noted for having high degrees of power distance and collectivism, as well as a LTO. These cultural and country-specific characteristics may have an impact on manager perceptions of risk (Straub & Welke, 1998). Therefore, in this study, we seek to understand the risks associated with IT projects from the Chinese SE’s perspective as well as the PM’s perspective.

The guiding research questions that we sought to address in this study are:

1 What are the top-ranked risk factors as perceived by Chinese SEs and PMs?

2 What are the areas of agreement and disagreement between SEs’ and PMs’ perspectives on IT project risk?

3 On the areas of agreement, what are some approaches that can be used to deal with the risk factors?

In this paper, we report the results of a Delphi study designed to examine the IT project risk perceptions of both SEs and PMs in China. Zones of concordance and discordance between these two stakeholders are mapped for comparison purposes. Based on the data collected, we analyse and discuss how SEs and PMs perceive risk and how the differences in their perceptions can be explained. We also provide some approaches for dealing with risk factors that are seen as important by both SEs and PMs.

The results of the study suggest that while there are some similarities in the risk factors that SEs and PMs perceive as most deserving of their attention, there are also important differ ences in their perceptions as well. It is also suggested that PMs will benefit from a bette understanding of SEs risk perceptions. Specifically, we posit that when PMs understand the SEs’ perspective they will be more likely to garner strong support from SEs and the information systems they manage will be more widely accepted by users.

## BACKGROUND

IT project risk management is widely regarded as a means of improving the success rate on IT projects (Schmidt et al., 2001). Prior literature on IT project risk has focused on six areas: (1) identifying risk factors and components of risk (McFarlan, 1981; Barki et al., 1993; Neumann, 1995; Keil et al., 1998; Lyytinen et al., 1998; Ropponen & Lyytinen, 2000; Schmidt et al., 2001; Wallace et al., 2004); (2) methods and techniques for risk assessment (Tiwana & Keil, 2004; Cuellar & Gallivan, 2006; Costa et al., 2007); (3) frameworks for risk management (Boehm, 1991; Fairley, 1994; Powell & Klein, 1996); (4) the relationship between risk and proiect performance (Nidumolu. 1995: Jiang & Klein. 1999: Na et al.. 2004: Wallace et al.

2004; Han & Huang, 2007; Na et al., 2007); (5) exploring risk mitigation strategies to improve project performance (Alter & Ginzberg, 1978; Keil et al., 1998; Barki et al., 2001; Bahli & Rivard, 2003; Jiang & Klein, 2004; Iversen et al., 2004; Taylor, 2006); and (6) understanding risk perceptions (Keil et al., 2002; Camprieu et al., 2007; Keil et al., 2008).

## The project manager perspective on risk

Numerous risks have been identified in the information systems (IS) literature as contributing factors in IT project failures and a variety of risk checklists have been developed. Boehm (1991) presented a checklist of the top 10 risks associated with software projects. Barki et al. (1993) developed a list of 35 risk variables from the literature and organized them into five categories (technological newness, application size, expertise, application complexity and organizational environment) based on a survey of 120 ongoing software projects from 75 organizations. In 1993, the Software Engineering Institution (SEI) created a ‘taxonomy based risk identification’ tool which is a questionnaire with 194 items (Carr et al., 1993). Since risks are viewed as dynamically changing in different periods, the SEI perspective is that they should be periodically and systematically assessed and controlled. Moynihan (1997) showed some gaps in Barki et al. (1993) list and presented 113 risk factors which are classified into 22 themes based on a survey of 14 Irish PMs. The purpose of these various risk checklists is to provide a basis for identifying risk factors at the outset of or during the course of a project.

In addition to the risk checklists, several conceptual frameworks have been developed to identify and explain different types of IT project risk. McFarlan (1981) introduced a ${ 2 \times 2 }$ grid that focuses on degree of structure and whether the project involves technology that is new to the organization undertaking the project. Keil et al. (1998) mapped different types of risk into a ${ 2 \times 2 }$ grid in which the four quadrants were: customer mandate, scope and requirements, environment and execution. Wallace et al. (2004) identified six dimensions of software projec risk: team, organizational environment, requirements, planning and control, user and complexity. In order to measure these dimensions, a survey instrument was designed and validated using a sample of over 500 PMs. Some other research has focused on IT project risks arising outside the development process (Keen, 1991; Kemerer & Sosa, 1991; Erdogmus, 2002; Kumar, 2002). The argument is that even if IT project development risks are controlled, environmental uncertainty outside the organization (e.g. competitive threats, market shifts and regulatory changes) can still pose threats to the project and limit the expected benefits. This perspective may be especially relevant to strategic IT applications (Benaroch, 2002).

However, several lists of IT project risk factors have been published based on the experience of PMs (Moynihan, 1997; Schmidt et al., 2001; Mursu et al., 2003), and some lists have even focused on more specialized areas such as e-commerce projects (Ngai & Wat, 2005; Wat et al., 2005). Among these efforts, Schmidt et al. (2001) provide what appears to be the most comprehensive list of risk factors ranked in order of importance from the perspective of the PM. To create an updated ranked list of risk factors and to investigate the effect of culture, Schmidt et al. (2001) conducted a rigorous ‘ranking-type’ Delphi survey in three different settings: Hong Kong, Finland and the USA to produce a rank-ordered list of risk factors. They recruited experienced PMs in each cultural setting and developed a list of 53 risk factors. They were then able to compare the similarities and differences in the risk rankings obtained in the different cultural settings. The set of risk factors that came from each panel of IT PMs will be used late as a comparison base for examining our sample of Chinese PMs. The full list of risk factors will also be compared with the list of risk factors generated in our study for examining the cultura difference.

## Beyond a single view of risk

While IT PMs are in a good position to respond to questions dealing with project risk, there are other stakeholders who play important roles in IT projects, such as users and SEs. Since these different stakeholders might be impacted in different ways by benefits and risks (Sabherwal, 2003; McManus, 2004), it is important to understand their perceptions of IT project risks. In some cases, IT projects can fail because some stakeholders or ‘hidden’ decision makers are overlooked in the project (Schneider & Sarker, 2005). Research has suggested that one common characteristic of unsuccessful IT projects is that planning is conducted to some degree in secret and without sufficient input from stakeholders (McManus, 2004). Different stakeholders may also hold different expectations regarding what constitutes a successful or unsuccessful project. Thus, SEs may have very different perceptions from PMs regarding the risks associated with IT projects. It is important to understand these differences so that they can be appropriately managed.

Fortunately, the IS literature recognizes the significance of integrating multiple stakeholders perspectives, and there has been some limited attempt to do this in the area of IT project risk. Keil et al. (2002) conducted a Delphi study, revealing that PMs and users have different perceptions of risk factors. They also identified zones of concordance and discordance that must be reconciled, providing a basis for more thorough analysis of risk in IT projects. Addison (2003) explored e-commerce project development risks through a Delphi survey which included the viewpoints of developers, PMs, clients/users and academics. The results sug gested that different stakeholders would apply different rankings to the same set of risk factors

Although prior work on the risk perceptions of IT PMs and users is a contribution to our understanding and is a prerequisite for managing risk, our understanding of risk remains incomplete. First, the risk perceptions of other stakeholders, particularly SEs who have much influence on the project, have never been examined. We make this claim based on our literature search. We used the keywords: senior executive, top manager, senior manager and top management together with risk to search for papers before August 6, 2008 from many databases including ABI/INFORM Complete (at ProQuest), ScienceDirect, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar, Computer Source and Business Source Complete (at EBSCOhost). We downloaded papers with titles and abstracts relating to IT project risk perceptions but could find no papers that empirically examined SEs’ perceptions of IT project risk. Second, though Keil et al. (2002) suggest the importance of understanding the perceptions of different stakeholders, more work is needed to develop strategies for dealing with those risks agreed by different stakeholders.

The need to involve various stakeholders in IT projects is well recognized and the literature on user involvement and the importance of top management support attest to the importance of stakeholder involvement. McManus (2004) suggests that stakeholder analysis should focus on the stakeholder’s ability to influence behavior and should account for the stakeholder’s power and legitimacy. There is no doubt that SEs wield tremendous power and that they can influence behaviors. To improve project performance, strong relationships with SEs should be established (Simonsen, 2007). Thus, one can reasonably expect that how SEs perceive the risks associated with IT projects will influence how they approach IT projects, which in turn wil likely affect project outcomes.

SEs are often key decision makers that operate behind the scene to marshal key resources that projects need in order to be successful. Lack of support from SEs has been identified as the number one risk in IT projects from the perspective of IT PMs (Smith et al., 2006). One reason why lack of support from SEs continues to represent a major risk may be that SEs themselves do not realize the critical role that they can play in helping to deliver successful projects. Surprisingly, however, SE’s perceptions towards IT project risk have not been examined. This study seeks to remedy this gap in our understanding by examining the SE perspective on IT project risk and comparing it to the PM perspective. The potential for conflicts among stakeholders will be raised if the risk perceptions of SEs and PMs are fundamentally different. Thus, the perception of SEs towards risk in the development process may yield important insights into mitigating risks.

## METHODOLOGY

Our goal was to identify important IT project risk factors from the perspectives of both SEs and PMs. While a number of approaches could have been used to do this (e.g. interviews, cognitive mapping, and survey questionnaire), we wanted a technique that did not constrain us to a fixed set of a priori risk factors (as would be the case in a survey questionnaire), while also allowing us to not only identify these factors, but also to rank them. Consistent with prior studies in this area (Schmidt et al., 2001; Keil et al., 2002; Addison, 2003; Mursu et al., 2003), we therefore chose the Delphi method for identifying and ranking risk factors. While the Delphi approach involves a limited sample and does not lend itself to sophisticated quantitative analysis, it is a structured, iterative, process to achieve group consensus among a panel of experts and, for that reason, it was ideally suited for our study (Keil et al., 2002). In the Delphi process, a panel of domain experts is assembled, and each panellist is asked to respond to a particular question or task. The Delphi approach allows researchers to collect and aggregate individual responses and then present this back to the group. Individuals are then able to change their responses and the cycle is repeated until some level of consensus is reached within the group. Several variations of the Delphi method have been employed both within and outside the field of information systems (Dalkey & Helmer, 1963: Nevo & Chan. 2007).

## Composition of panels

In this study, SE is defined as a top-level organizational member who is a key decision maker in the business and who cares about whether the IT project can produce benefit for the organization. They provide executive leadership and sometimes attend important project meetings. They are also able to provide sponsorship for the project. We recruited a total of 64 SEs and PMs in China to serve on one of two expert panels. The first panel was composed of 30 SEs who had three or more years of experience participating in IT projects in their organizations, including five chief executive officers, 10 vice presidents and 15 SEs of majo functional areas. The second panel was composed of 34 IT PMs with two or more years of experience leading large projects (i.e. all over CNY 100 000). The panellists worked in different corporations spanning a wide range of industries and the panel sizes were consistent with what is suggested in the literature (Addison, 2003). Table 1 shows descriptive statistics for both panels.

## Data collection

This study followed a modified version of the Delphi method developed by Schmidt (1997) to measure the relative importance of IT project risk factors, as shown in Figure 1. Data were collected using both paper-based questionnaires and electronic mail. Each panellist received both regular mail and email versions of the material and was encouraged to respond in whichever format he or she preferred. Data collection took 6 months and involved fou sequential phases.

In phase 1, a brainstorming round was conducted to produce as many items as possible from both panels. Each panellist was asked to submit at least five risk factors and to provide short descriptions of each factor. Exact duplicates were dropped. Then, the combined list o unique risk factors was categorized based on established risk dimensions found in the literature (see Appendix A). This information was returned to the panellists for corrections, additions and further validation to assure that each factor had a clear definition. This process produced a list of 57 grouped risk factors.

In phase 2, we separated the two panels, allowing each to independently narrow down the list of risk factors. Panellists were then asked to select the 10 most important risk factors from a grouped list of 57 risk factors derived in phase 1. The panellists were also given the opportunity to provide open-ended feedback on any risks viewed as important but not included in the initial list. None of the panellists created additional risks, suggesting that the initial lis was viewed as a complete one by the panellists. At the end of this phase, 14 risk factors were selected by a majority of PMs and 13 risk factors were retained by a majority of SEs.

In phase 3, each panellist was presented with an ordered list of 14 or 13 risk factors depending on which panel they belonged to. The ordering of the list was based on the percentage of panellists who selected each factor in phase 2. Some researchers suggest that an ordered list is more appropriate than a randomized list (Turoff & Hiltz, 1996; Keil et al., 2002), because the group can receive consistent feedback at each stage. Presenting randomized risk factors here would also not take advantage of the feedback that the group provided in phase 2 (Keil et al., 2002). The panellists were asked to rank and rate each risk factor on a 5-point Likert scale by its relative importance. In addition, each panellist was asked to provide an open-ended explanation for the selection of his/her top ranked factor. At the end of phase 3, both mean rank and rating for each factor were computed, and the degree of consensus was determined by calculating Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) (Schmidt, 1997). As suggested by Schmidt et al. (2001), the ranking rounds stopped when either (1) the coefficient of concordance indicated a strong consensus (W > 0.70), or (2) the level of consensus for the panel levelled off in two successive rounds.

In phase 4, we extend the process described by Schmidt et al. (2001) to surface approaches for dealing with the identified risks. In this phase, we presented both ranked lists of risk factors to a subgroup of 10 panellists which included six PMs and four SEs. Each member of this subgroup was asked to create strategies or approaches to deal with the risk factors embedded in both ranked lists. After combining similar expressions of the same underlying approach and removing duplicates, a set of 21 unique approaches were identified.

In a high power distance culture, those stakeholders with high power will view themselves as decision makers and tend to ignore the opinions of subordinates (Hofstede, 2001). In such environments, stakeholders with less power will communicate with their superiors only reluctantly and ineffectively. As a result, we can expect that communication problems wil ensue and that this may lead to conflicts in defining system requirements.

According to Hofstede (2001), individuals in a low uncertainty culture may welcome innovations but may not be willing to put enough energy into their application. Moreover, people may exhibit scepticism towards technological solutions (Hofstede, 2001). Therefore, users are more likely to resist technological change and hold negative attitudes towards IT initiatives. As a result, we can expect that there will be conflicts between users and developers, as the latte will be viewed as pushing technology change.

In a collectivist society, the personal relationship prevails over the task and organization (Hofstede, 2001). One rationale for this is that in a highly collectivistic culture, the responsibility for a project rests with the team, and thus at the level of the individual, diffusion of responsibility may set in.

Managers operating in a LTO culture have a tendency to think more about the future of the project and its impacts on the organization. They may be concerned about whether the system will impact organizational structure and business processes. They will also tend to be more attuned to the long-term business value associated with implementing IT applications. Thus, they will naturally be concerned with establishing success criteria for IT projects so tha business value can be realized.

In addition to the aforementioned cultural dimensions, the short history of using IT and managing IT projects coupled with the dynamic business environment in China may explain why certain risks are more salient there, as compared with Hong Kong, Finland and the USA Specifically, the short history of using IT in China may translate to a greater prevalence of technical risks (e.g. large number of links to other systems, high level of technical complexity, immature technology). Finally, given the dynamic business environment, Chinese managers appear to be more attuned to the risk of market changes as well as unanticipated reactions from other organizations.

User satisfaction is an important criterion for evaluating IT success, but SEs seem to ignore or downplay the important role of the user. Here a significant divergence is seen between SEs and PMs which can explain why IT PMs sometimes feel that top management commitment is lacking and why so many IT projects end in failure.

## Differences in how PMs and SEs appear to perceive risks

It seems that PMs tend to focus on lower-level risks (i.e. those involving the technology, requirements and user involvement), while SEs appear to focus on higher-level risks (i.e. those involving politics, organization structure, process and culture) Based on the qualitative data collected from our panellists, we classified the 14 risk factors ranked by PMs and the 13 risk factors ranked by SEs (Table 8) into lower and higher-level risks, as shown in Table 9.

As can be seen in Table 9, 10 of 14 risk factors selected by PMs were lower-level risks, while the other 4 risk factors selected by PMs were higher-level risks. This result suggests that PMs do indeed focus more of their attention on lower-level risks with particular emphasis on requirement related risks. In contrast, 7 of 13 risk factors selected by SEs were higher-level risks. This result suggests that SEs focus their attention on both lower and higher-level risks. It is particularly interesting to note that the SEs ascribe greater importance to the higher-level risk factors which received an average ranking of 4.4, as compared to the lower-level risk factors which received an average ranking of 10.

While prior research provides prescriptive advice from the PM’s perspective, our study provides insights from both the PM and the SE perspec tive. ## CONCLUSIONS AND IMPLICATIONS

IT projects are influenced by a variety of stakeholders who may hold different views concerning the risk profile associated with a project. This study used the Delphi method to capture the risk perspectives of two important stakeholders: SEs and PMs. Both SEs and PMs were asked to independently rank what they viewed to be the most important risk factors. By comparing each group’s ranked list of risk factors, we identified the zone of concordance representing areas of agreement on risk factors, as well as areas of disagreement. Tactics that enable PMs and SEs to deal with the agreed upon risks were elicited from a subgroup of our panellists.

In our study, a comprehensive list of 57 risk factors were identified by both SEs and PMs, adding 18 new risk items that did not surface in a previous study that had considered only PMs (Schmidt et al., 2001). Six country specific characteristics are provided that may explain these 18 new risk factors: (1) high power distance, (2) low UA, (3) collectivism, (4) LTO culture; (5) relatively short history of using information technology and managing IT projects, and (6) dynamic business environment. Our results also indicated that there are 14 risk factors that are judged important by PMs and 13 risk factors judged to be important by SEs. However, there were seven factors that both SEs and PMs agreed were important. Seven risk factors that were ranked by PMs were not judged important by SEs and six risk factors selected by SEs, were not judged important by PMs. Thus, there are similarities as well as differences in the perceptions of PMs and SEs regarding IT project risk. In particular, PMs focus on lower-level risks such as those associated with technology, requirements and users, whereas SEs focus on higher-level risks such as those involving politics, organization structure, process and culture. Our findings also suggest that nine approaches can be taken by SEs and 15 approaches can be taken by PMs for dealing with common set of risk factors that are agreed to be important.

Before turning to the implications of our study, it is important to point out its limitations. First, as is the case with all Delphi studies, the expert panels are not intended to be statistically representative. However, the PMs in our sample did have at least two years of experience leading IT projects and both panels were relatively large and diverse and included PMs and SEs working for companies in a wide range of industries with both medium and large infor mation systems departments.

Second, in phase 4, the approaches for dealing with risk factors agreed to be important by both SEs and PMs were created based on input from a relatively small subgroup consisting of 10 panellists. It is possible that more panellists participating in phase 4 might have led to additional insights for dealing with risk factors in zone of concordance.

While this is the first study of its kind to examine IT risks in China, the fact that we focus on only one country can be seen as a limitation as well. It is difficult to know the extent to which our findings generalize to other countries throughout the world. Thus, SEs and IT PMs in different countries/cultures may have different perceptions.

## Implications for research

Much of the prior research on IT project risk adopts the perspective of the PM. While a handful of studies (Keil et al., 2002; Addison, 2003) have begun to examine IT project risk from the perspective of other stakeholders such as users, our research explored the similarities and differences in perceptions between PMs and SEs. The study provides further evidence that different stakeholders do indeed have different risk perceptions (Keil et al., 2002). Further research is needed to examine the perceptions of other stakeholders such as project sponsors, functional managers, auditors and outside vendors. An examination of the vendor perspective would be especially valuable given that more and more projects are being outsourced or offshored.

A second extension to our research would be to examine if our approaches for dealing with risk factors agreed to be important by both SEs and PMs have significant impact on mitigating IT project risk. Many of the existing approaches for mitigating risks in IT projects focus only on what the PM and the development team can do (Alter & Ginzberg, 1978; Keil et al., 1998). However, our study suggests that risk mitigation efforts may be more effective when they are broadened to include the perspectives of other stakeholders such as SEs.

A third extension would be to examine how the perceived risk profile associated with a project changes over time as the project moves from one stage to another during its life cycle. In particular, it would be interesting to examine if the perceptions of SEs and PMs converge or diverge during the course of a project.

## Implications for practice

This study also has several implications for practitioners. For PMs, a key finding is that PMs tend to perceive lower-level risks, while SEs appear to focus on higher-level risks. PMs should be sensitive to this and should keep SEs informed concerning risks that go beyond the usua concerns relating to technology, requirements and users. Additionally, while the literature emphasizes the importance of meeting users’ needs, PMs should be careful not to ignore the needs of SEs. While it takes many dissatisfied users to bring down a project, it only requires one dissatisfied SE to cancel a project. By revealing the perceptions of SEs, this study provides useful input to PMs, allowing them to better satisfy the needs of SEs, and potentially garnering further support from them.

For SEs, the study highlights the differences in the roles and responsibilities of PMs and SEs. If SEs are willing to expend political capital and provide greater support for the project, one of the key risks that often results in failure–lack of top management support – can be avoided. SEs should also recognize the importance of user acceptance in IT projects. To a large degree, users will follow the cues and incentives provided by SEs in deciding whether or not to embrace a particular system. SEs have the power to reward and drive users and are therefore in a unique position to lend support to organization change processes.

Our country specific findings relating to China should be of particular interest to practitioners from other countries outsourcing work to China. For this audience, the study provides usefu insights that may help them to manage projects successfully in such a culture.

One of the more surprising findings of this study was that SEs (and not just PMs) selected ‘lack of top management commitment’ as the most important risk factor. It is perplexing that this risk factor continues to plague IT projects even though SEs openly acknowledge its importance. One explanation is that SEs are unwilling to provide the necessary support if they need to expend large amounts of political capital in order to so. SEs may not wish to impose their wil upon other members of the organization for fear of being perceived as autocratic. Quite simply SEs may not want to stick their neck out to support a project if they fear a backlash.

On the other side of the coin, there may be situations in which SEs really think they are providing the necessary support, but the PMs feel otherwise. Thus, PMs may still regard lack of top management support as a key risk factor because what SEs provide is not always what the PM really wants or needs. In some cases, SEs may give lip service to the importance of a particular project, but their actions speak otherwise. For example, an SE may give a speech in which s/he indicates that the project is strategic, but when the PM requests specific resources needed to complete the project his/her requests may be denied. In such instances, the PM may rightfully conclude that there is a lack of top management support. Thus, SEs should ask themselves the question: Am I doing everything that I can to truly support this project? If SEs and PMs can learn to understand and appreciate each others’ perspectives, this may go a long way towards making IT project risks easier to control.
