# Social media-based innovation: A review of trends and a research agenda

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Social media Innovation Literature review Research agenda

## A B S T R A C T

Literature investigating social media-based innovation has grown considerably in the last years, making a timely review. Accordingly, we systematically review studies of the so-called “latency phase” (2003–2013) outlining a framework based on two main actors – innovation seeker and innovation provider – and three main questions – why (determinants), how (activities), and what (outcomes). This framework is instrumental to unveil the foundational ideas and concepts, gaps, and contradictory results. On the basis of their impact and evolution in the “growth phase” (2014–2017), we outline a research agenda, suggesting research directions that remain unaddressed and deserve further study.

## 1. Introduction

The use of social media heralds a completely new perspective on established innovation approaches and has become a critical part of the innovation process. Social media allows quite novel and useful ways of interacting and collaborating in the innovation process (e.g., [1,2]) and provides direct customer relationships that are invaluable sources for developing innovations (e.g., [3,4]). Literature investigating social media use and potential in the innovation context is growing fast, as shown in this paper.

Several scholars have made eforts to systematically review this topic using diferent lenses [5–9], but we believe this theme deserves further examination. In fact, Ngai et al. [7] developed a systematic and structured literature review based on the analysis of a set of social media research papers but without a specific focus on innovation. By contrast, Bogers et al. [5] and Greer and Lei [6] reviewed the literature about user involvement in innovation but not necessarily by means of social media. Khan [8] focuses on social media and innovation, but his selection concerns only computer science and information systems journals. Adamczyk et al. [9] reviewed the literature on innovations pursued by means of a specific type of social media (innovation contest platforms).

What seems to be still lacking is a comprehensive and systematic literature review to explore the phenomenon of innovation mediated by social media tools in its entirety, which includes the actors participating with diferent roles, their motivations, goals, the activities connected to the innovation process performed through social media, and efects of those activities.

The aim of this paper is to illustrate the development of this research issue by considering the academic papers addressing it, independently from the type of social media involved or the actors considered. The term “social media,” as defined for the present review, includes a variety of applications with the deliberate intention of obtaining a broad picture of the phenomenon rather than narrow snapshots of individual applications. The tools investigated include, for example, innovation contest platforms [10–14] and diferent types of communities [15,16], both firm-driven and user-driven [17] (for definitions, see the Methodology section). Then, we deliberately adopt the broader definition of Kaplan and Haenlein [18] and call “social media” a group of Internet-based applications that builds on the ideological and technological foundations of Web 2.0 and allows for the creation/exchange of user-generated content.

We identify two main types of actors with reference to their respective roles in innovation processes enabled by social media. The first type is what we call the “potential innovation provider” who uses the social media platform and can participate actively in the innovation process at diferent stages (providing ideas, solutions, sketches, prototypes, and final products). We refer to these actors as “potential” because it is not granted that all those who participate in social mediabased (SMB) innovation processes will indeed provide innovation (hereafter “innovation providers” for the sake of simplicity). These actors may include the crowd, specific categories of professionals, firm’s employees, business partners, suppliers, and customers. The second type of actor is what we call the “innovation seeker” who designs, develops, manages, and organizes to reap the benefits of SMB innovation. Innovation seekers may include firms, public agencies, government bodies, or non-profit associations. It is worth noting that, beyond in novation seekers and innovation providers, a third group of actors ex ists, i.e., intermediaries who bridge the former and the latter. Despite the key role that they play in creating the necessary organizational systems and conditions for resource integration and exchange between innovation seekers and innovation providers, we decided, for the present review, to explicitly focus only on the first two actors. Future work could focus on intermediaries as non-neutral actors in matchmaking innovation seekers and innovation providers. Indeed, they can no longer be considered as merely technical service providers but, on the contrary, actors able to influence and catalyze innovation by efectively nurturing their ecosystem.

We assume – in line with a broad portion of the literature [19–25] – that a topic is subject to evolution over time. Its lifecycle (the so-called “discourse lifecycle”) is generally the result of academic articles and company documents, but in this paper, we focus only on papers pub lished in the scholarly literature. The lifecycle is, in general, assumed to consist of three phases: latency, growth, and decline [20,26]. To distinguish the evolution of the discourse lifecycle, we consider the rise of interest with regard to the topic by measuring the number of papers published on the topic each year. At the beginning (latency phase), the number of papers usually is low and grows slowly, generally in fits and starts; then, a sharp rise occurs, which signals the move to the next phase (growth). The literature (e.g., [20,25,27]) recognizes the importance of the latency phase in paving the way for a specific topic, as it is the phase where foundational concepts and ideas are formulated, although they have not received widespread attention yet; therefore, we decided to focus on this, proposing a few key research questions to be answered by means of a systematic literature review (SLR). As shown in the methodology section, the latency phase ended in 2013 when a sharp rise occurred, signaling the move to a phase of rapid growth in popularity. Gaps and contradictory results have been discussed, and their impact and evolution in the next phase (2014–2017) have been investigated.<sup>1</sup>

The literature review, the analysis of emerged gaps and contradictory results, and analysis of how the literature in the growth phase is starting to address them allowed the authors to suggest important research directions that remain unaddressed and deserve further study.

## 2. Methodology

## 2.1. Database construction

An automatic search was performed from January 2003 to June 2017, which included the Science Citation Index Expanded (SCIEXPA NDED), Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI), and Arts and Humanities Citation Index (A&HCI) databases of the Web of Science (WoS), to collect relevant contributions about SMB innovation. WoS was preferred to Google Scholar, which has a wider coverage but is frequently accompanied by inadequate, less frequently updated citation information [28]. As several terms may potentially refer to SMB innovation, we used a number of words or phrases, as well as Boolean combinations of these words, in searching titles and keywords (see Appendix A for de tail).

Pilot searches were performed to refine the keywords in the search string using trial and error. Terms that did not yield additional papers in the automatic searches were removed. As relevant articles may not be included in the databases, the references of the identified contributions were also reviewed. By applying the same inclusion criteria, expert knowledge complemented the automated search. Inclusion and exclusion criteria for selecting the 2003–2017 article set (Table 1) were specified, and 2003 was selected as the starting date, as it corresponds to the large-scale dissemination of the open innovation concept [29]. The open innovation mantra combined with the social media revolu tion, in terms of collaboration and co-creation potential, brings to an entirely diferent scale of previously limited and isolated experiences of user involvement in innovation.

Using the defined search string, the result of the initial search identified 727 studies from the automatic search. The identified contributions were reviewed separately by the authors for relevancy, and the authors applied the inclusion/exclusion criteria to the abstract and conclusion of each study. Based on Kitchenham and Charters’ [30] recommendations, we excluded studies that were clearly not related to the subject of this review. Full-text scanning was used for the remaining studies with consideration of the inclusion/exclusion criteria. In this step, we manually searched the references of each study to trace any missing studies. Finally, we applied quality assessment criteria, and 278 studies were identified, as shown in the 2003–2017 article set in Appendix A<sup>2</sup> (Supplementary Material). For each study, we performed a quality assessment based on four quality criteria (see Table 2).

The QA criteria were applied to the 2003–2017 article set. After their application, 37 studies were eliminated. Most of the exclusions were due to QA1. The eliminated papers included those with a strong marketing focus [31–33]. Indeed, the high level of the journals selected already guaranteed that the Q2-Q4 criteria would be met. For each paper of the 2003–2017 article set (278 articles), we extracted in a spreadsheet the main bibliographic information (title, authors, year, publisher).

The first dimension concerns the main actor in the SMB innovation literature (either the innovation seeker or the innovation provider), while the second dimension concerns the three main questions (in line with several authors dealing with innovation literature reviews, e.g., [36]) in the SMB innovation literature: why (determinants), how (ac tivities), and what (outcomes).

The framework is discussed by row. That means, for a given row – a stage of the sequence, why (1), how (2), and what (3) – we analyze the perspective of the providers (a) and the seekers (b and c). For the how row, in discussing the seekers, we distinguished two cases: activities on the platform (b) and of the platform (c).

Also, for the what row, two levels are identified that relate the seekers: the innovation process level and the firm level. The outcomes of the innovation process investigate the performance of the SMB innovation process itself in terms of its eficiency and efectiveness, while the outcomes at the firm level refer to papers investigating the efects of SMB innovation at a broader level, both in terms of financial/ economic performance and in terms of strategic value for the firm.

## 4.1. RQ1a. What are the innovation providers’ determinants for being engaged in SMB innovation processes?

Papers investigating why people participate in SMB innovation ac tivities try to identify comprehensive sets of antecedents afecting the willingness to participate in such activities. Most studies rely on the well-established typology of human (intrinsic and extrinsic) motivation (e.g., [37]). A few papers refer to additional motives such as social capital-related ones (Hau and Kim, 2012). The overarching theory in these papers is primarily the Ajzen model of planned behavior [38]. Motivations are depicted per se or as being influenced by personal or contingent characteristics.

Among the papers describing motivations per se, intrinsic motivations emerge as salient factors that drive the participation of users in innovation activities. Nevertheless, when the focus of the papers aims at investigating the quality of participation, the debate is still open. For example, Füller, et al. (2007) argue that most users participating in SMB innovation activities are driven by fun $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the major source of intrinsic motivation, according to papers in the sample), but those that are more creative are driven by professional intent, i.e., they dream of becoming professional designers (extrinsic motivation). In contrast, Frey et al. [39] reported that those who are driven by fun tend to produce substantial (i.e., new and relevant) contributions, whereas those driven by monetary rewards (i.e., extrinsic motivation) tend to produce non-substantial contributions.

The literature depicting motivations as being influenced by personal characteristics or needs generally refers to personality traits research. Füller [13] recognized two main drivers leading to involvement in SMB innovation activities: consumer interest in innovations and innovation processes and consumer interest in web-exploration. Individuals with high web exploration-related characteristics and low innovation-related characteristics engage in SMB innovation activities because they are curious and enjoy browsing the Internet. Individuals with low innovation-related characteristics and low web exploration behavior engage in such activities only because they have a strong need for a solution.

Among the papers depicting motivations as being influenced by contingent characteristics, some authors focus on the characteristics connected to the innovation activities themselves and others on elements of platform design. Battistella and Nonino [40] argued that motivations can be a function of the lifecycle stage of the innovation process. Research finds that the more a phase becomes “concrete” (from foresight to design), the more motivations become “concrete,” i.e., monetary incentive becomes the most important reward for partici pating. Among the latter, quality content [41] and task variety [42] are identified as elements of platform design, which may influence motivations to participate in SMB innovation activities.

A broad variety of innovation providers’ determinants emerges. In the wide range of motivations, intrinsic ones, such as fun and pursuit of enjoyment, prevail. They can be linked to personality traits, e.g., curiosity, interest in innovation or in web-exploration, and can be influenced by contingency factors such as quality of contents and task variety. Many authors recognize higher novelty, relevance, and creativity in the contributions coming from people driven by intrinsic motivations rather than from those driven by extrinsic ones (e.g., monetary rewards).

## 4.2. RQ1b. What are the innovation seekers’ determinants for being engaged in SMB innovation processes?

Not surprisingly, for an issue in its latency phase, special attention is devoted to innovation seekers’ preliminary considerations connected to the innovation process as a whole. Some papers investigate under what contingencies open innovation models are to be preferred to the closed ones [43–45]; others focus on how and when to make use of conven tional or virtual methods and on what grounds in case an open innovation model is preferred [46,47]. These papers adopt a meso level perspective. Other papers investigate why organizations decide to invest in SMB innovation processes [48], adopting a micro-level perspective. Also in this case, the overarching theory is the Ajzen model of planned behavior [38]. Managers’ cognition, attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral controls are considered factors affecting their intention to invest in such activities.

Researchers investigate alternative models and methods to approach innovation issues and relate the choice for a company with the characteristics of the problem, the knowledge required for the solution, the crowd, and the solutions to be evaluated.

## 4.3. RQ2a. What are the innovation providers’ activities in SMB innovation processes?

This group includes papers investigating what people do when they are engaged in SMB innovation activities. The literature tries to identify diferences in the behaviors of innovators and non-innovators. Exploratory studies analyze the processes by which innovators (com pared to non-innovators) gather the information and assistance to develop their ideas and how they share and difuse the resulting in novations within online communities [15]. Other aspects of innovator behaviors include the tendency to span multiple communities [49] and to modify digital information goods found on Web 2.0 [50]. Spanning multiple communities seems to allow for fruitful collaboration and cross-fertilization of ideas.

Some papers focus only on innovators, dealing with the duration of their innovation engagement and analyzing the innovation process they enact. Empirical studies report contrasting conclusions on the duration of the innovative engagement. Some studies find that innovators are not “one-time shoppers.” In fact, their innovative engagement is rather long-lasting, continuous, evolving, and intense [51]. In contrast, Bayus [52] shows that innovators are unlikely to generate additional ideas once some of their ideas have been implemented. Other authors in vestigate how innovators create, evaluate, share, and commercialize their ideas on SMB platforms. Innovators’ behavior is studied with re ference to diferent types of cultural backgrounds [53], diferent types of online communities (brand communities [54], communities of practice [55]), and contrasted with the classical model of entrepreneurship process of opportunity identification, creation, and development of a solution [56]. In brand communities, specific behavior (complexity reduction, consensus reaching and clear articulation) are implemented by user innovators to increase the probability of the adoption of their innovation by the brand [57]. Most papers [15,54,56] underline the importance of free revealing [58] and peer-to-peer assistance in SMB innovation activities. Several studies focus on the behavior of very proactive users who are referred to as change agents [57] or lead users [54–56]. Lead users are characterized by two element [59]: being ahead of the trend and expecting high benefits from innovation. Von Hippel [59] argues that lead users are likely to be in novators because they will try to find novel solutions to their unique problems.

Most papers do not explicitly distinguish between innovator and non-innovator activities. Some of the papers focus on micro-practices enacted on a platform [60,61]. Some authors [62,63] claim that user interactions are enacted without structural mechanisms and are self governed. Some papers try to define the determinant and moderating factors of interactions, such as trust [64] and user perceptions of interaction-based benefits [65]. Interactions are assumed to be always cooperative and positive, with a few exceptions. Gebauer et al. [66] focus on both positive and negative aspects of interactions in SMB innovation activities, underlining that such interactions may also create frustration and provoke angry reactions. Hutter et al. [14] claim that, in these interactions, both cooperation and competition are present and should be simultaneously considered.

User activities in SMB innovation processes are mainly based on cooperative and positive interactions. Focusing on innovative users, including lead users, their activities are largely based on pro-social behaviors (i.e. free-revealing their innovations, peer-to-peer assistance).

4.4. RQ2b. What are the innovation seekers’ activities on the platform (design, management, and governance)?

The papers in this group address platform efectiveness and efi ciency in supporting the innovation process and provide design and management guidelines to improve them. A subset of these papers focuses on the aspects of user experience on the platform and identify activities to make it attractive [2,67–78].

Several authors investigate factors influencing the quantity and/or quality of users engaged in SMB innovation activities and the positive spillovers on the innovation process. To increase the likelihood of obtaining innovative solutions, the platform should be designed to reach the most diverse possible population [79], ofer tangible (financial) and intangible (recognition and reputation) prizes [80,81], provide activation-enabling functionalities [11,82], provide specific and flexible procedures and mediating artifacts [83], and carefully design duration and dificulty level of tasks related to the entity of the prize [84]. Some authors identify diferent design strategies for customer integration in the diferent phases of the innovation process, from idea generation to commercialization [85–87]. Diferent types of platforms are defined based on the phase they focus on, from the open innovator, which supports each phase with high intensity, to the idea seeker, which focuses on idea generation and selection. For each typology, specific design guidelines are provided. Prandelli et al. [86] reported on an empirical study that maps the web-mechanisms supporting collabora tive innovation in five diferent sectors. According to their analyses, SMB innovation occurs mainly in the initial and final stages of the process. The use of tools at diferent stages of the innovation process varies considerably across the studied industries. Finally, the study shows that only large companies and multinationals utilize social media to its fullest potential. Almost five years later, Ryzhkova [87] performed a similar study extending the analysis to one service industry and supported Prandelli et al.’s [86] evidence.

Some authors [88] highlight the fact that creating and fostering a common culture of information sharing and collaboration is the most important aspect of the development of a successful virtual community for open innovation in B2B contexts.

While the diversity of participants positively correlates to the likelihood of finding an innovative solution for which the general design indication is to engage participants in varied fields [79], the role of prizes is still under debate. Some authors find no significant correlation between the size of the prize and the likelihood of finding an innovative solution [80], whereas others consider prizes an important tool in fostering innovations [81]. Others still consider prizes to be only one of the elements that, in synergy with others (the duration and dificulty level of the tasks), can have an impact on platform success [84]. Specifically, higher awards, easier tasks, longer durations, and lower competition intensity lead to a higher number of solvers and a higher ability of the winners.

Only in limited cases do platform management activities imply actions performed by the company on the social media innovation plat form in terms of support provided by firm members to users [89,90] or the adjustments of technological features or governance choices by the company to better address its innovation goals [91]. Jeppesen and Molin [92] argue that the SMB innovation process, far from being the prerogative of solitary and disconnected users who innovate, can be structured, motivated, and partly organized by a commercial firm that organizes the infrastructure for consumers’ interactive learning in a public online domain. Then, the innovation seeker sets up support functions that assist user-driven innovation, hands out user tools that open a solution space, and makes systematic use of interactions with consumers to benefit from their eforts and innovation. This kind of infrastructure should be able to evolve with the capabilities of the users, and the innovation seeker should make an efort to nurture, develop, and moderate interactions to enhance its efectiveness.

Haegeman et al. [91] presented one of the few cases where the in novation seeker is not a firm but an institute for innovation, using Web 2.0 technology to develop foresight projects.

A significant subset of papers focuses on the activities aimed to make the social platform attractive for the user, a place where users love spending their time for several reasons such as ease of use, satisfaction, fun, etc. These activities are integrated into broader and more articulated constructs, which refer to theories and concepts from diferent areas, including innovation management, computer-mediated communication, psychology, sociology, and ICT management. These authors may refer to the empowerment literature as a useful theoretical background in providing suggestions for efective tool design to improve the user’s experience on the platform [70]. In this cluster, empirical studies widely outnumber theoretical studies, and qualitative approaches, mainly (n)ethnography, and field research, are preferred to quantitative approaches. Several papers are based on the authors’ direct observation of implementing online innovation platforms; indeed, in some cases, the authors also participated in the design and development stages as well [2,67,68,71–73].

Several authors underline the importance of the user experience and perceived empowerment on SMB innovation platforms and derive precious insights for the design and management of efective environments. The design of customer experiences, as well as the messages and contexts proposed in online communication, assumes a primary importance for the creation of rich relationships, with customers aimed at obtaining user contributions to the nonfunctional dimension of products in industries such as fashion, where innovation is much more related to communication and esthetics [78]. In such contexts, the web becomes a customer management platform for experience sharing and brand interaction, and the firms promote an intense and interactive marketing approach.

Nambisan and Nambisan [75] propose an analytical framework suggesting that SMB innovation platforms have to consider four experience dimensions to serve user needs. Based on these four components of experience, the authors suggest a set of implementation principles and strategies commonly used in SMB innovation platforms. They contribute to the knowledge based on which further research is built (e.g., [71–73]). User experience analysis is a common research framework used to derive implications about the design and management of other types of social tools such as Internet-based toolkits [76,93]. The use of Internet-based toolkits to facilitate user activities is considered a way to prevent “mass confusion” in co-design [76].

From all these contributions, it emerges that basic principles of participant self-selection and self-motivation have to be considered when setting up an SMB innovation platform, and possibilities for social interaction among participants have to be provided. For them to be efective, autonomy, joy, and competence should characterize the experience on these platforms.

To support innovation processes, innovation seeker should apply platform design strategies for user motivation and integration, which can be specific for each phase of the innovation process. In general, the diversity of participants should be pursued, as well as a good balance between task dificulty and entity of prize. The design of the user experience on the platform plays a central role in the debate. During these years, there is a growing awareness that there is no one-size-fits-all way to efective platform design, management, and governance, but on the contrary, they should result from an intense work of the innovation seeker.

4.5. RQ2c. What are the innovation seekers’ activities of-platform (redefinition, re-design, and tuning of organizational practices, decision-making processes, routines, and business models)?

The adoption/implementation of SMB innovation processes requires innovation seekers to perform a wide range of of-platform activities such as the re-definition, re-design, and tuning of organizational practices, decision-making processes, routines, and business models to fully exploit the potential of social media as a game changer.

Some authors underline the importance of considering the large ramifications of SMB innovation processes and the need to adapt strategy coherently [94–97]. Compelling cases (e.g., [98–103]), such as Connect-and-Develop by P&G, Starbucks, and Lego, are enthusiastically described and commented upon as inspiring applications of radical open innovation strategies, which are enacted around social media tools. Other authors propose that firms should re-define their entire business models to take advantage of social media innovation initiatives, i.e., make business models more user-centric [104] and more open to external ideas and technologies [105] by means of appropriate organizational, technological, and managerial choices [106]. Other papers focus on opportunities ofered by Web 2.0 technologies for companies, both incumbents and newcomers, to create completely new businesses [107].

Another large stream of literature focuses on organizational issues, highlighting how organizational boundaries have to be redefined to allow firm collaboration with online communities in pursuing innovation. These boundaries are constantly negotiated between diferent actors (e.g., platform intermediaries, community members, and content owners) and can be interactively managed through appropriate organizational practices and routines [108,109]. What is currently described as tensions and trade-ofs that arise at the boundaries between communities and firms are indeed mutually enabling synergies [110]. Moreover, to fully exploit the Internet as an enabler of innovation, companies may need to complement direct channels of customer interaction with indirect, or mediated, interactions, which can be carried out by third parties functioning as “knowledge brokers” [111,112] or by internal “socialization agents” [113]. Studies describing firm–community interactions predominantly overlook the phenomenon of intermediaries [110].

To fully exploit the potential of social media for innovation, innovation seekers should adapt their open innovation strategy coherently and, in some cases, even go as far as to revise completely their business model (e.g., to make it more user-centric and open to external ideas). More openness also implies redefinition of organizational boundaries to manage collaboration for innovation with external parties, which may involve intermediaries within or outside the organization.

4.6. RQ3a. What are the efects of SMB innovation on the innovation providers?

We did not find any paper in the latency phase addressing this issue.

4.7. RQ3b. What are the efects of SMB innovation on the innovation seekers at the process level?

This group includes papers investigating the eficiency and efectiveness of the SMB innovation process itself. Innovation process performance is measured by various indicators: percentage of acquired ideas/total number of ideas submitted; number of new potential markets identified for a given product/service; quality of insights and ideas generated in terms of novelty, feasibility, and distance between the original field of application and the target market; maturity level of contributions (need for further development); positioning with reference to the current business (inside/outside and far/close). Cheng et al. [114] claim that both novelty and meaningfulness of new product creativity derive from the interaction of a creative climate in the online community and firms’ creativity capabilities.

The performance of the SMB innovation process has been compared to that of traditional methods in terms of the cost–benefit relationship between idea generation and the new product development process.

Empirical studies show that online idea competitions lead to more and better ideas at a lower cost per idea than focus groups, whereas the latter yield richer interactions with users [115]. With regard to the NPD process, preference markets provide distinct benefits over conventional methods (e.g., survey-based) in terms of the interaction, the competition, learning among participants and the possibility of easily scaling up the number of respondents [116]. Moreover, once compared with actual sales, the sales forecasts for new products by means of SMB innovation platforms are accurate [117].

Some papers relate the type of users who provide ideas to the effectiveness of the SMB innovation process in terms of idea novelty, feasibility, and customer benefit. Empirical studies show that user ideas, compared to those of professionals, score significantly higher in terms of novelty and customer benefit and somewhat lower in terms of feasibility [118]. Lead user contributions, compared to those of ordinary users, are especially valuable in providing solutions instead of simply describing problems or stating customer needs. Lead users technical expertise also makes them particularly well suited to developing new functionalities but less suited to developing the design and usability improvements [119].

The performance of SMB innovation processes, compared to that of traditional methods, is frequently superior in terms of eficiency and efectiveness. Specifically, the combination of a creative climate in the online community and firms’ creativity capabilities afects efective ness. It also does so the type of user who provides the idea (ordinary user vs. professional, lead user vs. ordinary user).

## 4.8. RQ3c. What are the efects of SMB innovation on the innovation seekers at the firm level?

This group of papers investigates the efects of SMB innovation at a broader level, in terms of both financial/economic performance and strategic value for the firm.

Tangible impacts include cost savings and revenue gains as well as new-to-market products/services [120], while intangible impacts include increased customer satisfaction, employee engagement, customer purchase intentions, and willingness to pay and/or to recommend the firm to others [121]. Some value can be realized in the short-run, while other types require a longer timeframe. It is worth noting that gains in terms of intangible value afect not only customers who participated in SMB innovation activities but also those who did not [122,123]. Customer integration is found to lead to increased levels of perceived customer orientation, more favorable corporate attitudes, and stronger behavioral intentions (purchase, loyalty, positive word of mouth, corporate commitment) among customers from the “periphery” (the mass that does not participate).

SMB innovation seems to have positive financial as well as in tangible (e.g., recognition, reputation, image, customer satisfaction, and employee engagement) impacts on firm performance.

## 5. Emerging gaps and contradictory results

## 5.1. Gaps

This section details the most important gaps emerging from the literature review of the latency phase. Some of these gaps were ad dressed, at least partly, in the growth phase, while others are still open and, in our opinion, deserve further research. We analyze the gaps and look at their evolution in the 2014–2017 phase (Table 7). In the next section, we identify possible future developments of research on SMB innovation, including further eforts needed to fill the gaps.

## 5.2. Gap 1: Little focus on multiple participants

In the latency phase, researchers mainly focused on pair of actors, firms, and customers, where the firm is the innovation seeker that endeavors to engage its customers in its innovation processes by means of increasingly attractive platforms. Business customers, employees, suppliers’, and industrial partners are less investigated ([124,125]; [126]). A possible explanation for this gap concerns the real novelty of social media, i.e., the possibility of engaging final consumers on a large scale. The other actors in the supply chain, as well as small groups of consumers, have always been involved in industrial innovation processes by means of traditional systems. The truly new characteristic of social media is its large scale as well as the viral and pervasive character of initiatives launched on them. In the growth phase (2014–2017), the involvement of citizens in innovation projects for civil society receives increasing consideration, developing the so-called “civic open innovation approaches” [127,128]. Some governments have proven the potential of social media to generate value through co-creation and citizen participation, and municipalities are increasingly using these tools to become smart cities [127,129,130]. Governments, municipalities, and private multinational firms are increasingly using these tools to encourage the civic crowd to co-create solutions to great societal challenges.

In addition, the involvement of internal communities of employees in the innovation processes of large enterprises is receiving increasing consideration [131,132]. SMB internal crowdsourcing has recently emerged as a phenomenon [133]. Forms of internal crowdsourcing with employees have been documented at enterprises including Deloitte [134] and IBM [135,136], but information about such approaches has only recently been published in primary journals ([137] concerning Deutsche Telekom).

## 5.3. Gap 2: Little focus on outcomes of SMB innovation

In the latency phase, we did not find any paper investigating the outcome of innovation providers engaged in SMB innovation. With regard to innovation seekers’ outcomes, only a few papers investigate the outcome of SMB innovation in terms of the efectiveness/eficacy of the innovation process itself, and even fewer papers make the efort to assess its impact on innovation seeker’s performance. What seems to be completely absent is an attempt to evaluate the impact of SMB innovation activities on firm performance by means of either explicit financial indicators or innovation-related indicators. To this purpose, scholars in the field prefer to use elements such as the impact on customers “behaviors and feelings" (in terms of increased purchase intention and willingness to pay or recommend the firms to others). These seem to be indicators mainly emphasizing the marketing results of initiatives rather than the impact on innovation. Indeed, many of these papers consider investment in SMB innovation merely as marketing, customer care/retention, or brand enhancement eforts. The evidence that firms do not measure innovation performance connected to social media initiatives by means of traditional indicators – or do not measure them at all – seems to indicate that the goal of innovation is not so clearly pursued, even when it is claimed. This gap in firm performance evaluation is not surprising. In fact, explicit performance indicators are often matched with the explicit goals and motivations underpinning them, which do not emerge from papers investigating firm determi nants to invest in SMB innovation. In fact, some of these papers conceive of motivations in a rather peculiar way, which is as the personal motivations of managers to invest in these kinds of efort. Others analyze the possibility to invest in SMB innovation from the firm perspective in rather general terms, making preliminary considerations about when and why and preferring open to closed innovation models and virtual to traditional methods.

In 2014–2017, the issue of the impact of social media technologies on innovation performance receives increasing consideration in the literature, both in service and in manufacturing industries [138–140]. These contributions show that access to social media tools does not necessarily benefit innovation performance. It is the support and appropriate usage of social media tools that can benefit customer focus and innovation performance [139]. The influence of social media tools on firm performance is defined as “indirect” and is exerted by developing distinctive innovation competencies [140]. Current studies, far from closing the debate, leave several questions open regarding the relationship between social media usage and innovation performance, providing a fruitful avenue for future research.

## 5.4. Gap 3: Little focus on contingency factor

Only a few studies investigate the contingencies of the SMB in novation–performance relationship, including the strategic orientation of firms [46], individual-level characteristics [53], characteristics of the problem, knowledge required for the solution, characteristics of the crowd, and the solution to be evaluated [43]. Some papers investigated the impact of contingency factors related to the product: its complexity ([44], [141]) and the NPD project characteristics [45,47]. Some scholars conduct empirical studies on SMB innovation in specific industrial sectors [78,86,87,142] or in firms characterized by homogeneous size, e.g., SMEs [97], and note diferences and peculiarities while not adopting a real contingent approach.

Recent work suggests that SMB innovation may be more appropriate at later stages of the technological lifecycle [143] and is subject to other contingencies, e.g., the extent of a country’s cultural tightness [144]; individual-level characteristics, such as the level of engagement (e.g., [145]), or the impacts of benefit and cost factors as well as trust (Ye and Kankanhalli, 2017); and platform/task-level characteristics, such as task and knowledge design dimensions [146], network structure [147], task division, and resource allocation in team-based competitions [148].

Thus, notwithstanding the fact that several papers recognized the importance of a contingency approach in SMB innovation research and addressed specific contingency factors, a comprehensive analysis of the factors, which may have an impact on the efectiveness of this process at diferent levels of understanding, is still lacking.

## 5.5. Gap 4: Little focus on risks, critical aspects, or failures

In the latency phase, a generally optimistic view of SMB innovation is largely predominant. Altruistic behaviors on both sides, the innovation seeker and the user, connected, e.g., in free-revealing, peer-to-peer support, the openness of the NPD process, and the potential gains for firms of investing in SMB innovation seem to definitely overwhelm the possible risks ([15]; Harhof, 2003; [18,149]). The literature, in this phase, is rich with successful case histories of firms such as Dell [52], Lego [102], Swarovski, Volkswagen, and Osram [71], while experiences of failure and cases of social “frustration” in online interactions are rarely mentioned [66]. Such an abundance of papers reporting stories about the successful application of SMB innovation is in line with the concept of “bandwagon discourse” [25,26], which reasonably prevails in latency phase.

In the growth phase, 2014–2017, a more realistic view of SMB innovation emerges in which risks, dificulties, failures, unintended consequences, and threats are highlighted [150,151]. The value of firm openness is called into question as well as the community members identification with an efort on behalf of the firms’ innovation projects (Balka, 2014). Firm openness–closeness is treated as a balance and, unlike prior literature, diferent forms and loci of openness are distinguished, specifically the transparency, accessibility, and replicability of diferent components of the product being developed.

Moreover, recent literature increasingly recognizes the dificulties inherent in SMB innovation connected to the lack of a clear understanding of crowds, the various forms they can take, the value they can ofer, and the absence of a well-defined process to engage them. Some authors [152] claim that crowds need to be constructed in order to generate the so-called “crowd capital” that can bring unique benefits to organizations. Piezunka and Dahlander [153] observe that, notwithstanding the possibilities ofered by SMB innovation to access suggestions that capture distant knowledge, organizations are more likely to pay attention to suggestions that are familiar, not distant, thus failing to focus on the knowledge that has the most potential for innovation. Moreover, due to the unintended and uncontrolled revealing that occurs in public format SMB innovation (through organizations’ websites), organizations that solicit suggestions from external contributors might even help their competitors achieve breakthrough innovations derived from their oversights.

## 5.6. Contradictory results

This section details the most important contradictory results that emerge from the literature analysis of the latency phase. We also look at their evolution, if any, in the phase 2014–2017.

## 5.6.1. Contradictory Result 1: lead user vs. Other theoretical approache

As stated elsewhere, almost all the papers refer to the lead user theory to explain both motivations and behaviors of users on social media platforms. A few papers move beyond the theoretical reference and delve into the concept in detail. Some papers confirm the applicability of the theory [15,51,119,154,155], while others do not [17,49]. Results reported by Dahlander and Frederiksen [49] are contrary to von Hippel’s theory, which stresses the importance of identifying the “right”

set of technically skilled users (i.e., lead users) and the correct incentive structures to promote innovation by these users. In contrast, Dahlander and Frederiksen [49] underline the significance of looking beyond in dividual user characteristics and afirm the idea that spanning multiple communities by users may trump the efects of lead user attributes. In Fuller et al. (2007), innovators in the context of social media are driven by fun rather than the pure need for product improvement, which is in contrast with the lead user theory, according to which innovators have a significant urge to solve a given problem, and this is their main motivating driver.

What seems to emerge in the 2014–2017 literature is the awareness that lead users and user communities are two complementary approaches for SMB innovation, and therefore, this implicitly implies that they need diferent sets of analytical constructs. For example, according to Parmentier and Mangematin [156], when the firm innovates with an online user community, the question is no longer about identifying lead users but about how to manage sustainable interrelations with the community (mainly composed of ordinary users) and benefit from the heterogeneity between the firm and the community. Furthermore, while the lead user approach maintains the boundaries between communities and firms, with lead users being selected and invited by firms to participate in innovation, the online community approach blurs the firm-community boundary. This has an impact on several dimensions, such as appropriability (community involvement may prevent the firm from fully appropriating or patenting SMB innovation outcomes) and control (the firm cannot always decide who to invite). Hienerth et al. [157] also confirmed the complementarity between the lead user and user community approaches, highlighting the benefits and limitations of the two perspectives. Nevertheless, some authors aim to identify lead users inside online communities (e.g., [158]), and therefore, in this case, they do apply the lead user theory.

## 5.6.2. Contradictory Result 2: positive vs. Negative impact of rewards

Another contradictory result emerged concerning the impact of rewards on SMB innovation activities. Specifically, some authors claim that rewards have a positive impact [84], whereas some others claim the contrary [17]. Furthermore, the debate about the typology of rewards (monetary versus nonmonetary) that are more suitable for these activities provides other contradictory results. For some authors [40], monetary incentive is, under certain conditions, the most important reward. Frey et al. [39] claim that monetary rewards could even be counterproductive heading for non-substantial contributions.

In the phase 2014–2017, the debate about the impacts of rewards on the participation and performance of innovation providers seems to be still open [159]. Parmentier and Mangematin [156] suggested companies not to ofer only monetary rewards because it may disappoint the community members who are rather expecting other types of rewards (individual and community recognition). Nevertheless, the peculiar aspect of the current research on this topic [160] is the increasing awareness that rewards may play diferent roles among heterogeneous community members. It becomes imperative to explore how to design diferent types of rewards to motivate diferent types of individuals to participate.

## 5.6.3. Contradictory Result 3: low vs. High degree of the platform structure

A third contradictory result of this latency phase with deals with a specific platform design issue: the degree of the platform structure. Some authors claim innovation providers’ activities on the platform should be governed by means of a certain degree of structure [92], whereas others claim that the structure is not needed and innovation provider interactions and activities should be self-governed [62,63].

Challenges associated with the structure of social media platforms seem to be in need of further empirical evidence in the growth phase (2014–2017) as well. Indeed, although innovation provider contribu tions can emerge spontaneously (i.e., without a high degree of structure), it seems to be commonly accepted that such contributions can be amplified by specific and proactive eforts from the host firm [161].

## 5.6.4. Contradictory Result 4: positive vs. Conflictual interactions

According to most authors, user interactions are cooperative and positive [64,65]; for others, they may even create frustration and provoke angry reactions. Specifically, two articles introduce the “dark side” of SMB innovation eforts [14,66] and examine potential conflicts in innovation communities connected to perceived injustice and dissatisfaction with a company’s actions as well as consequent forms of misbehavior and negative actions against the company involved in the innovation project.

In the phase 2014–2017, authors more broadly recognize the pos sibility that tensions both between firms and communities and within communities themselves may exist, which hinder innovation. Lauritzen (2017) investigated the use of innovation intermediaries to resolve firm-community tensions (but finding that they may even fuel new tensions), while others [162] focus on firms’ and communities’ conflicting demands. The former demand control of the innovation process and appropriation of results, while the latter demand transparency and influence in the decision-making process. What emerges from the current literature is that the optimistic view that prevailed in the latency phase has been replaced by a more realistic one (see Hutter et al. [163] about uncooperative and even amoral, manipulative, and unethical behaviors in social media contexts).

## 6. Research agenda

## 6.1. Research Line 1: wider variety of participants

The involvement of a wider variety of participants in open innovation is an issue that deserves further research. According to Chesbrough [164], today, the open innovation concept is being used to coordinate a large number of players across multiple phases of the innovation process, and in the near future, it will become even more extensive, collaborative, and engaged with a wider variety of participants. We expect that scholars will extend the perimeter of SMB innovation research beyond the classic firm-customer pair to include the broader context of industries, innovation systems, and societies. Even more important is to better understand the reasons of this wider variety of participants to contribute, and how they are incentivized over the diferent levels of analysis. In fact, according to several scholars [165], too often users or external solution providers are seen as an unlimited, free input resource, which is exploitable by the innovative activities of a firm.

## 6.2. Research Line 2: SMB innovation efectiveness and related firm performance

The measurement of SMB innovation outcomes and its link to innovation seeker performance remains an open issue in the debate in the current literature. Further research is needed to achieve more clarity about how firms can leverage diferent tools and approaches, which are frequently examined in an aggregate fashion or considered separately, for successful open innovation processes. Perhaps even more important is to pay attention to the question of how connecting the front end of open innovation to the back-end businesses that must take these inputs to market [164]. Further research is needed on how firms should innovate their business models to take full advantage of open innovation. The absolute importance of this issue was underlined by Chesbrough [166], and already received much attention in the latency phase of the relevant literature (see “Innovation seekers’ of-platform activities” section).

## 6.3. Research Line 3: relevant contingencies in SMB innovation efectiveness

Given the uncertain and complex nature of SMB innovation, a number of contingencies at diferent levels of analysis also seem relevant for explaining its efectiveness across diferent settings. Bogers et al. [165] discussed several opportunities for further research on open innovation at diferent levels of analysis, identifying the need to investigate contingency factors relevant at the intra-organizational, interorganizational, extra-organizational, industrial, regional, and societal levels of analysis. Concerning SMB innovation, industry-level contingencies seem to be a promising avenue for further studies aimed at assessing the factors that impact the efectiveness of the process and the degree to which that occurs. Specifically, these include R&D intensity and uncertainty [167], industry modularity [168,169], and the locus of relevant knowledge [170,171]. Future research could attempt to gain a better understanding of the complex entanglement of industry-level contingencies and firm-level specificities to provide some guidelines on the convenience of pursuing SMB innovation and the expected payof.

At levels of analysis higher than the firm, SMB innovation is in creasingly recognized at the city level, particularly in the context of smart cities, regions, and even nations or governments [127,129,130,172]. The diferent types of knowledge involved as well as the mix of public, private, for-profit, and nonprofit organizations are possible contingency factors that afect SMB innovation efectiveness [165].

6.4. Research Line 4: organizational boundaries and a revised theory of the firm

SMB innovation initiatives foster unprecedented creativity, but at the same time, they challenge the contemporary organizational boundary literature. Understanding more about the locus of innovation may help us to disentangle why firm boundaries can be dynamic [5]. In the context of the risks emerging from blurring organizational bound aries, secrecy and intellectual property rights issues deserve investigation [48]. Moreover, the risk of deceptive behaviors allowed by hidden identities on social media should be more thoroughly examined in the literature [72] for the risks connected to anonymity in a pseudonymous environment). According to some authors [162], the integration of sources of innovation that remain outside firm boundaries requires specific capabilities to identify, access, and assimilate their knowledge and to capture the value thus created [193.57]. To date, relatively few studies have explored these capabilities and the ways of building them [5,173].

However, concerning the theoretical foundation of the firm itself, the impact of social media technology and open innovation becomes even more disruptive. Existing theories of the firm exhibit significant shortcomings when questions turn to intra-organizational power and extra-organizational relationships. Both governance work, based on Coase’s [174] reasoning about the eficiency advantages of firms over markets, and the competence approach, which conceives of firms as bundles of valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable resources [175,176], employ sterile conceptions of power and instrumentalist views of stakeholders that limit the understanding of the social side of firms [177]. Alternative theories of the firm are emerging to address these shortcomings and to reach a better understanding of firms as complex social systems that incorporate a variety of interests. One of these emerging fields, referred to as the communicative constitution of organizations, seems particularly promising to address the social side of firms, as it theorizes and analyses about how organizations as discursive material configurations are reproduced and coproduced through

ongoing interactions [178].

According to one of the founders of this emerging field ([177], p. 1247), “building an alternative theory of the firm is a long-term project that is best considered the responsibility of a community,” and thus, it constitutes a promising future research direction.

## 6.5. Research Line 5: diferent theoretical lens

What seems to be lacking in the latency phase is a socio-material perspective that considers the diferent kinds of interactions related to any technological system in a social context. In fact, a sociological stream of literature on information systems conceives of the process of information systems development and use as an emergent phenomenon that involves the intertwining of two kinds of agency, human and material. According to Jones [179], interaction in technological systems is double, as human agents seek to marshal material agency (of the technology itself) to direct the actions of other human agents. Only a few papers from the latency phase recognize the intertwining of this double interaction in SMB innovation activities [89,180–182]. This is surprising, as social platforms are, by nature, the locus of multiple interactions. Indeed, most of the literature addresses interactions among users or between users and technology, such as papers describing innovation activities in virtual worlds yet recognizing the existence of diferent types of interactions. What seem to be lacking are papers that identify the goals and motivations of human agency, ascribing intentionality to it and not considering the material agency of platforms neutral. Several papers do address the issue of firm objectives in undertaking SMB innovation activities and, even more, explore user motivations. Only rarely [89] are these diferent agencies considered as a whole with the relative actions and reactions on a platform, which is capable of exerting agency in its own right. In our opinion, this would be a promising future avenue of research that [194] have already undertaken.

Another theoretical perspective that sounds promising for further research is the adoption of a relational/social view [183], which goes beyond the focus on individual characteristics of engaged users to involve the complex network of relationships that connect individuals. With a few exceptions [49,184,185], studies in the latency phase surprisingly neglect to focus on the social structures in which the users are embedded and the efects of which may have bearing, both positively and negatively, on individual characteristics [186]. It is worth noting that the features of social media introduce opportunities and capabilities that are not available in traditional social networks. For ex ample, social media platforms allow for the quantification/formalization of relationships or interactions between nodes by explicitly representing them in a formal data structure [187]. This formalization provides relational capabilities in social media networks that are not present in traditional social networks [187]. However, the relational formalism of social media platforms also limits relational capabilities, for instance, by limiting the number of nuances people can attribute to labels such as “friend” or “follower” [188]. It could be interesting to understand what diferences are introduced by social media platforms in terms of how relationships form and develop and how these characteristics may afect SMB innovation compared to traditional social networks. It could be beneficial to theoretically synthesize the frameworks used by traditional social network analysis [189] and those used for social media networks [183]. Research in the growth phase (2014–2017) shows that a social perspective seems to gain momentum ([161,190,191]).

## 7. Conclusions and limitations

In this article, we adopted a discourse lifecycle approach to investigate the topic of SMB innovation. We focused our attention on the latency phase because of its importance in paving the way of a specific discourse. After identifying the limitations, gaps, and contradictory results of the latency literature and how literature in the growth phase is starting to address them, we identified important research directions that remain unaddressed. We hope that we have provided scholars of management a springboard from which they can make deeper and further-reaching contributions to the theory in the field.

Despite its value, this study has several limitations. The complete set of papers was indexed solely in the WoS database, which indeed is one of the most relevant and prestigious in the academic environment. Thus, contributions from several other outlets (conference proceedings) were not included even though these may be relevant to identify emerging topics. Another limitation is that, when retrieving the sample, an attempt was made to include all commonly known keywords related to social media phenomena; however, social media is a fast-evolving phenomenon, and some keywords may have been excluded. Future developments should overcome the above-mentioned limitations and further the work on cause–efect relationships among the elements, which have been individually examined by means of our research questions.

Her main research interests concern the area of knowledge management and innovation, including themes such as the role of social media for innovation, the role of knowledge and collaboration in innovation processes, open innovation practices, and sustainable innovation in the food sector. She has published several articles in prestigious international journals (e.g., Economic Geography, Technovation, Information & Management, Organization and Management, Technological Forecasting and Social Change, International Journal of Production Research).

Francesco Paolo Appio, PhD, is Associate Professor of Innovation at SKEMA Business School (Paris La Défense, France). He received his PhD in at Scuola Superiore Sant’Anna in Italy.
