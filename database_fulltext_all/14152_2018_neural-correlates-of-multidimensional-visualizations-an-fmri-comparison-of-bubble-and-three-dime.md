# NEURAL CORRELATES OF MULTIDIMENSIONAL VISUALIZATIONS: AN FMRI COMPARISON OF BUBBLE AND THREE-DIMENSIONAL SURFACE GRAPHS USING EVOLUTIONARY THEORY<sup>1</sup>

Eric Walden, Gabriel S. Cogo, David J. Lucus, and Elshan Moradiabadi

Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University, Box 42101,

Lubbock, TX 79409 U.S.A.

{eric@ericwalden.net} {gabriel.cogo@ttu.edu} {david.j.lucus@ohio.edu} {elshan.moradiabadi@ttu.edu}

Roozmehr Safi

Department of Management, University of Missouri–Kansas City, 5110 Cherry Street, Kansas City, MO 64110 U.S.A. {safir@umkc.edu}

In this article, an evolutionary argument to explain how people comprehend graphs is put forth. A theory of evolutionary fit, which argues for the correspondence between information presentation and evolutionarily adaptive brain structures, is proposed. This is complementary to cognitive fit, which argues for a correspondence between task and information presentation. In two fMRI experiments, we test this theory by comparing brain activation during a graphic comprehension task using two different graph types: bubble graphs and three-dimensional surface graphs. In accordance with our hypotheses, we find that comprehension of three-dimensional surface graphs results in greater activation of the ventral stream and greater accuracy in graphical comprehension than bubble graphs. We argue that this is because the human visual system is evolutionarily adapted to the comprehension of three-dimensional surfaces. The implication is that choosing graphical representations that match what the brain is evolutionarily specialized to process can enhance graphic comprehension.

Keywords: Graphs, visualization, fMRI, NeuroIS, three-dimensional

## Introduction

Big data offers new opportunities for those organizations that can make sense of the massive amounts of data being generated across all business functions (Chen et al. 2012). One of the most important ways organizations can help people to make sense of big data is through visualizations (Baker et al. 2009). Estimates suggest that the human visual system can read visual data from the environment at roughly the speed of a 10 Mb Ethernet cable (Koch et al. 2006), making it an excellent candidate to transfer large amounts of data from the environment. This, together with advances in computer graphics has made the development of data visualization tools a thriving industry, and has led to a proliferation of new types of data visualization systems (e.g., dashboards). However, while there has been a great deal accomplished with respect to what can be done with data visualizations, there is still much work to do in developing theories to explain how and why data visualizations are effective (Ware 2004). Although there has been progress in this sort of theorizing (Kumar and Benbasat 2004; Lim and Benbasat 2000; Tractinsky and Meyer 1999), our knowledge in this area is still very limited (Ware 2012). Currently, the development of visualization tools and techniques has outpaced research on the fundamental question of what makes a high-quality data visualization. This is unfortunate, because to make full use of the power of data visualizations it is not only necessary to know what can be done, but to understand why. The goal of the current study is to further our knowledge in this underinvestigated domain.

This research begins with the premise that the human visualization system has evolved to process visual displays that are encountered in nature and have proved crucial for humans survival. In two experiments, we demonstrate that people are both more accurate and efficient when viewing data representations that take advantage of evolutionarily hard-wired (i.e., genetically predetermined) parts of the visual system. To better understand this interaction between human visual processing and data visualizations, this research makes use of functional magnetic resonance imagining (fMRI) to observe brain activation while subjects perform graphical analysis tasks using two different types of graphs.

With a few exceptions (e.g., Li et al. 2014), research in the domain of graph comprehension has relied on behavioral experiments. Neuroimaging techniques can complement these behavioral studies to provide a more nuanced view of the brain mechanisms at work. This allows researchers and practitioners the opportunity to develop ever more potent theories of data visualizations, which can in turn lead to better predictive power and even new design techniques (Dimoka et al. 2011). In particular, neuroimaging techniques allow us to theorize about brain structures which are the products of evolutionary pressures.

The next section goes into more detail on the literatures in both data visualization comprehension and human visual processing. This is followed by an exploration of neural theory and of data visualization comprehension based on the structure and function of the human visual system, followed by a description of the experiment and presentation of the findings. The paper concludes with a discussion of the implications and limitations of the work, and suggestions for future research.

## Literature Review

Most research on the comprehension of data visualizations has been done in the geographical information system (GIS) literature under the appellation graphicacy, which is the ability to perceive, comprehend, and present information in a graphical form (Balchin 1976). It is analogous with literacy, numeracy, and articulacy, but represents a visual-spatial dimension of human intelligence rather than a narrative, mathematical, or spoken dimension. Graphs<sup>2</sup> can often offer better comprehension of data than other types of representations (Lambon Ralph et al. 1999; Larkin and Simon 1987; Mayer and Sims 1994; Walther et al. 2001) because they have three major advantages (Aldrich and Sheppard 2000). First, visual information is concise: a graph or picture gives the user a holistic view of the scene instantly. Second, graphics are memorable so that it easy to recall when needed. Third, graphics make relationships obvious; in fact, that is the raison d’etre for graphics.

Despite the advantages inherent in graphs, they are not without issues (Lambon Ralph et al. 1999; Mayer and Sims 1994; Walther et al. 2001). While pictures are a good way of conveying information, they are not a panacea—text, numbers, and the spoken word also convey information well. Moreover, people do not always interpret the meaning of graphics in the same way or with the same level of proficiency. Although graphs should make relationships obvious, background knowledge and training play an important role in graph comprehension (Boardman 1990; Cox et al. 2004; Postigo and Pozo 2004). Thus, while graphics have advantages, they do not enable perfect communication.

The management information systems (MIS) literature on graphics largely derives from the Minnesota experiments (Benbasat and Schroeder 1977; Dickson et al. 1977), which recognized the effect of mode of presentation and decision makers’ characteristics on efficiency of decision making. Furthermore, the Minnesota experiments established speed and accuracy as the two central measurements of graph efficacy. Although accuracy and speed may not be the only goals of viewing graphics (Tractinsky and Meyer 1999), both are often important. Following the guidelines of the Minnesota experiments, several studies investigated the effect of different modes of presentations on decision-making quality (accuracy and speed of a decision) in different task settings (Benbasat and Dexter 1985, 1986; Remus 1987). The equivocal results of these studies called for a theory to encompass all these tasks and modes of presentation in one model. Currently, the leading theory to understand the interplay between task demand and mode of presentation in MIS is cognitive fit theory (Vessey 1991; Vessey and Galletta 1991). Cognitive fit theory posits that if the internal and external environment of the situation match, the problem-solving performance with respect to accuracy and speed will be maximized.

Using theory from the graph construction literature (Pinker 1990) to continue trying to build a solid theory of graphs in MIS, Kumar and Benbasat (2004) found that threedimensional surface graphs<sup>3</sup> performed better than twodimensional graphs even for simple tasks, leading to the conclusion that three-dimensional graphs perform better than two-dimensional graphs under all task conditions that involve more than two variables. The surprising finding that under all conditions three-dimensional graphs out-performed twodimensional graphs provides the impetus for the current research. Is there something that three-dimensional surface graph offers that other types of graphs do not?

The focus of most research has been on purely behavioral aspects of graph comprehension (Correll et al. 2012), but less is known about the cognitive processing of different types of graphs (Scaife and Rogers 1996). However, some research has begun in this direction. For instance, Baker et al. (2009) proposed that sense-making—an ability to comprehend, assimilate, and make inferences—is the main goal of data visualizations. This model offers four characteristic of visual representation which affect sense making: support for basic human visual perceptual approaches,<sup>4</sup> strong gestalt properties, consistence with the viewer’s prior knowledge, and support for analogical reasoning. In a similar vein, Parsons and Sedig (2014) formulated 10 properties of interactive visual representations whose values could be adjusted through interaction. Other researchers have examined the importance of colors in line graphs (Kim and Liu 2010, 2011), how techniques used in visualization could enhance Web search results and evaluation of results (Chau 2011), or how cognitive fit interacts with working memory capacity and information load (Zhu and Watts 2010). To sum up, while research on graphic comprehension has turned increasingly toward understanding the role of the human information processing, there is still a great deal of work to do on the subject.

## Theoretical Development

The underlying assumption of our theory is that human physiology is adapted by evolution to solve specific problems that were present in our environment of evolutionary adaptedness—that is, the environment in which humans evolved (Cosmides and Tooby 1992; Darwin 1859; Kock 2009; Marr 1977). Specifically, for our purposes the human visual system is adapted to solve specific visual problems that were present in our environment of evolutionary adaptedness. We call the use of these adapted visual problem-solving structures native processing to draw an analogy between those processes that are part of a CPU’s instruction set and those processes that are part of some higher-level set of software commands. We theorize that graphs that take advantage of these evolutionary adaptations are more effective graphs. The particular type of graph we propose as native in this work is a three-dimensional surface graph. We propose that threedimensional bubble charts, as a representative from a class of graphs that are commonly used but do not resemble anything specific in our evolutionary past, are not processed natively.

Kock and Chatelain-Jardón (2011) offer a set of four principles for theorizing about evolved information processing traits, which we use as the guiding framework for our theorizing. The first principle is that “there must be perceived similarities between the modern technology-mediated task environment and the EEA [environment of evolutionary adaptedness]” (p. 690). In the environment of evolutionary adaptedness, early humans faced many problems pertaining to three-dimensional surfaces. One environmental feature that was certainly faced by early humans was that they lived in terrain that was a three-dimensional surface. The gross geography of earth consists of hills and valleys of varying steepness. These similarities between three-dimensional terrain features and three-dimensional surface graphs are certainly perceived by humans, who use the same nomenclature (hills, valleys, peaks, slopes, etc.) to describe both three-dimensional terrain features and three-dimensional surface graphs. Similar arguments cannot be made for bubble graphs. It is difficult to conceive of an environmental feature that bears similarity to floating circles.

The second and third principles are closely related. The second is that “the events that have created selective pressures for the evolution of the information processing trait must have impaired fitness in the EEA” (p. 692).” The third is “the information processing trait must have reduced the probability of a fitness impairing event in the EEA” (p. 692). What this means is that there must be things that can occur to an organism that reduce their fitness level through death or impaired reproductive success, and there must be adaptations that mitigate these events. One example that Kock and Chatelain-Jardón use are falls from high altitudes and fear of heights. Falls from high altitudes can cause death and fear of heights can reduce the chance of falling from a high altitude.

Specifically, one can trace back the origins of depth discrimination and three-dimensional vision (stereopsis) in today’s humans to early primates who evolved many major adaptations for this purpose, most notably, forward-looking eyes that provide overlapping fields of view. Our primate ancestors evolved stereopsis for depth perception, an ability necessary for arboreal locomotion which requires choosing branches in the right proximity to grab and move. Arboreal environments are also characterized by clutter by leaves, branches, and other visual obstacles. Stereopsis provides primates with the ability to “see through” obstacles when the vision of one eye is blocked (Changizi and Shimojo 2008) and therefore the ability to avoid being preyed upon. Later, hominids (a family of primates that includes ancestors of recent humans) adopted additional traits in response to the change in their environment from jungle to Savannah, environment characterized by openness (Richmond and Strait 2000). Among other things, living in the new terrestrial environment required adopting an upright posture for better locomotion and for seeing greater distances. This introduced new threats to survival, however, with falls being a major one, given the larger body size of those bipedal ancestors compared to earlier tree-dwelling primates. Falling from altitude is an event that could have occurred, and would have significantly reduced fitness in the environment. Even in a modern world, falls are a major cause of injury and death (Kochanek et al. 2016), killing only slightly fewer people that car accidents (the leading cause of accidental death). For a primitive hunter-gatherer, a broken ankle could be a death sentence, and a simple sprain could significantly interfere with fitness. In response to the fitnessimpairing consequences of falls, early bipedal hominids evolved adaptations that built on previous adaptations of their primate ancestors: not only could they perceive depth, but also they developed a fear of height—an additional quality not vital for arboreal primates. Besides fear of heights, another trait that could mitigate this is an ability to comprehend three-dimensional surfaces in the terrain. Upon reflection it becomes clear that humans are not afraid of heights; what our ancestors evolved was a fear of long steep slopes, when looking down from a position where the probability of falling would be greater than zero in our ancestral past. Humans are not afraid standing on a sidewalk in Denver, but might be very frightened standing on the white cliffs of Dover, even though Denver has 100 times the altitude. Likewise, most humans are not afraid of stepping off a sidewalk, but most would not like to step off a second story balcony. The point is that the ability to comprehend and make estimations about three-dimensional surfaces most definitely helps cope with uneven terrain (principle 3), and the ability to cope with uneven terrain most definitely increases fitness (principle 2).

The final principal is that “the information processing trait must be a human universal” (Kock and and Chatelain-Jardón 2011, p. 692). Combined with the other three principles, this one says that not only does this adaptation make sense, but humans actually have it. We argue that humans the world over have the ability to comprehend three-dimensional surfaces. Our evidence comes from research on fear of heights, which has long been considered a human universal trait. Recent research suggests that it is not actually fear motivating avoidance, at least in infants, but rather a difficulty making affordance for locomotion (Adolph et al. 2014). Humans universally seem to realize that a three-dimensional surface feature with a long, steep slope poses special problems for locomotion. That can only be the case if humans universally comprehend three-dimensional surfaces. For humans to physically respond to the difficulties of navigating threedimensional terrain, they must have neural mechanisms that allow them to comprehend three-dimensional surfaces. Table 1, adopted from (Kock and Chatelain-Jardón 2011), summarizes this discussion.

Now that we have argued that comprehending three-dimensional surfaces has adaptive value, we would like to go into more detail about what we mean by comprehend. Navigation over a three-dimensional surface involves more than simply avoiding falls. It also involves plotting paths. This requires an organism to be able to estimate the slope and distance of alternative paths with enough precision to make trade-offs between effort and time, as a steep path takes relative more energy, but typically less time. Moreover, pathfinders must be able to do this from a variety of angles, as there is no guarantee that potential paths will be lined up symmetrically. It is also necessary to understand the relation between different terrain features to effectively estimate whether a different, unseen path around a corner might be more promising than a current visible path. Furthermore, pathfinders must make locomotion decisions based on fine three-dimensional terrain features. If two paths have similar overall slopes, but one is much more variable, pathfinders must take that variability into account. All of these calculations involve comprehending three-dimensional surfaces. Pathfinders must have neural mechanisms to allow them to calculate the slope between two points, the relative distance between two points, the rate of change of the functional form of the terrain, and the variability of the terrain; and they must be able to do it from a variety of vantage points. All of these neural mechanisms are also useful for the use of three-dimensional surface graphs. So, when we use the term comprehend we mean that a person is able to make a variety of estimations and calculations about three-dimensional surfaces from multiple viewpoints.

This brings us to the question of exactly what mechanism in the brain comprehends three-dimensional surfaces. In general, visual processing in the human brain is divided into the dorsal stream and ventral stream (Goodale and Milner 1992). The optic nerve runs from the eyes to the posterior part of the brain where the primary visual cortex resides. This area transforms visual signals from the eye into a brain map of what is being observed. This information is then sent forward and up into the dorsal stream, which is the stream that converts the map to locations in space and begins processing information about how to take action on the scene (Goodale and Milner 1992; Ungerleider and Haxby 1994). The information is also sent forward and down into the ventral stream. Figure 1 shows the localization of both the dorsal stream and the ventral stream in the brain.

While the dorsal stream is implicated in both the where and how dimensions of visual processing, “the characterization of the ventral stream as a ‘What’ pathway is relatively uncontroversial” (Kravitz et al. 2011, p. 217). The ventral stream receives the brain map of the vision from the primary visual cortex and determines what is being seen. The ventral stream contains special regions that process faces, body parts, and even tools or houses (Dekker et al. 2011). Whereas the dorsal stream may tell us that there is an object five meters away moving toward us, the ventral stream will tell us whether it is another person or an automobile, and whether it is a person we know or an automobile that we own. Thus, the ventral stream primarily handles the meaning of the scene and the sense-making about a scene.

When viewing a graph, the goal is generally not to take action on the graph, but to understand the meaning of the graph. Understanding a graph should depend mainly on the what of the graph, rather than the where of the graph. Clearly, there is important spatial information that must be processed in a graph, but, more importantly, one must make sense of the gestalt properties of the graph (i.e., how the graph works as a whole; see Baker et al. 2009). The first, and thus far only, neuroscience paper on graphical comprehension supports this finding that graphical comprehension depends mainly on perceptual processing in the ventral stream (Li et al. 2014).

For our theoretical purposes, the important issue is that we believe that there are brain structures evolved to process three-dimensional surfaces natively, but there are no such structures evolved to process bubble graphs. This is because three-dimensional surfaces occur in the evolutionary environment and being able to process them has survival value, thus they are the solutions to an adaptive problem. However, there is no naturally occurring analog to bubble graphs, in the evolutionary environment, so there will be no brain structures that deal directly with understanding bubble representations. Specifically, we hypothesize that visual content of a threedimensional surface graph will be processed natively in the ventral stream. Thus, relative to bubble graphs we expect to find greater activation in the ventral stream for subjects processing three-dimensional surface graphs. Our brain data hypothesis is

## Native Processing Hypothesis: Three-dimensional surface graphs will produce greater activation in the ventral stream than bubble graphs.

The primary advantage to native processing is that it should be better than appropriative processing (Fodor 1983). It should be better because the native structures are specialized to solve the problem at hand. There are a few caveats to consider here. First, we must consider the tasks for which the graph is being used. In this case, the tasks are estimating the relative heights of the points on the three-dimensional surface, and making sample predictions. There are other tasks, such as estimating the relative height of two different points, or estimating area, that we are not considering. In this research, we are only asking subjects to read the graphs; we are not asking them to make a higher-level decision based on the graph.

The second caveat is that better must be defined. Because we are asking subjects to read graphs, there are two main measures of better we use. As we discussed in the literature review, speed and accuracy are two main measures of the effect of the mode of presentation (Benbasat and Schroeder 1977; Dickson et al. 1977). Speed is a good evolutionary variable. Evolutionary theorizing principle three above states that the evolutionary adaption must have reduced the probability of a fitness-impairing event. Certainly, being able to comprehend three-dimensional surfaces faster would have reduced the probability of a fitness-impairing event. Thus, if, as we theorize, the human brain has evolutionary adaptations for comprehending three-dimensional surfaces, we would expect them to be fast. On the other hand, if the human brain lacks adapted structures, then it would have to rely on slower, more deliberative processing, which is what we predict in the case of comprehending bubble graphs. Thus, we hypothesize

Speed Hypothesis: Subjects will be faster at processing three-dimensional surface graphs than bubble graphs.

The second measure of better is accuracy of answering. Again, the application of principle three leads to similar arguments. Accurate processing of three-dimensional surfaces reduced the probability of missteps, which could prove detrimental to fitness. Thus, we hypothesize

Accuracy Hypothesis: Subjects will be more accurate at processing three-dimensional surface graphs than bubble graphs.

## Methodology

To investigate the hypotheses, we conducted two experiments. The experiments are similar in structure and complement one another. Both experiments involve having participants answer a set of questions presented to them using the two types of graphs. In conducting the experiments, we used the fMRI technique to measure brain activity. There are numerous techniques available in the toolbox of neuroscience for this purpose. For this study, fMRI is a particularly appropriate technique as it has a high spatial resolution and a high anatomical coverage. These capabilities allow us to localize specific neural structures involved in processing different types of graphs.

## Experiment 1

## Procedures

The subjects for Experiment 1 were 20 students<sup>5</sup> in a U.S. university, who received course credit for participating in a research subject pool. There were 11 males; 17 were righthand dominant.<sup>6</sup> The average age was 22.05 (st. dev. 1.79). Institutional review board (IRB) approval was obtained prior to the experiment. Upon arrival, the participants were greeted and asked to fill out a brief demographic questionnaire along with a consent form. After completion of the questionnaire and consent form, the participants were given an explanation of the task to be done when inside the MRI scanner. During this explanation, the participant could use a laptop that simulated the controls and interface to be encountered during the experiment to remove confusion about the task.

Once inside the MRI scanner, the participant received a scout scan which captures a low-resolution outline of the head to allow the technician to aim the scanner during subsequent scans, followed by a magnitude and phase scan to allow for B0 correction, and finally, a high resolution T1 scan. These scans took about 10 minutes. After these scans, the participant began the experiment by answering questions related to bubble graphs and three-dimensional surface graphs, while being scanned. The participant had 35 minutes to answer the questions presented and would be stopped if the participant exceeded the 35-minute time line. In all, 75% of participants were able to answer all questions in the stated time period.

## Task

Thirty data sets were created and both a bubble graph and a three-dimensional surface graph were created for each data set. For each of these datasets, two questions were created, which represented an extraction question (answer existing on the graph) and an integration question (answer existing off the graph, thus requiring determining trends in data) (for a discussion about these two types of graph comprehension tasks; see Ratwani et al. 2008). Thus, there were 60 trials presented to subjects. To account for ordering effects or issues with “easier” questions for certain graphs, the questions (extraction or integration) were randomly asked for each graph. All questions and graphs are available upon request. Each subject received one extraction and one integration question from each data set and one bubble and one three-dimensional surface graph from each data set. The questions were balanced so half of the three-dimensional graphs were extraction-based and half of the bubble graphs were extraction-based. However, the question-to-graph pairings were random across subjects. All questions consisted of presenting the participant with three dimensions of information and asking for a value for the fourth dimension of data (see Figure 2). All answer options were presented as ranges of values, with four choices. The ranges were all the same size and each of the choices was correct 25% of the time. After answering a question, the participant would be returned to a blank “rest” screen (displayed for a uniformly distributed time ranging between 0 and 2.5 seconds). This is a common procedure in fMRI studies with the goal of allowing brain activity to go back to its baseline level before the next stimuli is presented (for details, see Dimoka 2012). After completing all the questions or reaching the 35-minute mark, the participants were thanked for their help and released.

The questions were self-paced (i.e., a subject could take as long as they needed to answer the question, and the screen advanced after the subject answered) for several reasons. First, in pretesting we found significant variation in how long it took subjects to answer a question. Thus, we would either need a very long interval for the answers or have a large number of unanswered questions, both of which are problematic. Second, reading graphs is not typically a time constrained activity in the natural environment, so adding a time pressure component could result in confounding effects. Subjects might begin guessing, which is not what we wanted to study, or simply give up, which is also undesirable. Ultimately, we want to study how subjects solve graph problems, not how subjects solve those problems under extreme time pressure. Third, self-paced graphs allowed us to explore the behavioral consequences of different types of graphs; specifically, does the time taken to solve a problem depend on the type of graph? Fourth, it allowed us to collect more data. Not only do we have answer accuracy and speed for each question presented, but we are not wasting time having subjects look at the screen after they make a decision. Finally, it reduces unwanted noise. If a subject chooses and answer, then has to wait before moving on, their brain might be doing something other than solving graphs. If we average together the time they are solving graphs with the time they are doing something else, then we are adding noise to our variable of interest. Thus, for this experiment self-paced answers made more sense than the-fixed time answers often used with simpler stimuli. Figure 3 represents one set of the task performed by the subjects.

To test the speed hypothesis, we ran a random effects model with response time as the dependent variable and graph type, question type, graph type × question type interaction, as regressors, and the subject as a random effect. Type of graph was significant (p-value = 0.0279), but in the opposite direction to what we hypothesized. Type of question did not show a statistically significant effect (p-value = 0.1440). The interaction term was significant (p-value = 0.0253) and negative, indicating that integration questions displayed as surface graphs took less time to solve than would be predicted by each regressor alone. This indicates that subjects were only slower at answering three-dimensional surface graphs of the extraction type. They were faster at three-dimensional integration questions than at bubble graph integration questions. Thus, the speed hypothesis was supported for integration questions but not for extraction questions. We discuss this below. Table 3 presents the results.

Subjects were fastest at answering integration questions in three-dimensional surface graphs (29.49 seconds, st. dev. = 0.94), but slowest at answering extraction questions based on three-dimensional surface graphs (31.99 seconds; st. dev. = 1.00). Integration and extraction questions based on bubble graphs took on average 31.38 (st. dev.= .90) and 30.16 (st. dev. = 0.96) seconds respectively. This is an unexpected result, and rather than offer an ad hoc explanation, we simply note that the speed hypothesis was supported for integration questions, but not for extraction questions. To summarize, behaviorally, subjects were more accurate answering threedimensional graph questions than bubble questions. Speed showed a mixed effect.

## Experiment 2

While Experiment 1 focused on the differences between how humans process bubble graphs versus three-dimensional surface graphs from an evolutionary perspective, Experiment 2 sought to answer questions that arose from the stimulus used in Experiment 1. Specifically, past research has shown that color of an image may be processed differently by the brain (Adleman et al. 2002). Furthermore, the nature of displaying four dimensions of data in a three-dimensional graph necessitated the use of two graphs being shown, which could potentially confound the results of Experiment 1. This experiment compared the bubble graphs and three-dimensional surface graphs using only three dimensions of data to determine if the findings of Experiment 1 were robust.

Experiment 1 examined three-dimensional surface graphs in the context of four-dimensional data. This was because Kumar and Benbasat (2004) examined three-dimensional surface graphs with respect to three-dimensional data and that may have made them unfairly superior to two-dimensional graphs. This does not seem to be the case. However, presenting four-dimensional data on three-dimensional surface graphs results in some issues with neuroimaging studies. The first problem is that we needed to display 2 three-dimensional surface graphs and only 1 four-dimensional bubble graph. The second is that to fit four dimensions onto one graph we needed to use color. Both the quantity difference and the use of color could, and probably do, lead to differences in neural activation. To separate out these two effects, in Experiment 2 we used the same types of graphs to display three dimensions of data. This allowed us to remove the color from the graphs and to display the same number of graphs in both types of trials. In addition, in Experiment 2 the labels/precision on the axes of the graphs were made further consistent. Questions and graphs used in this experiment are available upon request.

To keep the two experiments comparable, we used the same scanning parameters, and the same testing procedures as described above. There were two differences. First, the stimuli were rendered in grayscale with only three dimensions of data, as shown in Figure 7.

The second change is that, in addition to other demographic questions, we assessed subject familiarity with the graph types. We used a seven-point scale for responding to the statement, “I am familiar with this type of graph,” and anchored the scale with strongly agree and strongly disagree. We also used a five-point scale with the question, “How familiar are you with this type of graph?” This scale had the choices “Not at all familiar,” “Slightly familiar,” “Moderately familiar,” “Very familiar,” and “Extremely familiar.” To increase measurement reliability, the five-point Likert scale had maximum familiarity as the highest score, whereas the sevenpoint Likert scale had maximum familiarity as the lowest score. There were no statistically significant differences in familiarly on either scale (4.95 versus 4.00; p-value 0.13 and

1.9 versus 2.3; p-value 0.12), although the direction of the answers by subjects on both scales suggested that they were more familiar with bubble graphs than three-dimensional surface graphs. We were initially surprised by this, but conjecture that while in scientific publications three-dimensional surface graphs are more common, in the popular press bubble graphs are used more and, therefore, college students are probably more accustomed to them. In any case, there is no evidence that a familiarity effect explains the superior performance of three-dimensional surface graphs.

Again we used 20 subjects with an average age of 23.85 (st. dev. = 6.27), 12 males, 17 right-handed. Other details follow the procedure followed in Experiment 1 (e.g., course credit, etc.). We report the findings below. These findings are robust to permutation testing as described in the Appendix.

The performance results followed the same pattern. Accuracy was significantly higher for three-dimensional surface graphs than bubble graphs as was time to complete the graphs (speed). Tables 6 and 7 as well as Figure 8 present the results. As the graphs were simpler in this experiment, accuracy in both conditions went up and time to complete graphs went down. Just as in the previous experiment, threedimensional surface graph questions took longer to answer, but subjects were more likely to be correct.

For the brain imaging results, we used the same analysis as above and find similar findings. Tables 8 and 9 present the results. For three-dimensional surface graphs, there is greater activation in the ventral stream as hypothesized. This is shown in Figure 9. Thus, again our natural processing hypothesis is supported in a second sample using only three dimensions of data.

There were some additional areas of activation for threedimensional surface graphs that were not present in the previous experiment. These seem to correspond roughly to the dorsal attention network (Beckmann et al. 2005; Kastner et al. 1998; Ptak 2012; Yuan et al. 2016). We have no theory to explain these additional activations or why they appear in this experiment but not the first one, and thus must leave this as a topic for future research.

The reverse contrast, looking at areas where there were greater activations for bubble graphs, showed a single large cluster of activation in the cuneus, particularly on the righthand side. This area was also more active for bubble graphs in Experiment 1. This region is activated frequently in studies of numerosity. The standard stimuli in numerosity studies are a field of circles, which are visually very similar to bubble graphs. Unlike Experiment 1, there was not a great deal of additional activation beyond this region, as shown in Figure 10.

## Discussion

## Theoretical Contributions

In this research, we argue that the human brain contains evolutionary adaptions for processing three-dimensional surfaces, which allows it to comprehend three-dimensional surface graphs better than bubble graphs, for which there is no such adaptation. Evolutionary theorizing allowed us to build “IS theories that correspond to the brain’s functionality,” which is among the unique opportunities that cognitive neuroscience research provides (Dimoka et al. 2011, p. 688). When answering questions about three-dimensional surface graphs, subjects showed greater activation in a set of structures in the ventral stream, which is where the brain integrates the various components of a visual image to understand what is being viewed. Conversely, bubble graphs gave rise to a diffuse set of brain areas in Experiment 1 and concentrated activation in the primitive visual cortex in Experiment 2. Moreover, we found that subjects are much more accurate (although not consistently faster) at answering questions using three-dimensional surface graphs than they are when using bubble graphs.

Overall, these results suggest that when subjects viewed three-dimensional surface graphs, they made more accurate decisions because they used the best-optimized set of brain areas, areas that are known to be important for understanding what is being viewed. We believe they use these brain regions because evolution has endowed Homo sapiens (and many other sighted animals) with very powerful machinery for processing the meaning and implications of threedimensional surfaces.

Our findings should not be misinterpreted as saying threedimensional surface graphs are the best sorts of graphs. Rather, what we claim is that graphs that can be processed natively are the best sorts of graphs. Natively means that there are brain structures, adapted by evolutionary pressures in the natural environment, to address the sort of visualizing produced by the graph. Three-dimensional surface graphs are one of these, but there are likely to be other types of visualizations that are also adaptive. One example might be graphs based on facial recognition, like the Wong-Baker pain scale (Wong and Baker 2001). The important point is that there are graphs that represent data in a way the brain is evolved to interpret, and graphs that represent data in a way the brain is ill equipped to interpret, because there was never any evolutionary pressure to develop an arrangement of neurons that would quickly and efficiently process that sort of image.

To be sure, brains are plastic and can recruit regions that are specialized for task X through evolution to help with task Y. With training and practice, the brain’s ability to do the processing needed for task Y will increase. However, the whole point of a graphical representation is to present data in a way that is intuitively obvious.

Our theory is slightly different than cognitive fit theory (Vessey 1991), which is often applied in the study of graphical comprehension (Dennis and Carte 1998; Huang et al. 2006; Umanath and Vessey 1994). Cognitive fit argues for a correspondence between task and information presentation. Evolutionary fit argues for a correspondence between information presentation and evolutionarily adaptive brain structures. These theories are not in conflict; they simply examine different links in the chain from task to information presentation to comprehension.

In addition to a comparison of graphs, we propose a neural correlate of good graphs. Good graphs are those that comparatively cause more activation in the ventral stream. The ventral stream is the part of the brain built by evolution to solve the adaptive problem of understanding what is being seen. The ventral stream contains multiple structures that process different parts of the meta-task of understanding what is being seen, so there are likely multiple ways of understanding what is being seen. Nonetheless, the ventral stream is the primary area for structures that understand visual inputs, and it is the ventral stream that forwards the meaning of visual input to the rest of the brain for further processing. Thus, graphs that stimulate the ventral stream more will tend to be those that are being processed natively, and hence that are best understood.

Our findings in the domain of visualization correspond closely to the findings of media naturalness theory (MNT) in the domain of technology-mediated communication (Kock 2009). Based on the evolutionary argument that face-to-face interaction was the only mode of communication in our ancestral past, MNT predicts this mode of communication to be the most natural and “fluent.” The theory predicts any electronic media laying to the left or to the right of face-toface communication on the “scale of naturalness” to be less effective (Kock 2005). This is an analogue to what we find about data visualizations: that a three-dimensional graph, as a type of graph that most closely resembles natural scenes, outperforms other modes of visualization.

We find visualizations that are either “simpler” than threedimensional graphs (as shown with two-dimensional graphs by Kumar and Benbasat (2004)), and those that are “richer” than three-dimensional graphs (as shown with fourdimensional bubble graphs in Experiment 1) to be inferior to three-dimensional surface graphs. Also, according to MNT, any dissimilarity between an electronic media and face-toface communication will necessitate a “compensatory encoding” process (Kock 2007) on the part of the receiver, increasing the cognitive effort required to comprehend the message while also reducing the effectiveness of the communication channel. This too is parallel to our observation about the need for appropriative processing (as opposed to native processing) in the brain when interpreting graphs with no naturally occurring correspondents.

## Implications for Practice

Data visualization has become an important method for representing information in user interfaces. From graphics in Excel, to proprietary packages like Periscope or open source solutions like D3.js, graphics are increasingly being added to information systems. While there is a great deal of interest in capturing data and representing it graphically, currently there is little knowledge about how human brains make use of these graphs. In an insightful reflection on his decades of research and practice of visualization, Few (2009) advocates the use of visualization tools and techniques that make it possible to “think with the eyes.” He describes good visualization as one that “takes the burden of effort off brain and puts it on the eyes” (Few 2017). We take this a step further and offer a scientific theory about how the eyes might think, and what sorts of visualizations they might be good at thinking about. We conjecture that Few’s “eyes” are in the ventral stream, and that the ventral stream is adapted by evolution to understand naturalistic graphs. The use of fMRI allows us to test this conjecture, and thus allows us the ability to offer additional insights for IS practice.

First, we have found that three-dimensional surface graphs facilitate comprehension more than bubble graphs. Bubble graphs are based on Gapminder’s Trendalyzer software, which was acquired by Google in 2007 for an undisclosed amount. Thus, at least one company spent money to specifically acquire bubble graph software. It would certainly be worth it for Google to have known before acquiring the company how much bubble graphs facilitate comprehension relative to three-dimensional surface graphs. In general, it is worthwhile for designers of interfaces to know what effect their chosen graphs will have on the users of the interface.

In addition to our findings, Kumar and Benbasat (2004) also found three-dimensional surface graphs do dominate twodimensional graphs. Thus, the simple finding is to use threedimensional surface graphs when possible, or at least in preferences to bubble or line graphs. Of course, similar to the use of any other type of graph, the use of surface graphs can involve certain limitations. Occlusion (where some parts of the graph are obscured by some other parts), for example, can be a problem when representing certain datasets. Provisioning for interactive rotation of surface graphs, when possible, can help to overcome this limitation. Also, like other types of graphs, surface graphs can be made more informative by incorporating additional features such as brushing (selecting a data point on the graph and seeing additional information about it).

In general, our prescription is to use graphical interfaces that are processed natively by the human brain. The only type we are currently confident about is three-dimensional surface graphs, but there are potentially others. The important thing is that not all graphs are comprehended equally. Therefore, organizations that use and organizations that design graphics need to understand which graphs are natively processed by the human brain to encourage comprehension.

Of course, comprehension does not need to be the goal of including a graph in an interface. Persuasion, comfort, or even entertainment could also be reasons to include graphs. Thus, before including a graph in an interface, the system designer should ask what is the purpose of the graph. If the purpose of the graph happens to be comprehension, then graphs that are processed natively in general, and threedimensional surface graphs specifically are good choices. Also, consistent with prior research, in our experiments we investigated extraction and integration as the two main question types. There may be other types of questions (e.g., finding approximate values, persuasion, or high-level decision making) that can be studied in future research.

## Limitations

There are several limitations of this work that suggest directions for additional research. The first is that this work only looks at two different types of graph, thus additional research is needed to see how three-dimensional surface graphs compare to other representations of three dimensions of data. Furthermore, this work only postulates one type of natural graph. Other types likely exist—for example, the Wong-Baker pain scale (Wong et al. 2001)—and future research is needed to identify them. Finally, as with any evolutionary theory, separating nature from nurture is difficult. While our subjects did not indicate any differences in their experience with the two types of graphs we examined, it is worthwhile to perform further research to examine the effects of training on graphical comprehension.

## Conclusion

Information systems are supposed to convert data into information that can then be used to make decisions. As the quantity and variety of data that can be brought to bear on a decision increases, it becomes necessary to present complex multidimensional data to decision makers in a high bandwidth, intuitive manner. Graphs are an ideal instrument for achieving this end, which is why data visualization is one of the most important topics in information systems today. To design good data visualizations, researchers need to have an in-depth understanding of how to convert data into graphs that facilitate decision making. Unfortunately, while there is a rich literature on how to convert data into a dizzying array of different visualizations, there is very little research on how the brain processes these visualizations. This is unfortunate because it is the brain that converts a graph from a set of colored pixels into a set of mental constructs that can be manipulated to make decisions (Pinker 1990). Therefore, to fulfill the mission of designing information systems that provide good data visualizations for decision making, it is imperative that we understand how the brain processes data visualizations. In the absence of brain theories, there cannot be serious scientific inquiry into data visualizations.

In this work, we develop an evolutionary theory of brain function that proposes that the brain has adaptive structures to process three-dimensional surfaces. We further propose that these brain structures can be used to natively process threedimensional surface graphs, leading to better comprehension and hence better accuracy in reading graphs and in estimating graph trends. Finally, we propose that these structures are located in the ventral stream of the visual system. This last point is particularly powerful as it puts the brain squarely in the spotlight. Moreover, it allows future researchers to draw upon an abundant literature on brain function to develop new and better models of data visualization, and ultimately the data visualizations themselves.

In addition to the conclusion that three-dimensional surface graphs are more effective than bubble graphs, we have higherlevel implications about how to research data visualizations. The fact that we can pinpoint the structures implicated in three-dimensional surface graphs, suggests that there is something hardwired into human brains by evolution that can be brought to bear on the problem. This suggests that there may be other brain structures developed by evolution that facilitate the comprehension of other types of graphs. A large literature in evolutionary psychology (Cosmides and Tooby 1994; Kock and Chatelain-Jardón 2011) gives us methodology to search for other such brain structures, and hence for other highly effective types of data visualizations. Demonstrating not only behavioral differences in graph comprehension, but also that there is a specific structure associated with those differences, strongly argues for the need for evolutionary theory in the study of data visualizations.

In conclusion, this paper contributes to the literature on data visualizations not only by showing that three-dimensional surface graphs are more comprehensible, but also in developing a theory of why they are more comprehensible and where the brain makes them more comprehensible. Threedimensional surface graphs are more comprehensible because the evolutionary environment of humans was made up of three-dimensional surfaces, which humans had to comprehend in order to survive. Natural selection instantiated a variety of computational capacities for understanding three-dimensional surfaces into the ventral stream of the brain, and these computational capacities can be used to comprehend threedimensional surface graphs. So, if you have three dimensions of data, and you want decision makers to comprehend it, a three-dimensional surface graph is a good choice of data visualization.

## Appendix

In this appendix, we include a variety of nontechnical information, technical details, explanations, and robustness tests. We follow the methodology of Dimoka (2012). We will offer a brief outline here, but for a more detailed and complete explanation, see Dimoka’s study. We try to explain concepts and procedures in laymen’s terms where possible, but some details are, by nature, technical. We offer those details in the technical section and omit some of them from the nontechnical section.

## Nontechnical Section

The variable that we measure is the blood oxygen level dependent contrast, or BOLD contrast for short. BOLD is simply the amount of oxygenated blood in a region of the brain. We must use a contrast because there is always oxygenated blood everywhere in the brain. A lack of oxygenated blood in the brain causes death. So, we measure the difference in oxygenated blood during different tasks. Specifically, fo our work, we measure the differences between the task of answering a question about a bubble graph and a three-dimensional surface graph. There is always oxygenated blood in all regions of the brain when solving bubble graphs and when solving three-dimensional surface graphs, but there is more in some regions and less in others

The scanner divides the brain into voxels, which in our case are 3 mm cubes. For each voxel, we measure the BOLD signal every 2.5 seconds. Note that the scanner measures in slices rather than measuring the whole brain at once. It can cause measurement issues if the onset of a task and the onset of a scan occur at the same time because one part of the brain is being measured when the task starts and another part of the brain 2.5 seconds later. We control for this in two ways. First, between each trial we add a random time delay that is uniform between 0 and 2.5 seconds. This is equivalent to starting each scan at a random place in the brain and completely removes correlation between stimulus onset time and when a certain region of the brain is scanned. Second, rather than scan from top to bottom in 2.5 seconds we scan every other slice (e.g., slice 0, 2, 4, 6, …) in 1.25 seconds then scan the other slices (e.g., 1, 3, 5, 7, …) in the next 1.25 seconds. Along with smoothing that we do, this helps remove temporal artifacts based on slice order. Ideally, we would scan slices at random, but the physics of scanners makes that intractable at short scan times of 2.5 seconds.

When neurons do work, they require oxygenated blood to replenish their ability to do more work. However, oxygenated blood does not flow instantly to neurons in a brain region. The oxygen level builds up, peaks at about 6 seconds, then tapers off when demand decreases. This is called the hemodynamic response function (HRF). Thus, while a task might start at time t1 and continue to time t2, the oxygenated blood flow (which we measure) will begin to ramp up at t1, peak at t1+6s, then begin to taper off from t2 to around t2+6 seconds. Thus, we must convert all dummy variables into variables that ramp up and ramp down with the hemodynamic response function. We do this by convolving our timing with a gamma function.

For example, if we have a screen that is up from 3 seconds to 6 seconds we have a dummy variable that looks like Figure A1. A gamma function with a mean of 6 seconds and a standard deviation of 3 seconds looks like Figure A2. When we convolve the two we get a signal that looks like Figure A3.

Henceforth we will refer to our timings as dummy variables, but technically they are dummy variables convolved with gamma distributions to account for the hemodynamic response function.

For each voxel, we have an observation of oxygenated blood levels every 2.5 seconds. We take these numeric observations and fit a regression of the form $\mathrm { Y } = \beta \mathbf { X } + \mathrm { e r r o r } .$ Y is the level of activation as measured by the scanner. Note, activation is the numerical value the scanner produces and is proportional to oxygenated blood levels, but it is not oxygenated blood level;it is signal strength from nuclear decay. X is a set of vectors of dummy variables measuring which screen the subjects were observing at a specific time (convolved with gamma functions). β is a vector of parameters to be estimated. The error term is corrected for autocorrelation using prewhitening (Woolrich et al. 2001).

The main model we report has the form

$$
\text { Activation } _ {\mathrm{t}} = \beta 1 \text { Bubble\_Extraction } _ {\mathrm{t}} + \beta 2 \text { Bubble\_Integration } _ {\mathrm{t}} + \beta 3 \text { Surface\_Extraction } _ {\mathrm{t}} + \beta 4 \text { Surface\_Integration } _ {\mathrm{t}} + \beta 5 \text { Fixation } _ {\mathrm{t}}
$$

In this case Bubble\_Extraction means that the subject is viewing the screen where a bubble graph is being paired with an extraction question. The other dummy variables are obvious, except the fixation dummy, which refers to the time between the other screens. Note that this regression is run for each voxel, where there are approximately 50,000 3mm voxels in a typical brain.

To test the hypothesis that bubble graphs yield greater responses than three-dimensional surface graphs in a particular voxel, we test the hypothesis $\beta I + \beta 2 > \beta 3 + \beta 4$ . This yields 50,000 t-tests. We convert the t-values to z-values (i.e., the normal distribution) so that the maps are comparable without having to account for degrees of freedom. It is not reasonable to display the outcomes of each of these tests. Moreover, spatial location is important. Rather than produce a table of coordinates and z-values, we spatially project the z-values onto an image of th brain and use color to denote the z-values. This will give us the image shown in Figure A4, where at voxel 12, -72, -8 (the location of the cross hairs) the t-value is 5.16362. As the color moves from red to yellow, the z-value increases along the positive axis; as the color moves from blue to white, the z-value decreases along the negative axis. Roughly speaking, yellow voxels are significantly positive and light blue voxels are significantly negative.

## Technical Details

The scans were done using a 3-tesla Siemens Skyra scanner. Functional images had a repetition time of 2500 ms, echo time of 30 ms, and flip angle of 90 degrees. 44 transverse slices were acquired in an interleaved fashion. Voxels were 3mm<sup>3</sup>. The number of volumes taken depended on how fast subjects answered but was limited to 35 minutes. High-resolution T1 images with 192 slices in the sagittal plane were also taken with a voxel resolution of .9375 mm × .9375 mm and a slice thickness of .9000 mm.

The functional images were registered to the T1 images using FSL’s FLIRT (Jenkinson et al. 2002; Jenkinson and Smith 2001) BBR algorithm (Greve and Fischl 2009). The high resolution images were then registered to the standard 2mm Montreal Neurological Institute 152 brain using

FSL’s FNIRT (Andersson et al. 2007a, 2007b) using a warp resolution of 10 mm and a 12 degree of freedom search. Motion correction was done using FSL’s MCFLIRT (Jenkinson et al. 2002). B0 unwarping was carried out using an effective echo spacing of 0.58 ms. a change in echo time of 2.46 ms, and a signal loss threshold of 10% (Jenkinson 2003, 2004). Images were spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a 5mm width. High pass filtering was applied using a least-squares straight-line fitting, with a normal kernel with a sigma of 50 seconds Non-brain matter was extracted using BET (Smith 2002).

To analyze the fMRI data, a least squares model was fit to the individual level data. Five dummy variables were created for the time periods that corresponded to (1) fixation times, (2) answering in-sample surface graph questions, (3) answering out-of-sample surface graph questions, (4) answering in-sample bubble graph questions, and (5) answering out-of-sample bubble graph questions. In the paper, we also include a continuous variable measuring the visual complexity of each image. For each of the screens subjects viewed, we calculated the visual complexity score (Rosenholtz et al. 2007).<sup>1</sup> We then centered the score and used this as an additional regressor to explain neural activation. Each variable was convolved with a gamma function with a lag of 6 seconds and a standard deviation of 3 seconds to approximate the hemodynamic response function (Woolrich and Jenkinson 2004). We added temporal derivatives to compensate for minor differences in the hemodynamic response function. Statistical parametric maps generated at the first level were all converted into standardized (2mm MNI152) space, and a higher-level random effects analysis was run on the standardized data using FSL’s FLAME 1 + 2 (Woolrich Behrens 2004). Images were thresholded at a z-value of 2.3 and then random field theory was used to retain only clusters of activation that were less than 5% likely to have occurred by chance (Worsley 2001). This corrects for multiple comparisons.

## Robustness Tests

There are a variety of ways to control for false positives in whole brain activation maps. We use cluster-based thresholding based on FSL’s Flame (Woolrich Behrens 2004). FSL’s Flame1 was found to have a false positive rate under the null hypothesis close to the predicted false positive rate (Eklund et al. 2016). We use Flame1+2, which runs Flame1 then takes all the values that are close to the margin and preforms Markov chain Monte Carlo simulation to get an enhanced measure on those voxels. Flame1 is relatively fast, but Flame1+2 is computationally intensive, which is presumably why it was not tested. In principle, Flame1+2 should be at least as good as Flame1, but that is currently unknown.

While Flame1 showed good statistical properties, other cluster-based thresholding methods showed inflated false positive rates, and thus alternative methods have been recently proposed (Eklund et al. 2016). The first method is permutation testing (Winkler et al. 2014). Unfortunately, true permutation testing only works for a multi-sample t-test. For a one sample t-test, we use a sign flipping procedure, for which the statistical properties are not consistent (Eklund et al. 2016)

A second method is to use voxel-wise thresholding instead of cluster-based thresholding (Poline et al. 1997). Voxel-wise inference is known to be overly conservative, but it does eliminate the false positive problem. Actually, it causes the false positive problem to manifest in the opposite direction—too few false positives—but it is worth examining.

The third method is to increase the cluster defining threshold from 2.3 (one-sided p-value of 0.01) to 3.1 (one-sided p-value of 0.001). It is not clear why this helps, but it is conjectured that the assumptions underlying cluster-based thresholding are more realistic for small clusters, and a higher cluster defining threshold generates smaller clusters (Eklund et al. 2016).

While none of these methods are a panacea for the issues surrounding one sample t-tests, they all produce similar results in this sample, which suggests that our results are robust.

For our permutation test, we used FSL’s Randomise (Winkler et al. 2014) to perform 100,000 permutations and a cluster-defining threshold of 2.3. Following Eklund et al. (2016) we use cluster mass thresholding.

The results are shown in Figure A7. Note that Randomise reports the whole cluster p-value rather than individual voxel p-values. Thus, th cluster is a uniform color.

## Other Tests

Early neuroimaging studies excluded left-handed individuals from studies because of potential lateralization issues. However, this has created problems for scientific learning in an era of large-scale databases of subjects. Moreover, there are ethical issues with excluding any demographic based on inborn differences which are merely correlated with problems, particularly when subjects are compensated. Thus, following developing scientific standards for inclusiveness (Willems et al. 2014), we include left-handed individuals. While we do not propose lateralization hypotheses, for robustness we statistically test the differences between right-handed and left-handed individuals in our study. There were no significant differences between left-handed and right-handed individuals in this study. This could be due to the power of the test. However, a comparison of the right-handed individuals to the whole sample shows similar patterns across the two samples and are shown in Figure A9.
