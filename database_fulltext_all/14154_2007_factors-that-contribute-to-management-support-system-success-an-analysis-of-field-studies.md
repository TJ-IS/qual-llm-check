# Factors that contribute to management support system success: An analysis of field studies

Edward Hartono <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Radhika Santhanam <sup>b</sup>, Clyde W. Holsapple <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Administrative Science University of Alabama in Huntsville, United States Gatton College of Business and Economics University of Kentucky, United States

Received 9 June 2005; received in revised form 6 September 2006; accepted 13 September 2006 Available online 27 October 2006

## Abstract

This study is conducted to summarize and consolidate core success antecedents of management support system (MSS) implementations that have been identified in empirical studies. This study quantitatively analyzes correlations of success antecedents to success measures and ranks them in order of importance by using the formulas advocated in prior research. Results indicate there is no one dominant success antecedent across all success measures and sub-types of MSS. Rather, the importance of a success antecedent depends on the type of success measure and the type of system. Therefore, the implication is that when organizations implement systems, they must prioritize the outcome that they want to focus on and pay attention to the corresponding success antecedent. The findings also provide information and guidelines for the selection of factors to be investigated in future research on MSS implementation. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Implementation; Management support systems; Success antecedents

## 1. Introduction

Management support systems (MSS) are comprised of systems that support managerial work, and provide functionality to summarize and analyze business information [24]. These systems have also been defined as computer based system that collect (or retrieve), store, process, and distribute information to support decision making and control in an organization. A number of researchers have classified and described these system [3,12,13]. Based on these classifications/taxonomies, this study considers the following MSS: Decision Support Systems, Expert Systems, Data Warehouses, Group Decision Support Systems, Organizational Decision Support Systems, and Executive Information and Management Information Systems. The definitions and characteristics of these systems are provided in Table 1.

Organizations implement MSS in order to improve the delivery of information to decision makers and to support their decision making activities. Yet, studies indicate that not all systems are successfully implemented and anticipated benefits are not always realized [35,44]. It is no wonder, then, that both researchers and practitioners are very keen on identifying those factors, which we refer to as success antecedents, that contribute to a successful MSS implementation [1,16,20,21,29,38,40,42,52,53]. In fact, a survey by Lai and Mahapatra [33] indicates that this topic is among the most frequently discussed issues in information system (IS) research.

Success antecedents are those key factors that organizations can manage so that the MSS system is favorably received and the implementation is deemed as successful. Research studies have evaluated a variety of constructs as success antecedents. These include among others, degree of management support [1,16,20,21,40,42,52,53], nature of organizational attitudes and commitment [16], perceived user friendliness of the system [42], degree of developer skills [1,16,42,52], task characteristics such as degree of problem difficulty supported by the system [20,21,40,53], level of user experience [20,21,38,40], level of user participation [16,20,21,29,42,52,53], degree of perceived value [16], and level of user training [20,40,42].

Studies have also employed an array of constructs to measure outcomes that denote a successful MSS implementation. These include constructs such as degree of decision making satisfaction [16,20,21,29,38,40,42,52,53], degree of system usage [16,38], degree of information quality [16,38], degree of system quality [1,16,52,53], degree of improved decision quality [40,42,52], and degree of improved business performance [42,52].

While these studies have definitely contributed to a better understanding of factors that facilitate a successful

MSS implementation, research findings in this stream are very fragmented, and therefore, not in a useful form. Although these individual studies are rich in content, there is hardly any consolidation of results, nor an understanding of how these study results interrelate, and no identification of the cumulative knowledge that has been gained.

Here, we review empirical studies on MSS implementation success and organize their various success antecedents into 9 classes. Moreover, we examine the strength of relationship between these classes and success measures. The antecedent classes are then ranked based on strengths of their impacts on system success. By identifying patterns or specific antecedents that rank high in terms of their impacts on MSS implementation success, the results can help managers and researchers decide on a few key antecedents that they must focus on to achieve MSS implementation success. This is important given that organizations have to deal with limited resources of funds and time.

## 2. Literature review

IS researchers have been attempting to identify ways to successfully implement IS [32]. Early research suggested that managers identify and use organizational critical success factors in the planning and development of IS [5,36,39]. Then, implementation models were proposed to draw managers' attention to the different stages in implementing an IS, with suggestions on how to effectively manage each stage [6,32]. These models spurred some interest in identifying factors that can help achieve implementation success [19]. Theory-based models that identify only a few system specific characteristics to increase system usage have been proposed [8]. Despite the abundance of work on identifying factors that lead to system success, research findings remain fragmented and relationships among these factors have not been identified [34,45].

Due to this lack of organization, several researchers have attempted to consolidate findings from variance analysis studies and develop an organizing framework. For example, DeLone and McLean [8] reviewed measures of IS implementation success (the dependent variables) and organized them into a six factor taxonomy. This taxonomy lists and captures the multidimensionality and interdependence in IS implementation success measures.

Seddon [43] extended this original model and, recently DeLone and McLean [9] updated their original model. The consolidation of dependent measures into an IS success model is very useful and is now being widely used to help researchers and practitioners select and use categories of success measures.

Shaw [45] examines the category of IS implementation success factors that have been identified in implementation research studies and organizes them into an IS implementation hierarchy. Shaw suggests that in order to achieve implementation success, organizations must pay attention to, and successfully manage, the lower level technical factors listed in the hierarchy before tackling the higher level organizational factors.

Hwang et al. [28] conduct a meta-analysis of 82 studies that specifically focus on environmental variables that affect system implementation success. They validate a system success model that relates 6 independent variables (external environment, organizational environment, user environment, IS operations environment, IS development environment, and information system) that they term as environmental variables to 4 measures of system success (use, satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact). Their study shows that environmental variables are most strongly correlated with organizational impact.

More recently, Larsen [34] examines the antecedents that lead to IS implementation success (the independent variables). Larsen reviews several thousand articles published in eleven journals between 1956 and 1999 to conceptualize antecedents to IS implementation success. Arguing that research findings on success antecedents must be systemized, Larsen identifies similarities in antecedents and groups them into meaningful categories. From the vast number of studies, 83 focal concepts are identified (e.g. task uncertainty, personal innovativeness, and management support). Based on similarities, these concepts are structured into a twelve-category taxonomy that includes categories such as Task, IT and Support, IS Expertise. This categorization scheme highlights areas that are highly researched and those that are not. This taxonomy also helps researchers and practitioners choose and use factors from various categories and understand the relationships among them.

These reviews provide a good synthesis of the many studies on IS implementation success and much useful information to IS practitioners and researchers. But in this paper, we focus on one class of systems generally referred to as management support systems and examine sub-category systems within this class. We also examine the full range of success antecedents and success measures and do not constrain these to a few factors as was done in some prior studies. In MSS implementation, it is not clear which of the antecedents are most effective and correlated strongly with implementation success. If this can be identified, then researchers and practitioners can focus on those few antecedents that have the highest impact. Secondly, do the strengths and impacts of these antecedents differ across system types?

As Larsen [34] suggests, the large volume of available research makes the goal of understanding all IS implementation success antecedent research in one step unattainable (p. 170). Hence, we choose to focus on a single IS class (i.e., MSS) that contains notable diversity. Consolidating within this relatively narrow scope, to come up with specific findings and a framework that can be later expanded and tested, furnishes a useful stepping stone to reach the larger goal of understanding IS success antecedents and outcomes. Moreover, it provides specific information about MSS success outcomes and their antecedents. For example, a firm that is implementing an expert system can use the results to identify factors that are most important for its successful implementation.

## 3. Data collection and summarization

The objective of this study is to examine the strength of relationships between MSS success antecedents and success measures. Our review indicates that a diverse set of study variables and contexts have been addressed. Hence, it does not lend itself to a quantitative, metaanalytic approach to synthesize the findings. Therefore, as we describe below, we use a four-step procedure to achieve our study goals.

## 3.1. Step one. Identification of published studies to be included

We identify and collect empirical studies that investigate the success antecedents for implementation of various types of MSS as defined in Table 1. Hence, if the study was a qualitative or descriptive study, it was not included. We examine published studies in peerreviewed IS journals. We exclude publications in conference proceedings because this research usually is either formative or appears later in major peer-reviewed IS journals. Thus, duplication is avoided. The search for relevant studies was conducted in several online journal databases (e.g., Science Direct, EBSCOHost, and Info Trac) that typically contain journals listed in IS journal rankings [15,22,25,37,49,51], yielding only about 20 published empirical studies that related MSS success antecedents to MSS success measures.

Furthermore, because our interest is on identifying correlations of MSS success antecedents to MSS success measures, we include those studies that provide Pearson's correlation-coefficients between antecedents and success measures. Further, because we can transform Kendall's correlation-coefficients to Pearson's using a method provided by Kruskal [31], we also include studies that provide Kendall's correlationcoefficients. This results in 10 unique published studies (see Table 2) that are further analyzed here.

## 3.2. Step two. Compilation of MSS implementation success antecedents, MSS implementation success measures, and correlation coefficients between them

Each of the 10 studies identifies a set of MSS implementation success antecedents (i.e., the independent variables), MSS implementation success measures (i.e., the dependent variables, measures of success), and correlation coefficients between them. These three types of data are compiled and organized into a list of 276 items. Each item consists of a success antecedent that correlates to a success measure.

The studies use various constructs to measure MSS implementation success. We consolidate and synthesize the constructs using DeLone and McLean's taxonomy of IS implementation success measures [8,9,43]. Each success measure in the data is mapped into its corresponding success measure category in DeLone and McLean's taxonomy: satisfaction, information use, information quality, system quality, individual impact, organizational impact. The mappings are shown in Table 3.

For example, some studies use “end-users have been satisfied with the experience of the meeting supported by the system” [29], “end-users have been satisfied with the output of the meeting supported by the system” [29], “end-users have been satisfied with the experience of the decision making process supported by the system” [20], “end-users have been satisfied with the output of the decision making process supported by the system” [40,42], and “end-users have been satisfied with the overall experience of using the system” [20,21,38,40] as MSS implementation success measures. All these measures fall within the definition of the category labeled “satisfaction” by DeLone and McLean [8,9,43]. Hence, all these measures are relabeled as belonging to the “satisfaction” category.

DeLone and McLean's six success measure categories. Group 1 consists of antecedents that correlate to satisfaction, group 2 consists of antecedents that correlate to information use, group 3 consists of antecedents that correlate to information quality, group 4 consists of antecedents that correlate to system quality, group 5 consists of antecedents that correlate to individual impact, and group 6 consists of antecedents that correlate to organizational impact. For each of these six success measure groups, we develop a rank of antecedents based on the Pearson's correlation coefficient values.

After examining each group, we find that authors use various labels for success antecedents. We even find different labels are used for similar constructs. Creating a rank using varied labels of success antecedents would yield a complicated framework that would not be particularly meaningful. Thus, we group these different antecedents into broad semantic categories, as was done for the success measures. Based on similarities among the definitions and the items used to measure the success antecedent, two researchers not associated with the data collection were asked to relabel the success antecedents. There were a few differences among the two researchers coding results. For example, one coder had categorized the description of a measure “user finds the system to be valuable” into system characteristics while the other had categorized it into user perceived benefits. The senior authors made a decision to categorize this into user perceived benefits. Similarly, one coder had categorized the description of a measure “organization has adequate resources to the system implementation” into management support while the other had categorized this into organizational characteristics. The senior authors made a decision to categorize this into organizational characteristics. In all there were about half a dozen discrepancies.

Some studies use “the system is user-friendly (easyto-use)” [42], “the system has a good data source system” [52], and “the system is interactive” [38] as MSS success antecedents. These antecedents are judged to be similar in that all are related to system characteristics. Hence, they are grouped into a single antecedent category that is labeled as system characteristics. The aggregate Pearson's correlation-coefficient for these three antecedents is calculated for the category of system development characteristics.

Similarly, some studies use the label “system developers possess necessary skills and attributes” [21,52,53], “system development tools possess necessary attributes” [21,52,53], and “system development strategy is good” [38] as success antecedents. The definitions and items in the measures of these antecedents are similar in that they refer to system development characteristics. Hence, we compute the aggregate Pearson's correlation-coefficient for these antecedents for the category of system development characteristics.

The final set of antecedent categories is comprised of: Management Support, Organizational Characteristics, System Characteristics, System Development Characteristics, Task Characteristics, User Experience, User Participation, User Perceived Benefits, and User Training. This is not unexpected, as we are investigating only one class of IS.

For instance, the success antecedent category of Management Support has an aggregate Pearson's correlation-coefficient calculated as the weighted average of Pearson's correlation-coefficients of all antecedents within it.

In order to rank MSS success antecedent categories for each MSS implementation success measure category, we compare the aggregate values of Pearson's correlation-coefficients. Because each aggregate Pearson's correlation-coefficient is standardized by the sample size, it is fair to compare aggregate Pearson's correlation-coefficients among various categories and identify which of these contribute most significantly to a MSS implementation success measure category. A success antecedent category that has a higher aggregate Pearson's correlation-coefficient can be seen as being more influential to the success realized in terms of the corresponding MSS implementation success measure category. In other words, the highest Pearson's correlation indicates that empirical field studies have found this success antecedent category to be most highly associated with the specific success measure category. As an example, for the satisfaction success measure category, we find that the aggregate Pearson's correlation coefficient for User Training with Satisfaction is greater than that of Management Support with Satisfaction. Therefore, for the success measure category Satisfaction, User Training ranks higher than Management Support and is considered more important as an antecedent of satisfaction success.

## 3.4. Step four. Ranking the success antecedent based on the type of system

In addition to ranking each MSS implementation success antecedent category for each MSS implementation success measure category, we also rank each MSS implementation success antecedent category for each type of MSS (i.e., Data Warehouse, Decision Support System, Executive/Management Information System, Expert System, Group Support System, and Organizational Decision Support System).

For each type of MSS, the MSS success antecedents are grouped and ranked using the same procedure described in Step 3. We divide the list of 276 antecedents and success measures into six groups based on the type of

MSS. For each of these six MSS groups, we develop a rank of antecedents by comparing the aggregate Pearson's correlation coefficient values between MSS success antecedent categories and the success measure. In this case, all success measures are assumed to be equivalent and scores are aggregated over all the measures of success. This assumption is necessary so that we can group the success antecedents based on the MSS type.

## 4. Results and discussion

For each MSS success measure category, the set of MSS implementation success antecedent categories that positively correlate to this measure is presented in Table 5. Each set of antecedents is sorted based on the degrees of correlation with the success measure. Given that organizations have to deal with limited resources of funds and time, the results can assist managers in deciding on a few key antecedents that they must focus on to achieve a particular kind of implementation success for a management support system. For example, in optimizing the impact of an implemented system to the overall organizational performance, managers can allocate more resources to develop certain organizational characteristics that contribute to implementation success, as well as to invite individual user's participation in the system development and implementation project.

Table 6 shows rankings of MSS implementation success antecedent categories for each MSS type. The rankings are based on the degrees of correlation between antecedent categories and success measures. Because of resource limitations, implementers may want to focus on those antecedents that are most important for the particular type of MSS being implemented. To implement a data warehouse, for instance, managers can focus on ensuring various system development characteristics and securing support from management.

The results shown in Table 5 are somewhat surprising in that there is no one antecedent that is dominant across the various measures of system success. However, this is consistent with some prior studies that found through meta-analytic procedures that success antecedents relating to the organizational environment were correlated very differently with different success measures [28]. Taken together, these findings suggest that it is even more important that organizations do not assume that focusing on one success antecedent will lead to across the board implementation success on all measures. Organizations will have to prioritize where they want to see the most implementation success and then focus on the corresponding success antecedent that is most likely to provide this success.

As a success antecedent, User Perceived Benefits ranks highly for two measures of success: Information Use (rank 1) and Individual Impact (rank 1). Note that while User Perceived Benefits (measuring the extent to which the user perceived the system to be important, valuable, successful, and to affect their jobs) may seem like a dependent measure of success, in this context, it refers to user expectations about the importance, value, success, and impact of the system at the early stage of the development [16]. It relates to the construct of perceived usefulness used in Technology Acceptance models [7,48].

Ginzberg [16] presents a number of studies that suggest that users who hold more realistic pre-implementation expectations about a system are more likely to use that system, and therefore, gain more benefits from the system, than are their colleagues who held less realistic pre-implementation expectations. The findings on the importance of User Perceived Benefits suggest that setting realistic user expectations, and convincing them of the perceived benefits of the system at early stages of system development, are crucial to attaining success, particularly for obtaining a high level of system use and individual impact on end users.

System Development Characteristics ranks highly for two success measures: Information Quality and System Quality. Sharma and Yetton [44] have argued that both system quality and information quality are success measures that are related to the system development stage. Our results offer confirmation of this relationship, indicating that factors playing an important role during the development stage are the most influential antecedents of the quality of the system and the quality of information produced by the system. Interestingly, Management Support ranks second for both of the Quality success measures. Where such support is lacking, we should expect quality of a management support system and the information it delivers to suffer. This may be due to inadequate resources or attention being brought to bear during the system's development.

It is not surprising to see that Organizational Characteristics is highly influential for achieving organizational impact. Organizational contingency theory [46,47] suggests that the “fit” between an organization's characteristics and its context (e.g., the use of system in the organization) helps the organization to reap the benefits from the context [17]. When an organization's efforts to implement a new system are accompanied by the necessary organizational traits (e.g., organization has positive attitude toward change, organization has a champion in the system implementation, organization hires external consultants to support the system implementation), the organization will have a better chance to successfully implement the new system and derive its full benefits.

The rankings for Organizational Impact show that User Participation is also a strong antecedent for successful organizational impact. User participation in the development and deployment of a management support system not only helps shape the system to address users' needs and desires, it also fosters a “buy-in” by those in the organization who will be using the systems. These two traits clearly contribute to a system's likelihood of high organizational impact. A management support system that does not fully address the needs/preferences of prospective users and does not enjoy some sense of ownership by these users is likely to experience resistance or disinterest by intended users, lessening its opportunity for positive organizational impact.

To better satisfy end users, the organization must focus on providing adequate user training. Many research studies on user training have shown that such training can significantly affect user acceptance and utilization of a system [41,48]. User training helps the user understand and be proficient in system use, and therefore be more comfortable in using the system. This translates into a user being more satisfied with the experience of using the system and the output of the system. Management support also rates highly as an antecedent of Satisfaction. Where such support is lacking, user training may be a casualty, the enthusiasm or priority for using the system may languish, or users many experience a dissonance between their own inclination to use the system and management's indifference. None of these possibilities promotes an atmosphere consistent with user satisfaction.

Fig. 1 summarizes the most important (e.g., primary) antecedents for the various dimensions of measuring success of model management systems. Six primary antecedent categories emerge from our analysis of empirical MSS studies. Implementers of MSSs should ensure that careful attention is given to these antecedents. In cases where a subset of success dimensions is of particular significance, then primary antecedents for those success measures need to be emphasized. Our analysis finds that three success antecedents, while having positive correlations with some success measures, do not appear to be primary factors in determining MSS success: system characteristics, task characteristics, and user experience. These secondary antecedents may well be reflected in various primary antecedents. For instance, user experience presumably is taken into account in user training and qualifies the nature of user participation.

When examining different MSS types, it is clearly seen that there is no one dominant success antecedent. Because a data warehousing system requires substantial data integration, cleaning, and validation, it makes logical sense that system development characteristics are a key to successful delivery of such systems [2]. In the case of a decision support system, successful adoption depends on the ability in effectively managing users' expectations about the system (e.g., users expectations about the importance and value of the system, users' expectations about the impact of the system on his/her job). And therefore, we find that user perceived benefits is a key antecedent. An Executive Information System often changes information flows in an organization, and therefore it is necessary to lock in support from a politically secure senior executive [1]. Such a system usually requires a substantial amount of resources, and hence, it is essential that top management allocates adequate resources [1]. For group support systems, task characteristics play an important role. GSS is usually suitable for semi and unstructured tasks. The negative correlation indicates that if tasks become too ambiguous and too complex, then they are not suitable for a group support system context. Such tasks are difficult to communicate among the group members, and therefore, hinder success [29]. As success of group support systems is often measured by the number of solutions generated, such tasks will impact the success adversely [29]. For Organizational Decision Support Systems, User Participation forms an important success antecedent as such participation develops users' sense of ownership [4].

## 5. Conclusions

This research study has some limitations. First it focuses on one major class of system: MSS. Because the dependent measures are somewhat similar we could group them and conduct a meaningful quantitative analysis. Generalizability to other classes of IS implementation is an open question. Second, we focus only on those studies that have been published in major IS journals and might have missed others. Third, we include only those studies that provide Pearson's or Kendall's correlation-coefficients between the antecedents and the success measures. This is necessitated by our quantitative analysis method. Though we used quantitative measures, this research study is more exploratory in nature because of the small number of studies that are included. We also could not conduct a meta-analysis of these studies because the measures of success were not the same. Our approach using synthesis of measures seemed to be the most suited to the situation.

Limitations aside there are several implications of value to research and practice. IS implementation requires the investment of considerable resources and failures are expensive. By consolidating and quantitatively synthesizing research findings on implementation success, we sought to identify one or two key success antecedents. However, our study, like some prior studies, shows that there is no one key success antecedent and thus no uniform focus for achieving implementation success [28]. Instead, these findings indicate that organizations must carefully think through what are the benefits they need most and manage the corresponding success antecedent (s). Overall, user perceived benefits, system development characteristics, management support, user training, user participation, and organizational characteristics are important factors that must not be overlooked. Management support system types differ in their key success antecedents. Hence, organizations should also identify the type of system they are implementing and focus on its pertinent critical success antecedents.

From a research perspective, the findings indicate that IS success is not an easy target and that there are a variety of factors that influence the success of various systems.

Keen and Scott Morton [30] state that evaluation is the most difficult aspect of MSS development because such systems are evolutionary in nature and lack a defined completion date. They state further that there is no clear criterion for assessing performance or definition of exactly how the decision is to be supported. As a result, the effectiveness of MSSs largely depends on their evolving usage.

In future studies, user perceived benefits, system development characteristics, management support, user training, user participation, and organizational characteristics must be more closely examined because they appear to be more frequent success antecedents. Research studies can examine the different ways by which management can communicate and manage user expectations and their perceptions of possible benefits from the system. Because system development characteristics are important to obtaining a significant level of information and system quality, research can examine what structures and incentives management can put in place to ensure high quality system development characteristics. Future research can also try to investigate how management can demonstrate support and enthusiasm for the system and its development activities.

In this exploratory study, we examine a particular class of systems and identify the importance of factors that influence their success. The study provides some initial insights to build upon. Other classes of systems can be studied and results consolidated so that we develop a deeper understanding of what antecedents can yield system success across system classes and contexts. While there is a need to continue to conduct field studies on implementation success, it is also important to consolidate research as is done in this study.
