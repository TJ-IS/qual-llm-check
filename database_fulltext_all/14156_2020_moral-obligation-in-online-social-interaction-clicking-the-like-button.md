# Moral obligation in online social interaction: Clicking the “like” button

## Author Details

Author 1 Name: Xi Xu Department: School of Management Engineering University/Institution: Qingdao University of Technology Town/City: Qingdao Country: China

Author 2 Name: Zhong Yao Department: School of Economics and Management University/Institution: Beihang University Town/City: Beijing Country: China

## Abstract

Social media greatly facilitate online social interaction among friends, and user behavior in social interaction may be influenced by interpersonal relationships. While individuals sometimes do enjoy the content shared by friends, they may also feel that they have a moral obligation to like what their friends share. Drawing on the stimulus-organism-response model, this paper examines whether the characteristics of interpersonal relationships are associated with moral obligation as well as whether moral obligation is associated with “like” intention. By using data from 348 users of WeChat Moments, we provide empirical evidence that the stimuli of interpersonal relationships (perceived authority, perceived closeness, and peer referent) are positively associated with the sense of moral obligation, which in turn, is positively associated with user intention to click the Like button on their friends’ postings on social media. Theoretical and practical implications of the findings are discussed.

## Keywords

Online social interaction; Moral obligation; Interpersonal relationships; “Like” intention; S-O-R model; Social media

## 1. Introduction

In the last 10 years, social media has become a key online activity for communication and socialization, which brought about a new cultural and social phenomenon (Pelet et al., 2017). Social media as communication platforms or channels facilitate users to broadcast text, with or without photos or videos, about what they are thinking and experiencing in their daily lives (Zhao and Lu, 2012). At the same time, their friends who see such messages in social media can “like,” “comment,” and “share” these contents to conduct online social interaction. Such online social interactions are increasingly treated as important interpersonal communications that enable individuals to manage self-presentation or receive feedback from peers (Jiang et al., 2013). A recent survey report from CNNIC (2016) indicated that about 72.2% users of social media in China aim to interact with their friends, of which the proportion using WeChat among acquaintances is about 80.3%.

Among various feedback patterns, the concept of the Like button is a significant innovation as it provides users with a comprehensible feedback signal. Since the “like” feature was introduced by Facebook in 2010, the tiny Like button is clicked by millions of people worldwide every day and has become a standard feedback metric in online social interaction (Mariani and Mohammed, 2014). The clicks of “like” create social impact that could become valuable (Ding et al., 2017). In general, by clicking on the Like button in social media, you are expressing the endorsement of the contents shared by your friends. For instance, if your friend shared his or her wedding photos, you would click the Like button to express your sentiment. When you see a funny video shared by your friend, you may click the Like button to show your appreciation. However, have you ever clicked the Like button, not because you enjoyed the shared content, but because of moral obligation to “like” what your friends posted?

Moral obligation represents an individual’s inner perception of responsibility when he or she decides to perform or refuses to perform a certain behavior, which significantly impacts ethical human behavioral decision-making (Kock and Davison, 2003; Moores and Chang, 2006). As the underlying motivation, it seems to be particularly relevant to behavioral intention. Haines and Leonard (2007) contended that moral obligation played a more important role in predicting behavioral intention than personal normative beliefs. Moreover, many empirical studies have found that moral obligation relates positively to behavioral intention in the IT context (Thong and Yap, 1998; Herath and Rao, 2009; Lee, 2011). Nevertheless, little work has been conducted to examine the moral obligation in online social interaction, especially its relationship with “like” intention. This study seeks to fill this gap. We draw upon the stimulus-organism-response (S-O-R) model to theorize what characteristics of interpersonal relationships are associated with users’ moral obligation, which in turn, is associated with their intention to “like” posted content in online social interaction.

This study provides several key contributions. First, although online social interaction has been a popular research topic in the last few years, most prior studies have examined the Like button at the organization level (Naylor et al., 2012; Beukeboom et al., 2015). Our research is one of the first that empirically investigates the “like” intention at the individual level. Understanding “like” intention at the individual level is important because it enhances our understanding of why individuals do so, and consequently also enhances our understanding of how to influence individual behavior. The “like” button enables practitioners to better analyze and understand why certain postings are liked. This would enable practitioners to better frame their messages on social media to increase user engagement. Second, although moral obligation has been extensively studied, no prior research has assessed the impact of moral obligation on “like” intention in the online social interaction context. In doing so, our research sheds light on the relative importance of inner experience for user behavior in social media. In other words, individuals may click “like” because they feel obliged to do so to maintain social relationships. By understanding the relationship between moral obligation and intention to click the “like” button, practitioners can better devise ways to utilize moral obligation to motivate “like” intention. Third, although many scholars have pointed out the importance of interpersonal influence in online social interaction, few of them focus on two types of characteristics of interpersonal relationships (shared friends and common friends) in online social interaction simultaneously. Shared friends refer to a user’s friends who shared contents in social media, while common friends refer to friends between them. To capture key features of interpersonal relationships at the individual level in social media environments, our study concurrently introduced these two aspects of interpersonal relationships into our research model. Finally, the S-O-R model is introduced to link the characteristics of interpersonal relationships to user behavior in online social interaction. Although extant studies have examined the influence of human-computer interface on user behavior by using this framework in the social media context (Zhang et al., 2014; 2015), our research focuses more on social environments of social media instead of technological environments. In doing so, we show the applicability of the S-O-R model in enabling better understanding of the social environment in the context of social interactions.

The rest of the paper is presented as follows. We first present the theoretical background, followed by developing the research model, and hypotheses. Subsequently, we explain the research method and present the empirical results. Finally, we conclude the paper by discussing the findings, implications, and limitations with future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. The S-O-R model

Based on environmental psychology, the S-O-R model assumes that the environment contains various stimuli (S) that cause changes to people’s internal organisms (O), which in turn affect their behavioral responses (R) (Mehrabian and Russell, 1974). By using this model to conceptualize the individual’s reactions to environmental information, it offers an opportunity to capture elements of the complex process of users’ behavioral response and decision-making (Bagozzi, 1983). Since the S-O-R model was introduced, it has been extensively tested in the retail context, including traditional retail and online retail, to understand consumer behavior (Donovan and Rossiter, 1982; Sherman et al., 1997; Animesh et al., 2011). Under this theoretical framework, the stimuli occur in different formats, such as technological and spatial environments (Animesh et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2016), which stimulate participants’ online social experiences. Such internal states occur between the perception of the stimulus and the behavioral intention, including various emotional, cognitive, perceptual, and psychological activities. Previous studies on the organism aspect of the S-O-R model examined a wide range of inner experiences, including social support (Zhang et al., 2014), social presence (Animesh et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2014), flow (Animesh et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2014; Gao and Bai, 2014), pleasure and arousal (Koo and Ju, 2010; Vieira, 2013; Kim and Johnson, 2016), interactivity (Mollen and Wilson, 2010; Zhao and Lu, 2012), etc. Response refers to attitude or intention that is valid in predicting an individual’s subsequent behavior, such as satisfaction and purchase intention (Animesh et al., 2011; Ha and Im, 2012).

Applying the S-O-R model to the social media context, we argue that interpersonal relationships in social media, acting as social environmental stimuli, are associated with psychological reactions in online social interaction. Therefore, we examine how three dimensions of interpersonal relationships (perceived authority, perceived closeness, and peer referent) are associated with users’ sense of moral obligation as well as how such feelings are associated with their “like” intention in online social interaction. In doing so, we contribute to theory by examining the relative importance of different dimensions of interpersonal relationships, and their relationships with moral obligation and “like” intention. Thus, the use of the S-O-R model is appropriate in this study as it helps us to better understand the interpersonal relationship, moral obligation, and “like” intention in online social interaction.

## 2.2.The theory of symbolic interactionism

With the rapid diffusion and continuing development of social media, the technological environments, playing an important role as environmental stimuli in online social interaction, have been broadly investigated (Animesh et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2019). Moreover, the motivations of general social media use typically include maintaining existing relationships and seeking new friends (Kim, 2018). This suggests that interpersonal relationships for everyday communication are also inseparable elements of social environmental stimuli in online social interaction, and are important among acquaintances. Interpersonal relationships can be defined as the patterns of perception and behavior when individuals interact with others (Plutchik and Conte, 1997).

According to the theory of symbolic interactionism, the symbolic cues conveyed by the medium itself are above and beyond the literal message (Trevino et al., 1990). In online social interaction among acquaintances, the affective and relational patterns of communication are relatively similar to face-to-face communication because users still have a need to maintain relationships. They should not only look at the online information, but also focus on the static and dynamic cues from the social environment. Static cues originate from the presence of social media friends (e.g., the head portrait and name of leaders or close friends), whereas dynamic cues originate from behavioral changes in surroundings (e.g., the interactions between others) (Tremayne et al., 2008). Once users perceive such social environmental stimuli, both static and dynamic cues can create ethical cognitive interpretations and even elicit behavioral changes.

In “like” interaction, environmental stimuli from shared friends are their head portrait and name, which can be seen as static cues, while stimuli from common friends are the “like” interaction between shared friends and common friends, which can be considered as dynamic cues. Therefore, we mainly focus on two distinct aspects of interpersonal relationships in online social interaction: shared friends and common friends. For example, user A and user B are WeChat friends and user A posted contents on WeChat Moments are visible to user B; then, user A can be considered as shared friend of user B. Common friends are friends of both user A and user B; the postings and interaction between user A and user B are all visible to them (Please see Figure 1). These two perspectives draw the outline of interpersonal relationships and recognize the social environments among environmental stimuli in social media context.

Figure 1. A venn diagram depicting shared friends and common friends

The first viewpoint of interpersonal relationships—the shared friends, bring together the information sender and the information receiver. This study focuses on two characteristics of the relationship between users and their shared friends: perceived authority and perceived closeness. Perceived authority reflects the degree to which an individual felt the characteristics of power through the interaction with shared friends, which often occur in leader-follower relationships (Deluga and Perry, 1991), such as superior-subordinate relationships in organizations and teacher-student relationships in schools. The presence of a leader can transmit dominance through behavior and project an image of authority. For example, if your bosses share information in social media, you might have a feeling of perceived authority when you see such content. Perceived closeness describes the sense of closeness and intimacy an individual experiences with the shared friends in online social interaction (Ng, 2013). Closeness has attracted attention as a variable associated with several patterns of online communication behavior, including online relational maintenance (Ledbetter, 2009) and frequency of online communication (Valkenburg and Peter, 2007). In addition, emotional closeness is also an indicator of the existence and strength of a social bond (Brown and Brown, 2006), which is a key aspect of interpersonal relationships. Therefore, we take both perceived authority and perceived closeness to shared friends as the factors of social environmental stimuli in online social interaction.

The second perspective of interpersonal relationships—the common friends, bring together internal interaction and external influence. Under the mechanism of peer influence, an individual’s action may be influenced by his or her friends’ behavior and the observed correlation of the behavior exists among online friends (Bapna and Umyarov, 2015). When people are strongly influenced by the decision of others in various online social situations, online herd behavior occurs (Chen, 2008). The emergence of social media has made it important to understand the potential of online herd behavior in online social interaction. Adapting the definition of herd behavior in a consumption setting, the product evaluations, or purchase intentions and behavior of consumers may change after contacting with referent others (Asch, 1956). In other words, a change in the intention of online social interaction, or participant behavior resulting from exposure to the decisions of referent online friends can occur. Hence, we consider peer referent to common friends as one of the social environmental stimuli in online social interaction.

Furthermore, “likes” are typical symbolic cues conveyed by social media itself and are beyond the literal message. Compared with various feedback features or functions in online social interaction, such as “comment” and “share”, clicking the Like button is convenient, meaningful, and requires little effort. As there is no verbal communication in the interactive process through social media, the reasons why a user would click on the Like button is an interesting research issue. Past research has found that behavioral intention has met with some degree of success in predicting a variety of behaviors (Conner and Armitage, 1998). Consequently, the intention to click the “like” button is introduced into this research as response in the S-O-R model. In the context of social media, “like” intention refers to the perceptions and intentions to click the “like” button when browsing information shared by friends on social media (Nel and Halaszovich, 2015).

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Through the application of the S-O-R model, we contend that such framework could partially explain users’ intention to click “like” in online interaction through social media. To incorporate interpersonal relationships as social environmental stimuli into the model, we postulate that perceived authority, perceived closeness, and peer referent are positively associated with users’ moral obligation, which in turn is associated with their “like” intention. On this basis, we have interconnected the set of theoretical constructs in the S-O-R model and elucidated the underlying mechanisms in the research model (Figure 2). ## 3.1. Effects of interpersonal relationships’ characteristics

## 3.1.1. Perceived authority

An individual’s authority in online social interaction is conveyed by nonverbal cues, such as head portrait and username. This is similar to face-to-face communication. As a process of interpersonal influence, leadership unfolds across time and situations until individuals claim and grant leader and follower roles (Marchiondo et al., 2015). Leadership, commonly defined as a social influence, is one of the most pervasive and important phenomena in human social life (Wellman, 2017). When talking about leadership power, some probably have in mind hierarchical relationships, which lead to a feeling of perceived authority. In other words, perceived authority refers to the feeling of authority and dominance, involving revere and obedience, which are often based on real hierarchical relationships (Wellman, 2017). Leadership presence as a blending of personal and interpersonal skills sends the authority signals to their followers in any type of communication (Goman, 2016). Moreover, followers are sensitive to the authority signals of their leaders in interpersonal communication verbally or nonverbally. For instance, subordinates tend to be careful in responding to e-mails sent by their superiors. Students may be nervous when talking with teachers. Thus, perceived authority, in general, exists in such hierarchical relationships.

A review and analysis of perceived authority in particular literature have indicated that relationship with authority is a significant factor of inner dynamics of moral obligation (Rao, 1975; Marchiondo et al., 2015). Studies have shown that the relationship between leaders and followers should be based on support and trust (Liden and Graen, 1980). These two important aspects encompass the combinations of friendliness, encouragement, and sincerity (Zahn, 1980). To achieve a better relationship with the leader, the follower may feel obliged to provide support and trust to his or her leader in interpersonal communication. It means that moral obligation could be important in hierarchical relationships. Because perceived authority in online social interaction has the characteristic of “we know it when we see it,” such stimuli will trigger user psychological activity. Therefore, high perceived authority in interpersonal relationships could motivate a sense of moral obligation to provide endorsement when interacting with leaders online. On the basis of this line of argument, we hypothesize that:

## H1. Perceived authority is positively associated with a sense of moral obligation.

Authority means power, reputation, or competence, which always rests upon those who acknowledge it (Rao, 1975). Therefore, authority always comprises a relation. In general, users in online social interaction have the need for online relationship development and offline relationship maintenance. The most immediate guide to perception authority is reading static cues from the social environment in social media (Tremayne et al., 2008). When users are browsing online information shared by their social media friends, it is unavoidable to notice head portrait and name of shared friends. Such presentations can convey leader’s authority and evoke users consciousness of interpersonal relationships.

When users engage in symbolic stimulation and interaction behavior, they are pushed to think differently and adapt behavior accordingly (Wellman, 2017). In other words, once people perceived social context cues, they would adjust their communication behavior (Sproull and Kiesler, 1986). This means that perceived authority originates from social contexts and determines communication behavior (Tremayne et al., 2008), such as “like” behavior. Thus, perceived authority may trigger more intention to click the Like button. We, therefore, propose the following hypothesis:

H2. Perceived authority is positively associated with “like” intention.

## 3.1.2. Perceived closeness

Perceived closeness is an important aspect of interpersonal relationships in social interaction, which includes a variety of relationship types, such as friendship, family, and romantic relationships. It reflects the existence and strength of social bond based on fitness interdependence (Brown and Brown, 2006). It also represents the degree to which individuals perceive others to have caring feelings for them and to be affectionate physically or verbally (Flores and Berenbaum, 2012). Hence, perceived closeness refers to the feeling of closeness and emotional bonding, involving intense liking and moral support from social media friends, and the ability to tolerate social media friends’ mistakes (Ng, 2013). Previous studies have shown that close relationships are positively associated with relational maintenance and both frequency and depth of interpersonal communication, thus further promoting relational longevity (Valkenburg and Peter, 2007; Ledbetter, 2009).

However, it is not an easy task to keep close relationships in social interaction.

Close relationships require you to provide moral support and tolerate mistakes in interpersonal communication (Ng, 2013). The obligation of reciprocity permeates many facets in interpersonal relationships, and is closely intertwined with relational closeness between users involved in online social interaction (Cadsby et al., 2015). Hence, it is likely that perceived closeness motivates the feeling of moral obligation in social media. For example, when your close friends shared content about voting request in social media, you may feel obliged to vote for them. If your close friends shared product information to collect “likes”, it is a duty for you to click the Like button. Such close relationships make you embarrassed to refuse or ignore, which leads to a sense of duty to support them. Following this line of argumentation, relationships with high closeness are likely to engender more moral obligation in fulfilling their needs. Therefore, we hypothesize:

## H3. Perceived closeness is positively associated with the sense of moral obligation.

According to interpersonal solidarity concept, solidarity relationships refer to those in which “closeness” is derived from “similarity” (Wheeless, 1987). As such, it reflects that degree of psychological, social, and perhaps even physical closeness between people. Perceived closeness encompasses perceived satisfaction in relationships as a result of sharing similarities in personal characteristics, symbolic expressions, pleasant sentiments, and behaviors (Pornsakulvanich et al., 2008). People tend to have similar sentiment with those they feel close to more than those they do not. Thus, the possibility of endorsing shared information is higher among close relationships than others. In online social interaction, users prefer expressing their agreement and appreciation through clicking the Like button below the shared contents. Therefore, perceived closeness in interpersonal relationships has a direct relationship with “like” intention. Compared to general relationships, close relationships enhance users’ intention to “like” in online social interaction. Thus, we posit:

H4. Perceived closeness is positively associated with “like” intention.

## 3.1.3. Peer referent

Research into peer-to-peer social influences in the social media environment finds that user characteristics and behavior tend to cluster both in space and in time (Aral and Walker, 2011). The mechanism underlying this phenomenon is most frequently cited as being due to peer influence or peer referent. Peer referent refers to the feeling of peer pressure, leading to the observed correlation of the behavior of social media friends (Bapna and Umyarov, 2015). But what are the social-psychological processes by which peer pressure shapes individual interaction behavior? Previous studies suggest that there are influence processes inherent in social relationships and implicit pressures for agreement even without instructions (Turner, 1991). Hence, the influence processes drawn from peer referents’ interaction behavior cannot be ignored.

In social media context, although the internal influence from shared friends is apparent, the external influence from common friends is also important to users’ inner experience, and thus, is likely to have important implications for online social interaction. Because the interactions between shared friends and common friends are visible to you, pressures for agreement from common friends may be associated with your sense of moral obligation. For example, if most common friends agree with the opinion of shared friends, you may feel obliged to also show your agreement. Peer referent produces a psychological pressure (especially conformity pressure) when an individual compares his or her action with the actions taken by friends (Lord and DeZoort, 2001). Such stimuli are associated with users’ perception of moral obligation during the decision-making process. This indicates that peer referent may be an important antecedent of moral obligation in the context of online social interaction. Based on the arguments mentioned above, we hypothesize the following:

## H5. Peer referent is positively associated with the sense of moral obligation.

Research in the area of interpersonal relationships has repeatedly demonstrated that peer referent plays a major role in influencing ethical decision-making and behavior; the more the individuals observe peers engaging in ethical behavior, the more likely they are to engage in the same or similar activities (e.g., Beams, 2003; Bapna and Umyarov, 2015 ). Peer influence, which is defined as a directive role that “actors” play in shaping the beliefs and actions of an individual (Sedera et al., 2017), has the potential to impact all aspects of our decisions. Thus, the role of peer referent cannot be ignored in influencing online users’ decision-making and behavior; the more users observe peers engaging in online social interaction behavior, the more likely they are to engage in the same or similar activities (Chen, 2008). In terms of online herd behavior, greater peer referent tendency increases the intention to click the Like button. Moreover, in an interactive environment in which the majority of common friends have clicked the Like button, his or her intention to click “like” will be relatively high. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H6. Peer referent is positively associated with “like” intention.

## 3.2. Effect of Moral obligation

Previous studies mainly focus on two aspects of internal states: psychological state reflecting virtual experience (Animesh et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2014) and affective state concerning pleasure and arousal (Koo and Ju, 2010; Vieira, 2013; Kim and Johnson, 2016). In this study, we explore moral obligation as the first type of internal state in social media environments. Moral obligation is concerned with the extent to which an individual experiences a sense of responsibility to act morally or not when faced with an ethical situation (Beck and Ajzen, 1991).

The literature in social and cognitive psychology suggests that moral obligation may be inseparable from the social-relational context (Rai and Fiske, 2011). The impact of social-relational context on social behavior can be demonstrated by social psychology (Ross and Nisbett, 2011). The moral obligation is developed based on theoretical explorations in social psychology, which function to sustain interpersonal relationships. For example, subordinates should show respect to their superior(s) in an organization. Children should provide both spiritual and material support to their old parents unconditionally. In a similar vein, if we are close friends, it is our moral obligation to provide support for each other in difficult or challenging situations. Therefore, it is necessary to integrate moral obligation into social-relational cognition.

Morality is a system of rules and values that facilitate the regulation of social interactions and interpersonal relationships, which is at the heart of the ethical decision-making process (Moores and Chang, 2006). Moral psychology, as embedded in social-relational cognition, provides insight into social behavior (Rai and Fiske, 2011). Moral obligation as a personal internal state construct is a key aspect of moral psychology. It describes the sense of duty of what one ought to do when facing ethical situations. Previous studies have noted that moral obligation would affect people’s behavioral intention (Ajzen, 1991; Bang et al., 2014). In online platforms, moral obligation reflects a sense of duty or obligation to support or help others on the basis of social media friends. For example, there would be a higher chance for consumers with a strong sense of moral obligation to spread electronic word-of-mouth in online consumer-opinion platforms (Cheung and Lee, 2012). The moral obligation seems to be particularly relevant to the motivation underlying associated duty and support. Likewise, given that moral obligation is embedded in individuals and people tend to keep a friendly interpersonal relationship, one would feel obliged to show endorsement in online social interaction. Therefore, moral obligation is particularly important in social behaviors such as online interaction in social media. The perception of moral obligation will elevate one’s propensity to click the Like button and increase “like” intention in online social interaction. Thus, we hypothesize:

H7. Moral obligation is positively associated with “like” intention.

## 3.3. Control Variables

We included several gender, age, and education as control variables to account for the differences among individuals. In addition, we also included user experience and frequency of use, as prior literature on social media has indicated that they affect behavioral intention (Song et al., 2014). We also control for the number of friends because users who have a mass of social media friends may feel numb to interpersonal influence (Xu et al., 2019). Therefore, we select these particular variables because of their potential impact on “like” intention as suggested in extant research.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Research setting

This study chooses the users of WeChat as target respondents. First, WeChat (WeiXin in Chinese) is one of the most popular social apps not only in China, but also overseas. With the continued developments in mobile internet and smart phone apps, WeChat has attracted 1.08 billion monthly active users by September 2018 and is available in over 200 countries and supports 18 different languages (Tencent, 2019). WeChat provides multiple mobile services, including Moments, which is a main function embedded in WeChat to provide an online social interaction platform for users (see the Appendix B for the screenshot of WeChat Moments). It allows users to share what they are thinking about and experiencing in their daily lives with their WeChat friends by broadcasting text with or without photos or videos. This indicates that WeChat Moments is a very popular communication channel for social interaction activities to occur.

Second, compared to other social media (such as Weibo and Twitter), the distinctive characteristic of WeChat is the familiarity among social media friends. Most WeChat friends recognize each other in real life. Such acquaintance relationships allow users to maintain offline interpersonal relationships in online social interaction. In this regard, users are familiar with most of their WeChat friends and should be cognizant of the feelings of their friends in online interactions.

Third, although WeChat Moments provide services similar to those of Facebook, in terms of user privacy protection, WeChat Moments is perhaps more stringent. For example, the online interaction activities between a user and his or her friend who shared content are only visible to their common friends. As mentioned previously, the social environment, from the perspective of interpersonal relationships, encompasses two aspects: shared friends and common friends. Therefore, based on the reasons mentioned above, we believe that WeChat Moments is an ideal platform to conduct our research.

## 4.2. Instrument

To test the research model, a survey instrument was developed with each construct measured using multiple items. Most items were adapted from existing measures in the related literature with confirmed content validity and reliability, and then modified to fit our research context. Perceived closeness was measured by three items adapted from Ng (2013). On the basis of our review of literature on perceived closeness and the definition of perceived authority, we created three items to measure perceived authority. Because perceived closeness and perceived authority are constructs that are both related to interpersonal relationships, the items measuring perceived authority are directly adapted from the items measuring perceived closeness. This is done through certain changes in keywords. Peer referent was measured by three items adapted from Goodrich and Mangleburg (2010). Moral obligation was measured using three items adapted from Cheung and Lee (2012). “Like” intention was measured by three items adapted from Zhang et al. (2015) that manifested the intention of future participation in social media.

The survey instrument is shown in Appendix C. The response format was a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). In addition, because the data collection took place in China, a translation and back-translation approach was adopted to ensure the quality of the translation. First, we consulted with three information system professors on the meaningfulness and readability of each item. Moreover, with their help, the English version questionnaire was translated into Chinese. Second, we asked two doctoral students uninvolved in this research to translate the Chinese version questionnaire back to English. Third, we compared the back-translated items with the original English version items. To keep the consistency of the two English versions, we refined the final translation and resolved any discrepancies.

Furthermore, with the purpose of identifying potential problems, we conducted a pilot study to collect feedback about the questionnaire. The questionnaire was distributed to users of WeChat who provided their feedback in 2 days. A total of 58 users with WeChat Moments experience were included. The results of a pilot test showed that all Cronbach’s alpha surpassed the standard threshold value of 0.70, which revealed good instrument reliability. According to the feedback of these subjects, the wordings and format of the questionnaire were appropriate.

## 4.3. Data collection

To empirically test the research model, we obtained data from an online survey targeted at WeChat users in China. Note that a complete directory of social media users does not exist, and it is impossible to select a real random sample (Trammell and Keshelashvili, 2005). In this study, we adopted similar techniques as past research (Johnson et al., 2007; Gan and Li, 2018; Lin et al. 2019) by dispersing the invitation messages through a professional online survey website (www.sojump.com). The URL of the final questionnaire was distributed through WeChat app in September 2017. We sent invitation messages to 16 group chats (grouped by interest, school, address, shopping, and other purposes) and posted an online invitation in Moments. The respondents could click the hyperlink to access the questionnaire and respond to it online. To promote the quantity and quality of response, we provided an electronic red envelope including money from 1 RMB to 200 RMB randomly after completing the questionnaire. In addition, we restricted multiple submissions from the same IP address to avoid the possibility of repeated survey participation from the same respondent.

In total, 386 completed questionnaires were received for this study. However, 38 questionnaires were excluded because of invalid responses, including those with all same answers and those who had no experience in using WeChat Moments. Thus, a total of 348 valid questionnaires were used to test the research model and hypotheses. The characteristics of respondents are presented in Table 1. Respondents were relatively balanced in gender distribution and most of them were aged between 20 and 40 years old. The age profile of our respondents (with majority age between 26 and 35 years old) is consistent with the age profile of WeChat users (Brennan, 2017). Furthermore, the majority (85.6%) had used WeChat Moments more than one year and over three-quarters (78.7%) used WeChat Moments every day.

## 5. Results

In our data analysis, we used partial least squares (Smart PLS 2.0), a variance-based latent variable structural equation modeling technique, which is frequently adopted in the IS literature (Ahuja and Thatcher, 2005; Ou et al., 2014). PLS is suitable as it requires a relatively small data set with few restrictions on normal distribution (Chin et al., 2003).

The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.897, above the recommended value of 0.6. Also, the factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, indicating that 70.33 percent of the variance was explained. As shown in Table 2, all items loaded highly on their related factors and had low cross-loadings on other factors, suggesting good convergent and discriminant validities.

## 5.2. Structural model test

After evaluating that the measurement was satisfactory, we assessed the structural model. The hypotheses were examined by the percentage of variance explained and the significance of the structural paths. Figure 3 shows the test results from PLS analysis including the control variables. The model explained 42.7% of “like” intention. Moral obligation (β=0.216, p<0.001) had a significant positive relationship with “like” intention, thereby supporting H7. Peer referent (β=0.229, p<0.001) and perceived closeness (β=0.277, p<0.001) had significant positive relationships with “like” intention, but the effect of perceived authority on “like” intention was not significant. Thus, the results supported H6 and H4 but not H2. Moreover, the model explained approximately 43.9% of the variance in moral obligation. The findings showed that peer referent (β=0.261, p<0.001), perceived authority (β=0.372, p<0.001), and perceived closeness (β=0.195, p<0.001) were strongly associated with moral obligation, thus supporting H5, H1, and H3. Additionally, the links between control variables and “like” intention were not significant.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Main findings

The literature consistently emphasized the importance of online social interaction, which produces enduring value for social media developers and managers. The objective of this research was to extend our understanding of online social interaction behavior and its implications, principally in focusing the issue of whether moral obligation helps “like” intention. We do this by introducing the S-O-R model into our research, which provides the theoretical basis to investigate the association of moral obligation with users’ intention to “like” posted content under the context of interpersonal relationships in social media. Overall, our results show that moral obligation does play a key role in online social interaction, which motivates users to click the Like button.

First, regarding the research context, the sense of moral obligation is particularly salient in China as a traditionally collectivist society. The obligation of reciprocity permeates many facets of interpersonal communication, which affects the likelihood of helping, especially among acquaintances. Previous studies have shown that moral obligation precedes moral intent in the ethical decision-making process (Rai and Fiske, 2011), such as volunteer intention and taxpayers’ compliance intentions. However, the impact of moral obligation in online social interaction among acquaintances cannot be ignored. This study partly explained the underlying reasons that users of WeChat Moments engaged in online social interaction. Our results verified that moral obligation is positively associated with “like” intention.

Second, regarding the characteristics of interpersonal relationships, previous studies have shown that the perceptions of interpersonal relationships are antecedents that affect users’ internal state (Ng, 2013). Yet, very little research has addressed both shared friends and common friends simultaneously in interpersonal relationships in online social interaction. From the perspective of shared friends, as friends of most WeChat Moments users are acquaintances, this study takes perceived closeness into consideration. Another distinctive characteristic of interpersonal relationships in WeChat Moments is hierarchical relationships; hence, we also take perceived authority into account. From the perspective of a common friend, peer referent is found to be positively associated with moral obligation in WeChat Moments. This is consistent with previous results about peer influence mechanism, where users tend to emulate the behavior of their online friends (Bapna and Umyarov, 2015). The results of this study indicated that perceived closeness had the strongest direct effect and total effect on “like” intention, whereas perceived authority had the strongest indirect effect. Also, the association between peer referent and “like” intention was significant. This means that the influence from shared friends and common friends are both non-negligible for users in WeChat Moments.

Finally, regarding “like” intention, prior research shows the influence of endorsement on “like” intention in Facebook (Nel and Halaszovich, 2015), while our results help explain situations beyond endorsement in WeChat Moments. That is, moral obligation is essential to “like” intention under the consideration of interpersonal relationships. Our results note direct effects of perceived closeness and peer referent on “like” intention. Yet, their indirect effects through moral obligation are also significant. As to perceived authority, it is positively associated with users internal state about moral obligation, which is in turn positively associated with “like”

intention in online social interaction. Taken together, these results suggest that moral obligation can behave as an internal motivation that pushes users to click the Like button.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

There are several important theoretical implications of our study. First, it advances our understanding of online social interaction. Although users’ interaction behavior in social media has recently received much attention, the fact remains that much of the scholarly effort has been limited to market-level determinants (Naylor et al., 2012; Beukeboom et al., 2015). This study contributes to existing literature by providing individual-level insights on users’ “like” intention. More importantly, unlike previous studies that regard the Like button as an endorsement, this study explores the inner experience beyond enjoyment and endorsement that motivate users to press the Like button. The results demonstrate that moral obligation is positively associated with users’ “like” intention during the process of online social interaction.

Second, to our knowledge, no study has established the link between the characteristics of interpersonal relationships and online social interaction through moral obligation. Moreover, we consider two perspectives of interpersonal relationships in social media: shared friends and common friends. Our study identifies three characteristics of interpersonal relationships as environmental stimuli: perceived authority, perceived closeness, and peer referent. Among them, perceived authority has seldom been mentioned in online social interaction. Our study contributes to the literature by theoretically highlighting the three aspects of social environments and empirically demonstrating their relative importance in online social interaction. The findings indicate that these three factors are direct or indirect predictors of “like” intention.

Third, our study recognizes the crucial role of moral obligation in determining interaction behavior, especially in an acquaintance context. Although there is physical separation between the information sender and the information receiver in online social interaction, it is relatively important for users to maintain the interpersonal relationships. Our study shows that moral obligation can bridge the perception of interpersonal relationships’ characteristics and behavioral intention. It helps to partially explain why users engage in online social interaction. Although perceived authority has no direct effect on behavioral intention (β=0.072, t=1.338), it was found to have a significant, positive relationship with moral obligation, which in turn had a positive relationship with “like” intention. Moreover, in the relationships between perceived closeness, peer referent, and “like” intention, the mediating effect of moral obligation is also significant.

Finally, this study extends the applicability of the S-O-R model to online social interaction. Although the S-O-R framework has been used to explain users’ behavior in social media (Zhang et al., 2014; 2015), our results further verified that it can also be applied in online social interaction from the perspective of interpersonal communication.

## 6.3. Practical implications

Our findings not only have the potential to enrich the stream of research on online social interaction, but also provide practical implications for social media developers and managers. First, our results provide support to the general belief that moral obligation plays an important role in online social interaction. We show that moral obligation is positively associated with users’ intention to click the “like” button. Based on this point, practitioners can devise ways to utilize moral obligation to motivate WeChat users’ liking behavior. To increase user engagement, managers can develop more leaders and users who have good relationships as shared users, which is an effective way of information dissemination and feedback. Also, it is a good way to ensure user aliveness. In the commercial usage aspect, practitioners can utilize moral obligation to promote production. For example, users who shared production information in WeChat Moments that obtained many “likes” can have a discount. Therefore, how to make good use of moral obligation experience of users is a key issue that managers have to think carefully while implementing their social media strategy.

Second, this study reveals new insights on relationship maintenance in online social interaction. Given the significant positive influence from both shared friends and common friends in WeChat Moments, we recommend that social media practitioners should carefully consider not just shared friends, but also common friends. In general, users’ behavior can be often impacted by the decision of common friends in online social interaction. From the perspective of marketing promotion, practitioners should think carefully about the number of “likes” for discount, because more “likes” from common friends can trigger more “like” intention. Therefore, it is critical for practitioners to set a reasonable “like” number to stimulate user engagement and promote marketing.

Finally, we also recommend that social media developers further improve technical features to effectively enhance the impact of interpersonal relationships. They should not only look at the direct cost benefits of different technological alternatives, but also examine the indirect effects of interpersonal relationships on interaction through inner experience (such as moral obligation). Thus, providers should help fulfill their users’ need for social connections and enhance their interpersonal relationships. Through embedding various features to create a social media environment that is beneficial for relationships maintenance, communication and interaction are enhanced.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

This study has a few limitations that could be addressed in future research. First, the focus of our study about user behavior in online social interaction is on “like” intention, which is an embodiment of online social interaction. As such, depending on the design of interactive functions in social media, the type of interaction behavior may vary, such as comment and share. However, the implication of “like” in social media could provide insights to better understand online social interaction. Given that social media providers are currently introducing new interactive functions (e.g., unlike), future research could provide interesting insights by comparing our results with the impact of such functions.

Second, caution should be taken in generalizing our findings because WeChat Moments is a relationship-oriented interactive platform. Compared with content-oriented interactive platforms, users in a relationship-oriented platform always keep an eye on interpersonal relationships. Based on the S-O-R theory, the environmental stimuli on these two kinds of platforms may be different, leading to different internal states and different behavior. Thus, future research on online social interaction could offer more salient implications for social media providers considering the variation between different types of interactive platforms. In addition, future research can test the model in the western social media context.

Finally, given the scope of the study, there are other variables that were not included in the research model. This study shows that interpersonal relationships are positively associated with moral obligation. Because of the cross-sectional design, the causality among the constructs cannot be assured. In this study, interpersonal relationships are presented by perceived authority, perceived closeness, and peer referent, which focus on the relationships between users and their friends. However, the relationships structure between shared friends and common friends is likely to be more complex, which reflects another aspect of relationships. In online social interaction, users might have both friends with strong ties and those with weak ties, which exhibit the quality of relationships. As such, future research could examine whether other relationship types or relationship quality might affect users’ responses to online social interaction, and if so, how.
