# Buyers’ Choice of Online Search Strategy and Its Managerial Implications

RAVI SEN, RUTH C. KING, AND MICHAEL J. SHAW

ABSTRACT: The Internet offers several tools such as shopping bots and search engines that help potential buyers search for lower prices. This paper defines buyers’ online search strategy as using one or more of these tools to search for lower prices, and empirically investigates the validity of economics of information search theory in explaining buyers’ choice of a particular online search strategy. We find that buyers attitudes toward the price offered by their preferred online seller, their perception of online price dispersion, and their awareness of shopping agents have a significant effect on their choice of online search strategy. An understanding of buyers’ choice of online search strategies can help an online seller to estimate its expected probability of making an online sale, optimize its online pricing, and improve its online promotional and advertising activities.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: e-commerce, e-markets, multinomial logit, online pricing, online search, online search tools, price search.

A STUDY OF EXISTING LITERATURE on Web-based business-to-consumer (B2C) electronic commerce reveals that in online markets buyers can discover new sellers, product information, and prices more easily due to Internet technology [47]. This technology makes exhaustive searches easy and efficient—that is, performed in-depth at a relatively lower cost (e.g., [9]). The literature on online searches, however, has primarily focused on the impact of the lower search costs on e-market characteristics such as market structure (e.g., [3, 6]) and online prices (e.g., [11, 16, 17, 23, 24]). Some have studied the role of specific online search tools such as shopping bots or shopping agents [41] in shaping online search behavior. However, there is a dearth of electronic commerce research focusing on the factors that influence an individual’s online search behavior. Interestingly, substantial work has been done to analyze search for product information in offline markets (e.g., [5, 29, 30]) and its impact on offline market structure (e.g., [1, 39, 42, 44]). However, the conclusions drawn in these studies are not fully applicable to e-markets because (1) offline sellers derive their estimate of offline search patterns from their knowledge about their location with respect to the competitors and buyers (e.g., [33]), whereas, in e-markets, all sellers are at the same location and equidistant from all the buyers [4]; (2) offline search pattern is dominated by sequential search (e.g., [33, 46]), whereas the Web-based technologies offer more than one way to search in the online markets, which include both sequential (e.g., www.google.com) and parallel (e.g., www.mysimon.com) search strategies; and (3) offline buyers are homogeneous in their search strategy (i.e., all follow a sequential search pattern that differs only in search intensity), whereas online buyers could be differentiated on the basis of their awareness of various online search tools and, therefore, their search strategies. Given these differences in online and offline markets, we feel that further investigation is necessary to investigate buyers’ choice of online search strategy and the determinants of this choice.

One of the major theories extensively used by economists to explain consumers search for information is the economics of information search theory [43, 46]. Its basic argument is that consumers search for any information as long as the marginal gains from the search are higher than the marginal costs. This study applies economics of information search theory to identify the determinants of buyers’ choice of online search strategy, and empirically investigates the strength of relationship between these determinants and buyers’ actual choice of online search strategy. It differs from the existing literature based on the economics of information search theory because (1) existing studies focus on sequential search behavior in the offline markets, whereas this study focuses simultaneously on sequential search, parallel search, and some combination of the two in the context of online markets; (2) it studies online search behavior in greater detail, by analyzing the empirical ramifications of the economics of search theory and its ability to explain online price information search; and (3) it pays particular attention to the role of consumers’ prior beliefs about online price dispersion, their knowledge of online search tools, and their attitude toward their preferred online seller’s price in shaping their choice of online search strategies.

An understanding of consumers’ online price search strategy is essential for several reasons:

• While the existing research on electronic commerce has done a good job of explaining the role of seller and Web site characteristics on Web site traffic (e.g., [31]), we still need to fully understand the role played by buyers’ choice of online strategies on this traffic.

• An understanding of buyers’ choice of online search patterns can help online sellers improve their online pricing and promotional strategies.

• Currently, search engines are busy competing with each other (e.g., [45]). An investigation of online search patterns can help the vendors of search engines to better understand their true competition (e.g., shopping bots), and modify their competitive strategy accordingly.

• Existing research in B2C e-commerce has found that online retailers, in contrast with offline firms, adjust prices on a more frequent basis [7], and literature on offline search has established the relationship between search intensity and equilibrium prices (e.g., [39, 46]). Although some studies have investigated the relationship between the use of specific online search strategies such as using shopping bots on the evolution of online price (e.g., [23]), we still need to understand the joint impact of various online search strategies on the evolution of online prices. A better understanding of buyers’ preference for one or more of these search strategies will take us further toward understanding this relationship.

• An empirical study such as this can help academics demonstrate that the economics of information search theory can be enriched by relating it to empirical findings and thus reconcile findings from behavioral literature and analytical literature on consumers’ information search behavior.

## Model for the Choice of Online Search Strategy

WHEN A DEPENDENT VARIABLE can have multiple discrete outcomes and these outcomes are not ordered, the ordinary least square estimators cannot be used. Instead, a maximum likelihood estimator such as multinomial logit (MNL) is the appropriate estimation method (e.g., [10]). The MNL model is a general extension of the binomial choice model to more than two alternatives. It is the most commonly applied model to explain and forecast discrete choices due to its ease of estimation and its foundation in utility theory. The model is based on the assumption that a potential buyer would choose an online search strategy that maximizes his or her expected utility from search activity. Given a choice between M alternatives (i.e., online search strategies in our case), the utility that buyer i derives from the jth alternative is represented by $U _ { i j } .$ The model assumes that buyers are rational and buyer i would choose the online search strategy $j = m$ if and only if this choice offers, of all the available choices, the highest level of utility—that is, $U _ { i m } = \operatorname* { m a x } ( U _ { i 1 } , . . . , U _ { i M } )$ . Suppose that these utilities are a linear function of some buyer-specific characteristics (e.g., gender, education, age) that remain the same for any buyer $i ,$ for all the available choices. If there are R such characteristics and, for a buyer i, each of these characteristics represented by $X _ { i r } ( r = 1 , . . . , R )$ , then the utility function for this buyer if he or she chooses option j can be written as

$$
U _ {i j} = \sum_ {r = 1} ^ {R} \Big [ \beta_ {j r} X _ {i r} \Big ] + \varepsilon_ {i j} = V _ {i j} + \varepsilon_ {i j},\tag{1}
$$

where $\beta _ { j \prime }$ is the coefficient associated with jth alternative and the rth characteristic of the buyer and $\begin{array} { r } { V _ { i j } = \sum _ { r = 1 } ^ { R } [ \beta _ { j r } X _ { i r } ] } \end{array}$ . An increase in $X _ { i r } ,$ the value of the rth characteristic of buyer i, will cause $V _ { i j }$ and therefore his or her utility from choice j to rise if $\dot { \boldsymbol { { \beta } } } _ { j r } > 0$ and to fall if $\dot { \beta } _ { j r } < 0$ . As is common for such models, the relationship between utility and its deterministic variables is not an exact one. Even if we assume that the buyers are rational in their decision making, there are many errors in their utility maximization because of imperfect perception and optimizations. In addition, there may be specification and measurement errors in the model. Therefore, the buyer’s utility is a random function [26] and the error term $\varepsilon _ { i j }$ is included in the equation to capture this randomness in the model.

Let us define a random variable $Y _ { i }$ such that $Y _ { i } = m$ when $j = m ,$ , and the probability that a buyer, with given characteristics $X _ { i r } ( r = 1 , . . . , R )$ , will choose option $j = m$ is represented by P $\mathbb { \Lambda } [ Y _ { i } = m \mathbb { X } _ { i r } ]$ . Suppose we observe that buyer i, with characteristics $X _ { i r } ( r = 1 , . . . , R )$ , chooses the online search strategy m. This implies that this buyer’s utility with option m is the maximum among all the available choices. The probability of this happening is given as follows: $\operatorname* { P r } [ Y _ { i } = m | X _ { i r } ] = \operatorname* { P r } [ U _ { i m } > U _ { i j } ] { \bf j } \forall \neq m$ . Substituting for $U _ { i m }$ and $U _ { i j }$ from Equation (1) gives

$$
\begin{array}{c} \operatorname * {P r} \Big [ Y _ {i} = m \big | X _ {i r} \Big ] = \operatorname * {P r} \Big [ V _ {i m} + e _ {i m} > V _ {i j} + e _ {i j} \Big ] \\ = \operatorname * {P r} \Big [ e _ {i j} <   e _ {i m} + V _ {i m} - V _ {i j} \Big ] \forall j \neq m. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

If the error terms for the M choices $\varepsilon _ { i j } ( j = 1 , . . . , M )$ are independently and identically distributed with a Weibull distribution whose cumulative distribution function (CDF) is $F ( e _ { i j } < \varepsilon ) = \exp [ e ^ { ( - \varepsilon ) } ]$ , and whose probability density function (PDF) is $f ( e _ { i j } ) =$ $\exp [ - e _ { i j } - e ^ { ( e _ { i j } ) } ]$ , then the probability that a buyer will choose the alternative m is given by McFadden [26]:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} \left(Y _ {i} = m \mid X _ {i r}\right) = \frac {e ^ {\left(V _ {i m}\right)}}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {j = M} e ^ {\left(V _ {i j}\right)}}.\tag{3a}
$$

The MNL is defined by Equation (3a). However, only M – 1 of the probabilities can be determined independently, because

$$
\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {j = M} \operatorname * {P r} \left(Y _ {i} = j \mid X _ {i r}\right) = 1
$$

for any buyer i. Consequently, the MNL model of Equation (3a) is indeterminate, as it is a system of M equations with only M – 1 independent unknowns. A convenient normalization that solves the problem is to use one of the M choices as the reference category. If the reference category is $j = 1$ , then set $\beta _ { { \scriptscriptstyle 1 r } } = 0$ , where $r = 1 , . . . , R .$ . Under this normalization, $V _ { i 1 } = 0$ , and so, from Equation (3a):

$$
\begin{array}{c} \operatorname * {P r} \left(Y _ {i} = 1 \mid X _ {i r}\right) = \frac {1}{1 + \sum_ {j = 2} ^ {j = M} e ^ {\left(V _ {i j}\right)}} \text {and} \operatorname * {P r} \left(Y _ {i} = m \mid X _ {i r}\right) = \frac {e ^ {\left(V _ {i m}\right)}}{1 + \sum_ {j = 2} ^ {j = M} e ^ {\left(V _ {i j}\right)}}; \\ m = 2, \dots , M. \end{array}\tag{3b}
$$

The next section describes the buyer-specific characteristics (i.e., X) that are used in the MNL model defined by Equation (3a).

## Determinants of Buyers’ Choice of Online Search Strategy—Variables and Hypothesized Relationships

THE PROPOSED MNL MODEL IS BASED on the assumption that buyers’ choice of online search strategy is influenced by the utility generated by each of the alternative search strategies. Furthermore, the model assumes that the utility from various online search alternatives is influenced, among other factors, by the traditional variables from the economics of information search theory framework—that is, economic returns to search and search costs associated with each online search option. Also included in the model are some demographic variables. Before describing these variables, we define the set of online search options available to the buyers.

## Online Search Strategies

Actual search strategies can vary a lot among individuals.<sup>1</sup> Therefore, to keep the model simple, we identified four broad categories of search strategies used by those who use the Internet to search for price information. These categories were defined on the basis of existing literature on online search and anecdotal evidence from trade publications. Two of these—that is, use of search engines or directly going to the preferred seller’s Web site—seem to be the dominant methods used by buyers to search online sellers as evidenced by surveys published in trade magazines (Table 1). ## Economic Returns from Search

In the offline markets, the measure of intentions to search are related to lower prices paid for nondurables (i.e., gasoline and groceries) (e.g., [15, 19]) and durables (i.e., automobiles) (e.g., [37]). It is safe to assume that online price searches are also dictated by the same intentions. Therefore, we define consumers’ economic gain from online search activity as the lower prices obtained by using one or the other online search option. In many of the existing analytical models, consumers’ beliefs about their expected economic returns from search (i.e., expected price savings) is a function of their perceived price dispersion (e.g., [48]), referred to as PPD in our model. The economics of information search theory argues that the higher the perceived price dispersion in the market, the larger the expected economic gains from search (e.g., [43, 46]).

In addition to perceived online price dispersion, our model also includes another measure of buyers’ economic returns from search, i.e., buyers’ attitude toward their preferred online seller’s prices (PSP). This factor has not been used in any of the existing studies on buyers’ search behavior in online or offline markets and is unique to the proposed model. We believe that buyers’ estimates of expected gains from online search are also influenced by their attitude toward their preferred online sellers’ prices (e.g., [14]). For example, there is empirical evidence to suggest that those loyal to a particular brand spend less time searching for alternatives.<sup>2</sup> Therefore, one could argue that buyers who have a preferred online seller (i.e., are loyal to an online seller) would be more likely to get their price information directly from the preferred seller. Furthermore, if buyers perceive that the prices offered by their preferred seller are the lowest in the market, then the buyers, in all likelihood, will not indulge in online price search. For example, if buyers are satisfied with the prices offered by their preferred online seller, buyers are less likely to do extensive online search for lower prices, because they believe that the price savings are not worth the opportunity cost of time spent in search. On the other hand, if buyers believe that their preferred seller offers relatively high prices, then they are likely to search for lower prices.

## Search Costs

Time spent in searching has proven to be a major element of costs associated with search in the offline markets (e.g., [25, 36, 37]). Online search tools have significantly reduced this cost component. However, some time is still spent in online search activity. Since each search strategy requires a different amount of time to implement, they have different costs associated with them. For example, using shopping agents is likely to be far less time-consuming (and therefore less costly) than using search engines, while going directly to the preferred seller (i.e., not searching) requires the least amount of time (and therefore will be the least costly alternative). This study includes a self-reported index of perceived time constraint (TMCOST) adopted from Urbany et al. [48]. The opportunity cost of time spent in search is generally considered to be the cost associated with the search activity (e.g., [21]), and is assumed to have an adverse impact on the expected gains from search (e.g., [43]).

## Awareness of Online Search Options

Awareness of online search options influences the set of online search options available to buyers. Online buyers are assumed to be heterogeneous in their awareness about various online search strategies. This is captured through a variable called buyers’ awareness of shopping agents (AWARE). All online buyers are always aware of search engines and portals because they have access to online markets only through their Internet service provider’s (ISP) portals (e.g., AOL) or they start their browsing through some search engine (e.g., Google). However, not all buyers are aware of shopping agents or price comparison Web sites for the product they normally buy or intend to buy online. Buyers’ awareness of online search tools, other than search engines, defines the set of options they can choose from. For example, buyers who are unaware of shopping agents for any product can buy directly from their preferred seller, use a search engine/portal, or use both to look for lower prices.

In the study of consumers’ search for lower prices in the offline markets, demographics such as age and gender have frequently been used as measures of search efficiency (e.g., [15]). The general assumption is that with experience (reflected in age, female gender, and better education), consumers are more efficient. Therefore, we have decided to investigate whether these demographic variables influence buyers’ choice of online search strategy. Table 2 summarizes the variables used in our model. ## Empirical Estimation of the Model

THE SURVEY METHOD IS USED TO COLLECT DATA on Internet users who have either bought or intend to buy (1) books, (2) travel services (e.g., airline tickets, hotel reservations), or (3) digital information products (e.g., music CD, movie DVD, software). The study focuses on these products because (1) there is a well-established Web-based market for these products, and (2) they are undifferentiated products and therefore the main decision criterion for buyers of these products is price. The survey uses undergraduate, graduate, and doctoral students and postdoctoral researchers as subjects. While the undergraduate and graduate students were offered extra credit points to participate in the survey, the participation of doctoral and postdoctoral subjects was voluntary.

One reason for this choice of subjects is that they form a major segment among Internet users.<sup>3</sup> Since the research intends to study the online search behavior of buyers who go online with the intention to purchase products such as books, music CDs, DVD movies, and so on, college students are as good a subject as any other consumer group. Furthermore, since this study is to test an existing theory (i.e., economics of information search, in the context of Web-based markets), it is not necessary to have a representative sampling of subjects (e.g., [12, 13]). Therefore, students can be used as valid subjects for the study. Finally, the use of students as subjects helps to control for factors external to the model, such as education and e-market experience (e.g., [25]) and income (e.g., [2]), which may influence a buyer’s online search strategy and make it difficult to isolate the effect of variables critical to the model.

A pretest with 20 students was carried out to evaluate the appropriateness of the language used in the questionnaire, average time taken to complete the questionnaire, and overall ease of use of the questionnaire (online-based survey). After minor modifications based on the feedback from the test subjects, survey hosting Web site QuestionPro.com was used to host and administer the final questionnaire. The survey was activated on April 2, 2003, and remained active until May 10, 2003. The survey received 330 responses, of which 273 complete responses were from people who had made an online purchase. Since we are interested in understanding the online search behavior of online buyers, we decided to use only these responses for further investigation. ## Model Fit

The pseudo R-square measures for the MNL model indicate that the model performs fairly well (Table 5). However, pseudo R-square by itself is not considered to be a reliable measure of model fit. Therefore, additional statistics are used to check for the fitness of the proposed model to the sample data. High significance (p-value = 0.000) of the chi-square statistic for log likelihood (Table 5) implies that we can reject the null hypothesis that all effects of the independent variable are zero. This implies that one or more of the indicator variables have a significant effect on buyers’ choice of online search strategy. Furthermore, Pearson’s chi-square statistic is not significant, implying that we cannot reject the null hypothesis for this test, which states that the observed probabilities for the choice of online strategy are equal to their expected values.

## Significance of Independent Variables

The likelihood ratio tests for the model (Table 6) suggest that PPD (i.e., buyer perception of online price dispersion) and PSP (i.e., buyer perception of the price offered by his or her preferred online seller) are significant at $\alpha = 0 . 0 5$ , while AWARE is significant at $\alpha = 0 . 0 0 1$ and AGE is significant at $\alpha = 0 . 1$ . This implies that the null hypothesis that the effects on all log odds ratios of the dependent variable are simultaneously equal to zero can be rejected for the intercept, PPD, PSP, and AWARE at $\alpha = 0 . 0 5$ . In other words, a buyer’s perception of online price dispersion, perception of preferred seller’s price, and awareness of online shopping agents have a significant influence on the ratio of the buyer’s likelihood of choosing a mixed search strategy, using shopping agents, and using search engines to the likelihood of choosing not to search (i.e., buy direct).

Compared with going directly to his or her preferred seller (i.e., not searching), a buyer whose attitude toward the preferred seller’s price increases by a unit (i.e., the buyer thinks that the preferred seller’s price is relatively higher) is about 25 percent less likely to use shopping agents, about 4 percent less likely to use a search engine to look for a lower price, and about 55 percent less likely to use a mixed search strategy. This result is counterintuitive because one would expect that when a buyer perceives that the preferred seller has relatively high prices, the buyer would look for more price information to find a lower price. Our data suggests that the higher the perception about the preferred seller’s price, the less likely a buyer is to do any online search for lower prices. The reason for this might be found in literature on brand loyalty, which suggests that if a customer is loyal to a store or brand, then there is a willingness to pay more for this brand (e.g., [20, 22]). Thus, even if buyers perceive their preferred online seller to be selling at a higher price, they are still more likely to buy direct because they are willing to pay higher prices in return for the value they get from this preferred seller. In the case of online sellers, this value could be manifested in a better Web site, high trust associated with the seller’s brand, high switching cost for the buyer, and higher convenience (e.g., personalized interface, valuable suggestions). Furthermore, in the absence of other relevant information, or when consumers are uncertain about the quality of the brands (in our case the services offered by online sellers), they use price as a signal for quality, with relatively high prices indicating relatively high quality (e.g., [18, 27, 28, 32, 34]). Therefore, these buyers are more likely to buy direct from their preferred seller if they believe that the seller’s higher prices are a reflection of higher quality of service.

Interestingly, the only variable associated with the cost of search—that is, TMCOST (time constraint of the buyer)—is found to be insignificant. One reason for this could be that the amount of time it takes to do an online price search is not enough to have any significant effect on the time constraint of the buyer. Another reason could be that students do not assign much value to the opportunity cost of time. We might get a different result if we were to use a wider cross-section of consumers and products. For example, a busy executive or a working mother might be more constrained in terms of time than most undergraduate students. Overall, however, there is support for using economics of information search theory framework to explain buyers’ choice of online search strategy.

Online sellers who understand their potential consumers (i.e., know their age distribution, their gender distribution, their attitude toward online prices, etc.) can estimate the probability that these consumers will choose one or the other online search strategy. These sellers can then use this information to (1) plan their online advertising and promotion strategy, (2) estimate their probability of making a sale, and (3) improve their probability of making a sale.

## Managerial Implications

## Planning Online Advertising and Promotion

ONLINE SELLERS INVEST IN VARIOUS online advertisement and promotional activities in order to improve their chances of inclusion in a buyer’s consideration set. For example, they invest in search engine optimization to improve their listing on the search results pages by modifying their site code to make it more relevant and therefore more search engine compatible (e.g., [38]). Many sellers invest in paid placements—that is, pay search engines for placement in the sponsored section of the search results pages (e.g., [38]). Others purchase key word–linked banner advertisements on search engines and shopping agents to attract potential buyers. With all these options to choose from, a seller has to balance the expenses involved with the expected business generated by these activities. If sellers have an understanding of the dominant search strategy used by their potential buyers, they can invest more wisely in their online promotional activities. For example, if the potential buyers are more likely to search using search engines, then it makes sense to invest in search engine optimization or paid placements on search engines; if they are more likely to use shopping agents, then it makes sense to advertise price cuts on price comparison Web sites and buy key word–related banner advertisements on these Web sites. On the other hand, if potential buyers are more likely to go directly to their preferred seller’s Web site, then online sellers would be better off investing in customer loyalty programs and investing in activities such as improving their Web site stickiness, implementing Web site personalization, and thinking of ways to increase the switching cost for their customers.

## Estimating the Probability of Making a Sale

Simply put, the probability of an online seller making a sale equals the product of the probability that this seller is in the consideration set of a buyer and the probability that this seller has the best offer among all the sellers canvassed by the buyer. Now, the probability that a seller has the best offer depends, among other things, on the offers made by the other sellers in the consideration set, and the probability that a seller is in the consideration depends on the online search strategy used by the buyers. For example, if buyers use a shopping agent, then they get price information from all the online sellers and, therefore, their consideration set can be assumed to contain all the online sellers. On the other hand, if buyers do not do any search and go directly to their preferred seller, then their consideration set contains only one seller—that is, their preferred online seller. Before modeling online sellers’ probability of making a sale, we describe various components of the model, and the related assumptions are as follows:

## Sellers

The online market is assumed to have S profit-maximizing sellers who sell the same undifferentiated product. All these sellers are registered with all the search engines and allow shopping agents access to their prices. Finally, based on their price rank, each online seller is denoted by $S _ { j } ,$ where price rank $j = 1 , . . . , S$ . This implies that if a seller j is assumed to be selling at a price $P _ { j } ,$ , then $P _ { 1 } < . . . < P _ { j } < . . . < P _ { s }$

## Buyers’ Online Search Strategies

The model assumes that a buyer is searching with the intention of buying online [40]. Buyers’ online search pattern is classified into four categories identified in the Online Search Strategies subsection—that is, buy directly or do not search, use shopping agents, use search engines, and use a mixed search strategy. In addition to the aforementioned definition of buyers’ search strategies, some other assumptions used in the model are as follows:

1. If buyers decides to search, they are assumed to be searching for a lower price (e.g., [35, 36]).

2. Buyer i’s preferred seller is assumed to be selling at a price ranked j in the market.

3. The number of buyers who would prefer to shop (everything else being equal) directly from their preferred online seller, who is price ranked j (i.e., seller S ), is $b _ { _ i }$ . The number of buyers whose preferred seller is not seller S are denoted by $\overset { \prime } { b } _ { j } ^ { \prime } . \mathrm { A }$ buyer can have only one preferred seller for the same product. These buyers are referred to as locked-in buyers (or loyal buyers) for any seller price ranked $j .$ Furthermore, the model assumes that $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { j = S } b _ { j } \le B , } \end{array}$ where B is the total number of buyers in the online market.

4. The buyers can perform any number of searches—that is, visit any number of online stores to find a lower price, and the number of searches performed by buyer i is denoted by $n _ { _ i }$ , where $0 \leq n _ { { } _ { i } } \leq S$ , where S is the total number of online sellers.

## Probability of Making a Sale

The probability that a seller price ranked $k ( \mathrm { i } . { \mathrm { e } } . , S _ { k } )$ makes a sale to buyer i (whose preferred seller is price ranked j) is denoted by

$$
q _ {k} = q _ {k} ^ {1} + q _ {k} ^ {2} + q _ {k} ^ {3} + q _ {k} ^ {4},\tag{7}
$$

where

$$
q _ {k} ^ {1} = q 1 \times \mathrm{Probability}\tag{8}
$$

$( S _ { k }$ is the preferred seller of buyer i)

$$
q _ {k} ^ {2} = q 2 \times \text { Probability }\tag{9}
$$

$( S _ { k }$ has the best offer among the $S$ sellers)

$$
q _ {k} ^ {3} = q 3 \times \mathrm{Probability}\tag{10}
$$

$( S _ { k }$ Web site is among the $n _ { i }$ Web sites visited by the buyer and has the best offer in the set of $n _ { i }$ sellers canvassed by the buyer)

$$
q _ {k} ^ {4} = q 4 \times \text { Probability }\tag{11}
$$

(S has the best offer among all sellers in the consideration set and $k < j )$

In Equations (8) to (11), q1 is the probability that a buyer chooses to buy from his or her preferred seller, $q 2$ is the probability that the buyer uses a shopping agent, $q 3$ is the probability that the buyer uses a search engine, and $q 4$ is the probability that the buyer uses both the search engine and the price from the preferred seller. Note that $q 1 , q 2 , q 3$ , and q4 can be estimated using the empirical technique presented earlier in the paper (refer to the previous section to see how these probabilities are computed). Probabilities given by Equations (8) to (11) are computed in Appendix B. Substituting these probabilities in Equation (7) gives the probability that an online seller price ranked k will make a sale as follows

$$
q _ {k} = q 1 \times \frac {b _ {1}}{B} + q 2 + q 3 \frac {n _ {i}}{S} + q 4 \times \frac {1}{B} \left(\frac {n _ {i}}{S} \sum_ {j = 2} ^ {j = S} b _ {j} ^ {\prime} + b _ {1}\right) \text {   for   } k = 1\tag{12a}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} q _ {k} = q 1 \times \frac {b _ {k}}{B} + q 3 \frac {C _ {n _ {i} - 1} ^ {S - k}}{C _ {n _ {i}} ^ {S}} + q 4 \times \frac {1}{B} \left(\frac {C _ {n _ {i} - 1} ^ {S - k}}{C _ {n _ {i}} ^ {S}} \sum_ {j = k + 1} ^ {j = S} b _ {n} ^ {\prime} + b _ {k} \frac {C _ {n _ {i}} ^ {S - k + 1}}{C _ {n _ {i}} ^ {S}}\right) \\ \text {for} 1 <   k \leq S - n _ {i} + 1 \end{array}\tag{12b}
$$

$$
q _ {k} = q 1 \times \frac {b _ {k}}{B} \quad \mathrm{for} k > S - n _ {i} + 1.\tag{12c}
$$

As we can see, the probability of making a sale depends on factors such as buyers choice of online search strategy, number of online buyers loyal to the seller, and the seller’s price rank in the online markets.

## Improving the Probability of Making a Sale

As one can see from Equations (12a) to (12c), for any seller, the lowest price rank in the e-market results in the maximum probability of making a sale. This probability begins to drop as the price rank of the seller increases. The rate of this drop depends on the average search intensity of online buyers. For low search intensity, the drop is not as fast, whereas for relatively high average search intensity, this drop is steep. Even though all sellers know that a lowest price rank will maximize their probability of making a sale, they also realize that a lower price rank results in reduced revenues and therefore reduced profitability.

At price rank k, the probability that a seller will sell x quantities of the product is given by the binomial distribution $( q _ { k } ) ^ { x } ( 1 - q _ { k } ) ^ { B - }$ <sup>x</sup>. The expected profit of this seller, when it sells x units, is given by $P _ { k } ( q _ { k } ) ^ { x } ( 1 - q _ { k } ) ^ { B - }$ <sup>x</sup> (assuming production costs to be zero (e.g., [8])). At a higher price rank, the seller earns more revenues per unit sold. However, because the probability of making a sale goes down, the expected profits are not the same. If the seller intends to sell at least x units, then it will sell at a higher price rank k only if its expected profits at this price rank are higher than the expected profits at the lowest price rank in the market—that is,

$$
s _ {1} (q _ {1}) ^ {x} (1 - q _ {1}) ^ {B - x} \leq s _ {k} (q _ {k}) ^ {x} (1 - q _ {k}) ^ {B - x}
$$

$$
\frac {s _ {1}}{s _ {k}} \left(\frac {q _ {1}}{q _ {k}}\right) ^ {x} \times \left(\frac {1 - q _ {1}}{1 - q _ {k}}\right) ^ {B - x} \leq 1.
$$

Once an online seller realizes that, under certain conditions, a higher price rank can lead to better profits, the seller has to decide on the optimal price rank that maximizes this profit.

To achieve this optimal price rank, we propose the simple heuristic rule that says that a seller is better off by continuing to increase its price rank in increments of one as long as the expected profits from higher price rank are greater than or equal to the expected profits from the immediately preceding price rank. If a seller wants to increase its price and move from price rank k (i.e., price $P _ { k } )$ to price rank $k + 1$ (i.e., price $P _ { k + 1 } ) ,$ , it is better off doing this if $q _ { k + 1 } * P _ { k + 1 } \ge q _ { k } * P _ { k } ,$ that is, $P _ { k } / P _ { k + 1 } \leq q _ { k + 1 } / q _ { k }$ (assuming production costs to be zero), where $q _ { k + 1 }$ and $q _ { k }$ can be estimated from Equations (12a) to (12c). The ratio $P _ { k } / P _ { k + 1 }$ depends on the price dispersion between subsequent adjacent ranks, whereas the ratio $q _ { k + 1 } / q _ { k }$ depends on the magnitude by which the probability of making a sale is lowered when the price rank is incremented by one. A simple example is used to illustrate this point. Let us suppose that seller A is currently price ranked at 2, sells at \$10 per unit, and has probability of making a sale equal to 0.5. This seller wants to increase its price and become price ranked 3 in the market. Let us also assume that the existing price at rank 3 is \$20 (with probability of making a sale = 0.2), and the existing price at rank 4 is \$24 (with probability of making a sale $< 0 . 2 )$ . Expected revenues for the seller when it sells at its current price rank 2 is 10 \* $0 . 5 = \$ 5$ . If the seller plans to increase its price rank to 3, its probability of making a sale drops to 0.2. To achieve the same revenues as before, the seller will have to price its product at \$25 (because $2 5 * 0 . 2 = \ S 5 )$ . However, if the seller charges a price of \$25, it is now price ranked 5 (since price rank 4 is held by \$24), which further lowers its probability of making a sale, and therefore its expected revenues. Therefore, in order to ensure that it ends up in the price rank of its choice without compromising on its profits, a seller needs to be careful about the price dispersion when moving up in the price ranks.

## Conclusion

THIS PAPER EMPIRICALLY INVESTIGATES the validity of economics of information search theory in explaining buyers’ choice of online search strategy. It identifies some key determinants of buyers’ choice of online search strategy, empirically checks the significance of these determinants in explaining a buyer’s choice of online search strategy, and estimates the probability that a buyer would use one or the other online search strategy. The research uses an MNL model of unordered choice of online search strategies to evaluate the impact of demographics, perceived online price dispersion, perception about preferred sellers’ prices, time constraints, and awareness of online shopping agents on buyers’ choice of online search strategy to look for lower prices. We find that buyers’ attitudes toward the price offered by their preferred online seller, their perception of online price dispersion, and their awareness of shopping agents have a significant effect on buyers’ choice of online search strategy. An understanding of the probability distribution of buyers’ choice of online search strategy has significant managerial implications for an online seller. An online seller can use this knowledge to estimate its expected probability of making an online sale, optimize its online price, and improve its online promotional and advertising activities.

The empirical study is constrained by the assumptions inherent in the statistical model used for the empirical part of this paper, the use of students as subjects for the study, and focus on B2C e-markets for frequently purchased undifferentiated products (e.g., books, airline tickets, etc.). Therefore, the results obtained in the study should be interpreted within these contexts. Future research on the issue should involve empirical validation of economics of search theory in a more generalized setting involving a broader cross-section of buyers and products. Furthermore, any future empirical work on choice of online search pattern would do well to include online discussion forums, chat rooms, and blogs, because these forums are increasingly becoming a major source of product and price information.<sup>4</sup> Finally, more analytical work needs to be done to study the impact of buyers’ choice of online search strategy on e-market characteristics (e.g., price distribution and market structure). This paper presents a very simplistic approach that an online seller can use to identify its optimal price rank. However, a seller’s choice of its price rank is interdependent on what the other online sellers do. In any future research, this interdependence needs to be captured in order to get a better understanding of price dispersion in online markets.<sup>5</sup>

## Search Using Mixed Online Search Strategy

Potential online buyers can use any combination of the aforementioned pure search strategies. For example, they can use a search engine in addition to check for prices at their preferred online seller to look for lower prices. There can be several such combinations and we categorize all such search strategies as a mixed online search strategy or a mixed strategy. We can, however, ignore all mixed strategies that involve the use of shopping agents. With all these mixed strategies, buyers will end up buying from the lowest-priced seller. However, buyers will incur extra cost by using more than one search strategy and thereby will reduce their benefits from the trade. On the other hand, buyers can still find the lowest online price at a relatively lower search cost by just using the shopping agent. Therefore, buyers are better off using only a shopping agent rather than using a combination of shopping agent and other online search strategies. This process of elimination leaves us with only one more mixed search strategy—that is, buyers use a search engine to find a seller with the best offer in the consideration set of sellers obtained through the search engine, compare this price with that offered by their preferred seller, and then buy from the seller with the lower price among the two. When buyer i uses a mixed search strategy, he or she gets information from his or her preferred seller and information from the search engine before buying from the seller with the lowest price. If the search intensity of this buyer is $n _ { i }$ and the price rank of his or her preferred seller is $j ,$ then a seller price ranked k will never make a sale if $k > S - n _ { i } + 1 \mathrm { o r } k > j$ , irrespective of the relationship between price rank j and $S - n _ { i } + 1$ , because the buyer would always buy from the preferred seller or some other lower-priced seller. Therefore, seller $S _ { k }$ would be able to sell only to those buyers whose preferred seller is price ranked above or equal to the price rank of seller $S _ { k } .$ The probability that a buyer comes from the population of buyers with preferred sellers price ranked above k is

$$
\frac {\sum_ {j = k + 1} ^ {j = S} b _ {j} ^ {\prime}}{B},
$$

and with preferred seller price ranked equal to k is $b _ { i } / B$ . Also, seller $S _ { k } \mathrm { ^ { * } s }$ price should be lowest among all the sellers canvassed. The probability of this happening is

$$
\frac {C _ {n _ {i} - 1} ^ {S - k}}{C _ {n _ {i}} ^ {S}}

when $k < j ,$ , and is

$$
\frac {C _ {n _ {i}} ^ {S - k = 1}}{C _ {n _ {i}} ^ {S}}
$$

when $k = j$ (see Figure B1).
