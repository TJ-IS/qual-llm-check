# Verbal and visual representations in task redesign: how different viewpoints enter into information systems design discussions

Jarmo Sarkkinen\* & Helena Karsten<sup>†</sup>

\*University of Jyväskylä, Department of Computer Science and Information Systems, PO Box 35, FIN-40014 University of Jyväskylä, Finland, email: jarmo.sarkkinen@jyu.fi, and <sup>†</sup>University of Turku, Department of Information Technology, Lemminkäisenkatu 14A, FIN-20520 Turku, Finland, email: eija.karsten@it.utu.fi

Abstract. We explore an important phase of information systems design (ISD), namely task redesign, and especially how different viewpoints enter into the discussions. We study how one particular visual representation, a process diagram, is interpreted and how alternative, even competing, representations are produced verbally. To tie the visual and verbal representations and the representational practices to wider social practices, we develop and use the Extended Three-dimensional Model of discourse. Visual representations emerged as focal in bringing in the different viewpoints and as reference points for discussions. Our model provided a focused and powerful means to unveil for the outside researchers how the planned changes in tasks and authority relationships instigated a social struggle. The IS designer was an outsider to the client organization and therefore considered only the information system, not the social system in which it was intended to operate. Other participants did not recognize this, therefore, seeing the designer as furthering managerial interests. Seeing task redesign in the social context of a client organization can help IS designers and researchers to understand what the users see naturally, that is, the ISD as a dynamic, enabling but socially constrained process where different viewpoints are represented.

Keywords: critical discourse analysis, task redesign, verbal and visual representations, social struggle, social determinants

## INTRODUCTION

Information systems design (ISD) projects are established to construct not only new computerized information systems but also new work practices, task divisions between people, and organizational structures around the particular work activities. Moreover, appropriate information systems can increase the influence of organizational units and their representatives (Saunders, 1981; Lee, 1991). This may explain why several stakeholder groups participate in the ISD process. Common stakeholder groups include the company as the customer, the users of the future IS, and the software house as the provider. During ISD meetings, these groups and their representatives can be assumed to protect their interests by trying to influence the ISD process and its outcomes.

Influences on the ISD process have been studied widely. As early as 1984, Franz and Robey recognized that ISD is a political process in which different actors pursue their individual interests (Franz & Robey, 1984). This kind of political behaviour is typically intended to strengthen one’s position at the expense of others (Levine & Rossmoore, 1995). There can also be rituals that serve as ‘an acceptable cover for inexpressible political motives (Robey & Markus, 1984, p. 5). Howell & Higgins (1990) concentrate on specific influence mechanisms and study how champions of technological innovation use influence tactics. According to Markus & Bjørn-Andersen (1987), information technology (IT) professionals can exercise power over end users by technical, structural, conceptual and symbolic means. Myers & Young (1997) have done an in-depth study of the symbolic means (e.g. rhetoric, that is, effective uses of language to please or persuade) employed by user managers to push through hidden agendas. Thus, we know a great deal about ways and means of influencing the ISD process but not much about their particular uses during ISD discussions.

Robey & Markus (1984) emphasized that participants who are unaware of hidden political processes are likely to be less effective. We accept this view in the sense that IS designers who do not know hidden social aspects may act politically ineffectively by contributing directly to the political processes within the client organization. By examining how verbal and visual means of influence are used during ISD discussions, we strive to make the political processes transparent and thereby more comprehensible to all participants, especially to IS designers. We study the interaction of different stakeholder groups through power relations, as relations of struggle (Fairclough, 2001, p. 28). The stakeholders participating in ISD meetings engage in this struggle with the help of different kinds of representations, that is, with particular portrayals of something or someone. Our focus here is on verbal representations produced in the talk, and on visual representations such as wall charts, screen layouts, and the like which play a pivotal role during ISD meetings depicting, for example, past and future work processes.

Organizational structures (i.e. institutional status and authority) are naturally an important source of power during the ISD process (e.g. Noble & Newman, 1993; Thanasankit, 2002). Especially in the Scandinavian Collective Resource Approach (Ehn & Kyng, 1987) the structural shaping of the ISD process has been emphasized. The Collective Resource Approach challenges the structural determination manifested in the capitalist labour process (Bansler & Bødker, 1985) with the strategy of ‘changing the balance of the contradiction between labour and capital, by strengthening the labour side’ (Bjerknes & Bratteteig, 1995, p. 78), that is, by strengthening the influence of the user participants.

Focusing only on organizational structures, however, is insufficient to understand their actual implications. The participants involved in ISD discussions are not ‘helplessly subjected to immovable systems of power’ (Fairclough, 1992, p. 57) but, in principle, the powerful structures can also be ‘transformed’ by these participants by means of verbal and visual representations (Fairclough, 1992, p. 58). Knights & Murray (1994) take a look at the management practices in the use and development of new IT. However, their view of political practices and the underlying ‘conditions of possibility’ takes a considerably hybrid and, for this reason, unoperationalizable form to help in the actual analysis. By contrast, Alvarez (2001; 2002) has provided some preliminary but more accessible investigations into how power and identity are (re)produced, but she concentrates only on verbal means. She has shown how individuals try to retain their place in the organization during identity negotiations through uses of language (2001); how user interviews conducted by the IS designer can produce and challenge client identities through uses of verbal representations (2001); and how power inequality, identity formation and symbolic control may explain these kinds of uses of language (2002).

In studies of user–developer interaction (O’Neill et al., 1999), both verbal and visual representations have been investigated. For example, Trigg et al. (1991) show that even though one user was reluctant to play with a future presented by the prototype, the stories, triggered by the unsuccessful prototype, enabled the user and the designers to sustain a mutual learning process. Bowers & Pycock (1994) in turn found that prototypes can enable arguments and counter-arguments without the need for direct requests or refusals that could make the situation even more tense. Moreover, Karasti (2001) shows how video collages of actual work practices can help a heterogeneous design group coconstruct shared understandings of the work practice. In this group of studies, however, structures have not been studied as central elements.

To take into account the whole arena, therefore, we include both verbal and visual representations and structural determinants as produced and reproduced through uses of these representations. Based on Fairclough’s three-dimensional model of discourse (Fairclough, 1992), we develop and use our Extended Three-dimensional Model (E3-D model) to examine and explain the power-political ISD discussions, both as socially constructed and as socially constrained (and enabled) in relation to how visual representations are interpreted, rerepresented or strengthened with verbal representations, and how alternative verbal representations are produced.

## VERBAL AND VISUAL REPRESENTATIONS IN RELATION TO INTERACTION

Interaction is a meaning-making process in which one person produces a verbal or visual representation that another person then tries to understand, with the associated connotations, in order to attach a primary meaning to it (Mingers, 1995). This second person, Mingers argues, responds to the representation by ‘converting an intention into a specific form’ (Mingers, p. 301), that is, a new verbal or visual representation is produced. This reception–production cycle is the foundation of any interaction.

Verbal and visual representations provide descriptions that focus only on some elements of objects or events and leave others out. They can be used to add qualities and attributions to these objects and events. Potter (1996) describes this as follows:

A central feature of any description is its role in categorization; a description formulates some object or event as something; it constitutes it as a thing, and a thing with specific qualities. The description presents something as good or bad, big or small, more violent or less violent, although often with more subtle options. Another common role of descriptions is to present some action as routine or, conversely, exceptional. (p. 111)

Descriptions are subjective re-presentations (Potter, 1996; Hall, 1997) of the particular properties of the reality that is designed during the ISD meetings. With these representations that highlight ‘a few intentionally selected qualities of that which is represented’ (Kyng, 1995, p. 46), participants can also influence each other. As receivers, they can treat ‘descriptions as if they come from groups and individuals with interests’ (Potter, 1996, p. 110, italics in the original). Thus, verbal and visual representations can be used to instigate action by the other participants.

These representations cannot further the interests of a stakeholder group independently, as representations do not have agency. Human-made artefacts cannot have what Aanestad (2003) calls ‘the intentionality component that is a distinctive feature of human agency’ (p. 7, emphasis in the original). For this reason, they cannot have any significance separate from the human interests applied to them during the interaction.

Agre (2003, p. 285) emphasizes that ‘understanding does not reside in representations’ but that representations need to be interpreted. The contextual and situated interpretations are constrained (and enabled) jointly by the representation itself and by the circumstances in which this representation is interpreted. Not only the interpreters’ personal qualities can have a role during this process but also other people who are present can have an influence through their power positions and their attempts to use power to influence the interpretations.

As representations accumulate, a ‘chain of re-representations’ (Star, 1989; Bowers, 1992) emerges. For example, when moving from concrete, rich and informal work experiences, revealed through narratives, to more abstract and formal representations such as process diagrams, ‘the deletions of layers of detail’ (Berg, 1997, p. 410) is inescapable. This can cause problems in understanding representations. Furthermore, diagrammatic or other symbolic representations may be incomprehensible to some participants because one needs to know ‘specialized codes’ (Henderson, 1998, p. 158), the language and the grammar used in producing representations.

Visual representations are likely to constrain discussions more than verbal representations. Talk and written language construct rather abstract versions of the subject matter, leaving it open to various interpretations. Visual representations such as in a poster, however, bring with it more culturally relevant cues constraining the imagination more than those provided by verbal ones. It is, however, easy to agree with the common argument that, for example, a software process diagram with a strict notation constrains the audience’s imagination more than, for example, a quite freely drawn rich picture diagram. Talk can be used to set the ‘limits on the placement and the content of recipients’ responses’ (Amann & Knorr Cetina, 1990, p. 94) whereas visual representations can be used more powerfully because they provide ‘a grammar for constructing the object’ (Karsten et al., 2001, p. 90, emphasis in the original) either verbally or visually. This kind of visual grammar for constructing and discussing the object, embedded in the visual representation, describes how to organize and classify the talk around it. Thus, visual representations not only ‘enlist[s] group participation’ (Henderson, 1991, p. 456) but also constrain it (Karsten et al., 2001).

Texts are read (Figure 1). Talk and written language are used to construct verbal texts. Visual representations produced with different types of symbolic languages can also be seen as texts to be read (Woolgar, 1991). Reading these visual texts emphasizes that individuals need to interact with them in order to interpret and understand what they mean.

However, only ‘a limited set of readings’ is possible (Woolgar, 1994, p. 205) as a result of the embedded grammar. The participants ‘interact not only with each other but also with the object to which they attach their comments’, and their talk ‘appears to some degree to be organized by’ (Amann & Knorr Cetina, 1990, p. 92, italics in the original) the grammar of this visual text, structuring the mutual orientation of the interacting participants (Suchman, 1990). Despite this, one may read a visual text from one’s own perspective and this reading can even be brought to the discussion along with verbal representations, but the grammar always guides or frames the reading to some extent.

## STUDYING VERBAL AND VISUAL REPRESENTATIONS IN DISCOURSE

## Discourse analysis and social critique

Discourse normally comprises verbal texts as parts of social practices (Potter, 1996). Several specific approaches to discourse and its analysis are available. The kind of approach we are looking for should be critical, focusing on how verbal and visual representations are intentionally used to further the interests of participants. Sinclair & Coulthard (1975) for example, are primarily concerned with developing non-critical accounts of ‘systematic organizational properties of dialogue’, while Fowler et al. (1979) and Kress & Hodge (1979) produce critical readings of ‘the text as product’. Even though critical, these two do not focus on the actual processes of text production and interpretation. Potter & Wetherell (1987) study these but the focus of their non-critical approach is on ‘the variable constitution of the self in discourse’ (Fairclough, 1992, p. 25), and not on what is being constructed.

Yet another approach, Conversation Analysis (Hutchby & Wooffitt, 1998) aims at ‘discovering structures in texts’ that are products of interaction (Fairclough, 1992, p. 17), but structural determinants are excluded from their non-critical discoveries. Foucault (1972; 1979; 1981) brings this social determination to the production and interpretation of text. He sees discourse as contributing to the constitution of the social by allowing and limiting certain ways of doing things:

Just as a discourse ‘rules in’ certain ways of talking about a topic, defining an acceptable and intelligible way to talk, write, or conduct oneself, so also, by definition, it ‘rules out’, limits and restricts other ways of talking, of conducting ourselves in relation to the topic or constructing knowledge about it. (Hall, 1997, p. 44)

Fairclough (1992; 1995; 2001) goes a step further and develops a critical approach for describing, interpreting and explaining how ‘discourses operate’ (Potter, 1996, p. 87), that is, how talk is intentionally used to further certain issues:

. . . language is both a site and a stake in [class] struggle, and those who exercise power through language must constantly be involved in struggle with others to defend (or lose) their position. (Fairclough, 2001, p. 29)

From the perspective of our study, it appears that Fairclough’s approach would allow us to tie the interests of the various participating stakeholders to the actual interaction during ISD meetings. In this sort of a study, the aim is to produce knowledge through the practical (or hermeneutic) and emancipatory (or critical-emancipatory) cognitive interests (Habermas, 1972). The method of inquiry in the former is interpretive, and reflection and critique of ideology and discourse in the latter. We take a critical view of the analysed discourse but do not take any explicit and effective action for change even though this is the other side of the critical-emancipatory knowledge-constitutive interest. The current study is a social critique focused on the hidden management practices. It simply shows under which sort of constraints and coercion participants are acting. This stance can be called ‘critical interpretivism’ without the explicit emancipatory intent.

## Fairclough’s three-dimensional model of discourse

Fairclough (1992, p. 78) sees discourse to be ‘language use as a form of social practice’ (Fairclough, 1992, p. 63). He does not confine himself, however, to dealing only with the processes through which the social objects and subjects and their relationships are constituted but he also sees these processes as structurally enabled and constrained. That is, he ties what takes place here and now to its broader context and history. Fairclough’s (1992; 1995; 2001) Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) is a method based on this understanding.

In CDA, interaction is studied with a focus on what is talked about (talk), in what kind of discussion (discursive practice), and within what kind of social process (social practice):

The method of discourse analysis includes linguistic description of the language text, interpretation of the relationship between the (productive and interpretative) discursive processes and the text, and explanation of the relationship between the discursive processes and the social processes. (Fairclough, 1995, p. 97, italics in the original)

This is called the three-dimensional model of discourse (Fairclough, 1995). It is composed of the three embedded dimensions, text/description, discursive practice/interpretation and social practice/explanation (Figure 2).

The three-dimensional model of discourse helps to go deeply into text (i.e. what is discussed) in order to describe it as a textual product. However, Fairclough emphasizes that processes of text production and interpretation and texts as products cannot be separated from each other during the analysis. He presents a set of questions to analyse the vocabulary, grammar and textual structure in these texts (Appendix 1, sections a–c).

With the second perspective on discourse, the focus is laid on discursive practice, on the processes of text production and interpretation. The analytical questions (Appendix 1, section d) help to understand discursive practice as a ‘combination of what is in the text and what is “in” the interpreter’ (Fairclough, 2001, p. 118).

With the third perspective, the aim is to explain discourse as a social practice in terms of its situational determinants (e.g. locally constructed actor positions and verbal acts placing limits on or enabling other verbal acts), institutional determinants (e.g. relations of power and age/ gender differences) and societal determinants (ideologies) (Fairclough, 2001). The purpose is also to illustrate the effects of the constituted discourse on the determinants.

Fairclough (2001) sees power as a function of social structures and interaction processes. Power manifests itself behind discourse as the structural determination of that discourse. There is ‘a matrix of relations of power’ (Fairclough, 2001, p. 135) in every organization, enabling or constraining organizational members in their activities. However, people are not wholly manipulated by the relations of power but can commit to a struggle where power manifests itself in discourse through ‘powerful participants controlling and constraining the contributions of nonpowerful participants (Fairclough, 2001, pp. 38–39, italics in the original). That is, the participants never participate equally, but the more powerful participants can influence the weaker participants directly.

## The E3-D model of discourse

Fairclough’s three-dimensional model does not explicitly include visual representations and representational practices even though some written and objectified representations have been used as examples in his writings. Here we present our E3-D model (Figure 3) where the ‘text’ and ‘discursive practice’ dimensions have been extended from their original form but where the ‘social practice’ dimension remains intact.

People do not merely talk but they very often use different kinds of representations to visualize and illustrate what they mean. We use the term ‘representation’ here to include not only verbal but also visual texts, which differ from verbal ones by their distinct characteristics of visible organization, embedded codes and grammars. With visual representations, people not only produce descriptions of things but also impose on others spatially presented and objectified frames for how to talk about the presented description.

As we want to study how people interact with both verbal and visual representations, we need to focus on representational practice instead of discursive practice. The process of text production and interpretation now differs from the original form in that there is something external to the interactants which can be used to amplify one’s description or to influence the others in how they should understand and talk about this description. Here, the focus of analysis is not only on what is in the talk and what is in the interpreter but also what is in the external visual representation and how it frames the ‘discursive universe’ of the participants.

To study ISD meetings as representational practice, the analytical questions proposed by CDA need to be supplemented with a method that helps to study how the visual representations are made part of the interaction. We chose to use Interaction Analysis (IA) by Jordan & Henderson (1995) to inform us about how the change process is enacted within the visual-spatial design milieu. The seven ‘analytic foci’ of IA include the structure of events, the temporal organization of activity, turn-taking, participation structures, trouble and repair, the spatial organization of activity, and artefacts and documents (pp. 56–79). These are analysed by studying closely video made of the meeting, supported by the detailed transcripts and other artefacts and documents when available.

With these two complementary approaches, the purpose is to show how the change process is enacted within the wider institutional context. To repeat, the focus in this article with relation to the E3-D model is on understanding how visually presented task redesigns are interpreted and re-represented in talk and how these alternative verbal representations are produced.

The relationship between visual representations and discourse is dialectic in the sense that the available visual representation as a resource constrains the discourse taking a form of text production and interpretation. However, at the same time, this structurally determined text production and interpretation as a process constrains the potential interpretations and uses of the visual representation.

The social practice in the E3-D model is the same as in the original model. The situational, institutional and societal structures are experienced internally by the participants as determinants external to them. They are aware of them tacitly. There is thus no need to extend the social practice in terms of representations because being confronted with social structures does not happen normally in an externalized, visual, way.

The E3-D model shapes the analysis and presentation of the results. With this model, the analyst does not focus merely on the outcomes of the task redesign but begins to see this task redesign process itself as socially constructed. The analyst can see how this process may appear as a social struggle that is constrained and enabled not only in the talk but also in relation to visual representations. In the task redesign process the model sensitized the analyst to see uses of representations as socially determined and these to have effects on the context.

However, we will not only extend but also limit our study. Our focus is on the situational and institutional determinants and on the situational effects of discourse we studied (Figure 4). To connect societal determinants or changes in them to the discussions in the meeting would have required a much wider access to the companies than we had. We do not aim to explain the institutional or societal effects taking place around the analysed session but focus on the local effects of the discourse on the situational structures. This is because, first, our data does not cover the excluded effects in a sufficient manner. Second, the microlevel analysis of discourse does not enable us to recognize these in the data. Third, we only studied a requirements determination project of limited duration (about 6 months), while more far-reaching effects of the discourse can be seen only over a longer period of time.

## The research process

To gain an insider view, the first author and his fieldwork colleague conducted ethnographic fieldwork for the whole duration of the requirements determination project. Data were gathered through participant observation, that is, ‘simultaneous emotional involvement and objective detachment’ (Tedlock, 2000, p. 465). We also videotaped the meetings to collect authentic data, and materials used during the meetings (use cases, process diagrams, screen models and other documents) were collected. Materials that were used in the sessions were regarded as particularly important because they represent institutionalized forms of determination of social action in one of the most effective forms. In this case, the process diagram appeared as the central form of determination.

Here we concentrate on one fragment of the whole information system, the handling of payment plans, as the analysis took a very detailed form. The interaction sequences during which payment plans were discussed were located on the video tape. This resulted in a sequence of about 5 min duration. In this sequence, interaction took place around a wall chart which represented how the payment plans were to be handled in the future. To enable closer analysis, this interaction sequence was then transcribed according to Gail Jefferson’s transcript notation (Appendix 2).

A group of five researchers (one senior and four juniors) gathered together, and the transcribed talk was analysed in this group. First, we watched the whole sequence from the videotape. Then, we analysed this sequence in detail. The participants were allowed to interrupt the video to explain their interpretations of what was taking place. This analysis session was also tape-recorded to allow the first author to revisit the interpretations presented. Throughout the process, the first author compared his own interpretations with those of the other analysts to find corroboration.

After the session, the first author continued with the preliminary interpretations. Three shorter episodes (53, 43 and 60 s in length) were chosen to enable a detailed study of the shift from harmonious interaction to conflict where different re-representations of the visual representation were produced. IA’s analytical perspectives and CDA’s questions were then applied. Meanings of utterances, pauses and overlapping talk were tied to the preceding and subsequent turns in interaction. The line of reasoning is explicitly spelled out with the help of the transcript to make it possible for the reader to follow the storyline and its arguments, and to make possible her or his own subjective judgements on the arguments. The analyst’s frame of reference moved back and forth between the three dimensions of discourse and between the visible and the hearable.

## THE CASE

The current study involves two organizations, the Funding Department of the Finnish Slot Machine Association (RAY) as the customer and the ‘Incognito’ software house as the provider for a new financial-administrative information system. RAY raises funds through gaming operations, and with these they support activities of several non-profit societies and foundations in health and social welfare.

In the Funding Department, the proposals for funding are prepared, the funds are paid according to the payment plan, and the use of the funding is monitored. In the project term definitions (updated on May 4, 2000), the term ‘payment plan’ is described as follows: ‘At the beginning of each year an organization can mail a payment plan for the certain due item with the expected due date and instalment’. A due item can be a building project, a purchase or the like. In the old system, payment plans were handled as paper files kept by the Funding Secretary.

In the Funding Department, the proposals for funding are prepared, the funds are paid according to the payment plan, and the use of the funding is monitored. In the old system, payment plans were handled as paper files kept by the Funding Secretary. The requirements determination project for the new system ran for the first 6 months of 2000. According to the project plan, its aim was to ‘determine a computerized information system to support the payment procedure so that this system and those systems supporting the payment supervision and preparation form a coherent whole, integrating all parts of the system’.

A new process for creating, updating, supervising and approving payment plans was developed during the process planning meeting on February 14, and the new process was talked through with the two Supervisors on February 25. Ten people attended the February 14 meeting: Designer Pekka from Incognito, Researchers Sari and Jarmo from the University, System Manager Tuula and Project Manager Sami from the RAY Funding Department’s Information Systems Unit along with Manager Timo, Senior Supervisor Asko, Junior Supervisor Erkki, Preparing Officer Hannu and Funding Secretary Marja as RAY office workers. Names except those of the researchers are pseudonyms.

In the organization chart, the persons highlighted in grey were present at the February 14 and February 25 meetings, those highlighted with cross-hatching attended the process planning meeting on February 14 only, and those with dotted lines represent central organizational members who, however, did not have a role in the project team.

Usually, inviting and conducting the meeting had been one of the responsibilities of Designer Pekka. On February 6, System Manager Tuula, however, temporarily took over the control of the project. She first sent an email to the whole project team and then a separate message to Designer Pekka and emphasized the point that

[t]he danger is, if we build too much on the old practice, that this practice will remain almost as it is and only the user interface will be changed from character-based to graphic. We have now access to a totally new technology, which gives a completely different platform and much more alternatives to change operating procedures, work practices, and task division. (Email message, dated February 6)

In this way, System Manager Tuula presented a preference for a radical change over using the old system documentation as a primary representational support during earlier requirements determination sessions. She suggested an alternative approach:

It would be worthwhile to describe processes first by using the wall chart technique: describing operations (and tasks inside them) in different phases of the process with paper sheets and the course of working with arrows . . . (Email message, dated February 6)

This appears to have been the view also of Manager Timo who, next day, sent an email message directly to Designer Pekka as if accepting the suggestion:

[t]he points Tuula highlights are good ones. Processes and their needs ought to be determined and then described. (Email message, dated February 7)

This implies that Manager Timo had already entered into an alliance with System Manager Tuula who soon made suggestions on how to proceed:

So that we could get at work as soon as possible on Monday, I thought I could lay the groundwork on Thursday afternoon: I could string up wall chart sheets across the wall, think a bit about that description method, and even begin a bit with the process description based on which we then would start. (Email message, dated February 9)

On February 14, the future process for handling payment plans was presented as a process diagram on the wall chart (a fragment of this is presented in Figure 6). The planned responsibilities for the ‘Approver’, ‘Supervisor’ and ‘Funding Secretary’ roles were shown in this process diagram. The diagram was then attached to the requirements determination documentation and used in the subsequent sessions. In this process planning meeting, Manager Timo and System Manager Tuula took over the determination of processes and needs. Setting up the wall chart happened swiftly and was, for the most part, controlled by these two Managers. In this way, they became the ‘owners’ of the process diagram.

Funding Secretary Marja, Designer Pekka and the two Supervisors had evident difficulty participating in the discussion. Funding Secretary Marja was invited to join in on these discussions only when the current process was talked through but she took no part when the new process was planned. The Supervisors were not consulted much although they were responsible for handling the payment plans. Designer Pekka was rarely invited to join in but he, however, decided to take part in the discussions around the wall chart and finally managed to become one of the ‘insiders’.

On February 23, Designer Pekka sent a meeting invitation again. The control of the project was back to him. The meeting invitation said that the purpose of the February 25 meeting was to ‘go through the planned payment process’. The aim, in particular, was to talk through the functions and the related tasks, and to expand the related concepts, as described. The process diagram accompanied the invitation. It was a duplicate of the wall chart, which was still in place in the meeting room during the February 25 meeting.

According to the process diagram (Figure 6), the Funding Secretary would create payment plan files on the system and enter the due dates and amounts of money into them. He or she could later update the plan if needed. The Funding Secretary could then forward instalments to the Approver who was responsible for the approval task. A manager would be the Approver. The diagram also indicated that the Supervisors could create payment plan files on the system and update them.

The redesign on the wall chart differs from the current work practice in two respects. First, the Approver was introduced to accept payment plans. This task had been one of the Supervisors’ main responsibilities so far. Second, the Funding Secretary was no longer the only person who was supposed to create and update the payment plan files into the system but also the Supervisors were planned to do that. That is, the process diagram suggests new work arrangements, with two major changes in the Supervisors’ work.

The main focus of the current analysis is on the February 25 meeting, with five people present. The two Supervisors, Asko and Erkki, and Designer Pekka used the wall chart, the handout versions of the process diagram and discussions to interpret and re-represent the revised payment process. Researchers Sari and Jarmo observed. Manager Timo told during the February 14 meeting that System Manager Tuula and he himself would only take part in the process planning but the others would be responsible for the requirements determination. Manager Timo also told that he would not want to influence too much by killing ideas too early. It is, however, difficult to say whether the absence of the managers in the February 25 meeting was for these reasons or for more political reasons of wanting the process diagram and the Designer to be present as confirming voices, speaking on their behalf.

## ANALYSIS OF DISCOURSE

## Overview of the episodes

During the first episode, the Supervisors and the Designer appeared to find the process diagram ambiguous. They made effort to understand the diagram by jointly interpreting and rerepresenting it together. During the second episode, however, perspectives differed. Supervisor Asko was concerned about the new process threatening the Supervisors’ status. For him, the process diagram suggested that the Supervisors would no longer approve payment plans and that some of the Funding Secretary’s duties would be assigned to them. Designer Pekka relied on the process diagram. During the third episode, the Designer suggested the new task division, represented in the process diagram. Despite the Supervisors’ counter-arguments, he adhered to the Managers’ view and went a step forward by suggesting that the Supervisors could scan papers and create payment plans into the system. With this suggestion, the Designer threatened the Supervisors’ status, leading to conflict.

In the following, these three episodes are analysed in detail using our E3-D model. Each episode begins with the transcribed text and the internal segmentation of that text. The tran scribed texts are translated from Finnish by the authors. This analysis section ends with an overall summary of the outcomes of the analysis.

The collective interest appears to be to understand the process diagram; the participants control the joint interpretation process together, as the whole episode illustrates. The aim with this narrative seems to be to anchor the joint interpretation process in the work practice, to deepen the discussion, but Erkki does not seem to have his own interests at stake. By looking at Asko, Erkki seems to hope that Asko could give a confirmation. Pekka then joins in on the discussion again and constructs a problem:

But why does it here go to ((turn 9)).

Pekka provokes thoughts regarding why payment plans are planned to go to the Decision-Maker in the process diagram. Contrary to Erkki, Pekka tries to make sense of the process diagram and not of the current process. This is indicated by the indexical word (‘here’) anchoring his talk in the process diagram. Pekka does not talk about the role name anymore but is concerned about how payment plans move around. Pekka refers to the representation of the new process (‘according to this’) and makes it problematic (‘doesn’t go’). Several uses of ‘but’ during turns 7, 9, 10 and 11 indicate that the whole process is open to multiple interpretations and this is what certainly appears to unite the participants during this first episode.

In sum, in the first episode, verbal representations were used to jointly construct interpretations of the process diagram. This visual representation was interpreted as contradicting the view of the participants. The participants acted as equal interactants in a joint interpretation mode and controlled the representational practice by taking turns.

He, however, disengages himself from the given narrative to conceal his interests. He constructs the current situation as valid also in the future process and justifies it with the practice-oriented voice (‘we have only got Supervisors and Decision-Makers’). ## EXPLAINING DISCOURSE: THE THREE EPISODES AS SOCIAL PRACTICE

Our purpose has so far been to describe and interpret how the different stakeholders discuss task redesigns, that is, how they both interpret and re-represent the process diagram and how they bring conflicting viewpoints to the ISD discussions with their verbal representations. We next concentrate on explaining the process analysed above. We discuss this process as a social practice by focusing on its situational and institutional determinants and situational effects. Organizational positions, organizational task divisions, distribution of authority, work practice knowledge, different abilities to read visual representations, and the properties of these visual representations are discussed as determinants that enable and constrain the unfolding process.

During the first episode, the participants had trouble understanding the process diagram. This was possibly because its structure conveyed only what the new process was but it did not explain how and why the Managers had modified the current process. By comparing the new process with the current process, the participants found out how it had been modified but they could not work out the rationale behind why it had been changed. The participants had acted as bystanders (Goffman, 1981, p. 132) during the 14th February process planning meeting and some of the decisions made in that meeting may not have been explained clearly. Therefore, it may have been difficult for them to understand the process diagram although they appeared to have fairly good skills in reading its notation.

The intangibility of the process diagram could also stem from the lack of layers of detail. For example, the design decision context was not included. The process diagram lacked justifications for decisions (Flood & Romm, 1996) and the rationale behind evolutionary steps of the process (Ramesh & Jarke, 2001). This appeared to be harmful to the joint interpretation process.

Nevertheless, the process diagram enabled the participants to act together because its grammar organized their talk and structured their mutual orientation. The participants based their arguments on the reading provided by the visual representation but they were able to suggest other readings, as well. Thus, the process diagram, as a visual text, provided a basis for discussion but did not limit alternative interpretations of the process.

The Designer did not take the visually represented task redesign as given during the first episode. Stolterman (1999) has emphasized that designers consider early designs as ‘organizing principles’ that are only tentative, generic and vague, according to Arnheim (1995). This may explain the Designer’s critical stance during the first episode.

During the second episode, however, the Designer gradually appeared to be constrained by the visual representation. Managerial authority may explain why the Designer soon clung to the process diagram and began to ground his arguments upon his ‘information systems’ reading of the visual representation. The Designer seemed to give higher priority to the process diagram and the task division it presented because the Managers had constructed them.

This argument is supported by our observation that already during earlier meetings the Designer frequently wanted to have the last word from the Manager and by the fact that the Designer once told me that things go much easier if the Manager attends the meetings because he knows so much about the current business. These may explain why the Designer did not go against the Managers’ view when the Supervisors began to resist the suggested change.

The Supervisors appeared to be worried about the erosion of their status. It is understandable that one does not want to have more routine work tasks after being one central organizational authority. However, the Supervisors appeared to have no ambition to achieve a more valued organizational position, rather they only tried to sustain the organizational status quo. This work-practice knowledge and the absence of the Manager made it possible for the Supervisor to constitute relatively strong verbal representations to support the current distribution of authority and to go against the new task division that was visually represented.

During the third episode, a social struggle emerged. The Senior Supervisor took a valuebound perspective on the Managers’ represented view and opposed it because the process diagram suggested a demotion of his work tasks and status. The more valued work tasks were centralized to the Manager and the Supervisors’ and the Funding Secretary’s tasks were decentralized.

The Designer still saw the wall chart as a description of the tasks and their order, separate from the social world. That is, he saw an abstracted and limited model of the future, detached from its social context. It was hidden from him, and so were the relationships of power in it, by his education and work experience, which made him comfortable with the abstractions and models. The wall chart was, for him, an easier depiction of the future information system than the talk of the participants who brought the social world into ISD. He neither saw representations (his own included) as means of influence nor himself as part of the social struggle.

The Designer, however, assumed the new process was a neutral proposal for the Supervisors, as it was for himself. He took a value-bound perspective on the represented task division when presenting his suggestion. He was not conscious of how value-bound his suggestion was but seemed to think it was just a neutral proposal having nothing to do with the redistribution of power within the client organization. He also seemed to think that the Managers’ original suggestion was neutral.

The Supervisors did not know that the Designer suggested the represented process neutrally. They, instead, seemed to think that the Designer was an active collaborator in the redistribution of authority and power. A struggle and conflict emerged just from this misunderstanding that the Designer had taken the Managers’ side.

The Supervisors’ interests were in conflict with the Managers’ will. This may explain why the Supervisors could not produce counter-arguments directly, even in the absence of the Managers, because, in their eyes, the Designer was the Managers’ agent and collaborator. For this reason, it may be understandable that the Senior Supervisor used implicit rhetoric to resist the new task division. Face-saving could also be an explanation. Face here denotes ‘the positive social value a person effectively claims for himself’ (Goffman, 1967, p. 5). Alvarez (2001) has shown that participants in ISD meetings appear to save each other’s face.

Protective manoeuvres (Goffman, 1967) were also used during the third episode in our study. One such manoeuvre was to leave ‘unstated [those] facts that might implicitly or explicitly contradict and embarrass the positive claims made by others’ (Goffman, 1967, p. 16). However, the Senior Supervisor’s vested interest finally required him to cause the Designer to lose face because the Designer did not seem to understand the implicit clues he was given.

## CONCLUSIONS

## Summary

The findings of this study are summarized in Table 1 in terms of our E3-D model of discourse.

Our findings show that verbal and visual representations (talk and the wall chart) do not represent only the payment process and the task division but also the re-distribution of authority. The social world is usually visible to the members of the user organization but often hidden for outsiders. In addition to the information system, this social system should also be made visible to the designers. This deeper, possibly hidden, social level complements the surface level of the verbal and visual representations. By understanding all these, the ISD process and its outcomes can be studied from a social point of view.

Our E3-D model of discourse helped us to see what kind of social world was hidden behind the representations and how the ISD participants – consciously or not – (re)produced the power relationships with the help of the representations. The hidden social struggle – in our case about redesign of authority relationships and tasks – became evident through the local relations of power. The social struggle was not detected by the designer. However, with the three levels of our model – representation, representational practice and social practice – the change process was made transparent and the emerging social struggle disclosed.

With the E3-D model, the situation was studied in its social context, going beyond the local representational practice. In this way we saw that the designer did not recognize the representations (not even his own) as a means of influence in the social struggle. The user representatives and the researcher had access to the level of social practice in addition to the visible representational practice, but for the designer this process remained invisible.

By studying the verbal representations that the different stakeholders produced as part of their representational practice, we unveiled how they interpreted the visual representations and the verbal representations produced by the others. Also, we unveiled for what purposes they produced and used verbal representations in the ISD discussions. These representations were a key in opening up the hidden social struggles in the design process. By studying specifically the situational effects, manifested via the representations, we saw that hidden level. On this level of social practice not only the situational determinants present in the discourse were seen to have an influence but also the existing institutional power relationships and organizational positions.

## Lessons learned for the researchers

The E3-D model of discourse enables the study of influence means, tactics and uses of verbal rhetoric as parts of political behaviour during the ISD discussion in the context of social practices. With the model, including the dimensions of representational and social practices, the focus is laid on explaining uses of verbal and visual representations in relation to what interests are preserved. Without seeing uses of these representations in the wider social context, it is impossible to tie what is represented to the interests of their users. This may hinder the real understanding of requirements. The wider political processes need to be made visible because only by knowing hidden processes can also outsiders to the organization become more equal as participants, as Robey & Markus (1984) also imply.

On the other hand, the E3-D model of discourse expands the viewpoint of those researchers who have emphasized the significance of organizational structures as the most important source of power. However, reducing events merely to structures cannot help in explaining why social struggles may be counter-productive if not addressed. Alvarez’s (2001; 2002) studies of political processes of user interviews in the context of the wider social practice takes a more comprehensive view but they lack the understanding of the role of visual representations. The E3-D model used in the current study enables to see that much of the talk is in relation to these visual representations and that the ISD discussion can be explained with the presence of these visual representations.

In terms of studying change, we have acknowledged that change is always contentious. For analysis, we picked only 5 min of the month-long process of task redesign. This was sufficient because we had already established that the discussion was coming to a head here. Our model is fine-tuned to study these ‘struggles’, as Fairclough calls them, in a very focused way. Under this almost microscopic lens, we could see in detail how the different interests entered and influenced the discussion. We saw what an important role the visual representations have in the discussions, as bringing in viewpoints and as a reference point in interaction.

In looking back, we can see how the model is also based on our values and assumptions of what issues matter in the contemplation of a social system. In the spirit of the Scandinavian tradition, we have sought means to make all participants equal in the ISD process. However, in our case, the designer is the one needing better access to information, not the user. Also, we are not engaged in action research in the emancipatory tradition, but only attempt to give the participants better means to participate in a more conscious way in a contentious process.

## Lessons learned for the practising designer

Our findings have consequences from the perspective of the designer. In our study, the outsider position appeared to be especially problematic during the ISD discussions. As an outsider, a practising IS designer is always on unstable ground when working with customers. Misunderstanding is very easy, as an outsider cannot ever fully grasp the totality of issues and interests that guide how the client understands the visual representation being discussed. Therefore, we support efforts for neutrality on the designer’s side. With an aware but neutral stance towards the redistribution of power and new task divisions within the client organization, the designer can avoid becoming a pawn in the associated social struggles.

Even by making visible viewpoints that appear neutral, the designer might potentially be interpreted as taking a value-bound position. This may make talk about diverse perspectives difficult for users or even prohibit it. It is possible that the active designer may even be unaware of the possibility that suggesting something may be interpreted by the users as an attempt to introduce the management’s viewpoint into the ISD discussions.

Based on our findings, we argue that primary skills for designers would be to read verbal and visual representations not only as neutral proposals, but also both as instruments of redistribution of power and as instruments of reproduction of existing relations of power. Reflective designers need to be sensitized to how different participants read and interpret the same representation differently and how these participants mean multiple things with their own verba representations.
