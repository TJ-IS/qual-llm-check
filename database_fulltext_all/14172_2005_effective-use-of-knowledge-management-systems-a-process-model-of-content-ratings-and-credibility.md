## Abstract

Knowledge management systems (KMSs) facilitate the efficient and effective sharing of a firm's intellectual resources. However, sifting through the myriad of content available through KMSs can be challenging, and knowledge workers may be overwhelmed when trying to find the content most relevant for completing a new task. To address this problem, KMS designers often include content rating schemes (i.e., users of the KMS submit ratings to indicate the quality of specific content used) and credibility indicators (indicators describing the validity of the content and/or the ratings) to improve users' search and evaluation of KMS content. This study examines how content ratings and credibility indicators affect KMS users' search and evaluation processes and decision performance (how well and how quickly users selected alternatives offered by the KMS). Four interrelated laboratory experiments provide evidence that ratings have a strong influence on KMS search and evaluation processes, which in turn affects decision performance. Finally, this study demonstrates that certain credibility indicators can moderate the relationship between rating validity and KMS content search and evaluation processes.

Keywords: Knowledge management systems, knowledge usage, decision making, content ratings, credibility indicators

## Introduction

Knowledge management systems (KMSs) are becoming ubiquitous in today's corporations (Davenport and Prusak 1998). KMSs store vast amounts of information, and knowledge workers often have difficulty locating the best content for their task, resulting in suboptimal decision performance (poor selection of content from the KMS) (Davenport and Hansen 1999; Nevo et al. 2003; Teece 1995). The problem is particularly significant in light of the fact that corporate KMS investment is forecast to grow to \$13 billion annually by 2007 (Anonymous 2004). To address this problem, this study focuses on KMS design features that are intended to enable knowledge workers to search and evaluate KMS content more effectively, resulting in improved decision performance.

To ensure effective usage, a KMS must be designed such that knowledge workers can readily find high-quality content without feeling overwhelmed (Alavi and Leidner 2001; Roussinov and Zhao 2003; Thomas et al. 2001). Identifying high-quality content is difficult given its abundance (Davenport and Beck 2001; Farhoomand and Drury 2002) and the variability of its quality (Constant et al. 1994; Hansen and Haas 2001).

Knowledge workers provide feedback (e.g., through ratings) about the quality of the KMS content (Shon and Musen 1999; Standifird 2001; Wathen and Burkell 2002), thereby potentially improving subsequent search and evaluation of KMS content. However, if the content ratings do not reflect the content quality accurately, they may mislead subsequent KMS users (Resnick et al. 2000). Ratings can be misleading because those supplying the ratings may manipulate the rating value intentionally or unintentionally (Cosley et al. 2003; Cramton 2001). Consequently, knowledge workers relying on content ratings may select highly rated, low-quality content to complete their task, degrading decision performance (Cosley et al. 2003; Melnik and Alm 2002).

This study examines how content ratings (and the degree to which these ratings reflect content quality) influence KMS content search and evaluation. Given concerns over rating validity, many KMSs include credibility indicators that assess the validity of the content and/or the ratings (Ba and Pavlou 2002; Resnick et al. 2000). This study investigates the moderating influence of three different credibility indicators on the relationship between ratings validity and content search and evaluation processes. Finally, this study also investigates knowledge worker content search and evaluation processes as these processes have differing effects on decision performance.

## Theoretical Background

This research peers inside the black box of human information processing by using a stimulus-organism-response research approach (Hull 1943) to better understand the relationships between ratings, credibility indicators, content search and evaluation processes, and decision performance. We first examine two broad topics: the role of ratings in search and evaluation of KMS content and the nature of human judgment, specifically anchoring on and adjusting away from content. These topics are then examined in four different, yet interrelated, experiments based on the research model depicted in Figure 1. The hypotheses, research method, and results for each experiment are presented separately, and the final discussion section integrates the findings.

## Content Ratings

To discuss the research model, we begin with a description of content ratings, their validity, credibility, and usage. The process for locating high-quality content is iterative, beginning when knowledge workers enter one or more keywords into a search engine to locate relevant content. This initial search typically results in a lengthy list of content that must be further searched and evaluated based on quality (Ansari et al. 2000; Balabanovic and Shoham 1997; Brajnik et al. 2002). Anyone who has conducted an Internet or database search has likely had the experience of trying to find specific content for a task only to end up with irrelevant or—even worse—incorrect information (Davenport and Beck 2001; Farhoom-and and Drury 2002; Gupta et al. 1997; Shenk 1997). To cope with the disorientation knowledge workers often experience when conducting a search, many make use of strategies to screen information and thereby reduce cognitive load (Dillon 2000; Miller 1960). Using content ratings is one such strategy.

Content ratings help knowledge workers search and evaluate content quality, as ratings of the content by current knowledge workers assist subsequent knowledge workers in their search for and evaluation of high-quality content (Shon and Musen 1999; Standifird 2001; Wathen and Burkell 2002). Several pertinent dimensions of ratings warrant discussion: rating validity, rating level, and credibility. Rating validity describes the degree to which the rating reflects the intrinsic quality of the content. Ratings validity may be low for a variety of reasons (Constant et al. 1994; Hansen and Haas 2001). First, content ratings are inherently subjective and voluntarily provided, resulting in a possible mismatch between the true quality of the content and the rating given (Jadad and Gagliardi 1998; Melnik and Alm 2002). For example, knowledge workers may use high-quality content in an inappropriate context and thus rate the content quality as low. Alternatively, those submitting ratings may manipulate ratings in an attempt to influence others to use content they have contributed, with the hope of enhancing their reputation and standing within the firm or to earn incentives associated with KMS use (Cosley et al. 2003).

A second dimension of ratings—rating level—is measured using a numerical or categorical scale describing the quality of the content where higher ratings presumably reflect higher content quality. A final dimension of ratings—credibility—describes indicators that help a knowledge worker determine if a rating level accurately reflects the content or if the content itself is credible.

We expect knowledge workers to use rating levels to determine whether the associated content is high quality or not and to use rating credibility indicators to determine whether the ratings or content are valid. A knowledge worker may not be able to assess content rating validity if content quality is not immediately and easily discernable. Rating levels and credibility indicators provide knowledge workers with information cues to help determine rating validity and, hence, content quality.

The challenge of accurately identifying quality content is even more difficult when one considers the type of employee typically using the KMS. Experienced senior employees often delegate the time-consuming task of searching a KMS to those more junior (Orlikowski 2000). Junior employees often have enough knowledge to understand the task and the context, but may be uncertain about whether the content is high quality or not (Brajnik et al. 2002; Nonaka and Takeuchi 1996).

## Anchoring and Adjustment

Next, we describe the KMS content search and evaluation processes represented in the research model. KMSs store content that can be used when completing a task. Knowledge workers must have sufficient contextual understanding of the content to reuse the KMS content effectively. Human information-processing research demonstrates that human judgment is often biased, and biased judgment will influence the manner in which KMS content is searched and evaluated (Lichtenstein and Slovic 1971; Slovic and Lichtenstein 1971; Tversky and Kahneman 1974). One such bias involves anchoring and adjustment, whereby knowledge workers anchor their judgments on specific content and insufficiently adjust away from this content as new content is evaluated (Hogarth and Einhorn 1992; Lichtenstein and Slovic 1971; Tversky and Kahneman 1974).

Anchoring is observed in a variety of contexts (Butler 1986; Northcraft and Neale 1987; Yadov 1994), occurs with both novices and experts (Arnold et al. 2000; Northcraft and Neale 1987), and occurs even when making very important decisions (Remus and Kotterman 1995). People unconsciously select anchors in a variety of ways. They may anchor on the first piece of content evaluated or they may look only at content that confirms (or disconfirms) an a priori hypothesis (Bamber et al. 1997; Hogarth and Einhorn 1992). People are more likely to anchor on inappropriate content if the task is very complex or there is ambiguity regarding the best answer (Hogarth and Einhorn 1992). Salient or handy anchors often influence people even when the content associated with these anchors is extremely implausible (Kataoka et al. 1997; Mussweiler and Strack 2001).

## Experiment 1

Our first experiment investigates the relationship between rating validity, KMS content search and evaluation processes, and decision performance.

## Ratings Validity Influencing Content Search and Evaluation Process

With respect to KMSs, content ratings may lead knowledge workers to anchor upon certain content when searching for and evaluating KMS content (Kataoka et al. 1997; Mussweiler and Strack 2001). Anchoring can occur regardless of the rating's validity; prior evidence demonstrates that knowledge workers assume that the initial rating and content are valid when evaluating both honest and deceptive messages (Zuckerman et al. 1987; Zuckerman et al. 1984). When the rating that forms the initial anchor is high in validity, it is easier to evaluate lower-quality content. However, if the initial rating is low in validity, knowledge workers mislabel high-rated KMS content as being high quality, making it more difficult to accurately assess the quality of other content (Fan et al. 1995). Thus, decision quality is higher with valid ratings because these ratings lead to knowledge workers anchoring on high-quality content.

When ratings are high in validity, knowledge workers are likely to select highly rated and therefore high-quality content. After evaluating the selected content, its high-quality nature will reinforce the selection of additional highly rated content. Alternatively, when ratings are low in validity, knowledge workers are likely to follow one of two content search and evaluation processes. They may strongly anchor on content associated with the low-validity ratings, evaluate the KMS content in a cursory manner, and thus not recognize the content's poor quality. In this case, they will likely continue to select highly rated but low-quality content. Alternatively, they may use the ratings to guide initial content selection but not anchor on the content as strongly. In this scenario, they will likely evaluate the quality of the content more rigorously, which will increase the likelihood that they will recognize a disconnect between the rating and the actual quality of the content. These knowledge workers are more likely to adjust their content search and evaluation processes by selecting content with lower ratings, as they recognize that the ratings do not accurately reflect content quality. In summary, the three most plausible content search and evaluation processes are (1) anchoring on content associated with ratings that are high in validity and making no adjustment; (2) anchoring on content associated with ratings that are low in validity and making no adjustment; and (3) anchoring on content associated with ratings that are low in validity but adjusting away. Thus, we state the following hypothesis:

H1: Knowledge workers will implement different content search and evaluation processes based on the validity of the content ratings.

## Content Search and Evaluation Process Influencing Decision Performance

Based on the scenario described in the prior section, knowledge workers who anchor on and continue to use highly rated, high-quality content are likely to make higher-quality decisions than those who anchor on and continue to use highly rated, low-quality content. What about those knowledge workers who start with highly rated, low-quality content and adjust? As suggested above, we expect that those initially anchoring on content with low-validity ratings and adjusting away from this content will make higher-quality decisions than those who do not adjust. As knowledge workers recognize the conflict between the rating value and the content quality, they will rely less on their initial anchor. Further search and evaluation of higher-quality content will reinforce their decision to broaden their search of the content. As for those knowledge workers who insufficiently adjust away from a misleading anchor, their decision quality is likely to be poorer. Therefore, we state the following hypotheses:

H2a: Anchoring on low-quality content but adjusting away from that anchor results in higher decision quality than not adjusting away from the anchor.

H2b: Anchoring on high-quality content and not adjusting away from that anchor results in higher decision quality than anchoring on low-quality content and adjusting away.

We also expect those anchoring on low-quality content and adjusting away from the anchor to take significantly more time than those using a content search and evaluation process where no adjustment occurs (Payne et al. 1993). Adjusting requires more time because knowledge workers must process more KMS content and invest more time evaluating this content as opposed to relying on the anchor. Knowledge workers may also invest time in devising a new strategy to try and find higher-quality content as they come to realize that their current content search and evaluation process is deficient. We state the following hypothesis regarding decision time:

H2c: Decision time will be longer when using a content search and evaluation process that initially anchors on low-quality content and adjusts than one that initially anchors on low-quality content and does not adjust or one that initially anchors on high-quality content and does not adjust.

## Method

The first experiment examines the influence of ratings validity on KMS content search and evaluation processes and decision performance. Based on the theory development, we expected three content search and evaluation processes to emerge from the data (anchor on high-quality content with no adjustment; anchor on low-quality content with no adjustment; anchor on low-quality content with adjustment), and we used analytical techniques to uncover the content search and evaluation process patterns.

Subjects and Task. Fifty-one junior and senior undergraduates enrolled in a business information systems course at a large Midwestern university participated in this study. Subjects received course credit (1.5 percent) for their participation and were awarded incentive pay based on decision quality and time.

Subjects completed a pretest assessing demographics and prior experience with KMS and the content used in the KMS before attending the experimental session held in a campus computer lab. Subjects were randomly assigned to an experimental manipulation, and each session began with a 10-minute tutorial on the task and the KMS. The experimental task took place in the setting of a professional services firm, where subjects completed a typical consulting task (Falconer 1999; Orlikowski 2000). Subjects were asked by their "manager" to determine what steps to include in a work plan for a data-modeling project by reviewing existing work plans (focused on data-modeling projects) in the KMS. The task was designed to have an optimal answer, and as such, better and worse solutions could be assessed objectively.

The data-modeling work plans in the KMS were identical in appearance to work plans provided by practicing consultants, upon which they were based. In the experimental scenario, they represented work plans from work previously performed by colleagues in the professional services firm. All work plans listed

\- data-modeling project steps based on the steps identified in an undergraduate knowledge systems textbook (Whitten et al. 2000)

\- necessary consultant rank for each project step, with the appropriate rank determined based on feedback from practicing consultants

Work plans varied in quality. The highest-quality work plans included (1) senior consultants assigned to all important tasks, (2) consultant level(s) for all project steps, and (3) informative, non-vague project steps. (See Appendix A for more details on the highest-quality work plans.) Work plans with objectively lower quality were created by (1) deleting senior consultant assignments for many tasks, (2) not assigning consultant levels to a project step, and (3) describing project steps in uninformative, vague terms (see Murch 2001; Rosenau 1998).

Subjects were told to reuse existing consulting work plans to construct a new work plan for a client. To support this effort, subjects were told that an initial search (using a keyword of “data modeling”) of the KMS repository had already occurred and the results of this search resulted in 14 data-modeling project work plans listed on the subjects’ computer screen. These work plans varied in quality such that one plan met all three quality criteria, six plans met two of the quality criteria, six plans met one of the quality criteria, and one plan did not meet any of the quality criteria. The search results screen was identical for all subjects and work plans were listed sequentially with a workplan rating level linked to each work plan. A subject clicked on the link associated with each work plan to view the detailed information. The list of work plans was identical across all treatments and experiments with one exception. Credibility indicators were provided in experiments 2, 3, and 4 (defined in the subsequent section) and were absent in experiment 1.

The experimental task was designed to be a typical task that first-year consultants might perform, and thus one that the subjects might perform as entry-level employees. Subjects had prior, but limited, experience with the task domain. Pilot tests of work plans demonstrated that the subject pool had sufficient understanding of the task and were able to distinguish between higher- and lower-quality work plans based on the described criteria.

The experimental materials were programmed in HTML, ASP, and MS Office products and placed on a host computer. The subjects participated in the study via the Internet while attending the experimental session.

Independent Variable. Each work plan was assessed a rating and rating validity was operationalized as either high (if the rating accurately described the content quality) or low (if it did not). Subjects were not explicitly told that ratings would vary in validity. The content rating scheme was designed as follows: 5 = highly valuable, 4 = somewhat valuable, 2 = somewhat worthless, and 1 = worthless (a list of variables and their operationalizations is found in Table 1). The neutral rating, 3, was excluded to strengthen the rating validity manipulation.

Process Variable—Content Search and Evaluation Processes. Clickstream data was captured for each subject's experimental session. This data provided insight into which work plans were opened and what content (steps) from each work plan was included in final solutions. The objective quality of each work plan and the order in which the subject searched and evaluated the work plans was coded to evaluate a subject's content search and evaluation process. The quality of the 14 work plans was measured based on a scale of 1 (met no quality criteria) to 14 (met all quality criteria).

Dependent Variables. Two dependent variables, decision quality and decision time, were examined. Decision quality was determined by the number of line items in the subject's work plan that matched the 19 line items in the highest-quality work plan. The maximum decision quality score was 19; the minimum was 0. Decision time was the duration (in minutes) of time spent on the task.

Control Variables. While individual differences between subjects should be addressed by random assignment of subjects to treatment conditions, we felt that some individual differences were important to control. One's expertise in content processing can influence decision making, and subjects were asked how much experience they had in work plan design and working with knowledge systems (Newell and Simon 1972). Differences in subjects' ability to understand work plan and broader information systems concepts is a second important control variable and was operationalized as the subjects' final grade in the business information systems course.

## Results

The data were analyzed using cluster analysis, chi-square, and MANOVA techniques. The subject pool was homogenous: chi-square tests indicated no significant differences for year in school, age, gender, or experience across treatments and none of the control variables were significant. ANOVA tests confirmed the significance of the rating validity manipulation check $^{2}$ (F = 32.65, p < .001). Subjects in high and low rating validity treatments perceived differences in rating validity as anticipated.

Analyzing the Process Data. Cluster analysis was implemented to categorize each subject's content search and evaluation process. The order and the objective quality (irrespective of rating) of each work plan clicked on by each subject was entered into SPSS. The number of work plans opened varied significantly with a minimum of 1 and a maximum of 79 (each time a subject opened any of the 14 work plans, the specific work plan number and when it was opened was captured). Hierarchical cluster analysis was run in SPSS, a procedure that clusters homogeneous groups of cases together based on selected characteristics (in our study, work plan clickstream data). The hierarchical cluster analysis method reveals natural groupings within a data set that might not otherwise be apparent. Hierarchical cluster analysis begins by separating each subject's clickstream data into its own cluster and then joining subjects' data together into larger clusters when similarities are identified. While the theoretical lens suggested three unique clusters, we did not preselect the number of clusters expected when running the cluster analysis. When clusters are significantly different, large coefficients (squared Euclidean distance) are generated. The coefficients between the three clusters we ended up with were large, indicating three homogeneous clusters.

While there was some variation by subject in the cluster centers, the analysis identified each cluster based on its overall pattern. Cluster 1 describes subjects who initially select low-quality work plans and do not adjust their strategy during the content search and evaluation process (low quality/no adjust). Cluster 2 describes subjects who start by examining low-quality work plans but who adjust their content search and evaluation processes to find higher-quality work plans (low quality/adjust). Finally, Cluster 3 describes subjects who start by examining high-quality work plans and do not adjust their content search and evaluation process (high quality/no adjust).

To assess the validity of these interpretations, we examined the objective content quality mean (ranging from 1, low to 14, high) and standard deviation of each cluster center: Cluster 1, mean = 5.15, s.d. = 1.09; Cluster 2, mean = 7.95, s.d. = 2.50; and Cluster 3, mean = 10.35, s.d. = .93. We ran t-tests to identify significant differences between the three clusters. Cluster 1 was significantly different from Cluster 2 (t (1, 49) = -4.878, p < .001), Cluster 1 was significantly different from Cluster 3 (t (1, 49) = -18.759, p < .001), and Cluster 2 was significantly different from Cluster 3 (t (1, 49) = -3.644, p = .002). Thus, all of the clusters were significantly different from one another.

Hypothesis Testing. Hypothesis 1 suggested that subjects would conduct their content searches differently depending on the validity of the content ratings they were given. Specifically, we expected to see differences in anchoring and whether or not subjects adjusted away from an initial anchor. A chi-square test was run to evaluate the frequency with which subjects in the high and low rating validity manipulation appeared in each of the three clusters. The frequency counts for chi-square analysis are provided in Table 2 and demonstrate a strong relationship between the validity of ratings and whether the subject adjusted away from an initial anchor (chi-square (2, 370) = 230.35, p < .001). Thus, hypothesis 1 was supported.

Hypotheses 2a, b, and c stated that a subject's content search and evaluation process would influence decision quality and time. There was an a priori belief that subjects might trade off accuracy for time, and our results indicate a significant correlation between decision quality and time spent (p = .024). We used MANOVA to evaluate these hypotheses, and found that content search and evaluation processes significantly influence decision quality and time simultaneously (F (1, 51) = 10.56, p < .001). The univariate statistics indicate that decision quality was significant (F (1, 51) = 36.83, p < .001), but decision time was not (F (1, 51) = .26, p = .771). Consistent with our expectations, subjects in Cluster 1 demonstrated the poorest decision quality (mean = 4.56, s.d. = 1.97) while those in Cluster 3 demonstrated the highest decision quality (mean = 14.73, s.d. = 4.33). Specific to hypotheses 2a and 2b, the decision quality results for Cluster 2 fell between those for the other two clusters (mean = 8.08, s.d. = 4.15). Post hoc Scheffe tests indicate that the differences between clusters are all significant: Cluster 1 compared with Cluster 2 (p = .014), Cluster 3 compared with Cluster 2 (p < .001), and Cluster 1 compared with Cluster 3 (p < .001). Thus, we find support for hypotheses 2a and 2b.

Decision time was equivalent across the three clusters: Cluster 3 (mean = 28.45 minutes, s.d. = 12.97), Cluster 2 (mean = 29.69 minutes, s.d. = 9.81), and Cluster 1 (mean = 31.25 minutes, s.d. = 11.46). Thus, hypothesis 2c was not supported.

## Experiments 2, 3, and 4

The remaining experiments examined the moderating effect of credibility indicators as shown in our research model. Many KMSs provide knowledge workers with credibility indicators—additional information beyond content ratings to guide their content search and evaluation processes. Credibility indicators assess the validity of the content ratings or the quality of the content itself. From an anchoring and adjustment perspective, specific types of indicators may encourage knowledge workers in their content search and evaluation processes, specifically to continue adjusting or to minimize adjusting from an initial anchor (Montgomery and Svenson 1983; Payne et al. 1993). Unexpected inaccuracies in ratings may raise doubts about the ratings' validity (Sedlmeier and Gigerenzer 2000; Van Overwalle and Van Rooy 2001; Weiner 1985), and knowledge workers may turn to credibility indicators to determine rating validity and to verify content quality during the content search and evaluation process (Melnik and Alm 2002).

We expect credibility indicators to influence the manner in which rating validity influences the content search and evaluation process. Strong (high) credibility indicators give knowledge workers a reason to believe that ratings are valid and encourage them to rely on the ratings and content retrieved in KMS searches—that is, they are encouraged to maintain their existing anchor. Conversely, weak (low) credibility indicators may encourage knowledge workers to disregard the ratings and content and to adjust their content search and evaluation processes, resulting in increased search and evaluation. Thus, credibility indicators may prompt knowledge workers to make more significant adjustments by providing insights about the ratings and the content quality.

We examine two types of credibility indicators in this study: those that indicate whether ratings are credible (by supplying information on the number of raters and rater expertise) and those indicating whether the system content is credible (collaborative filtering). Number of raters and rater expertise have been examined in prior literature as credibility indicators that influence content use (Higgins 1999; Nelson et al. 2001; Sternthal et al. 1978) and corporate KMSs frequently include these indicators in their design. Collaborative filtering (recommendations of additional content based on content already selected) is frequently used in many knowledge-based e-commerce sites, and several companies are considering integrating collaborative-filtering capabilities into their KMSs (Kohrs and Merialdo 2001).

## Experiment 2: Number of Raters

There is mixed evidence regarding the manner in which people evaluate sample size (in our study, sample size = number of raters) when making a decision. Generally, larger sample size suggests more input to a rating level and greater rating credibility (Tversky and Kahneman 1971). Some studies demonstrate that people often ignore sample size, paying too much attention to the positive or negative nature of the content (Griffin and Tversky 1992; Tversky and Kahneman 1971). Alternatively, evaluation of sample size may be conditional on the setting (Keren and Lewis 2000; Sedlmeier 1998; Sedlmeier and Gigerenzer 2000; Van Overwalle and Van Rooy 2001), and some event may trigger the use of sample size information (Gigerenzer and Todd 1999). In the KMS setting, knowledge worker suspicion that the ratings may not be valid might provide such a trigger.

Knowledge workers who adjust their content search and evaluation processes are likely to do so in response to low-validity ratings. Suspicions about the ratings trigger the need to understand the relationship between the ratings and content quality more fully. Strong credibility indicators (e.g., a large number of raters) would reinforce the validity of the rating and discourage adjustment, whereas weak credibility indicators (e.g., a small number of raters) would provide additional reasons to discount the rating and would encourage adjustment. Thus, we state the following hypothesis:

H3a: Given low-validity ratings, knowledge workers will adjust away from an initial anchor on low-quality content more when the number of raters is low than when the number of raters is high.

Method. The second experiment examined the moderating influence of the number of raters on the relationship between rating validity and content search and evaluation processes. A different group of subjects were drawn from the same population as for Experiment 1, and 108 subjects participated. The experimental task, definition of search and evaluation processes, manipulation of ratings validity, and control variables were identical to those for Experiment 1.

The number of raters was operationalized as the number of raters rating each KMS work plan. Each work plan in the high condition was randomly assigned a number-of-raters value between 93 and 97, while each work plan in the low condition was randomly assigned a number-of-raters value between 3 and 7. The value varied slightly to maintain the appearance of external validity: If every work plan in the high condition had been assigned the same high number-of-raters value and every work plan in the low condition had been assigned the same low number-of-raters value, it would likely look redundant and fixed. To induce a consistent interpretation of the high and low values for number of raters, the following statements were included in the KMS to calibrate the subjects in the high number-of-raters condition: "Throughout the system, the number of raters ranges from 3 to 97 depending on the item. The number of raters in your search results is HIGH compared to the average of 50." Similarly, subjects in the low number-of-raters condition found the following statement: "Throughout the system, the number of raters ranges from 3 to 97 depending on the item. The number of raters in your search results is LOW compared to the average of 50."

Results. As with Experiment 1, the subject pool was homogeneous on key individual differences and the control variables were not significant. ANOVA tests confirmed the significance of the number-of-raters manipulation check $^{3}$ (F = 26.43, p < .001). Subjects in the high and low number-of-raters treatments perceived differences in rating validity as anticipated.

Log linear techniques were used to assess the moderating role of each of the credibility indicators. In general, log linear techniques are used to examine the interrelationship among categorical-level variables (Bordens and Abbott 1991; Miller et al. 2002; Tabachnick and Fidell 2001). Log linear models allow us to evaluate the effects of categorical independent variables and their interactions on a categorical dependent variable (as opposed to using a chi-square analysis that would only tell us if there were significant differences in the frequency of occurrences among variables). In this analysis, the independent variables are ratings validity, each credibility indicator (separately), and the content search and evaluation cluster membership and the dependent variable is the frequency counts found in the content search and evaluation cluster membership.

Log linear analysis involves two steps: model selection and model estimation. In model selection, the most parsimonious, yet adequate model is sought. The model selection process was accomplished using a sequential step-down procedure that identifies the model that most parsimoniously explains the data (Miller et al. 2002). This process begins with the saturated model (e.g., the model that includes all possible interaction terms and, thus, perfectly accounts for data variation) and deletes the largest interaction term (e.g., in this study the three-way term of ratings validity × credibility indicator × cluster membership), testing the fit of the reduced model, and so on, until terms can no longer be deleted from the model without resulting in a poor fit to the data (Tabachnick and Fidell 2001). The fit of each step-down model is compared to the saturated model to assess model adequacy and, thus, non-significant goodness-of-fit tests are used because they indicate that the step-down model and data do not differ. The final model is the one SPSS selects as the most parsimonious yet adequate accounting for the data variation. Following model selection, the parameters of the model are estimated. These estimates can then be examined as to their significance, using standard z statistics to yield information on which ones vary significantly from chance.

Hypothesis 3a suggested that the number of raters would moderate the relationship between rating validity and content search and evaluation processes. The log linear analysis indicated that the best model has one 2-way association (rating validity × content search and evaluation process cluster) with the likelihood ratio = 6.252, p = .396. This represents a good fit between the model and observed cluster frequencies. The rating validity × content search and evaluation cluster interaction was significant with chi-square = 84.423, p < .001. Thus, the results indicate the importance of rating validity in predicting the content search and evaluation approach; however, the number of raters does not moderate this relationship. Thus, hypothesis 3a was not supported.

## Experiment 3: Rater Expertise

In addition to using the number of raters as a credibility indicator, many KMSs provide insight into the expertise of the raters so that knowledge workers can assess the raters' authority, competence, and reliability (Flanagin and Metzger 2000; Fritch and Cromwell 2001; Stewart and Strasser 1993; Strasser et al. 1995). For example, a rater's educational credentials, position in the company hierarchy, or business subunit might provide insight into the rater's expertise (Davenport and Prusak 1998; Thompson et al. 1999).

Rater expertise lends credibility to ratings by virtue of the rater's presumed competence (Ilgen et al. 1979). Someone with expertise in the domain of interest has greater authority and credibility, and therefore knowledge workers are more likely to anchor on content with ratings coming from experts (Birnbaum et al. 1976; Hovland and Weiss

1951; Olson and Cal 1984; Slater and Rouner 1992). Credible sources are particularly influential when knowledge workers have limited expertise relative to the decision task (Beach et al. 1978; Slater and Rouner 1992; Walster et al. 1966).

As with the hypothesis involving the number of raters, here too we hypothesize that low-validity ratings will prompt knowledge workers to use rater expertise information to determine whether or not to rely on the ratings. Thus, we state the following hypothesis:

H3b: Given low-validity ratings, knowledge workers will adjust away from an initial anchor on low-quality content more when rater expertise is low than when rater expertise is high.

Method. The third experiment examined the moderating influence of rater expertise on the relationship between rating validity and content search and evaluation processes. A different group of subjects were drawn from the same population as for Experiment 1, and 108 subjects participated. The experimental task, definition of content search and evaluation processes, manipulation of ratings validity, and control variables were identical to those for Experiment 1.

Rater expertise was operationalized as the percentage of raters who were experts. Each work plan in the high condition was randomly assigned a rater expertise value between 88 and 92 while each work plan in the low condition was randomly assigned a rater expertise value between 4 and 8. As in Experiment 2, the values associated with low and high rater expertise were allowed to vary along a very narrow range to increase external validity. To induce consistent interpretation of the high and low values of rater expertise, the KMS included the following statement in the high rater-expertise condition: "Throughout the system, the percentage of raters who are experts ranges from 4 to 92 depending on the item. The percentage of raters who are experts for your search results is HIGH compared to the average of 48." The statement in the low rater-expertise condition read: "Throughout the system, the percentage of raters who are experts ranges from 4 to 92 depending on the item. The percentage of raters who are experts for your search results is LOW compared to the average of 48."

Results. As with Experiment 1, the subject pool was homogeneous on key individual differences and the control variables were not significant. ANOVA tests confirmed the significance of the rater expertise manipulation check $^{4}$ (F = 30.44, p < .001). Subjects in the high and low rater-expertise treatments perceived differences in rating validity as anticipated.

Hypothesis 3b suggested that rater expertise would moderate the relationship between rating validity and content search and evaluation processes. The log linear analysis indicated that the best model has one 2-way association (rating validity × content search and evaluation process cluster) with the likelihood ratio = 3.653, p = .724. The rating validity x content search and evaluation cluster interaction was significant with chi-square = 57.743, p < .001. Thus, the results indicate the importance of rating validity in predicting the content search and evaluation approach; however, the number of raters does not moderate this relationship. Thus, hypothesis 3b was not supported.

## Experiment 4: Collaborative Filtering

While the number of raters and rater expertise help knowledge workers evaluate content ratings, collaborative filtering helps them evaluate the content directly by recommending content that is similar to the content that they have already found and by identifying content that has been used by others in similar contexts (Ansari et al. 2000; Balabanovic and Shoham 1997; Hill et al. 1995).

Collaborative-filtering algorithms vary in sophistication; highly sophisticated collaborative filtering will consistently refer knowledge workers anchoring on high-quality content to content similar to what they have already retrieved and evaluated. Alternatively, collaborative filtering that is less sophisticated will be less refined and may refer knowledge workers who have accessed high-quality content to similar quality content less consistently or possibly not at all. These less-apt recommendations may lead to the search and evaluation of lower-quality content. Thus, knowledge workers who have found high-quality KMS content but who have less sophisticated collaborative filtering may receive recommendations for additional content that is lower in quality.

Knowledge workers using highly sophisticated collaborative filtering are likely to find the recommendations useful, in part because this content was used by others addressing similar decision problems. Selecting content based on what other similarly situated knowledge workers have successfully chosen reduces the uncertainty about how to search and evaluate KMS content. Following the implicit decision processes of other knowledge workers (Hill et al. 1995) may reduce reliance on ratings and encourage greater emphasis on the content, facilitating continued adjustment away from low-quality content associated with low-validity ratings. Thus, once knowledge workers begin adjusting away from high-rated, low-quality content, highly sophisticated collaborative filtering should sustain this adjustment leading to the selection of higher-quality content. Meanwhile, knowledge workers using a low-sophistication filter are likely to assess the recommendations as less valuable and thus may discard them to rely on ratings instead. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H3c: Given low-validity ratings, knowledge workers will adjust away from an initial anchor on low-quality content more when collaborative-filtering sophistication is low than when collaborative-filtering sophistication is high.

Method. The fourth experiment examined the moderating influence of collaborative-filtering sophistication on the relationship between rating validity and content search and evaluation processes. A different group of subjects were drawn from the same population as Experiment 1, and 103 subjects participated. The experimental task, definition of content search and evaluation processes, manipulation of ratings validity, and control variables were identical to those for Experiment 1.

The collaborative-filtering credibility indicator was operationalized by providing subjects referrals to other items under the heading “recommended also.” Subjects in the high filter-sophistication condition were given additional work plan recommendations that were of equivalent quality to the plan they had retrieved (regardless of the content rating). Subjects in the low filter-sophistication condition were given work plan recommendations of opposite quality. To induce consistent interpretation of the high- and low-sophistication collaboration filter conditions, the KMS included the following statement in the high-sophistication condition: “Recommendations from the system can exactly or not exactly match the quality of the original item. Recommendations in your search results are known to EXACTLY match the original item in quality.” In the low-sophistication condition, the statement read: “Recommendations from the system can exactly or not exactly match the quality of the original item. Recommendations in your search results are known to NOT EXACTLY match the quality of the original item.”

Results. As with Experiment 1, the subject pool was homogeneous on key individual differences and the control variables were not significant. ANOVA tests confirmed the (marginal) significance of the credibility indicator manipulation check $^{5}$ (F = 3.56, p = .06). Subjects in the high and low filter-sophistication treatments perceived differences in rating validity as anticipated. While the manipulation check for collaborative filtering was only approaching significance, the relatively small sample size (only large effects could be detected based on a power analysis) and significant findings associated with the collaborative-filtering manipulation give us confidence that the manipulation was sufficient.

Hypothesis 3c suggested that collaborative-filtering sophistication would moderate the relationship between rating validity and content search and evaluation processes. The log linear analysis indicated that the best model has three 2-way associations: rating validity x content search and evaluation process cluster; collaborative filtering × content search and evaluation process cluster; and rating validity × collaborative filtering with a likelihood ratio = .416, p = .812. The rating validity x content search and evaluation cluster interaction was significant with chi-square = 102.613, p < .001. The collaborative filtering x content search and evaluation cluster interaction was significant with chi-square = 6.507, p = .039. Finally, the rating validity x collaborative filtering interaction was significant with chi-square = 4.079, p = .043. Thus, the results indicate the importance of rating validity and collaborative filtering in predicting the content search and evaluation approach. In addition, the interaction between rating validity and collaborative filtering also influences the content search and evaluation approach, demonstrating the moderating role of collaborative filtering. Thus, hypotheses 3c was supported.

## Discussion

Our results demonstrate that the degree to which ratings reflect the quality of KMS content directly influences content search and evaluation processes, which in turn impacts decision performance. More specifically, anchoring on high-quality content most frequently occurs with high-validity ratings, while anchoring on low quality content most frequently occurs with low-validity ratings with some knowledge workers adjusting away from that anchor. Decision quality was highest for those anchoring on high-quality content and lowest for those who anchored on low-quality content and did not adjust away from their anchor. There were no differences in decision times between these groups.

An important contribution of this study was its examination of how credibility indicators moderate the relationship between rating validity and content search and evaluation processes. Credibility indicators may give knowledge workers cues from which to adjust their search and evaluation processes. Interestingly, the two credibility indicators providing cues about the ratings (number of raters and rater expertise) did not influence content search and evaluation processes, while the credibility indicator providing cues about content quality (collaborative filtering) did. Specifically, more sophisticated collaborative filtering placed the emphasis on finding additional high-quality content, reinforcing the benefits for knowledge workers to adjust their search and evaluation processes (Im and Hars 2001).

This study answers calls for more in-depth examinations of decision making by examining the content search and evaluation process used and the moderating effect of credibility indicators on this process (Baron and Kenny 1986; Gefen and Straub 2000; Lee et al. 1997; Lin and Chan 2003). The findings of this study show how important it is to ensure that ratings are high in validity, since adjustment from an initial anchor on content is difficult. This study illustrates that two processes can occur when ratings are low in validity: One can either adjust away from an initial anchor on low-quality content, or one can fail to adjust. Knowledge workers who do not adjust away from their initial anchor appear to have anchored so strongly on the low-quality content that was highly rated that they chose not to search and evaluate content with lower ratings, even though this content would improve their decision quality. This tendency not to adjust is consistent with prior research that knowledge workers have difficulty accurately assessing the quality of content when their initial anchor is unreliable (Fan et al. 1995).

Knowledge workers who did adjust away from low-quality content presumably recognized that the content they were retrieving was somehow lacking in quality even though the ratings indicated otherwise. Consistent with the literature on anchoring and adjustment, those adjusting away from low quality content associated with invalid ratings made better decisions than those relying on invalid ratings; however, their decisions did not reach the quality of those relying on valid ratings and high-quality content.

Why do some knowledge workers adjust their content search and evaluation processes and others not? We examined the individual-differences characteristics measured as part of this study (gender, domain experience, computer experience, and broad information systems experience) to see if any of them would provide insight into what might adjust one's search processes. None of the characteristics was significantly more pronounced among either knowledge workers who adjusted or among those who did not. Additional research should investigate individual factors such as cognitive flexibility and field dependent or independent characteristics that ascertain the degree to which knowledge workers tend to rely on ratings or analyze content more fully.

This study also demonstrates that content (not rating) credibility indicators influence adjustment away from an initial anchor. The data indicate that those knowledge workers who adjusted away from their anchor on low-quality content associated with low-validity ratings adjusted even more when using more sophisticated collaborative filtering, resulting in higher-quality decisions.

The collaborative-filtering credibility indicator places an emphasis on “like content” and de-emphasizes ratings. Thus, collaborative filtering may be particularly helpful for knowledge workers who have found content that is valuable to them but who lack the domain expertise to describe or fully understand what it is about the content that makes it useful for their context. There is some belief that collaborative filtering guides knowledge workers to content that has been used by others in similar contexts or when making similar decisions and thus is particularly helpful in the absence of decisive first-hand knowledge (Hill et al. 1995). Knowledge workers who are aided by collaborative filtering may evaluate the content retrieved more carefully, perhaps because of the commonality in the decision context.

While the collaborative-filtering results provide support for the contention that credibility indicators are important and should, therefore, be incorporated into the design of KMSs, in our study neither of the rating credibility indicators provided strong enough cues to get knowledge workers to adjust away from an initial bad anchor. One explanation for these nonsignificant findings is that knowledge workers want to minimize their effort (Payne 1982; Todd and Benbasat 1992), and therefore they focus on what is needed to get the task done and ignore cues they perceive as extraneous, such as rating credibility indicators, in order to focus on the search and evaluation of KMS content. This behavior is consistent with the idea, described in other studies, that effort is weighted more heavily than accuracy because feedback on effort expenditure is more immediate, while feedback on accuracy is delayed and ambiguous (Einhorn and Hogarth 1981; Kleinmuntz and Schkade 1993).

Another explanation for these nonsignificant findings comes from research on the elaboration likelihood model (ELM), which suggests that central route processing may be used to examine KMS content and peripheral route processing may be used to assess ratings (Petty and Cacioppo 1986; Zhang and Watts 2003). In this model, content credibility indicators reinforce central processing, while rating credibility indicators only reinforce peripheral processing. Invalid ratings processed peripherally are not processed rigorously and thus may not trigger the need to examine the content more thoroughly. Future research should investigate possible differences in central and peripheral processing which might more fully explain why content credibility indicators might be more useful than rating credibility indicators. Further, research could investigate what changes could be made to rating credibility indicators to encourage knowledge workers to process these cues using central processing.

An alternative explanation for the nonsignificant findings involves error detection issues. Data quality indicators are most critical for those knowledge workers who have limited task domain knowledge (Fisher et al. 2003), yet their very lack of task domain knowledge means that such workers are less likely to become suspicious of low-validity ratings. Our results demonstrate the importance of providing accurate and meaningful content quality indicators, as knowledge workers may not have sufficiently developed mental models to detect errors or lack of quality consistently.

Failure to detect errors or to recognize lack of quality will lead to suboptimal or incorrect decisions (Biros et al. 2002; DePaulo and DePaulo 1989; Feeley and deTurck 1995; Maier and Thurber 1968; Norman 1983). While companies have long been concerned about “dirty data” in databases, our study demonstrates that they should be equally concerned about the content housed in KMSs (Davenport and Hansen 1999). Companies should develop robust processes both to evaluate the quality of content in a KMS and to ensure that the ratings of that content are accurate. One possibility is to allow only experts to rate KMS content, or experts could verify submitted ratings before the ratings are published on the system.

The failure of people to detect errors could be an issue of over-confidence. Similarly, knowledge workers may believe they know how to use rating schemes but are over-confident regarding the validity of the ratings. Knowledge workers often use decision aids ineffectively: They are overconfident when using redundant information, improperly believe some decision aids are better than others, and use aids that give incorrect information regardless of their own knowledge level (Coffin 1941; Davis and Kottemann 1994; Davis et al. 1994). Thus, knowledge workers may be overconfident in their use of ratings and find little additional value in using the rating credibility indicator information to more accurately infer the validity of the ratings. Alternatively, the content credibility indicator returns content used by others in a similar context, reducing the focus on the rating (and the possible over-confidence in interpreting the rating) and increasing the focus on the actual content, spurring greater adjustment. Future research should examine the overconfidence in information use and the related nuances in search process adoption.

A final explanation for the nonsignificant findings might relate to a broader concern of accountability. It is plausible that knowledge workers might select highly rated content, irrespective of the validity of the content, to reduce their personal accountability for any decision made using information from the KMS. Just as in the past, IT managers did not get fired for buying IBM equipment, knowledge workers could defend the use of poorer quality content as appropriate if the KMS indicated the content was rated as high quality. While we believe this explanation is unlikely in the context of this laboratory experiment, future research should investigate whether or not accountability plays a role in how knowledge workers select content.

Finally, the findings of this study could direct KMS designers to incorporate more useful metrics into search result feedback and ratings schemes. KMS designers should use the limited space on search results screens to display only those cues that are most useful to knowledge workers in helping overcome inaccuracies in rating schemes. Our study examined only a few of the many characteristics that could be built into system features, and the findings suggest that cues related to content credibility may be more important to disclose that cues related to rating credibility. However, more research is needed to determine how the strength and scale of content ratings affect how knowledge workers use them. Additional research could also examine the usefulness of text explanations of credibility indicators and the role of rating consistency.

## Limitations

The findings from any study must be assessed in light of the study's limitations. For this study, the increased control afforded by a laboratory experiment must be traded off against the inherent limitations of the approach, primarily that of generalizability. The use of student subjects, the nature of the tasks, and the operationalization of the credibility indicators and rating validity all limit the generalizability of our results.

Student subjects typically differ from business professionals in two ways: They generally have less experience with the problem domain and less motivation to perform the task successfully (Beltramini 1983; Morgan 1979). With respect to experience, knowledge workers searching a KMS must have some contextual and task knowledge to know what to look for, yet not so much experience that they already know the answer. Our subjects had experience using Web-based applications to complete information search tasks and had conceptual and hands-on experience in the task domain used in the study. Thus, they understood the general context, but had limited exposure to the specific task. It is not clear where the boundary condition exists between too little and too much experience and care should be taken when interpreting the results. Subjects were offered course credit and financial incentives to increase their motivation to perform well on the task.

The task involved selecting line items from work plan examples to build a new work plan, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to tasks involving a similar process. However, in general, when selecting from search results, knowledge workers are free to use entire items or parts of items, and there are many domains in which workers may be asked to cull through old documents in order to create new ones. The operationalizations of the independent variables were considered a strength of this study due to the tight controls implemented. However, these controls meant that individual subjects were given credibility indicators that were always either high or low. In a real-life situation, however, knowledge workers would more likely be faced with a mix of credibility indicators—some high and some low—and future research should investigate the role of credibility indicators in this context.

## Future Research Directions

The findings from this study offer some insight into the relationship between content ratings, content search and evaluation processes, credibility indicators, and decision performance. Additional research should examine how to assist individuals in overcoming misleading ratings. In this study, low-validity ratings did not trigger the use of rating credibility indicators to a significant extent, but did trigger the use of content credibility indicators. Researchers should examine more salient and motivating cues in the decision process, such as text comments, to see if they prompt greater attention to credibility indicators in the KMS environment.

Second, research on sensitivity to data quality has suggested that training and/or warnings may help knowledge workers detect errors in objective data. In other studies, warnings about data quality did lead to better rates of error detection, with concomitant improved decision quality but slower decision time (Biros et al. 2002; Wang and Strong 1996). The question remains whether training and/or warnings can help knowledge workers detect errors in subjective data, such as KMS content and ratings of KMS content. Future research is needed to determine how the type, level, and content of training and/or warnings could influence how ratings are used in the KMS environment.

A more thorough understanding of knowledge-worker confidence may also be warranted. Knowledge workers are often overconfident and, because they are sure of themselves and their results, may not bother using credibility indicator cues (Phillips 1973; Yates 1990). Research into how knowledge-worker confidence affects their use of contextually dependent content and ratings will help determine what process or system design features should be implemented.

The use of rating cues extends to contexts beyond KMSs; Internet shopping sites, communities of practice, and bulletin board knowledge-sharing systems also use them (Tan 2003). Ratings and credibility indicators associated with sales on eBay have been shown to influence purchase price, and are at times manipulated (Ba and Pavlou 2002; Keser 2003; Light 2001; Melnik and Alm 2002; Ottaway et al. 2003; Standifird 2002). People may expect manipulation and be more skeptical of ratings when they are shopping for a book on the Internet than they are when they are looking through old work plans in a KMS to create new work plans. Future work is needed to understand how applicable the results of our study are in different knowledge system contexts.

## Conclusions

The results of this research suggest that ratings influence KMS content search and evaluation processes and decision performance, and that content credibility indicators such as collaborative filtering can moderate the relationship between rating validity and search and evaluation processes. The findings help clarify the relationship between content ratings and decision making. Both KMS designers and knowledge workers stand to benefit from knowing how content ratings and credibility indicators influence KMS content search and evaluation (Davenport and Hansen 1999), and how the use of different content search and evaluation processes affects decision outcomes. The finding that collaborative-filtering recommendations are more effective tools than number of raters or rater expertise for assisting knowledge workers in using KMS content ratings is also significant. These findings and those of future studies will help system designers and knowledge workers develop and use KMSs more effectively.

## About the Authors

Cheri Speier is an associate professor of Information Systems at Michigan State University. Her research interests include the influence of work environments on decision making, individual acceptance and use of technology, effective user training environments, and the effective use of information technology to support supply chain relationships. Her work has appeared in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Decision Sciences, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, and Journal of Marketing among others. Dr. Speier was awarded the MSU University-wide Teacher Scholar award in 2001 recognizing her excellence in teaching and research. She earned a Ph.D. in Management Information Systems at Indiana University.

Robin S. Poston is an assistant professor of Management Information Systems at the University of Memphis. Her current research interests are in investigating the role of ratings and other user feedback on decision making in shared-system environments, the effect of credibility indicators on how recommender systems are used, and the influence of collaborative information retrieval in how knowledge is shared in information systems. Her work has appeared in publications such as Journal of Information Systems and International Journal of Accounting Information Systems. Dr. Poston earned a Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from Michigan State University.
