# Evaluating a decision support system for patient image pre-fetching: An experimental study

Paul Jen-Hwa Hu <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Chih-Ping Wei <sup>b</sup>, Olivia R. Liu Sheng

<sup>a</sup> Accounting and Information Systems, David Eccles School of Business, University of Utah, Salt Lake City, UT 84112, USA <sup>b</sup> Institute of Technology Management, College of Technology Management, National Tsing Hua University, Hsinchu, Taiwan, ROC

Received 13 July 2004; received in revised form 26 February 2006; accepted 27 February 2006 Available online 27 April 2006

## Abstract

When reading images from a newly taken radiological examination, a radiologist often needs to reference relevant prior images of the same patient to confirm a preliminary diagnosis, compare suspicious radiographic signs, or evaluate the progression of a known underlying pathological process, injury, or abnormality. To mitigate the stress and time requirements for the reading radiologist's image searches, some healthcare organizations have taken a pre-fetching approach to make relevant patient prior images conveniently accessible. Motivated by the importance of patient-image pre-fetching to radiologists' examination readings as well as by the limited scope and ad hoc evaluation of most previously reported systems, we develop the Image Retrieval Expert System (IRES) and experimentally evaluate its effect on the radiologist's examination-reading efficiency, service quality, and satisfaction using the current pre-fetching practice of the studied organization as a benchmark. Our overall analysis suggests tha image pre-fetching has an important effect on radiologists' examination readings and that radiologists (including residents) can become more efficient, effective, and satisfied when supported by IRES pre-fetching than by the benchmark practice. However, the exact magnitude or statistical significance of the IRES-induced improvements may vary with examination category and/or radiologists' experience. Our findings have several important implications for research and patient image management practices, which also are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Recent technological breakthroughs and the resulting increase in the performance–price index have made information technology (IT) increasingly important and affordable in patient care and management [19,26]. According to the Committee on the Quality of Health Care in America [3], the use of IT to improve clinical information access and decision support represents a critical challenge to healthcare organizations and clinicians. Of particularly salient importance is the expanding clinical role of radiology, which has greatly benefited from advances in medical imaging and IT. As a result, the use of radiological images in patient care has increased, which demands more effective patientimage management that can range from image creation to archiving and retrieval. Central to patient-image management is the reading radiologist's convenient access to patient prior images that are relevant to his or her current examination-reading task. The support provided for such patient-image referencing has significant ramifications for radiologists' examination-reading efficiency, service quality, and satisfaction.

When performing a clinical (primary) reading<sup>1</sup> of images from a newly taken examination, a radiologist often needs to review relevant radiological images, generic or patient-specific. Generic reference images include typical or textbook-type positive or negative images of particular pathological processes, injuries, or abnormalities. Whereas radiologists' access to generic images has been reasonably well supported (e.g., [10,27]), their need to reference relevant patient-specific prior images has not yet received sufficient attention. When reading a newly taken examination, a radiologist usually reviews relevant prior images of the same patient to establish an important baseline for confirming a preliminary diagnosis, comparing suspicious radio graphic signs, or evaluating the progression of a known pathological process, injury, or abnormality. To alleviate stress and time requirements for the reading radiologist, some healthcare organizations have taken a pre-fetching approach in which they institutionalize general rules of thumb or guidelines for selecting relevant patient prior images. Using such rules or guidelines, an assistant can select a subset from the patient's prior images that presumably is relevant to the current examinationreading task and make these images conveniently accessible, such as by placing them on reading panels side by side with current examination images. However, most existing pre-fetching practices are ad hoc, lack an adequate medical or radiological foundation, and have a limited scope with respect to examination-reading scenarios or imaging modality. Furthermore, the effectiveness of most existing patient-image pre-fetching practices has not been methodologically evaluated in clinical settings.

Inadequate support of radiologists' references to relevant patient prior images can reasonably be expected to affect their examination reading adversely. When a relevant patient prior image is not conveniently accessible through pre-fetching, the reading radiologist must devote time and effort to searching for that image (with no guarantee of success) or forgo image referencing to maintain the pace required to complete the assigned examination-reading task in a timely manner. In turn, this dilemma can lead to a forced choice between decreased examination-reading efficiency and reduced service quality. In either case, the radiologist may become dissatisfied or even frustrated. The effects of inadequate patient-image referencing support may be accentuated in digital radiology [23] or teleradiology [8], where all patient images are digitized and often reside in separate image databases; this makes on-demand image browsing and retrieval even more difficult.

We took a knowledge-based approach to develop the Image Retrieval Expert System (IRES) [18], which selects and pre-fetches patient prior images that are relevant to the radiologist's current examination-reading task. Our system supports radiologists' decision making for prior image retrievals and, at the same time, responds to the need to improve knowledge management and service quality in contemporary clinical practice [3]. In this paper, we report an experimental study in which we examined the effects of image pre-fetching on radiologists' examination readings and evaluated the effectiveness of IRES pre-fetching. Our evaluation included the current pre-fetching practice of the studied organization for benchmark purposes.<sup>2</sup> The reported experiment was conducted at a moderate-sized, comprehensive, tertiary medical center (hereafter referred to as MC) affiliated with a major public university in the southwestern United States. Overall, our evaluation results are encouraging. They suggest that image pre-fetching has important effects on the radiologist's examination reading and that IRES pre-fetching is more effective for meeting radiologists' image referencing needs than is the current MC practice. However, the magnitude or statistical significance of the IRES-induced improvements over the benchmark practice seems to vary with the examination category and/or the radiologist's experience. According to our analysis, radiologists are likely to become increasingly efficient, effective in their consultations, and satisfied when supported by IRES pre-fetching.

The organization of the remainder of the paper is as follows: Section 2 reviews relevant previous research and highlights our motivation. Section 3 describes the design and the knowledge base (KB) of IRES. Section 4 details our research hypotheses and evaluation design, followed by discussions of the important analysis results in Section 5. This paper concludes with a summary and discussions of the study's contributions and limitations in Section 6.

## 2. Literature review and motivation

Investigations of radiologists' references to relevant patient prior images have devoted only limited research attention to the topic. A handful of studies have suggested a pre-fetching approach, with particular focus on image selection rules [15,25,28]. In general, these proposed methods or systems lack an adequate medical/radiological foundation and often have a limited scope with respect to the examination-reading scenarios or imaging modality supported. Evaluations of the proposed methods or systems are ad hoc and have not assessed their effectiveness in clinical settings.

Some alternative approaches have been investigated. For instance, Wong et al. [31] propose a table look-up approach, based on examination type, disease category, reading radiologist and referring physician, to create a multidimensional pre-fetching table. The construction and maintenance of such look-up tables can be costly, if not prohibitively demanding. Because this method is purposefully tailored to a particular pairing of a radiologist and a (referring) physician, its effectiveness across different radiologists or healthcare organizations can be considerably constrained. On-demand retrieval support also has been examined. Kishore et al. [13] have developed an on-demand system using a regressionbased association analysis of previous usage of patient images, but its turnaround time for actual image retrievals is not acceptable to reading radiologists. In addition, automated image-selection rule discovery, using artificial intelligence-based supervised learning techniques, has been attempted [21]. Most learningbased solutions intrinsically embrace “black-box” processing and thus lack an adequate medical (radiological) foundation, which is important to their acceptance by radiologists.

Regardless of the approach taken, most reported systems have a limited scope in terms of examinationreading scenarios and imaging modality. Primarily, evaluations of the systems' effectiveness have been mostly ad hoc and not conducted in targeted clinical settings. An image pre-fetching system must be evaluated in relation to such fundamental aspects as pre-fetching accuracy and its effects on the radiologist's examinationreading efficiency, service quality, and satisfaction. These evaluation dimensions closely correspond to the critical information systems success aspects advocated by DeLong and McLean [5], such as information quality and impact on target users' task performance.

Furthermore, clinical efficacy is critical in this context because healthcare professionals appear to emphasize utility when making their technology acceptance decisions [12]. Most previous evaluations of clinical knowledge-based systems tend to concentrate on system correctness, typically based on the agreement between a system's recommendation and a documented (known) result or opinion (decision) reached by selected domain experts. Nohr [20] has reviewed representative knowledge-based system evaluation/validation studies in healthcare and concluded that most, if not all, failed to assess outcome or clinical impacts. In light of the emerging outcome-assessment movement and prevailing managed care practice in healthcare (e.g., [6]), clinical efficacy has become increasingly critical and hence requires our evaluation attention.

Motivated by the importance of patient-image referencing support for radiologists' examination readings, as well as the lack of methodological clinical (efficacy) evaluations, we examined the effectiveness of IRES pre-fetching, which embraces pertinent medical and radiological knowledge. Specifically, we conducted a controlled experiment to investigate the outcomes of image pre-fetching on radiologists' examination readings and to evaluate the effectiveness of IRES prefetching compared with that of the current practice of the MC. A total of 11 radiologists and residents voluntarily participated in the experiment; each read seven or eight examinations randomly drawn from a clinical case pool. Our study also included four other radiologists and residents who took part in the pretest, and yet another six radiologists served on review panels. By design, our evaluation preserved the normal examination-reading environment familiar to the participating radiologists and was conducted in a facility identical to the targeted clinical setting. As a result, our evaluation results arguably are reliable and valid with respect to radiologists' clinical primary reading.

## 3. Design of IRES and its knowledge base

For the design and implementation of the IRES, we followed an incremental development approach, using an iterative build–test–evaluate framework that consisted of complementary development methods and techniques [18]. “Reason-for-examination” is critical to our system design and serves as an anchor implicitly used and communicated by radiologists. Conventional knowledge engineering was used to identify and solicit image-retrieval heuristics from experienced radiologists (i.e., domain experts). Findings from a previously conducted protocol analysis [17], as well as nonparticipatory clinical observations, had suggested that a radiologist usually commences an examination-reading task by searching for the “reason” behind the examination at hand, asking questions such as “Why was this examination taken?” or “What does the referring physician want to find out from this examination?” The examination-reading anchor might evolve as the reading radiologist acquires new or additional information, generates alternative hypotheses, or considers different diagnoses. According to our prior protocol analysis, a reading radiologist often orients him- or herself according to an initial reason-for-examination that can be deduced from multiple sources, including the referring physician's clinical question(s), relevant patient clinical history and current medical condition, and combinations thereof. Most radiologists practicing at the MC infer this initial reason-for-examination from the relevant information available on a Consultation/ Procedure Requisition Form (CRF) and an Examination Form (EF).<sup>3</sup>

Fig. 1. Design of IRES knowledge base.

Our system supports a multi-stage examinationreading process in radiology [7] by facilitating a radiologist to establish an adequate initial focus for his or her current examination-reading task. We analyzed radiologists' patient-image referencing needs to identify the information they considered critical for deriving an initial reason-for-examination and then used that information to anchor our system design. As a result, our knowledge solicitation targets specific rules for determining the reason-for-examination in various examination-reading scenarios that involve different imaging modalities and clinical episodes.

To improve system performance, we coupled the IRES KB with the relevant databases (DB) in which the pertinent data/information reside, such as the referring physician's clinical questions and the patient's history and current medical condition. We implemented the coupled KB/DB design, using the Synthesized Object-Oriented Entity Relationship (SOOER) model [16], an object-oriented model designed for KB/DB integration in a distributed client/server environment. As shown in Fig. 1, the IRES KB contains two types of knowledge that is sequentially related: examination-classification knowledge and patient-image pre-fetching knowledge. The former takes relevant information (e.g., referring physician's clinical question(s), patient condition, patient history available on CRF and EF) as input and produces a specific reason-for-examination as output. Subsequently, the image pre-fetching knowledge takes as input the identified reason-for-examination together with the relevant information contained in the CRF and EF (e.g., anatomical portion examined, imaging modality used, patient gender, disease or abnormality type) and determines the particular patient prior images to be pre-fetched.

Every knowledge element in the IRES KB has a certainty factor (CF) for each suggested patient prior examination, which indicates its degree of relevance to the radiologist's current examination-reading task. We chose to represent CF with multiple discrete values for several reasons. First, the concept of relevance is relative rather than absolute; thus, a dichotomous classification is inappropriate. Second, the use of multiple discrete CF values enables image transmission and display prioritization, a desired feature in digital radiology and teleradiology environments. Third, multilevel CF values support image pre-fetching customization for different radiologists (or groups of radiologists) and allow pre-fetching optimization at an organizational level. For instance, a radiologist can select a pre-fetching threshold appropriate for his or her practice or preference. At the same time, a healthcare organization can adjust the image selection threshold to optimize the prefetching support for its radiologists as a group by using a multi-criteria analytical model that takes into account important cost considerations and operations constraints. A sample image pre-fetching rule is as follows:

IF Reason-for-Examination = “Post-operation” and Clinical Tracing Type = “Urgent” and Patient Condition = “Critical”

THEN Retrieve

• All images from the most recent examination (CF=1.0)

• All images from the first post-operation examination (CF=1.0)

• All images from the pre-operation examination (CF=1.0)

• All images from the second most recent examination (CF=0.8)

• All images from the third most recent examination (CF=0.6)

• All images from the examination taken 3 months or longer than but closest to 3 months prior to current examination (CF = 0.6)

A prototype IRES already has been implemented. Sybase, a relational database platform, was used to develop the IRES KB. Relevant information systems– including Hospital Information System, Radiology Information System, and Picture Archiving and Communications System–have been implemented and examined using Sybase and Versant, an object-oriented database management system. The system components (modules) for the described KB/DB coupling and knowledge inferences were implemented using C and C++.

## 4. Research questions, hypotheses, and evaluation design

In this section, we describe our research questions and evaluation design, including the pretest.

## 4.1. Research questions and hypotheses

The specific questions addressed by this study are as follows: (1) Does patient-image pre-fetching have a significant effect on radiologists' examination reading? (2) Does the use of IRES pre-fetching significantly increase radiologists' examination-reading efficiency, service quality, and satisfaction in comparison with the benchmark practice?

We focused on the time dimension of efficiency and assessed examination-reading efficiency based on the amount of time required to complete an examinationreading task. Service quality, crucial to the clinical role of radiologists, was assessed according to both objective and self-assessed quality measures of individual radiologists' examination-reading reports. In addition, we evaluated radiologists' satisfaction with the overall examination reading and the pre-fetched images. The particular hypotheses tested in the study are as follows:

H1. IRES pre-fetching is more accurate than the benchmark MC practice with respect to radiologists' image referencing requirements.

H2. The use of IRES pre-fetching will increase radiologists' examination-reading efficiency compared with the benchmark MC practice.

H3. The use of IRES pre-fetching will enhance radiologists' service quality compared with the benchmark MC practice.

H4. The use of IRES pre-fetching will improve radiologists' satisfaction compared with the benchmark MC practice.

## 4.2. Evaluation design

Our primary research method was controlled experimentation, supplemented by a questionnaire survey. Conducted in the targeted clinical setting, our experiment achieved a desirable balance between control and actuality, which increases the reliability and generalizability of the responses to causality inquiries about radiologists' clinical practices [32]. We adopted a nested factorial experimental design [14] to test the respective effects of image pre-fetching and experience level on radiologists' examination readings, while also taking into account our accessibility to radiologists and residents. We detail our evaluation design next.

## 4.2.1. Treatments

Two treatments were examined: image pre-fetching systems and radiologist experience. We included two pre-fetching systems; i.e., IRES and the current practice of the MC. The manner in which we assigned a prefetching system to an examination was random but balanced; that is, each system was used in an equal number of examinations. Such assignments were kept from the subjects to reduce any threats to the study's internal validity as a result of novelty effects, treatment diffusion, or compensatory revelry [4]. Each subject was asked to complete all assigned examination-reading tasks as if they were for clinical primary readings.

Radiologists' experience was the second treatment, defined at three levels: senior, junior, and resident. Senior radiologists had accumulated at least 10 years of post-residence practice, whereas junior radiologists had 3 to 7 years of post-residence practice. Residents were individuals currently fulfilling their residency requirements at the studied organization. As shown in Fig. 2, each pre-fetching system was crossed with an experience level, following a 2 × 3 factorial experimental design. According to our design, subjects were nested within experience; specifically, two subjects were included in each experience level investigated.

## 4.2.2. Tasks

We targeted musculoskeletal and chest X-ray diagnostic examinations, which at the time of our investigation accounted for approximately 70% of the radiological examinations performed at the MC. The inclusion of multiple examination categories also enabled us to examine the extent to which IRES prefetching effectiveness might be examination specific. A total of eight examinations were included for each category. Randomly drawn from a clinical case pool, each examination pertained to a unique patient and had had its primary reading completed at least 6 months prior to the study. The 6-month interval was enforced to reduce potential biases should a subject have read a selected examination for clinical purposes. One musculoskeletal examination was recalled for clinical use while the experiment was still in progress, and we removed the already completed reports on this examination from the subsequent data analysis. The actual number of the musculoskeletal examinations thus was reduced to seven, whereas the chest examinations remained at eight. Our unit of analysis was an examination-reading session; that is, a subject reading an examination as supported by a particular pre-fetching system.

## 4.2.3. Subjects

Our subject pool consisted of radiologists and residents practicing at the MC whose voluntary participation in the experiment had been solicited. An important subject selection criterion was that he or she had no previous involvement in IRES development. Two subjects were recruited at each experience level for a total of six subjects for each examination category. The particular type of examinations to be read by a junior or senior radiologist was consistent with his or her documented specialty area or routine reading assignments. Residents who lacked documented specialty areas and routine assignments could choose either category. Each subject read the examinations of one and only one category. In the chest X-ray category, one junior radiologist became unavailable due to an unexpected increase in clinical responsibility.<sup>4</sup> As a result, the actual number of subjects for chest X-ray examinations was reduced to five, whereas that for the musculoskeletal examinations remained at six.

Accessibility to healthcare professionals has been identified as a major challenge to research in health care [9]. Altogether, a total of 21 radiologists and residents were involved in the study: 4 in the pretest, 11 in the subsequent experiment (as subjects), and 6 on the review panels. ## 4.2.4. Dependent variables

Our evaluation concentrated on pre-fetching accuracy and radiologists' examination-reading efficiency, service quality, and satisfaction. We examined pre-fetching accuracy in terms of power and precision, two common accuracy metrics in information retrieval research. Specifically, we measured power using recall rate, defined as the portion of a radiologist's image referencing requirements that are correctly pre-fetched by a system. We measured pre-fetching precision using the precision rate, which refers to the portion of a system's pre-fetched images that are actually used by the reading radiologist. For each examination-reading session, we documented a radiologist's actual image referencing behavior and used it as a proxy for the radiologist's “genuine” image referencing requirements for that session. We explicitly distinguished between pre-fetched and actually referenced images by asking the subject to specify which pre-fetched images he or she had referenced (used) in the examination reading. The referenced pre-fetched images and the additionally retrieved ones (i.e., not pre-fetched) jointly constituted the genuine referencing requirements against which the system's prefetching accuracy was evaluated. These image referencing requirements may differ among radiologists as well as evolve over time. Such heterogeneity or fluidity makes it difficult (if not infeasible) to provide perfect pre-fetching support for a group of radiologists, thus necessitating a trade-off between pre-fetching power and precision.<sup>5</sup> According to the intended decision support and image-retrieval space reduction functionality of IRES, power is probably more important than precision. Therefore, recall rate was chosen as the primary accuracy measurement in our evaluation; precision rate was included as a secondary measurement.

Examination-reading efficiency was measured by the total examination-reading time; i.e., the amount of time elapsed between the commencement and the completion of an examination-reading session. Time spent on interruptions not directly related to the examinationreading task was excluded (e.g., answering page or telephone calls). We decomposed the total examinationreading time into multiple components that included image search time and information processing time. We used image search time as a secondary efficiency measurement to refer to the accumulated time that a radiologist spent searching for relevant patient images that had not been pre-fetched. When reading an examination, a radiologist could engage or attempt multiple image searches. Thus, the time required for each image search was operationally defined as the amount of time elapsed between a radiologist's accessing a patient jacket and placing the retrieved image(s) on the viewing panel or reading desk. In our experiment, we accounted for unsuccessful image searches by including the amount of time a radiologist spent in each abandoned search. Questionable attempts were clarified in discussions with the subjects after they completed each examination-reading session. Necessary adjustments were then made to the recorded search time.

We examined radiologists' consultation effectiveness using both objective and subjective measurements. Following the consensus-based model discussed by O'Leary and Pincus [22], we generated a goldstandard report for each examination and used it to evaluate the reports the subjects rendered in the experiment. All subjects were asked to generate a verbal report for each examination<sup>6</sup> and constantly reminded to perform examination readings as if for primary readings. We also collected subjective, selfassessed service quality evaluations by asking a subject to indicate his or her own assessment (confidence) of each report he or she made. We used the objective service quality score, measured on a 100-point scale, as the primary measure. The self-assessed evaluation was included as a secondary service quality measure and used a 10-point Likert scale with 10 being highly confident about the report quality and 1 being not confident at all.

We also used a self-reported method to collect subjects' satisfaction with the examination readings. After completing an examination-reading task, subjects were asked to specify their satisfaction with the overall examination reading, as well as with the pre-fetched images. The former was the primary satisfaction measurement. Both satisfaction assessments were based on a 10-point Likert scale with 10 being highly satisfied and 1 being not satisfied at all. ## 4.2.5. Experiment facility

We conducted the experiment in a newly renovated examination-reading room that was not in use clinically. Thus, our experiment setting was identical to that for clinical (primary) readings in terms of facility layout and equipment support. Our facility choice replicated the targeted clinical setting but provided greatly improved access and scheduling flexibility. More importantly, the use of a designated examination-reading room significantly reduced interruptions for our subjects, thereby making our evaluation results more reliable.

## 4.2.6. Experiment procedure

Before a subject commenced an examinationreading task, a set of patient prior images was prefetched by the system under evaluation. The prefetched images were placed on the viewing panels together with those from the current examination to be read by the subject. All the information commonly available for primary readings (including CRF, EF, and patient jackets) was conveniently accessible on the reading desk. Prior to reading the assigned examinations, the subjects listened to a prepared script that explicitly conveyed the study's purpose, as well as the intended data usage and management. When reading an examination, subjects had to search the patient jackets for images that had not been prefetched but would be considered relevant to the current reading task. All examination-reading sessions were videotaped and timed. Subjects generated verbal reports for each examination using a dictation system identical to that employed for clinical purposes; these verbal reports were later transcribed for the service quality assessments. After completing an examination, subjects also were asked to fill out a questionnaire that collected their self-assessed report quality rating and satisfaction with the overall examination reading and pre-fetched images.

## 4.3. Generating gold-standard reports

Subjectivity represents a challenge to service quality assessments in healthcare. O'Leary and Pincus [22] suggest a consensus-based approach appropriate for situations in which each individual (i.e., domain expert) has an accuracy probability greater than 50%. The reported approach can generate decisions or diagnoses better than those reached by individual experts. In our case, the minimal accuracy probability requirement (i.e., 50%) seemed reasonable and was assumed for senior and junior radiologists, primarily because of their clinical training and experience. Accordingly, we created a review panel for each examination category, each consisting of three senior and junior radiologists. Each panel radiologist had a documented specialty area and routine examination readings identical to the particular examination category assigned. All review panel radiologists were excluded from the pretest and the experiment. Each panel radiologist read all the examinations of the assigned category and generated an individual report on each examination. Information symmetry was strictly preserved; that is, each panel radiologist had access to exactly the same information as the subjects.

A senior coder with more than 15 years of experience in coding radiological examination reports was asked to consolidate individual panel radiologists' findings and diagnoses to establish a gold-standard report for each examination. Confidentiality was preserved so that the identities of the panel radiologists were not revealed to the coder. A majority vote-based scheme was used to determine whether a specific finding or diagnosis was to be included in the goldstandard report. According to this scheme, a finding or diagnosis had to appear in at least two panel radiologists' reports to be included in the gold-standard report for that examination. The same coder then used the resulting gold-standard reports to evaluate the reports rendered by the subjects in the experiment.

The nature of radiological examination coding and our coder's extensive experience warranted the use of a single coder. To a large extent, the coding of radiological examination reports is already systematic [2,24], partially due to health data collection requirements at various levels, as well as to financial and legal implications. In our study, the coder was considered the most reliable at the studied organization, as manifested by her frequently being assigned to conduct new staff training or in-service workshops.

A double-blind method was used to assess the quality of subjects' examination-reading reports. Neither the subject's identity nor the pre-fetching system used in the examination-reading session was revealed to the coder during her objective assessments of the subjects reports. Using the consolidated gold-standard report, the coder evaluated all transcribed reports of the same examination, without knowing which pre-fetching system was used or which radiologist had generated the report. False negatives (i.e., misses) were penalized, but false positives were not. This asymmetric penalty was chosen for several reasons. First, subjectivity is inevitable in clinicians' decision making. In our case, such subjectivity intrinsically makes “misses” relatively more measurable and conveyable in service quality assessment than false positives. Second, false positives are more likely to be corrected clinically, through additional evaluations or confirmatory tests, than are false negatives, which may remain undetected because they often require no additional assessments or tests. Taken together, these practices make false negatives arguably less tolerable than false positives.<sup>7</sup>

We assessed the finding/diagnosis consistency between and among panel radiologists. As shown in Table 2, inter-panel radiologist agreement ranged from 51% to 85%, which seems reasonable by several accounts. The agreement on clinical decisions by healthcare professionals has historically been challenging [1]. Compared with several clinical finding/ diagnosis consistency evaluations that include radiology [29,30], the observed level of agreement between or among panel radiologists was reasonably satisfactory. In addition, the finding/diagnosis agreement recognized in our analysis was relatively strict because it required that findings share a compatible if not identical code. As a result, a general finding (e.g., airspace disease) reported by one panel radiologist would be considered different than a more specific diagnosis (e.g., pneumonia) by another radiologist, even though both radiologists might be referring to the same underlying disease or pathological process. Similarly, the code for a specific finding (e.g., disorder of base) is different than that for a related but more general finding (e.g., radiological density), which in many cases might imply disorder of base. In clinical settings, any ambiguity between generality and specificity is settled by the coder's confirmation with the reading radiologist. However, such verifications were not allowed in our study because of the confidentiality requirements of the double-blind method used.

## 4.4. Pretest

We performed a pretest to fine-tune our study design and familiarize the experimenters with the data collection procedure. The pretest also allowed for an estimation of the time requirements, which were important for subject recruiting and scheduling. The pretest included both investigated examination categories, for each of which two radiologists or residents read two or three examinations according to the experiment procedure. The examinations used in the pretest were randomly drawn from those to be used in the experiment. The pretest radiologists and residents provided valuable feedback and suggestions, leading to several modifications to our questionnaire design (particularly wording choices) and data collection procedure. The pretest participants were excluded from the subsequent experiment.

## 5. Analysis results

Our overall analysis suggests that image prefetching has important effects on radiologists' examination readings. According to our comparative results, IRES pre-fetching was more accurate than the benchmark practice and able to make radiologists increasingly efficient, effective, and satisfied, though the exact magnitude or statistical significance of IRES-induced improvements seemed to vary with the examination category and radiologists' experience. We present the details of our comparative analysis next.

## 5.1. Effects of image pre-fetching on radiologists' examination readings

Analysis results from the musculoskeletal examinations suggested that image pre-fetching had significant effects on radiologists' examination-read ing efficiency, service quality, and satisfaction. As shown in Table 3, the effect of image pre-fetching on radiologists' satisfaction with the overall examination reading was highly significant (p-value <0.0001). Image pre-fetching exhibited a significant effect on radiologists' image search time, self-assessed report quality, and satisfaction with the pre-fetched images (p-value <0.05). The effect on total examinationreading time and objective report quality was noticeable but of marginal statistical significance, as suggested by a p-value between 0.05 and 0.1. Our findings also suggested that experience had significant effects on total examination-reading time, selfassessed report quality, and satisfaction with the overall examination reading and the pre-fetched images.

For the chest X-ray examinations, the effects of image pre-fetching were less prominent and significant than those observed in the musculoskeletal examinations. Image pre-fetching had a significant effect on radiologists' satisfaction with the overall examination reading but not on the other dependent variables. According to the ANOVA results summarized in Table 4, the factor subject (experience), which nested individual subjects within an experience level, was able to explain a significant portion of the variance in most of the dependent variables; namely, total examination-reading time, self-assessed report quality, and satisfaction with both the overall examination reading and pre-fetched images. By itself or together with a pre-fetching system, experience explained a significant portion of the variance in radiologists' self-assessed report quality and their satisfaction with the overall examination reading and pre-fetched images.

## 5.5. Analysis of radiologists' satisfaction

According to our findings from the musculoskeletal examinations, subjects were more satisfied with overall examination reading and pre-fetched images when supported by IRES pre-fetching than by the benchmark practice. As shown in Table 8, the increase in satisfaction averaged 50% in overall examination reading and even more in the pre-fetched images. First, junior and senior radiologists supported by IRES pre-fetching were highly satisfied with the overall examination reading, showing an average satisfaction score of 9 or higher on the 10- point Likert scale. The high satisfaction observed might have been attributable in part to the shortened examination-reading time, reduction or elimination of additional retrievals, enhanced service quality, or combinations thereof. Second, residents also exhibited significantly increased satisfaction with overall examination readings and the pre-fetched images (i.e., pvalue <0.05). The increase in satisfaction appeared to be least prominent and statistically significant for junior radiologists.

For the chest X-ray examinations, both residents and senior radiologists became increasingly satisfied with the overall examination readings and pre-fetched images when supported by IRES pre-fetching compared with the benchmark practice. However, we observed decreased satisfaction among junior radiologists, whereas senior radiologist subjects showed the largest gain in satisfaction. The increase in satisfaction might be attributed in part to reduced examinationreading time and image search time or enhanced service quality.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

To improve radiologists' examination-reading efficiency, service quality, and satisfaction, healthcare organizations must address their patient-image referencing requirements. In response to this challenge, we developed IRES, a knowledge-based system that prefetches the relevant patient prior images for the radiologist's current examination-reading task. In this study, we clinically assessed the effects of image prefetching on radiologists' examination readings and experimentally compared their examination-reading performances when supported by IRES pre-fetching versus the current practice of the MC.

According to our experiment results, image prefetching appears to have important effects on radiologists' examination reading, but the magnitude and significance of the effect may vary with the examination category or radiologists' experience. Furthermore, IRES pre-fetching is more effective in meeting radiologists' patient-image referencing needs than is the benchmark practice. Overall, our analysis suggests that IRES pre-fetching embraces desired clinical efficacy, which can make radiologists increasingly efficient, effective in their consultations, and satisfied.

To gain insights into the inconsistent results observed in the musculoskeletal and chest X-ray examinations, we conducted post-experiment interviews with a handful of radiologists who had participated in the study as subjects or review panel members. Complexity was the factor most frequently identified for explaining the effectiveness differentials observed. According to these radiologists' assessments, chest X-ray examinations in general are easier to read than are musculoskeletal examinations; many general practitioners can read chest X-ray images themselves but often depend on radiologists for interpreting musculoskeletal images. The importance of image pre-fetching support intuitively increases with examination complexity. In this same vein, the effectiveness of IRES pre-fetching can become more prominent or significant in musculoskeletal than in chest X-ray examinations. The lower consistency in the findings or diagnoses between and among the musculoskeletal panel radiologists than observed in the chest X-ray panel (see Table 2) also supports the moderating effect of examination complexity. In addition, the analysis of the IRES-induced improvement in service quality and satisfaction shows considerably greater improvements in musculoskeletal than chest examinations. Taken together, these observations imply that radiologists may become increasingly dependent on pre-fetching support when they read musculoskeletal examinations, which in general may be more challenging to read than chest X-ray examinations.

Our analysis of the results from the musculoskeletal and chest X-ray examinations also suggests that IRES pre-fetching appears more effective for residents and senior radiologists than for junior radiologists. The difference observed might be partially explained by the development and stability level of radiologists' image referencing requirements. A radiologist's dependence on image pre-fetching support may decrease with his or her clinical experience. As a result, the effectiveness of IRES pre-fetching can be more prominent or significant for residents than for radiologists. However, the stability of a radiologist's patient-image referencing requirements also might affect the clinical utility of image pre-fetching. Radiologists develop their individual image referencing requirements or patterns over time, and the stability of such image referencing requirements strengthens and solidifies as the radiologist accumulates clinical knowledge and experiences. As a result, providing effective pre-fetching support to junior radiologists might be more difficult than providing it to senior radiologists or residents. Senior radiologists arguably may have developed relatively defined and stable referencing requirements or patterns, whereas residents may have not yet have developed any such requirements or patterns and therefore can benefit greatly from image pre-fetching support. In either case, the effectiveness of IRES pre-fetching can be detected when matching the referencing requirements of senior radiologists or providing the examination reading support to residents. Somewhere between senior radiologists and residents, junior radiologists probably have developed more image referencing requirements than residents but have not reached the level of stability of senior radiologists.

The development and stability of specific image referencing requirements might also be partially analyzed by knowledge sharing among individual radiologists or residents. Residents, particularly those in competitive radiology resident programs, are a unique group and usually can be characterized by strong social ties among peers and close bonding with facility clinicians established through intense work environments. By and large, senior radiologists, particularly those practicing in the same institution for a long period of time, are likely to establish personal relationships and knowledge-sharing patterns among themselves. In contrast, junior radiologists, because of their current career stage, might practice in environments perceived to be competitive rather than collaborative, which might make them less comfortable about seeking advice from colleagues. In turn, their (partially) developed but yet evolving image referencing requirements might make effective image pre-fetching support difficult to achieve. The significance of the factor subject(experience) observed in both the musculoskeletal and chest X-ray examinations is congruent with the speculation that image pre-fetching has a stabilizing impact on examination readings by senior radiologists and residents.

This study makes several contributions to healthcare information systems research and patient image management practice in radiology. First, we have responded to the need for increased support of radiologists' patientspecific image referencing requirements by developing a pre-fetching system that selects patient prior images relevant to a radiologist's current examination reading. We also have addressed the need to assess the effects of a healthcare information system on clinical services and patient care/management by physicians or specialists. Specifically, we conducted a controlled experiment to examine the effectiveness of IRES pre-fetching in the targeted clinical setting and used a prevailing practice as a benchmark. Our use of healthcare professionals, realworld examinations, and the targeted clinical setting exemplifies adequate system evaluations in healthcare, an important service sector in the national and global economy. From a clinical point of view, the reported study is congruent with the increasing attention to service quality, as well as a general trend toward outcome assessments, including those that involve the use of information systems. At the same time, we contribute to advancing patient-image management practice by developing and validating a knowledgebased system that embraces an appealing level of clinical efficacy to enhance radiologists' examinationreading efficiency, service quality, and satisfaction.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when extrapolating our findings. First, our subjects were drawn from a radiologist group practicing at a teaching/tertiary medical center and therefore may differ from professionals practicing in other institutions (e.g., private clinics, community hospitals) in terms of their workload, time constraints, and/or practice style. Second, the examinations used in the experiment were randomly drawn from a clinical case pool. Therefore, we cannot comfortably rule out potential biases in our subject or examination selection, which might have resulted in some of the inconsistent results observed. Specifically, the examinations used in the study were randomly drawn, using a convenient sampling scheme, from a collection of clinical cases assembled in an intensive 3-week period. As a result, the examinations might have differed subtly from the underlying examination population because of seasonal<sup>8</sup> or other factors. Third, the number of examinations included in the experiment was also limited and therefore constrained the range of analysis performed. Fourth, another limitation resulted from the single case recall in the musculoskeletal examinations and, more important, the subject withdrawal from the chest examinations. A review of the findings from the chest X-ray examinations suggests considerable inconsistency at the junior radiologist level, in which we had only one subject. The subject withdrawal inevitably increased the influence of the remaining junior radiologist and therefore may have considerably reduced the reliability or accuracy of our analysis results. Although broader or more comprehensive than most previous research, the imaging modalities and examination categories examined in the study might be expanded to include, for example, Computer Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging or pediatric and mammogram examinations.

These limitations call for future research attention to some important issues. First, we must investigate the inconsistency of our findings by answering the following question: Are the effects of image prefetching and the effectiveness of IRES pre-fetching specific to the examination category or the radiologist's experience? If so, we then need to identify the key contributing factors and assess their implications for radiologists' examination readings. Second, replicating the current study on a larger scale is also important, preferably involving radiologists practicing at private clinics or community hospitals, as well as additional imaging modalities. Evaluations of IRES pre-fetching in a digital radiology or teleradiology environment are essential as well. The development of IRES has been motivated by the proliferation of digital radiology and teleradiology, in which radiologists practice and collaborate in a film-less virtual environment. Third, investigating an optimal balance between the recall and precision rates also deserves our research attention. One intuitive approach is to develop an analytical model that takes into account important benefits and costs associated with over- and under-retrievals. In addition, we must examine and compare the outcomes for patients when radiologists are supported by IRES pre-fetching versus the existing practice. The current study evaluates IRES from the perspective of radiologists (i.e., care providers), but further research should assess actual patient outcomes in clinical settings longitudinally.
