# EARLY BIRD VERSUS LATE OWL: AN EMPIRICAL INVESTIGATION OF INDIVIDUAL SHOPPING TIME HABIT AND ITS EFFECTS<sup>1</sup>

Ziqiong Zhang School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin 150001 CHINA {ziqiong@hit.edu.cn}

Zili Zhang School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin 150001 CHINA {zilizhang@hit.edu.cn}

Pei-yu Chen Department of Information Systems, W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University, Tempe, AZ 85287 U.S.A. {peiyu.chen@asu.edu}

Little research has focused on online shopping habits, particularly concerning time, missing the opportunity to potentially improve important outcomes by the simple innovative use of time. Based on a unique dataset that includes reviews as well as pertinent purchases at the individual level from a large online retailer, this study investigates whether consumers exhibit time habits for online shopping and whether following such time habits affects their satisfaction and revisit behavior. We employ activity-based metrics to assess individual shopping time habits, and the results show that consumers form shopping time habits and obtain higher consumer satisfaction and exhibit greater revisit behavior when the timing of shopping follows their shopping time habits. While prior works have documented that consumers exhibit time habits for physical shopping, driven mostly by time and location constraints, this study is the first, to our knowledge, to examine online shopping time habit and, most importantly, its effects on consumer satisfaction and revisit behavior. With the availability of detailed individual transaction data in online shopping and the advance of technology in providing personalized services that enable companies to act upon knowledge of individual behaviors, this research provides important practical implications for system and website design, marketing strategy, and customer relationship management.

Keywords: Online shopping, time habit, timing of shopping, satisfaction, revisit behavior

## Introduction

Habit, a settled tendency of behavior (Merriam-Webster Dictionary), is often acquired with frequently performed behaviors in a recurring context (Ouellette and Wood 1998).

Once a habit is formed, a knowledge structure linking context cues and an action or behavior becomes hard wired (Kim et al. 2005); when the context cues set in, the behavior is performed automatically (as in auto-pilot mode) and requires minimal mental effort (Wood et al. 2002) while achieving efficiency and accuracy (James 1890; Lindbladh and Lyttkens 2002). For instance, we brush our teeth after waking up without conscious intent or effort. Time of day has been acknowledged as one of the most important context cues that trigger habit formation for behaviors that are repeatedly performed (Ji and Wood 2007; Kim and Malhotra 2005; Liu-Thompkins and Tam 2013; Polites and Karahanna 2013; Wood and Neal 2009).

Shopping is a major activity for everyone, from everyday needs to occasional special desires. Previous studies have reported that consumers exhibit time habits for shopping in physical stores (East et al. 1994; Kahn and Schmittlein 1989). For example, the majority of consumers go shopping in the early evening (East et al. 1994). Liu-Thompkins and Tam (2013) and Shah et al. (2014) showed that consumers persistently shop in consistent time periods. Overall, these results, based on offline shopping, suggest that consumers exhibit habits to shop at certain times of the day, defined as shopping time habit (STH). STH is often initially caused by convenience (i.e., having less time/effort costs associated with shopping during a certain time; Berry et al. 2002; Liu-Thompkins and Tam 2013), and gradually becomes a habit. In offline shopping, convenience is primarily shaped by consumers’ work/school schedules and transportation time/costs to stores, and this explains the consistent STHs of offline shoppers (who tend to shop in the early evening).

Due to widespread Internet technologies and the convenience they offer, online shopping has become an essential path to purchasing for consumers. According to a recent Total Retail Survey by PwC, more than 40% of respondents across 29 territories have purchased products online daily or weekly. China is the largest retail e-commerce market in the world, and 52% of respondents are purchasing via their mobile phone on a daily or weekly basis. Compared to physical shopping, online shoppers literally could shop at will at any moment of the day (e.g., by using mobile phones to shop during a coffee or restroom break). We are interested in exploring, given the convenience of online shopping, whether online shoppers exhibit STHs, and, most importantly, the impact of following such habits on shopping outcomes, particularly, consumer satisfaction and revisit behavior. Despite prior findings of regular shopping times in offline shopping (e.g., East et al. 1994; Kahn and Schmittlein 1989; Liu-Thompkins and Tam 2013; Shah et al. 2014), there is a lack of understanding if following STH systematically affects shopping outcomes.

The goal of this paper is to investigate STH at the individual level and its effects. With a large-scale dataset from JD.com, consisting of 172,825 consumers and their 11,580,097 reviews and corresponding purchases posted between 2007 and 2014, we were able to infer individual STH based on real behavior frequency, which is commonly employed to measure habit in general (e.g., Kim and Malhotra 2005; Triandis 1977) as well as shopping time habit (Ji and Wood 2007; Liu-Thompkins and Tam 2013). Our results provide evidence on the existence of online STH, and show that STH has a remarkable effect on consumer satisfaction (proxied by consumer product ratings) and revisit behavior (measured by interpurchase interval). With several robustness tests and methods of instrumental variables, we show that the effects of following STH on satisfaction do not appear to be due to reverse causality or other forms of endogeneity.

These findings provide important implications for researchers and marketers. First of all, the theoretical underpinnings of habit effect represent a great opportunity for research, especially since individual-level data becomes increasingly available and personalization technology allows companies to act upon individual habits. Shah et al. (2014) stated that at a very fundamental level, any form of recurring behavior can be potentially habit forming and of interest to any company concerned with managing customer relationships over time. Understanding individual time habit and its impacts, therefore, has important implications for both consumers and managers. While prior studies have shown that consumers exhibit STH, those studies have not explored the effects of following or not following STH. To our knowledge, our research is the first to examine online STH and its effects. Second, most prior studies used self-reported questionnaires to study habits. This study joins some recent efforts that advocate for the use of individual-level, activity-based data to measure individual time habit and its effect. Activity-based metrics avoid several biases known to survey-based methods, and reflect true consumer behaviors. Finally, our results suggest that an effective personalization strategy should take into account each individual consumer’s STH to improve shopping outcomes. Compared to other often costly target-based marketing, a time-based personalization strategy is rather cost efficient.

## Theoretical Foundation and Hypotheses Development

Research on habit defines it as a behavioral disposition that is exercised frequently and in which responses are triggered directly by context cues (Liu-Thompkins and Tam 2013; Ouellette and Wood 1998; Triandis 1977; Wood and Neal 2009). The initial driver that causes the repeated behavior may be due to exogenous factors (e.g., convenience) or/and preference or biological states (e.g., circadian rhythm), and, as a behavior being regularly repeated in a consistent context (e.g., time of day and location), cue–response links are formed and habits are acquired (Lally et al. 2010; Liu-Thompkins and Tam 2013; Verplanken et al. 2008; Wood and Neal 2009). Specifically, the habit literature recognizes that the formation of habit follows a positive feedback cycle termed the habit loop, which is a three-part process: cues, repetition of behavior, and rewards (Duhigg 2012). The context cue leads to repeated behaviors, and with sufficient repetition in a consistent context (the cues), the individual becomes familiar with what to expect and how to react, such that the behavior can subsequently be performed automatically with less effort (the reward), which in turn reinforces the repeated behaviors in a consistent context (Limayem et al. 2007; Orbell et al. 2001). Lally et al. (2010) investigated the process of habit formation in the same cue of “after breakfast” and reported that, for the majority of subjects, automaticity increased steadily with behavior repetition and missing one opportunity to perform the behavior did not materially affect the habit formation process. Given the flexibility and convenience of online shopping at any time, it is interesting to study if time habits exist in online shopping. As discussed above, habits are formed gradually, following a positive feedback cycle (i.e., the tendency to repeat the behavior would increase with more recurring behaviors). Therefore, to prove that STH is present, we need to empirically demonstrate that this positive reinforcing loop exists (i.e., the more a consumer purchases during a particular time block, the more likely the consumer continues to purchase in the same time block). Hypothesis 1 is set up to test if such a positive loop exists. Support of H1 would provide evidence of the existence of STH.

## H1: A consumer tends to shop in a particular time block with repetition of online shopping in the same time block.

An important implication of habits is the consequence of following or not following them, due to the presence of cue– response links. A contextual cue is not a behavior itself, but a marker for the brain to perform an associated behavior (Martin and Morich 2011). A recurring contextual cue may launch one or many habitual behaviors independent of conscious intentions and goals (Wood and Neal 2009). While the cue–response link works as a fast pass and provides a shortcut to get the habitual behaviors done quickly and rather effortlessly (James 1890; Lindbladh and Lyttkens 2002), it creates slack, allowing the conscious mind to attend to other matters (Nilsen et al. 2008). Accordingly, once a time habit is formed, people likely achieve better outcomes when following the time habit for the behavior, because during the habitual time they can perform habitual behaviors automatically, while leaving the more conscious mind for other things (e.g., gathering and processing more information, comparing other products, performing cost–benefit analyses, etc.), and in turn increase cognitive efficiency with a potentially better outcome. Park et al. (1989) found that, under time pressure, consumers often shop more successfully when they are in a familiar context compared to in a non-familiar context, which is likely because they rely on habits (Wood and Neal 2009). We apply these theories on habits to online shopping.

A purchase decision (i.e., whether to make a purchase and what to purchase), often requires nontrivial information processing and consideration (Dimoka et al. 2012; Meyvis and Janiszewski 2002). For example, in the information gathering stage, consumers activate prior experiences about shopping and product stored in memory and retrieve information from the web (Bettman and Park 1980; Ho et al. 2017; Wright and Lynch 1995; Zhao et al. 2013). In the information processing and decision-making stage, consumers have to filter relevant information from the irrelevant and detect misleading marketing tricks (Dellarocas 2006; Ho et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2015; Mayzlin et al. 2014; Moe and Trusov 2011; Muchnik et al. 2013). Due to these requirements of cognitive processing in online shopping, we expect that a consumer, when following her STH, consciously gathers and processes additional information and is able to make more efficient and effective purchase decisions, compared with deviating from STH. When good decisions are made, consumers are satisfied, and a bad decision (e.g., they purchased something that should not have been purchased) leads to dissatisfaction. These observations lead to our next hypothesis:

## H2: Following STH is positively related to purchase satisfaction.

When a consumer is satisfied with her purchases from a store, she likely has a higher tendency to revisit the store. What we are interested in is if, after accounting for the effect of satisfaction, the following STH has an additional effect on revisit behavior. For online shopping, the consumer is not only a traditional shopper but also a computer user (Koufaris 2002). The influence of past use for an e-commerce website on future use can be substantial. Kim and Malhotra (2005) showed that once an information system is used frequently in a stable environment, past use has a critical impact on future use while overshadowing the effect of intention on future use. Wang et al. (2013) reached a similar conclusion that habit completely mediates the impact of intention on future usage of selfservice technologies. Per our argument in H2, following STH allows consumers to experience lower cognitive effort and higher efficiency in processing information. Chen (2017) showed that when consumers experience lower cognitive effort in a shopping system, their continuance intention to purchase increases. In addition, consumers may derive more enjoyment from the shopping experience at their habitual shopping times due to less effort being required. Enjoyment of an experience has been shown to positively affect the use intention of computer-mediated environments such as e-mail (Trevino and Webster 1992), software (Webster et al. 1993), Internet (Novak et al. 2000), and online shopping system (Koufaris 2002). Furthermore, enjoyment of the shopping experience has a positive effect on purchase intention (Elseidi 2018; Ettis 2017; Ozkara et al. 2017) and revisit intention for online stores (Chiu et al. 2009; Ettis 2017). This discussion suggests that when a consumer follows her STH, it can have a positive effect on subsequent visits of the website. Prior literature measures revisit behaviors either by interpurchase interval, the time gap between current and next shopping, or shopping frequency (or purchase rate). The two measures are perfectly correlated because shopping frequency is essentially the inverse of interpurchase interval. Given that we observe individual purchases, we take advantage of our data and adopt interpurchase interval measure for revisit behavior. We hypothesize that, after controlling for the effects of satisfaction toward both products and a store, a consumer who follows her STH would exhibit higher revisit behavior.

H3: Following STH is negatively related to the time interval between the current and next shopping.

## Data

We obtain data from JD.com to empirically study individual STH and its effects. JD.com is the largest online direct sales platform in China,<sup>2</sup> similar to Amazon.com in United States. This website is ideal for our study because it offers additional details in each consumer review, including not only a numeric rating, textual comments, and product being reviewed, but also the detailed purchase time of the product and the detailed written time of the review. As of 2014, the active users on the website exceeded 96.6 million.<sup>3</sup> We focus on the major consumer base of the website by identifying a large set of users who ever left a review in the dominant product category that JD.com has since it started business. Consumers who have written reviews tend to be more active and more frequent buyers, whom business managers most care about. Moreover, consumers who have written reviews could potentially influence other consumers’ decisions through their reviews.

We identified 172,825 such users, representing the major consumer base of JD.com. We tracked all reviews from these consumers and obtained all associated purchases spanning all product categories carried by JD.com. The data collection started at the beginning of August 2014 and lasted for three months until the end of October 2014. Finally, our data consists of 11,580,097 reviews and pertinent purchase information from the set of 172,825 users, covering 29 product categories (see Appendix A) carried by JD.com and ranging from January 2007 to October 2014.

## Evidence of Shopping Time Habits

The two key questions we aim to address in this study are whether STH exists in online shopping (H1), and if it exists, what its effects are (H2 and H3). In this section, we first address if STH exists.

## Empirical Strategy

We employ two strategies, drawing upon the habit literature, to test H1. Prior habit literature established that habit formation exhibits “the habit loop” (Lally et al. 2010; Limayem et al. 2007; Orbell et al. 2001). Following this literature on habit formation, as a consumer shops frequently in a particular time block, a time habit is gradually formed, which in turn increases the consumer’s tendency to make future shopping in the same time block, reinforcing the habit, and so on. Therefore, the repetition of behaviors is both an antecedent and a consequence in the habit loop. To prove that STH exists, we must be able to show that a consumer is more likely to purchase in a particular time block with the strength of recurring purchases in the same time block (measured by SameTime ). Specifically, we would like to test if a higher number of recurring purchases in the same time block increases the probability that a customer purchases in that same time block in her next purchase.

Therefore, our results provide evidence supporting the formation and existence of STH in online shopping. H1 is supported.

## Effects of Following Shopping Time Habits

The previous section provided evidence on the existence of time habit for online shopping. In this section, we first introduce an activity-based strategy to measure individual STH and then investigate the effects, if any, of following such STHs.

## Measurement Strategy of Individual Shopping Time Habit

When a habit is formed, an individual will have the propensity to follow the habit, for example by choosing the same brand (brand habit) or by doing something at certain time (time habit). Based upon this manifestation of habit, there are two common ways to measure habits. One approach that has been used particularly for brand habit is to assume a serial correlation of brand choice in terms of utilities (Roy et al. 1996). The more common approach is to use activity-based metrics $( { \mathrm { i . e . } }$ , behavior frequency) to measure habits (Ji and Wood 2007; Kim and Malhotra 2005; Liu-Thompkins and Tam 2013; Shah et al. 2014). For example, Golder and Macy (2011) measure individuals’ preferences for Twitter posting using their posting frequency in each time block (e.g., morning, afternoon, etc.). Liu-Thompkins and Tam (2013) report shopping time habits by calculating shopping frequency in different time blocks from transaction records in physical stores. Given our focus on time habit, we adopt the activitybased metrics to measure individual shopping time habits. We follow the approach used in Golder and Macy and Liu-Thompkins and Tam by comparing the purchase frequencies in each time block (e.g., daytime versus nighttime) for each consumer, and if the number of purchases in a certain time block, say $^ { b , }$ is significantly more (depending on different thresholds) than the number of purchases in other time blocks, then we infer the consumer as having STH on time block b. We explore different threshold values to measure STH, and results remain highly consistent. Refer to Appendix B for details of our STH operationalization.

Habit is likely to be stable; however, it may also change over a longer time horizon. Compared to previous studies that have shorter observation time windows of data to measure habits, our data observation window spans several years, and thus we have to accommodate the possibility of habit change. To account for potential change of STH, we divide our data into time periods (e.g., yearly periods), and measure an individual’s STH in each period. In the case that STH does not change, we will end up with the same measures of STH across different time periods, and measuring STH period by period allows us to capture potential change of STH, in case it happens. We separate the data to measure STH and to test its effects. Specifically, we derive a consumer’s STH, based on the purchase times observed in previous period (t-1), and then use purchases made in current period (t), whether each purchase follows this STH or not, to test the effects of STH. We have explored t = half year, one year, or two years. Results remain highly consistent across different lengths of time period, suggesting that individual STH is, in general, stable over time. In our main analysis and results, we use t = one year and two time-blocks (daytime versus nighttime) for STH measurement.

## Effect of Following Shopping Time Habit on Satisfaction

## Empirical Specification

As is with prior literature (Luo et al. 2013; Moon et al. 2010), we posit that consumer satisfaction can be proxied by product ratings, and a higher product rating suggests that a good purchase decision has been made. We model consumer satisfaction of an online purchase as a function of

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Rating} _ {i j} = \beta_ {1} \text {FollowSTH} _ {i j} + \beta_ {2} \text {WeekendBuy} _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {3} \text {WeekendReview} _ {i j} + \beta_ {4} \text {TxtLth} _ {i j} + \beta_ {5} \text {DiffDay} _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {6} \text {Repeat} _ {i j} + \beta_ {7} \text {SaleDay} _ {i j} + \beta_ {8} \text {ReviewSaleDay} _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {9} \text {FollowRTH} _ {i j} + \beta_ {1 0} j _ {i} + + \beta_ {1 1} r _ {i} + P _ {c} + B u y Y _ {y} + \\ B u y M _ {m} + B u y H _ {h} + R e v i e w Y _ {y} + R e v i e w M _ {m} \\ + R e v i e w H _ {h} + C _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i j} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

In Equation (2), the dependent variable $R a t i n g _ { i j }$ is the rating consumer i gave to her $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ purchased product, measured on a five-point scale with five being the most satisfied. A binary variable $F o l l o w S T H _ { i j }$ is constructed to indicate whether the timing of purchase j follows consumer i’s STH or not, which is coded as 1 if yes, and 0 if no. This is our main variable of interest. As discussed in the previous subsection, we measure consumer i’s STH from her purchase times in previous period (e.g., previous year or previous half-year). If following STH improves decisions, we should observe a positive coefficient on this variable.

Control Variables: We have added many control variables to ensure that our observed effect is not driven by other factors that may systematically affect satisfaction. First, we control for weekend effects: since consumers are generally happier (Golder and Macy 2011) and have more time during weekends, we control whether a purchase or a review is done during weekend with two dummies $W e e k e n d B u y _ { i j }$ and $W e e k e n d R e \nu i e w _ { i j } .$ We also control for length of review, $T x t L t h _ { i j } ,$ measured by the number of characters in the review text. Prior studies have indicated that people tend to post reviews sooner when they are dissatisfied (Cabral and Li 2015), so we control for the gap time (in days) between review time and purchase time, $D i f f D a y _ { i j } ,$ to make sure that this does not drive the results. We also include a dummy, $R e p e a t _ { i j } ,$ to note if a purchase is a repeated purchase since consumers are subject to little uncertainty with repeat purchase. Adding this control allows us to make sure that satisfaction is not driven by repeat purchase at the same time. We also control for the effects of promotions with two binary variables $S a l e D a y _ { i j }$ and $R e \nu i e w S a l e D a y _ { i j } ,$ indicating whether the purchase and review time of purchase j fell on a major sales day (e.g., official holidays, JD anniversary on June 18, and Internet shopping festivals on November 11 and December 12). It is possible that consumers also exhibit time habit for review, so we control for the potential influence of review time habit (RTH) on ratings. We measure RTH in the same way as we measure STH, and use the binary variable $F o l l o w R T H _ { i j }$ to denote whether the actual review time of purchase j follows RTH of consumer i. We also control for the number of purchases, $, j _ { i } ,$ that consumer i has had prior to the current purchase, to capture potential learning effects or loyalty of consumers to the website. $r _ { i }$ is used to control for the effect of review sequence of consumer i. Notably, we also include product category fixed effects $( P _ { c } ) _ { }$ , purchase year fixed effects $( B u y Y )$ , purchase month fixed effects $( B u y M _ { m } )$ purchase hour-of-day fixed effects $( B u y H _ { h } ) ,$ , review year fixed effects (ReviewY ), review month fixed effects $( R e \nu i e w M _ { m } )$ review hour-of-day fixed effects $( R e \nu i e w H _ { h } )$ , and consumer fixed effects (C ). Product category fixed effects would control for systematic rating difference due to product category;

year fixed effects control for time trends and time-specific exogenous shocks over the years; month fixed effects capture monthly and seasonal variations; hour-of-day fixed effects control for general effects of fatigue and/or affect over a day; and consumer fixed effects would absorb any consumer heterogeneity such as individual rating tendency and other consumer characteristics (e.g., gender) that may affect ratings.

We also conduct several robustness tests. First, potential contamination on ratings is more likely to occur when there is a long time gap between purchase time and review time, as other factors not related to the shopping decision may also contribute to the reviews, such as the changes of consumer taste or usage conditions. To avoid such potential contamination, Column (4) of Table 4 considers only those observations with no more than a 48-hour gap between review time and purchase time, and the results remain consistent. Second, since our research is to investigate habit effects at the individual consumer level rather than at the aggregate consumer level, how likely the effects can be identified depends on how confident we are in estimating individual STH. Confidence in measuring individual STH increases with the number of observations for each consumer. Moreover, since our identification relies on within-individual variation of shopping time, that is, we try to relate shopping time (whether following STH or not) to satisfaction, controlling for the same individual, a positive relationship could be by coincidence for a consumer with a small number of observations, while a positive relationship identified for consumers with a large number of observations will give us high confidence that the relationship is not random. Following prior literature (e.g., Li and Hitt 2008), we focus on those consumers with at least 300 observations. The estimation results shown in Column (5) of Table 4 remain consistent. Third, when consumers change their STHs, measurement error could occur, but a shorter time period (t) can alleviate the potential measurement error as we are more likely to capture the change with a shorter t. We test t = “half year” in Column (6) of Table 4, and the results are again consistent. We also note that our results are not sensitive to the cutoff of when period t starts and ends (Appendix D, Table D1) and the criterion x of STH measurement (Appendix D, Table D2). Finally, it may be argued that the boundary of daytime and nighttime is artificial with a onesize-fits-all 6:00 a.m. – 5:59 p.m. as the daytime across all consumers, since different consumers likely have different daytime–nighttime boundaries depending on the nature of internal and external factors. While China has only one timezone, the most eastern and the most western parts of China may experience up to a five-hour difference in terms of the sunrise and sunset time. To address this potential issue, we allow flexible time blocks for each individual STH given their purchase time distributions. We classify each purchase into hour of day, and allow different consumers to have their own boundaries of time blocks. For consumer i, we identify the hour of the day during which the number of her purchases is the highest among the number of purchases in each of the 24 hours of the day in period t-1. We treat this hour of the day with a fluctuation by 6/5/4/3/2 hours as her STH, and then test the effect of following this STH in period t. As indicated in Appendix D, Table D3, again, the results remain consistent.

## Addressing Alternative Explanations and Endogeneity Issues

The previous section described how we estimate the effects of following STH based on a multi-level fixed-effect model. Even though we have controlled for a comprehensive list of factors that can systematically impact satisfaction, the relationship between following STH and satisfaction may still be subject to alternative explanations and/or endogeneity issues. Therefore, we investigate further to see if STH is indeed at play for what we have observed.

First, consumers might purchase what they like or are familiar with during their habitual shopping time and purchase what they are not so sure about during other times; that is, the results may be driven by familiarity or uncertainty of the purchases. We address this concern by separating a consumer’s purchases into two groups: one group consists of product categories that were never purchased in the past prior to the current purchase, indicating that the consumer is unfamiliar with these categories at JD.com, and the other group includes product categories that have been purchased before, indicating that the consumer has some familiarity or preference toward purchasing these product categories at JD.com. Our results show that the observed effects are not driven by familiarity with or uncertainty about the purchases (see Appendix E, Table E1).

Second, we also try to provide evidence on the mechanism of STH. As discussed in the “Theoretical Foundation” section, when following STH, consumers likely make more efficient and effective decisions, leading to higher satisfaction. However, in the case of purchasing the same product that the consumer has purchased before, information processing requirement is minimal, so we should not expect following or not following STH to influence the satisfaction of the same product. To test this, for each consumer, we separate repeat purchases of products the consumer purchased from nonrepeat purchases. The results show that satisfaction is significantly higher when following STH for non-repeat purchases, but there is no significant difference on satisfaction ratings when following STH for repeat purchases of identical products (see Appendix E, Table E1). These findings confirm our expectation drawing upon the habit literature.

Third, it might be possible that the results are driven by certain product categories that consumers tend to purchase at the same time. Although we have included the fixed effect of product category in Equation (2), different product categories may exhibit different shopping patterns and evaluation styles, for example, shopping and reviews for experience goods versus search goods may vary. We thus conduct separate analyses of different product categories. Specifically, we consider clothing, health, food, sports, and bags categories, which represent experience goods and may be more likely subject to impulse purchases, and computer, cellphone, and cameras categories, representing search goods or purchases that may require intense decision making. The results in Appendix E, Table E2, remain highly consistent across these product categories, suggesting that the observed effects are not driven by certain product categories. In addition, we also test a product fixed-effect model, and the results remain robust (see Appendix E, Table E3).

Fourth, there may be consumers who do not exhibit a time habit for online shopping, and, therefore, they are not subject to any effects of habits, given they have no STH. That is, we should not expect any systematic differences on satisfaction due to shopping time differences for these consumers. If any effects were found, it would indicate that what we found was likely due to spurious correlation. Therefore, a study of consumers without STH presents a great test to see if what we found is related to habit or not. We adopted a similar falsification test used in Atasoy et al. (2018) and Xu et al. (2017) to rule out spurious correlation. We first try to identify consumers who do not appear to have STH. We consider a consumer as a no-STH type when the difference in her shopping activities across time blocks is small (we explored different criteria). For these no-STH consumers, if we randomly assign them a “fake STH,” we should not expect to observe any effects if they happen to “follow” that spurious

STH. If any effect is found, it would suggest that what we found was due to spurious correlation and not related to STH. Our results show that, for these no-STH consumers, the “accidental” synchrony of shopping time with the fake STH (i.e., a randomly assigned STH unrelated to habit) does not systematically affect satisfaction ratings (see Appendix $\mathrm { E , }$ Table E4). These results provide assurance that what we found earlier was not due to spurious correlation.

Finally, even after providing evidence of the relationship between following STH and satisfaction and addressing alternative explanations or factors that we could think of, some unknown factors could still exist that might contribute to following STH and satisfaction at the same time. Therefore, we need to account for the possibility of endogeneity issues. We use instrumental variable (IV) methods to address this concern. In particular, we consider two candidate IVs that are likely correlated with following STH but not with the quality of purchase decision (see Appendix E, Table E5). The results suggest that our OLS estimations are unbiased: following STH leads to high satisfaction.

Overall, grounded on habit theory, we are able to provide strong empirical support for the connection of following STH and purchase satisfaction.

## Effect of Following Shopping Time Habit on Interpurchase Interval

## Empirical Specification

Similar to previous studies (e.g., Aflaki and Popescu 2014; Fang et al. 2014; Khalifa and Liu 2007; Kim et al. 2009; Seiders et al. 2005), we take a website perspective instead of a product perspective. We define interpurchase interval as how long it takes a consumer to visit the same website again, regardless of what goes into a shopping cart (consumers may or may not purchase the same items). A major reason for conducting an analysis from the perspective of the website is that a website cares more about how often a consumer visits the site than how often a consumer purchases the same products (Khalifa and Liu 2007), which is more a concern of manufacturers.

To test H3, we reorganize the original dataset (172,825 users and their 11,580,097 purchases) in terms of different shopping “trips” (or shopping orders), and each order may consist of more than one product. The dependent variable Interval is the time interval (in hours) between the $k ^ { t h }$ and $( k { - } 1 ) ^ { t h }$ orders for consumer i. Note that interpurchase interval and shopping frequency both reflect consumers’ repurchase behaviors and represent two sides of the same token, because higher shopping frequency results in shorter interpurchase interval, and vice versa. We chose interpurchase interval as our dependent variable because this measure allows us to use individual shopping trip data, while frequency requires an aggregation of data by time (e.g., number of trips in a month or in a quarter). We log-transformed the variable $I n t e r { \nu } a l _ { i k }$ to achieve normality. The explanatory variable of interest $F o l l o w S T H _ { i ( k - 1 ) }$ indicates whether the shopping time of order k-1 follows consumer $i ^ { \ ' } \mathrm { s } \mathrm { S T H }$ . We establish the following model to test the effect of following STH on interpurchase interval:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \operatorname{LogInterval} _ {i k} & = \boldsymbol {\beta_ {1}} \text {FollowSTH} _ {i (k - 1)} + \beta_ {2} \text {OrderRating} _ {i (k - 1)} \\ & + \beta_ {3} \text {WeekendBuy} _ {i (k - 1)} + \beta_ {4} \text {ProductNum} _ {i k} \\ & + \beta_ {5} \text {ProductNum} _ {i (k - 1)} + \beta_ {6} \text {SaleDay} _ {i k} \\ & + \beta_ {7} \text {SaleDay} _ {i (k - 1)} + \beta_ {8} \text {AvgRating} _ {i (k - 2)} + \beta_ {9} k _ {i} \\ & + B u y Y _ {y (k - 1)} + B u y M _ {m (k - 1)} + B u y H _ {h (k - 1)} + C _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i k} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Control Variables: It is agreed that satisfaction is a crucial determinant of repurchase behavior or intention to revisit a store (Fang et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2009; Voss et al. 2010). For example, previous research found that consumers tend to purchase at a higher rate in a store when they are more satisfied with the last purchase encounter (Ho et al. 2006). Thus, we include $O r d e r R a t i n g _ { i ( k - 1 ) } ,$ the average rating of all products purchased in the $( k { - } 1 ) ^ { t h }$ order, to control for the effect of satisfaction with the most recent order. We also control for $P r o d u c t N u m _ { i ( k - 1 ) } ,$ the number of products purchased in the (k-$1 ) ^ { t h }$ order, to capture the extent to which last need is met. When a consumer purchased many products in the $( k { - } 1 ) ^ { t h }$ order, she may not need to shop again any time soon, compared to when she purchased fewer products. That ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ there is likely a longer wait time to next purchase when a consumer purchased more in the immediately previous order. On the other hand, $P r o d u c t N u m _ { i k }$ captures consumers’ current shopping needs, serving as a proxy about how urgent the current need is. For convenience or to economize on shopping trip/ shipping costs, a consumer may delay purchase until she has accumulated a list of products to purchase, especially when the need for those products is not urgent. In other words, when a consumer identifies something she wishes to purchase, she may either purchase it immediately if she cannot wait, or she can wait to purchase it until the next product need arises. A longer shopping list of products likely is due to non-urgent needs of these products and, therefore, is associated with a longer interpurchase interval. Again, this is something that we have to control for to ensure that our results are not driven by different levels of urgency. Further, a consumer’s overall attitude toward a website can also be affected by all prior experiences (in addition to the most recent purchase). Thus, we control for consumers’ prior experiences with JD.com by including the variable $\begin{array} { r } { A \nu g R a t i n g _ { i ( k - 2 ) } , } \end{array}$ which is the average rating of all products consumer i bought prior to the $( k { - } 1 ) ^ { t h }$ order. The definitions of other control variables in Equation (3) are in line with those in Equation (2) but at the order level.

Table 5 presents the descriptive statistics of variables used in this model.

## Results

H3 posits that following STH is negatively related to the interpurchase interval. The results are summarized in Table 6. As shown in Column (1), the coefficient of $F o l l o w S T H _ { i ( k - 1 ) }$ is significantly negative, suggesting that following STH reduces interpurchase interval. We show that this result did not lead to a break with STH. In fact, it is found that consumers who shop following their STH continue to shop during their habitual shopping time (see Appendix F, Tables F1–F6). The reduced interpurchase interval is a result of increases in purchase rates (therefore reducing average interpurchase time). Specifically, the coefficient suggests a purchase rate increase of almost 8%. Taking the fulfilled orders of 591.2 million in the second quarter of 2017 at JD.com, this coefficient sug gests an increase of 47 million orders, which is very significant economically. We also run several other robustness checks and find virtually identical results (see Columns (2)– (6) of Table 6 and Appendix F, Tables F7–F8). Therefore, H3 is strongly supported.

Overall, the findings regarding consumer satisfaction and interpurchase interval provide significant evidence that following individual STH is beneficial for both consumers and the retailer. Consumers are likely to be more satisfied with their purchases, while the retailer benefits from keeping its regular consumers coming back sooner when consumers shop following their STHs. These findings indicate that “time” should be an important dimension of an effective personalized strategy. For example, online retailers should consider sending promotional materials during a consumer’s habitual shopping time. This innovative use of time comes with little cost but could create significant benefits for retailers and consumers.

## Discussion and Conclusions

Habit is an understudied concept in consumer research (Verplanken 2008), and online shopping habit, particularly concerning time, has received little attention, creating a lost opportunity for improving the welfare of both consumers and retailers without much increase in costs. This research explores the effects of time habits in online shopping, which represents an increasingly important part of the economy. The results reveal that the effects of following time habits are significant and robust for online shopping. Specifically, consumers obtain higher satisfaction and exhibit greater revisit behavior when the timing of shopping follows their STHs.

## Implications, Limitations, and Future Research Directions

## Implications

Our findings provide useful insights to retailers and great potential for innovative use of time in marketing. Our research points out the need for retailers to know individual STH and to act upon it. An e-commerce website first needs to develop an information system to monitor and record activities of consumers in order to infer individual behavioral habit. Next, based on real-time monitoring of individual activities, the website can customize consumer shopping process and help facilitate decision-making. For example, we recommend that any notification about advertising and promotion be pushed to consumers according to individual shopping time habit. The current practice for many retailers is to send mass promotion emails, usually, in the early morning. Customizing communications with customers at the right time can benefit the website immediately from generating higher consumer satisfaction and more frequent patronage by exploiting effects of STHs. In particular, online retailers may cultivate STHs of their consumers in order to allocate resources accordingly.

This paper also makes several contributions to the literature. First and foremost, as far as we know, this study represents the first to investigate the effect of online shopping habits from the time perspective. Second, prior studies have looked at the effect of shopping habits on general consumer satisfaction and repurchase intention based on surveys (Chiu et al. 2012; Khalifa and Liu 2007), while we focused on the effect of shopping habits on satisfaction and repurchase at the individual purchase level through a large-scale data analysis of actual consumer behaviors. Consumer research in domains sensitive to habits should prefer behavioral measures (Herziger and Hoelzl 2017), thus our study contributes to consumer behavior literature by providing empirical supports for the effect of shopping habits on actual behavioral outcomes instead of behavioral intention. Finally, this study also makes a potential contribution in the methodology front for measuring habits and their effects. Our work presents the potential to update knowledge on available measurement for the time effects in online activities. With the popularity of Internet, many activities can be performed “24 hours a day” on the Internet, for example, learning and healthcare. It would be interesting to apply the activity-based method to other fields, where user behaviors across different times of day could be recorded and observed in information systems, and where timing can be utilized as an instrument in a strategy.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

This study represents several opportunities for future work. First, one limitation of this research is that it focused on one retailer, JD.com, which is one of the leading e-commerce websites in China. We believe that significant insights can still be obtained, as many high-impact studies in IS and marketing that also focus on a single website or store (e.g., Forman et al. 2008; Li and Hitt 2008; Mudambi and Schuff 2010). Regardless, it would be interesting to examine if consumers in websites of different natures or different cultures experience the same effects of STHs. We hope our research findings could spur interests in future research in this area.

Another limitation is that, we didn’t have detailed data on product attributes, such as price or discount, at the moment of the purchase encounter. However, we partially account for this by adding time fixed effects, product (category) fixed effects and major promotion controls. We do not expect missing the exact price or discount information at the moment of purchase would bias our results. In order for a product attribute, say discount, to be responsible for our results, it must be that consumers always purchase a discounted product when following their STHs (therefore higher satisfaction) and purchase full price products when deviating from their STHs. However, we do not think this is likely. Even though consumers would want to take advantage of product promotions, promotions can happen any time of the day, which may or may not fall into consumers’ habitual shopping time. However, it would be interesting to examine if product attributes may moderate the relationship. Future efforts in making such data available may improve the estimates derived from this study.

Third, we have examined the effect of following STH on interpurchase interval from a store/website perspective. Future studies may extend this research to test whether following STH affects revisit behavior for different product categories. In addition, existing research found that consumers have various motivations for online shopping (Childers et al. 2001). This study does not distinguish shopping motivation (e.g., instrumental/utilitarian or hedonic), and future studies may explore the moderating effects of shopping motivation on the effects of following STH.

Finally, the factors that drive the formation of STH for an individual are beyond the scope of this study; however, future study may complement our findings by using surveys or lab studies to gain a better understanding on what leads to the formation of time habits in online shopping and how time habits function in the process of purchase decision making. It will also be interesting to extend our research by exploring potential factors or mediating variables that may influence STH.

## STH Measurement

We follow the approach used in Golder and Macy (2011) and Liu-Thompkins and Tam (2013) by dividing a day into time blocks and comparing the behavior frequencies in each time block for each consumer. Figure B1 shows that the distribution of 11,580,097 online purchases across hours of a day has four clear cutoffs at 12:00 a.m., 6:00 a.m., 12:00 p.m., and 6:00 p.m. Accordingly, we explore time blocks over a day involving these times as cutoffs. There is a cost associated with measurement errors and data requirements when having too many time blocks (i.e., each block is short). After considering the tradeoffs among different number of time blocks, we operationalize on the two-block and four block models. In our two-block model, we classify a purchase as the daytime purchase if the purchase time fell within the block from 6:00 a.m. to 5:59 p.m. of a day, and for a purchase that fell outside this time block, we code it as the nighttime purchase. Similarly, the four-block model sorts a purchase into the following four time blocks: 6:00 a.m.–11:59 a.m., 12:00 p.m.–5:59 p.m., 6:00 p.m.–11:59 p.m., and 12:00 a.m.–5:59 a.m. The four-block model and its cutoffs are in line with Golder and Macy’s (2011) four blocks to infer individual posting preference on Twitters. For ease of illustration, we use the two-block model to illustrate our measurement for STH.

Let $n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { D }$ represent the number of daytime purchases of consumer i in period $t { \cdot } 1 ,$ and $n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { N }$ represent the number of nighttime purchases of consumer i in period t-1. Let $n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } = n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { D } + n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { N }$ represent the number of total purchases in period t-1 for consumer i. We infer the consumer’s STH in period t from her purchase activities in period t-1. We use stricter criteria depending on the number of observations a consumer has.

If the number of observations is no more than 15 (i.e., $n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } \leq 1 5 )$ , then consumer i is considered:

• daytime shopper, if $n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { D } - n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { N } \geq 5$

nighttime shopper, if $n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { N } - n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { D } \geq 5$

If the number of observations is greater than 15 $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } n _ { _ { i ( t - 1 ) } } > 1 5 )$ , then we consider consumer i:

• daytime shopper, if $n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { D } \geq x ^ { * } n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { N } ,$ in the main analysis, we set $x = 2 ( { \mathrm { i . e . } }$ , the daytime purchases must be at least two times the nighttime purchases).

• nighttime shopper, if $n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { N } \geq x ^ { * } n _ { i ( t - 1 ) } ^ { D } ,$ , in the main analysis, $x = 2$

Note that this result, however, does not mean that two thirds of consumers have no STH because many consumers (e.g., infrequent shoppers or new consumers) do not have sufficient observations for STH measurement. If we ignore consumers who have fewer than five or ten observations in a year for measuring their STHs, the average shares of consumers with STH represent 56.8% and 60.9%, respectively.

## Treating Online Rating as Ordinal Variable and Binary Variable

## Ordinal Variable and Binary Variable

The rating on a five-point scale has been used as the dependent variable in a linear regression by previous research (e.g., Johnson and Tellis 2008). Many previous empirical studies also showed that treating a scale as cardinal or ordinal makes no practical difference (e.g., Bentler and Chou 1987; Goldfarb and Tucker 2011a, 2011b; Johnson and Creech 1983). Further, Ferrer-i-Carbonell and Frijters (2004) made a comparison between the properties of the ordinary least squares (OLS) model and ordered logit model using consumer satisfaction rating data, and the comparison suggested that assuming ordinality or cardinality of satisfaction rating makes little difference. Acknowledging that satisfaction rating is a five-point scale, we recognize that there is no consensus about whether the scale should be treated as a continuous cardinal measure (Fink 2009). Given computational limitations and “rare events”<sup>9</sup> in our dataset, treating ratings as a cardinal measure may be particularly appro priate for the objective of this study. Still, we try to alleviate the concern that our results could be driven by potential scale bias. In particular, we conduct a number of additional analyses by treating satisfaction rating as an ordinal variable and a binary variable, respectively. We first supplement our earlier analyses using ordered logit/probit formulations. However, estimating an ordered logit/probit model with a large number of consumer fixed effects like ours is infeasible,<sup>10</sup> therefore, it comes at the expense of losing consumer fixed effects. The results of the ordered logit/probit models are reported in Columns (1)–(2) of Table C1.

We also conduct an additional robustness test to reduce the issues surrounding treating Rating<sub>ij</sub> as continuous cardinal. Following prior literature (e.g., Goldfarb and Tucker 2011a, 2011b, 2011c), we convert Rating to a binary variable Rating ${ \underset { - } { D } } $ indicating whether consumer i for purchase j reports the highest satisfaction (i.e., Rating $\underset { - } { D } _ { i j }$ is coded as 1 if Rating = 5, and 0 otherwise), and re-run the full data using a linear model where the coefficients are estimated with ordinary least squares.<sup>11</sup> This enables us to control for 109,637 individual consumer fixed effects using 9,024,790 observations. Moreover, compared with the logit/probit model, inefficiency of the linear model with the binary dependent variable is not a major concern due to the large number of observations (Goldfarb and Tucker 2011b), and the increased com putational efficiency comes at little practical cost (Angrist and Pischke 2009). Column (3) in Table C1 shows the results of the robustness check, and Columns (4)–(5) show the supplementary results of modeling binary-scaled satisfaction ratings using logit/probit specifications without controlling consumer fixed effects due to computational limitations. The results in Table C1 are consistent with our main results in Table 4.

## Aggregating Satisfaction Rating Data at the Quarter Level

We also aggregate data by quarter, and model quarterly average satisfaction as a function of quarterly purchase and review activities. Following prior literature on online ratings at the individual reviewer level (Goes et al. 2014), we constructed a panel dataset such that each unit of observations is a consumer, and each time period is one calendar quarter. The new dataset, therefore, contains quarterly observations about each consumer, including average ratings of purchases that the consumer made in a quarter and purchase/review activities in that quarter. We expect to find that, for a given consumer, the quarterly average rating will be higher when the consumer has a higher percentage of purchases following her STH in that quarter.

For the purchase and review activities of each consumer, we calculate the quarterly percentage share of purchases following individual STH $( F o l l o w S T H _ { i q } )$ as the explanatory variable of interest. We also include a number of control variables, such as the quarterly percentages of night time purchases $( N i g h t B u y _ { i q } )$ , weekend purchases $( W e e k e n d B u y _ { i q } )$ , nighttime reviews $( N i g h t R e \nu i e w _ { i q } )$ , and weekend reviews $( W e e k e n d R e \nu i e w _ { i q } ) .$ the quarterly average length of review texts $( T x t L t h _ { i q } ) _ { }$ , the quarterly average days between purchase time and review time $( D i f f D a y _ { i q } ) _ { \it }$ , as well as the quarterly percentages of purchases of repeat items $( R e p e a t _ { i q } )$ , and purchases and reviews on promotion days $( S a l e D a y _ { i q }$ and $R e \nu i e w S a l e D a y _ { i q } )$ . We also control for the percentage of reviews following individual time habit for review in that quarter $( F o l l o w R T H _ { i q } ) .$ Consequently, the new dataset includes a total of 109,637 unique consumers and their $^ { 6 2 5 , 3 1 8 }$ quarterly observations. Results presented in Column (1) of Table C2 provide the same conclusion: following STH leads to high satisfaction.

Further, it may not be meaningful to calculate the average rating when there is only one rating in a quarter (Goes et al. 2014; Li and Hitt 2008). Therefore, we examine the robustness of the results using different minimum numbers of ratings to calculate quarterly average ratings. Columns (2) and (3) of Table C2 present the results when a consumer has at least two or three ratings in that quarter. In addition, we also assess the robustness of quarterly results using different criteria to select a more meaningful set of consumers for our analyses. For example, we have considered the consumers who have at least a number of quarters of data (e.g., at least three or five quarters, see Columns (4) and (5) in Table C2). Our results across different specifications and data requirements, once again, are consistent with our expectation.

## Addressing Alternative Explanations and Endogeneity Issues

## Addressing Potential Effects of Familiar Purchases

To address the concern that the results may be driven by familiarity or uncertainty of the purchases, for a consumer, we separate her purchases into two groups: one group consists of product categories that she never purchased in the past prior to the current purchase (i.e., new product categories), indicating that the consumer is unfamiliar with these categories at JD.com, and the other group includes product categories that she has purchased prior to the current purchase (i.e., familiar product categories), indicating that the consumer has some familiarity or/and preference toward purchasing these product categories at JD.com. As an additional test of the mechanism, we also try an alternative separation: repeat purchases of products consumers have made before and non-repeat purchases (i.e., new products). Studying purchases of the same products that a consumer has bought before not only helps us test if results are driven by repeat purchases of the same products but also helps us understand the mechanism. Since repeat purchases of the same products require minimal information processing, we should not expect following STH or not to make a significant difference on purchase satisfaction. The results are summarized in Table E1. Overall, our results show consistent findings for new product categories (Column (1)), familiar product categories (Column (2)), as well as for new products (Column (4)), suggesting that the observed relationship is not driven by familiarity/uncertainty of the purchases. On the other hand, following STH does not have a significant impact on satisfaction of repeat purchases of the same products (Column (3)). This is reinforcing because fo identical product purchases, consumers do not have to gather and process information, and therefore we do not really expect satisfaction to be different across different shopping times. In other words, consumers would be able to formulate the right expectation for product quality of repeat purchases based on prior direct experiences, and shopping time would not make a difference because no-additional information needs to be processed. Overall, our findings suggest that when certain information processing is needed, a purchase decision following STH is associated with high purchase satisfaction.

## Ruling Out Spurious Correlation

We try to identify consumers who do not appear to exhibit STH based on their purchase activities across the whole data observation window. We explore different criteria to identify such consumers. In Columns (1) and (2) of Table E4, we define a consumer as no-STH type when the absolute difference between the number of all her daytime purchases and that of all her nighttime purchases is smaller than five (or ten as a robustness test) across all years, and for Columns (3) and (4) of Table E4, we identify no-STH consumers when the difference of the two time blocks is less than 10% (15%) across all years. We adopted the same falsification test that had been used in Atasoy et al. (2018) and Xu et al. (2017) to rule out spurious correlation. Specifically, for these no-STH consumers, if we randomly assign them a spurious STH, we should not expect to find any effects. If any effect is found, it would suggest that what we found was due to spurious correlation and not related to habit. We run Equation (2) for consumers with no-STHs, and the results show that coefficients of FollowSTH are not significantly different from zero regardless of the criteria we use to identify no-STH customers.

Overall, the results support our arguments that for no-STH consumers, the “accidental” synchrony of shopping time with the spurious STH (i.e., a randomly assigned STH unrelated to habit) does not systematically affect satisfaction ratings. These results provide assurance that what we found earlier was not due to spurious correlation.

## Addressing Other Unobserved Endogeneity Issues Using Instrumental Variables

Our IVs are whether shopping time follows RTH (review time habit). The logic is that the time arrangements for different tasks are inter-related, given a consumer having a daily routine. Any exogenous shocks that affect a consumer’s shopping times likely also affect her review times. While we expect that when consumers follow STH, they likely make better decisions due to the presence of a cue-behavior link related to shopping, we do not expect shopping time following RTH to have a direct impact on the decisions. This justifies shopping time following RTH to be a valid IV. Note that we have also controlled for the time fixed effects of shopping and the time fixed effects of reviews on ratings (to account for the possibility that consumers give higher or lower ratings at certain times). We construct two IVs: IV1 is whether shopping time in period t follows RTH derived from period t-1, and IV2 is whether shopping time in period t follows RTH derived from period t, with yes = 1 and no = 0.

Table E5 presents the OLS and two-stage least square (2SLS) results. The coefficients on FollowSTH with IVs regressions using 2SLS (Columns (2) and (3)) are very similar to that in the OLS estimation (Column (1)). Our instrument set passes the usual tests for underidentification and weak instruments (i.e., the Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic is far larger than the Stock-Yogo critical value for the 10% maximal IV size, Stock and Yogo 2002). In addition, in Column (3) we have two instruments but only one endogenous variable, and thus we can conduct tests of over identification restrictions (the Sargan test) and show that the instruments as a group are valid. For both Column (2) and Column (3), Hausman tests fail to reject the null hypothesis that the OLS and 2SLS coefficients are the same, suggesting there is no significant concern of any biases due to reverse causality or other forms of endogeneity. Columns (4) and (5) show that coefficients of our instruments are insignificant, providing evidences that our instruments are not correlated with the error term å in Equation (2).<sup>12</sup> In summary, these tests suggest that the OLS estimations of our multi-way fixed effects model are unbiased, and thus our main results are reliable: following STH leads to high satisfaction.

## Appendix F

## Does Reduced Interpurchase Interval Break STH?

The “Effect of Following Shopping Time Habit on Interpurchase Interval” section shows that following STH reduces the interpurchase interval between the current and next shopping, but would the reduced interpurchase interval break the STH of a consumer, which may mean that STH is not stable? We investigate if the reduced interval leads the next shopping to deviate from STH. The results in Tables F1–F3 show that next shopping is more likely to follow a consumer’s STH when the interpurchase interval is short than when it is long. On the other hand, habit theory suggests that a habit can be reinforced by repeated responses in a recurring context. When a consumer made an order during her habitual shopping time, she may experience the recurring context again, and in turn her STH would become stronger. That is, next shopping of a consumer is more likely to follow her STH when the current shopping is done following her STH than deviating from it. As expected, the results in Tables F4–F6 report a significantly positive relationship between the current and next shopping in terms of following STH. These findings provide further support for the stability of STH.

## Alternative Measurement of Shopping Outcome: Purchase Quantity

As discussed previously in the current study, following time habit can reduce cognitive efforts and increase efficiency. Therefore, when a consumer shops during her habitual shopping time, she is able to react efficiently for searching, comparing, choosing, evaluating products, etc., with minimal cognitive effort, and in turn she may have more opportunities to reach more products. If the impact of STH does really exist, we also should observe that a consumer would purchase more products when she follows her STH.
