## ABSTRACT

Technology has significantly changed the way humans live. Information and communication technologies have advanced and given their important spillovers to other economic sectors.

#

Sociocultural view of globalization posits that cultural values across the world are continuously changing. From the theoretical lens of technological determinism theory, technology of a society determines the development of its social structure and cultural values. Drawing on the sociocultural aspect of globalization and technological determinism theory, this study investigates the relationship between technology and national cultural values. Using secondary national-level data such as United Nations’ E-Government Survey and Global Innovation Index, we validate the proposed relationships. The results of the study indicate that technology is an important driver of cultural convergence pushing national cultures across the globe to converge toward two cultural values suggested by Hofstede: higher individualism and lower power distance. This study also finds that technology increases national confidence in financial markets through creation of increased transparency. The study has implications for policymakers at both national and global levels regarding how technology affects the evolution of human societies. Additional implications exist for the promotion of democracy.

Keywords: Information and communication technologies (ICTs), National culture, Globalization, Technology adoption, Technological innovation, Technological determinism theory.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Technology has significantly changed the way individuals perceive the world, how they relate one another, and how they live. Murphie and Potts, in their book of culture and technology [1], provide several examples of how technology affects human life. New technologies of transportation led tourism to become one of the world’s largest industries in the twentieth century [2]. New technologies in construction and communication let sports to gain significant popularity. The Olympics and the FIFA World Cup are now considered to be the world’s largest cultural events which were not possible without huge stadiums and extensive broadcasting technologies [3].

Perhaps information and communication technologies (ICTs) <sup>1</sup> have been the most influential technologies in recent years. ICTs have significantly affected how people are living. Smart phones continue to out-sell traditional phones and allow people to have access to a wide variety of information wherever they go. More people are watching movies online which allows them to choose from a large list of movies and television shows. E-books and E-readers are also rapidly replacing traditionally bound books. Email allows more people to connect via this information media at tremendous speeds formerly not imagined. Through the Social Web, dating websites are facilitating interpersonal relationships and challenging traditional modes of match-making. Politicians too are becoming savvier at campaigning with the help of ICTs. During the past two United States presidential elections, candidates made extensive use of social networking services such as Facebook and Twitter to solicit more votes [4, 5].

One constant assumption behind most cultural studies in the area of information systems (IS) is that culture remains constant and doesn’t change [6]. Although cultures are largely stable in the short-term [7], cultural change does occur as a result of a diverse set of factors such as immigration, the global media, and other social and technological trends [6, 8, 9]. Technological determinism theory (TDT) posits that a society's technology determines the development of its social structure and cultural values [10]. For example, the internet has been recently utilized by people of Middle Eastern countries as a tool for political reform. The reformist movements that occurred from 2009 through 2013 in the Middle East illustrate the power of ICTs in changing the structures, values, and perhaps culture of nations. Fukuyama [11] advances traditional theories by suggesting that, in combination with a global capitalist ideology, technology has a significant homogenizing impact across cultures. This is also known as Cultural Convergence [12].

Previous research has extensively examined the effect of ICTs on culture at the organizationallevel [e.g., 13, 14, 15]. The situation is different at the national level. Few studies investigate the effect of technology on national culture and they remain either limited to a single country [e.g., 16] or compare only a few countries [e.g., 17]. The only exception, to best of our knowledge, is a recent study of 160 countries which shows that ICTs help nations improve their well-being [18]. Nevertheless, the study does not attempt to measure the effect of ICTs on dimensions of national culture proposed by Hofstede [19, 20]. Since the complexity of interplay between culture and ICTs

#

remains largely unexplored [21], a comprehensive study on the effect of ICTs on national culture using valid and reliable data from several countries will make a significant contribution to both theory and practice. Such a study should consider different cultural and technological dimensions and show how these variables interact at the national level. From this perspective, technology should be observed as a social rather than a physical entity and social structure should be viewed as a process rather than an object [15]. Technology is a social entity because it carries certain values and norms of behavior [22]. For example, unlike most human interactions, it is impossible to negotiate with an automated ticket verification system. Social structure, on the other hand, is the result of ongoing interactions between people and the environment within which they live, which in turn, constraints individual’s behavior [23]. Based on the above arguments regarding the current role of ICTs in human societies, we arrive at our first research question: How do ICTs affect cultural values of nations?

On the other hand, ICTs have continually advanced and given important spillovers to other economic sector [24-26]. Despite the numerous studies on role of ICTs at individual and organizational levels [24], empirical research on the role of ICTs at the country-level remains limited. As a result, we propose our second research question: How do ICTs affect the technological development of nations?

This study aims to answer the above research questions by analyzing secondary data about culture and technology and by explaining how ICTs affect national cultural values around the world. This study has three primary objectives: (1) to propose a research model explaining the effect of ICTs on cultural values as well as their effect on technological development at the national level, (2) to empirically test the proposed model using secondary national-level data, and (3) to enhance scholarship by providing insights concerning the socio-technological impacts of ICTs and their implications.

The remaining parts of this paper are structured as follows: First, we review the theoretical background of the study. Then, we propose a research model and 8 hypotheses. Later, we present the research methodology including our sources of secondary data and measurement items. We also present the results of data analysis. Finally, we discuss the findings and implications of the study along with limitations and further research directions.

## 2 THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

Gallivan and Srite [6] identify four categories of cultural studies in the IS literature: (1) ICT adoption and implementation, (2) international ICT diffusion, (3) comparison of knowledge workers and human resource practices in different countries, and (4) ICT-related beliefs and practices of senior managers. While IS-culture studies can be categorized in the second group as international IT diffusion, IT diffusion is just one type of research on the relationship between IS and culture. Leidner and Kayworth [27] classify six themes of research on IS and culture: (1) culture and IS development, (2) culture, ICT adoption and diffusion, (3) culture, ICT use and outcomes, (4) culture, ICT management and strategy, (5) ICTs’ influence on culture, and (6) ICT culture. Research on culture in information systems has been done at both organizational and national levels. The fifth theme, ICTs’ influence on culture, is a relatively understudied area at both national and organizational levels [27] and requires more research.

While many countries are currently extending their ICT capabilities with increasing economic and social consequences, cultural impacts of ICTs have not received sufficient attention from academics. Most IS-culture studies view culture as a constant and unchangeable phenomenon [6, 8]. As a result, most IS-cultural studies have a ‘rational actor’ view [28] to the relationship between ICTs and culture focusing on how the independent variable, culture, is affecting the dependent variable that is technology. For example, the effect of national culture on ICT adoption and diffusion has received significant attention from scholars [29-39]. National culture is considered as a moderating variable in most IT adoption and diffusion studies [40]. A recent study suggest that technology-induced anxiety mediates the relationship between national culture and technology adoption [41]. Ford et al. suggest that culture can be also seen as a control variable in IS-culture studies [8].

Although culture is rather stable, it is not completely constant. Cross-cultural interaction in forms of exposure to international media, international political and economic competition, cross-border commerce, or other forms can result in cultural change [42]. Most previous studies have a ‘rational actor’ approach to understanding the relationship between culture and ICTs. According to the rational perspective, people and organizations evaluate alternatives and make rational choices. This is true in the case of technology adoption where culture determines technology adoption [29- 39]. The emergent view [28], in contrast, posits that the behavior of people is the result of dynamic

#

interaction between external factors and internal motives [43]. A large number of previous studies show how ICTs shape the behavior of their users respectively [e.g., 22, 44, 45]. Hence, we believe that an emergent view is more appropriate in studying the relationship between ICTs and culture.

## Technology

ICTs are an important part of every country’s national infrastructure. Different scales have been developed to measure ICTs at the national level. The Global Competitiveness Report 2011-2012 uses technological readiness as a measure of competetiveness of nations [26]. Technological readiness refers to the speed with which an economy utilizes existing technologies to improve the productivity of its industries, with specific emphasis on utilization of ICTs in daily activities and production processes to achieve increased efficiency and competitiveness [26]. The report posits that use of and access to ICTs is a key enabler of countries’ overall technological readiness becuase ICTs are the general purpose technology of the time. Technological readiness is composed of technology infrastructure and information technology (IT) human resources. IT human resources refer to IT specialists possessing the required knowledge and skills to implement Internet-related applications [46]. According to the Global Competitiveness Report 2011-2012, Technological Readiness has two dimensions: ICT infrastructure and technological adoption. ICT infrastructure consists of “the telecommunications, the Internet, broadcasting and other networks through which information is transmitted, stored and delivered” [47, p. 3]. The United Nations E-government Survey [48] defines the ICT Infrastructure Index as an arithmetic average of five indicators (Internet users, fixed telephone lines, mobile subscribers, fixed Internet users, and fixed broadband facilities) per 100 inhabitants. Technology adoption is another important technological index which refers to the availability of the latest technologies in a country and the ability of the country to absorb the available technologies. The Global Competitiveness Report 2011-2012 measures technology adoption as the average of three indexes: availability of latest technologies, firm-level technology absorption, and foreign direct investment in technology and technology transfer [26].

Technological innovation is another important technological measure. In fact, it is a vital determinant of economic growth of developed countries. Although considerable gains can be achieved by improving organizations, developing infrastructures, reducing macroeconomic instability, or improving the human capital of a society, all these factors eventually lead to diminishing returns. The same is valid about efficiency of labor and financial and commodity markets. In the long run, living standards can only be improved with the aid of technological innovation. Innovation is especially important for economies that are close to the frontiers of knowledge and integration of existing technologies [49]. Durbhakula and Kim [46] define technological innovation as the capacity of a country and its environment to conduct innovation in both public and private sectors. The main driver for technological innovation is the needs of a nation to increase productivity through newly created technologies. Innovation is an iterative process derived by the perception of a new opportunity for a technology-based invention [50] Innovation is uncertain, complicated, disorderly, and bound by changes of many sorts. Innovation requires close coordination of sufficient technical knowledge and outstanding market judgment in order to fulfill economic, technological, and other types of constraints simultaneously [51].

The Global Competitiveness Report [26] considers innovation as an important determinant of competitive advantage of countries. The Global Innovation Index [52] is an outstanding measure of innovation which classifies innovation-related factors into two broad categories: innovation inputs and innovation outputs. Innovation input is composed of institutional factors (political environment, regulatory environment, and business environment), human capital and research (education, tertiary education, and research and development), infrastructure (ICTs, general infrastructure, and ecological sustainability), market sophistication (credit, investment, and trade and competition), and business sophistication (knowledge workers, innovation linkages, and knowledge absorption). Innovation output is composed of two pillars: knowledge and technology outputs (knowledge creation, knowledge impact, and knowledge diffusion) and creative outputs (creative intangibles, creative goods and services, and online creativity). Different countries have different innovative performances based on the level of their inputs and outputs.

## National Culture and Social Capital

Hofstede defines culture as “the collective programming of mind which distinguishes the members of one human group from another” [53, p. 21]. He also believes that culture encompasses systems of values and “values are among the building blocks of culture” [53, p. 21].

Multiple frameworks have been developed to measure culture at the national level. The most widely used measure of national culture has been developed by Hofstede [54] which identifies five dimensions of national culture: individualism / collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, masculinity /

#

femininity, power distance, and short term / long term orientation. These dimensions have been used by hundreds of academic studies to justify the differences between nations. Individualism / collectivism dimension projects the culture on a bipolar continuum. The individualism pole is commonly defined as “a situation in which people are supposed to look after themselves and their immediate family only”, while the collectivism pole is defined as “a situation in which people belong to in-groups or collectivities which are supposed to look after them in exchange for loyalty” [55, p. 419]. Power distance is defined as the perceived degree of equal distribution of power by less powerful members of a group [55]. Uncertainty avoidance measures the level of tolerance in the members of a group for ambiguous situations [55]. The fourth dimension, masculinity, refers to “a situation in which the dominant values in society are success, money, and things” while femininity refers to “a situation in which the dominant values in society are caring for others and the quality of life” [55, p. 420]. Finally, short term / long term orientation dimension refers to people’s choice of focus which can be either future or present [54].

Global leadership and organizational behavior effectiveness (GLOBE) is another framework of culture. Many social science and management scholars across the world currently cooperate in GLOBE to study societal culture, organizational culture, and organizational leadership in 61 countries. The project is looking for globally accepted leadership attributes, differences in leadership style in different cultures, and the standing of different countries on each of the cultural dimensions [42]. The GLOBE Project identifies nine cultural dimensions: 1) uncertainty avoidance, 2) power distance, 3) societal collectivism, 4) in-group collectivism, 5) gender egalitarianism, 6) assertiveness, 7) future orientation, 8) performance orientation, and 9) human orientation. The first six dimensions have their roots in Hofstede’s cultural dimensions [56, 57]. The uncertainty avoidance and power distance dimensions are very similar to that of Hofstede. GLOBE has split the masculinity dimension of Hofstede in two separate dimensions: gender egalitarianism and assertiveness. For more information on the definition of the constructs refer to House et al. [57].

World Values Survey (WVS) is another prominent cultural framework studying 97 societies including 90 percent of the world’s population since 1981. The project investigates the proposition that mass belief systems are evolving and this evolution has significant economic, political, and social consequences [58]. Six waves of data collection have been conducted between 1981 and

2014. Wave 6, which was conducted between 2010 and 2014, includes 60 countries [59]. Data collection for wave 7 is currently underway and is expected to be completed by the end of 2018 [60].

Inkeles believes that Hofstede’s dimensions can be seen as a special category of social capital which he calls ”popular attitudes and values” [61, p. 254]. Social capital refers to collection of different entities which have two elements in common: they all contain some form of social structure and they direct actions of actors. It lies in the structure of relations among people. Similar to human and physical capital, social capital can be valuable too. The level of trust derived from social capital within a group can be an important determinant of productivity. Social capital may root in different entities such as family and religious ties, social relations, access to resources, and professional credibility [62]. Since social capital exists in different layers of a society and considering its important impact on social relations, it should be considered as an important part of value structure of a society.

According to Coleman [62], there are three forms of social capital: ”obligations and expectations, information flow capability of the structure, and norms accompanied by sanctions” (p. 119). Obligations and expectations are based on the trustworthiness of the structures. Certain institutions won’t exist without trust. Information-flow also affects social capital because information provides a basis for action. Sanctions also increase social capital by providing warranty that violators will be penalized.

## The Effect of Technology on Culture

Technological determinism theory discusses the effect of technology on societies. The theory posits that the technology of a society determines the development of its social structure and cultural values [10]. The theory can also be categorized as a theory offering an economic interpretation of history. In economics, this is seen as a 'technology-push' theory which opposes the 'demand-pull' perspective [63]. The main question behind this theory is: “Do machines make history?” [64]. The theory can be traced back to Karl Marx [65, p.119] who wrote that “The windmill gives you society with the feudal lord: the steam-mill, society with the industrial capitalist”. Perhaps the most prominent example of machines making history is the transition of human societies from feudalism to capitalism with the upsurge of a new level of technological capability. Fukuyama [11] provides a more contemporary example, stating that when it is used for military purposes, technology provides developed countries significant tools to export their culture and values to nations with less strength. Technological determinists believe that societies are experiencing dramatic increases in technology dependence and this worries them [66]. For example, recent research shows that technology addiction has dramatic effects on human brain similar to that of substance addiction [45, 67, 68]. Furthermore, technology has the potential to change how humans process information and it can prevent them from thinking deeply [44].

There are two primary approaches to technological determinism. Hard determinism assumes that technology develops independent of social concern. This view looks at technology as an autonomous, independent structural factor, which strictly follows its innate natural laws of constant progress and higher efficiency and leaves almost no possibility for human agency unless within the tightly defined limits set by technology’s requirements [64]. Soft determinism posits that although technology is the main driver of cultural evolution, human beings still have the chance to affect this driving force and make decisions [10] . This particular view of technology suggests that technology is never value-neutral and it always supports the interests of certain social groups, while overlooking the interests or rights of others [69]. On the other hand, the constructionist view of technology, which was developed in response to technological determinism, “treats technology as a social construction that interacts with other social forces rather than as an autonomous entity with its own unique rationality” [70, p. 89].

## Globalization

Over the past three decades, the concept of globalization has attracted significant attention. Despite the recent attentions, the process of globalization has been going on over the past 5000 years since international trade started [71]. Globalization is a multi-dimensional concept and should not be constrained to a single discipline. Multiple and diverse disciplines, particularly economics and sociology, examine varied aspects of globalization. Holm and Sorensen believe that globalization lays at “the intensification of economic, political, social, and cultural relations across borders” [72, p. 1]. Globalization is highly related to global integration. From an economical point of view, Dicken believes that globalization is a more complex form of internationalization which implies a degree of functional integration between national economies [73].

Sociocultural globalization is a vital component of globalization. Featherstone suggests that globalization makes diverse cultures become integrated into a dominant one which will eventually entail the whole world [74]. After the transition of societies from feudalism to capitalism as a result of the industrial revolution, we have recently seen that technologically-developed countries have started exporting their values to less-developed countries. This effect is known as Americanization or Westernization. The high penetration rate of American brands such as McDonalds and Coca Cola across the world is a good example of Americanization. Cultural globalization and Americanization are not the same phenomena and globalization is not a unidirectional phenomenon [75]. Today, cuisine from around the world can be found in most cities in the U.S. Globalization is necessarily accompanied with transformation [76] and sociocultural globalization is about transformation of different cultures into an integrated global culture. This is also called cultural convergence. Different factors such as immigration and trade have been seen as drivers of cultural convergence [12]. Technology has also been suggested to have a significant homogenizing impact across cultures [11].

Hence, we suggest that ICTs have a great potential to shape culture and values of nations. Moreover, we are interested in investigating the role of ICTs in sociocultural globalization. ## The Relationship between Technology and Culture

Technological determinism theory posits that the technology of a society determines the development of its social structure and cultural values [10]. Lianos and Douglas [22] discuss different ways through which automated systems change culture and values of societies. First, it is impossible to negotiate with an automated system. This shifts culture toward a “non-negotiative context of interaction.” Second, high reliability of automated systems as well as their focus on discrete aspects of the world undermine the social processes of value reproduction and reinforcement. Digital systems carry what we call digital values. To a ticket scanning system, there are two types of people: ticket holders and non-ticket holders. Such a systems is not concerned about how the user is dressed or behaves. Third, automated systems do not discriminate based on age, race, gender, or any other category of social division. All automated systems are concerned about is the quality of the person as a user. Hence, automated systems facilitate the transition toward an egalitarian society. Finally, automated systems are neutral; they, unlike humans, do not exert power although they show preference for specific relationships of power. Automated systems are used to eliminate the aspects of social interaction that prevent organizations from achieving their goals.

Most programming languages, such as C, C++, C#, VB, Java, and Python, use English words in their syntax. Other ICTs, such as databases, computer networks, and World Wide Web, use terminology based on English words. Hence, some degree of knowledge of English is required for ICT professionals. It can be argued that ICTs have contributed to what Phillipson [78] calls ”the linguistic imperialism of neoliberal empire,” which, in turn, has helped Anglophone societies advance their competitive advantage over other nations [79]. Moreover, considering the high degree to which language and culture are interwoven [80], it can be argued that ICTs have facilitated the export of Anglophone cultures to the rest of the world.

The Internet is another important tool for cultural change. The most recent example of the effect of technology on social values can be seen in the recent rise of Middle-Eastern people who extensively used ICTs to conduct political reform in their countries. Based on the above examples and building upon technological determinism theory, we propose that technology has a significant effect on national culture and social capital of nations. Hence, in pursuit of answers to our first research question, we suggest the following proposition:

Proposition 1: Technology drives social and cultural change.

Price transparency is an important aspect of financial markets. It is defined as "the degree to which market participants know the prevailing prices and characteristics or attributes of goods or services on offer" [81]. Use of IT-enabled mechanisms is an important facilitator of the increasing price transparency in electronic markets [81]. Mechanisms such as electronic catalogs with posted prices, electronic price negotiation, and electronic forward or reverse auctions can significantly increase transparency in financial markets [82]. This increased transparency can lead to higher levels of trust in financial institutions. One possible explanation is that trustworthiness and information are highly related. Social capital is highly dependent on trustworthiness. Information is an important determinant of social capital because it provides a basis for action [62]. Higher price transparency implies more accurate financial information and is therefore associated with lower perceptions about price manipulation and fraudulent behavior within markets. It assures traders that they are paying the right price for what they are buying. It can be associated with higher trust in the financial institutions which are key players in the financial market and provide financial services to customers. Moreover, information revelation through means of ICTs helps organizations to display greater openness, accountability, and transparency which benefit both the corporations and their stakeholders [83]. Higher levels of national technological adoption are generally associated with more use of technology in the financial sector and consequently more transparency in the sector. Since increased transparency in the financial sector tends to be associated with more trust in the sector, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1 - National technological adoption is positively associated with national financial market trustworthiness and confidence.

Technology has constantly helped people avoid uncertainty. The current technological level of human societies has removed many uncertainties people used to face. Extensive vaccination has removed the threat of many wide-spread diseases while machine learning has helped physicians in detecting diseases [e.g., 84, 85, 86]. Human-made dams prevent or reduce the threat of flooding in

#

many areas of the world. The parallel design in engineering systems creates high reliability that allows the systems to continue working even if one part malfunctions. As a result of extensive technological adoption, every day, people face less uncertainty and continue to become more familiar and comfortable with the high level of certainty brought to them by technology. As people are constantly exposed to less uncertainty, they become more unfamiliar with uncertain situations. It can be argued that not being exposed to uncertain situations also prevents people from developing uncertainty handling skills.

Although technology allows people to avoid uncertain situations, introduction of new technologies exposes individuals to uncertainty derived from unfamiliarity with the new technologies [87-89]. Moreover, some ICTs such as social media and e-commerce have created concerns regarding privacy and security of users [90, 91] although uncertainty associated with security and privacy concerns has not stopped millions of individuals from using online services. The adoption phase of new technologies is relatively short and, in many cases, the benefits outweigh the difficulties associated with technology adoption. Hence, it can be argued that the net effect of technology on uncertainty avoidance is positive. Thus we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2 - National technological adoption is positively associated with uncertainty avoidance dimension of national culture.

The individualism / collectivism dimension of Hofstede’s framework discusses the relationship between the individual and the collectivity in a particular society. Western individualism lies at one end of the continuum which forms a clear distinction between an individual as a separate entity and the setting in which the individual lives. At the other end of the continuum, the distinction between the individual and the group is unclear and people have a self-concept that embraces their social and cultural environment [92].

Computer-mediated communications encourage higher levels of personalization by providing individuals with more control over the source, time, and location of messages [93]. Broadband communications allow rapid exchange of large amounts of data and allow people to be always connected. Wireless connectivity allows people to connect from more remote locations. Recommender agents create personalized content for users [94]. Computer-mediated communications tend to be a great fit to individual preferences and needs, leading to a more

#

individualized way of interacting and a way of mobilizing fluid networks of partial commitment [93]. Since ICT infrastructure creates the basis for computer-mediated communications, we argue that development of ICT infrastructure facilitates higher levels of personalization.

Higher levels of personalization help individuals to make a more clear distinction between themselves as a separate entity, and the context in which they live. This distinction leans toward more individualism because it creates a bolder border between a person and society. On the other hand, some ICTs, such as social media, allow collective action toward certain goals which can be seen in the case of the Arab Spring. Nonetheless, it is the individual that decides in which actions he/she wants to take part and with whom he/she wants to communicate. Thus, development of ICT infrastructure allows individuals to improve personalization of their sources of communication which would ultimately lead to higher levels of individualism in a society. Accordingly, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 3 – National ICT infrastructure is positively associated with the individualism dimension of national culture.

Lack of information significantly contributes to uncertainty. Information decreases uncertainty by shedding light on the ambiguous. For example, when a person is lost, a built-in GPS on the person’s cellphone can significantly decrease the time it takes to solve the problem by displaying the location and route to the person’s destination. ICT infrastructure is the backbone for information technology. Having access to reliable high-speed ICT infrastructure, which provides people with real time information anywhere and anytime, is a good way to reduce uncertainty. As ICTs grow, they provide people with more information and decrease uncertainty. Every day, people face less uncertainty and continue to become more familiar and comfortable with the high level of certainty brought to them by technology. Free and instant access to information assists individuals to easily reduce the uncertainty related to information asymmetry. Facing uncertainty can be seen as an uncertainty handling practice. When people face high levels of uncertainty in their daily lives, they will adjust to it and tend to find different ways to handle uncertain situations. When level of uncertainty decreases, so will the uncertainty handling skills. In the long run, this can decrease the tolerance of people for uncertainty. Thus, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 4 – National ICT infrastructure is positively associated with uncertainty avoidance dimension of national culture.

Power distance is concerned with the degree of inequality between a less powerful and a more powerful person [92]. In organizations, this concept tends to be seen as a superior-subordinate relationship [92] and hierarchy [95]. At the national level, countries with higher levels of power distance are characterized with attributes such as authority, centralized decision structures, and use of formal rules [95]. In fact, power distance is about the power gap between different levels of the hierarchy.

Power in all its forms is derived from resources [96]. Access to information is an important source of power [97]. Power is not only about access to information but also about having control over information. High power distance countries like China and North Korea strive to have control over information. The most effective way for people at lower levels of the hierarchy to achieve power is “to obtain, maintain, and control access to persons, information, and instrumentalities” [98]. IS literature shows that ICTs have the potential to significantly affect power structures through changing the balance of information [99]. We argue that ICTs diminish the power gap in two distinct ways: (1) by decreasing information asymmetry through free and rapid dissemination of information for everyone, and (2) by preventing individuals with higher levels of power from controlling information. Wikipedia is a good example of the power of ICTs to distribute power through decentralization [100]. High levels of information and communication technology adoption in a country allows its people to have improved access to information and prevents the control of information by a limited group of people. This will lead to a decreased information gap and consequently a decreased power gap. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5 – National ICT infrastructure is negatively associated with power distance dimension of national culture.

Cultures with a long-term orientation are described by values such as persistence, adaptations of traditions to new circumstances, perseverance toward slow outcomes and the idea that most important events in life will come in the future. In contrast, cultures with a short-term orientation place emphasis on respect for past and tradition, personal stability, a reciprocation of favors and gifts, and tend to expect quick results [54]. Most consumer technologies offer instant gratification to their users [101] which is seen at odds with long term orientation [102]. Millennials, who are also more tech savvy than previous generations [103], are particularly considered to favor short term gratification [104]. It can be argued that the Millennials’ preference for instant gratification is partly because they have become accustomed to receiving instant gratification from consumer technologies, such as video games, since their childhood. Consequently, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 6 - National technological adoption is negatively associated with long-term orientation dimension of national culture.

## Relationship between ICT Infrastructure and Technology Development

Although previous research shows the benefits of ICTs to developed countries, recent research suggests that developing countries can also gain economic benefits from advanced ICTs [105]. Today, ICTs are seen as inseparable parts of every organization. Previous research shows that ICTs can have a significant impact on firm’s performance [106-108] and even lead to a sustainable competitive advantage [109]. Considering the previous research on the effect of ICTs on firm performance and the role of ICTs as the mother technology, we believe that ICTs act as the driving force of other technologies and lead the technological development of nations. Considering the above arguments and in pursuit to answering our second research question, we suggest the following proposition:

## Proposition 2: Information and communication technologies drive technology development.

Today, ICTs help countries to absorb more technology. There are several reasons for this argument. First, ICTs act as a vehicle for identifying new technologies. With a good ICT infrastructure, diverse areas around the world can follow the latest technological trends and learn about the latest technological innovations. Therefore, better ICT infrastructure in a country is correlated with better understanding among that country’s people regarding the latest technological advancements. This is a big step toward absorbing new technologies. Second, training is always required when an organization needs to absorb a new technology. ICTs can facilitate training by helping people to search and download required material and by allowing them to attend online classes and take online exams. Considering the above arguments, one can expect ICT infrastructure to have a positive association with technological adoption. Hence we propose that:

#

Hypothesis 7 - National ICT infrastructure is positively associated with national technological adoption rate.

ICTs also stimulate technological innovation of nations. To be innovative, diverse tools and resources are required. Access to information is an excellent source of innovation by allowing people to learn about past discoveries and preventing them from reinventing the wheel. ICTs also provide people with both knowledge and education which are vital for innovation. Moreover, ICTs also allow networks to be created that enhance the spread of ideas, receipt of feedback, and the knowledge of cutting-edge concepts and theories. Research on organizational innovation shows that technology is an important predictor of innovation in organizations [110]. ICT infrastructure can also act as a means of spreading innovation and encourage the creation of businesses. Today, a single innovative person can sit behind his/her computer, write a small application, upload it on a website such as Apple Store, and make a fortune. Consequently, ICTs also act as a motivator for people to be more innovative. Considering the above arguments, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 8 - National ICT infrastructure is positively associated with national technological innovation.

## 4 METHODOLOGY

## Data Collection

This study uses secondary data for testing the proposed research model. Since the level of analysis in this study is country-level, we need national-level data to validate the proposed research model and hypotheses. Considering the fact that there is no single data source that contains all the data required for this study, we collected data from various sources and combined them to make a suitable dataset for hypothesis testing.

A major issue in this kind of study is having access to up-to-date cultural value data at national level. Hofstede gathered his data within IBM between 1967 and 1973 [56]. Research shows that national culture is not stable over time and is subject to change [111]. Hence, we used the approach introduced by Beugelsdijk et al. [111] to reconstruct Hofstede dimensions using the $6 ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ wave of World Values Survey (WVS) [59]. Beugelsdijk et al. used waves 1 through 5 to reconstruct Hofstede dimensions. We used the most recent wave, wave 6, which was collected between 2010

#

and 2014 [59]. To do so, we first selected respondents born in or before 1958 to simulate the working population between 1967 and 1973 when Hofstede collected his data. Then we identified WVS items that showed absolute correlations larger than 0.6 with original Hofstede data. For the individualism dimension, 7 items were identified but four of them remained after factor analysis (V182, V203, V204, and V205). Power distance strongly correlated with 7 items of which 5 were selected after factor analysis (V45, V46, V52, V168, and V169). Uncertainty avoidance was reconstructed using 6 items (V112, V114, V115, V116, V117, and V118). Because the scores for long term orientation reported by Hofstede et al. [20] in 2010 were based on WVS items, we followed the way it was constructed. The three questions used by Hofstede measure the importance of service to others, the importance of teaching thrift to children (V17), and the degree to which one is proud of his/her nationality (V211). The question measuring the importance of service to others does not exists in wave 6. Hence, another closely related question (i.e., V32) that measures activity in humanitarian organizations and showed high correlation with Hofstede data was selected.

Then, all of the WVS responses, including responses from those born on, before, and after 1958 was used to calculate Hofstede dimension scores for the 60 countries that were surveyed in wave 6. For questions with binomial answers, percentage of people in one of the categories was calculated as country average. For continuous items that use a Likert-type scale, we calculated the average country score. At the end of this process, we rescaled our data to a 0-100 scale, to match the procedure used by Hofstede to calculate country scores on his dimensions.

Reliability of the reconstructed data was done by conducting factor analysis and calculating Cronbach’s alpha for the four cultural dimensions. Table 1 shows the results of factor analysis. Similar to the observation of Beugelsdijk et al. [111], in our study individualism and power distance seem to be part of a larger factor. The calculated Cronbach’s alpha scores for individualism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, and long term orientation were 0.86, 0.83, 0.92, and 0.52 respectively. The low reliability score for long term orientation is similar to the one calculated by Beugelsdijk et al. [111] which is 0.57. This is surprising considering that Hofstede measured his long term orientation dimension using WVS. Appendix 1 shows the scores for the four dimensions for the 60 countries surveyed in WVS wave 6.

## Construct Measurement

For measurement of the cultural dimensions, we used Hofstede dimensions reconstructed using wave 6 of WVS. The dimensions include individualism / collectivism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, and long-term orientation. These four constructs are measured as single-item. The ICT infrastructure data was extracted from the International Telecommunication Union Indicator database [112]. The report measures ICT infrastructure as weighted average of five indicators per 100 inhabitants: Internet users, main fixed telephone lines, mobile subscribers, fixed Internet subscriptions, and fixed broadband facilities. We dropped the number of fixed telephone lines since some countries directly jumped to mobile networks without significantly increasing the number of their fixed telephone lines [113]. As a result, ICT infrastructure was measured as a reflective construct with four dimensions. We used ICT infrastructure data from year 2000 to be able to study its effect on cultural values a decade later.

Technological adoption data came from WEF Global Competitiveness Report [26]. Based on this report, technological adoption has three dimensions: availability of the latest technologies, foreign direct investment (FDI) and technology transfer, and firm-level technology absorption. The three dimensions form the reflective construct of technology adoption in this study.

Technological innovation data come from INSEAD Global Innovation Index [52]. The index is based on two broad categories of variables: innovation input and innovation output. Innovation input measures the capacity of a country for innovation. Innovation output reflects the observable innovative products in two main categories: knowledge and technology outputs (knowledge creation, knowledge impact, and knowledge diffusion) and creative outputs (creative intangibles, creative goods and services, and online creativity). Knowledge creation measures patents, utility models, and scientific publications. Knowledge impact measures increases in labor productivity, entry density of new firms, spending on software, and number of ISO 9001 certificates. Knowledge diffusion measures royalty and license fees receipts as a percentage of GDP; high-tech exports (net

#

of re-exports) as a percentage of total exports (net of re-exports); exports of computer, communications, and other services as a percentage of commercial service exports; and net outflows of FDI as a percentage of GDP. Creative tangibles include statistics on trademark registrations by residents at the national office and under the Madrid System, as well as two survey questions regarding the use of ICTs in business and organizational models. Creative goods and services includes national feature films produced in a given country, the daily newspapers in circulation, the share of household expenditure in recreation and culture, and creative goods and services exports. Online creativity is measured as number of generic top-level domains (TLDs), Country-code TLDs, Wikipedia monthly edits, and video uploads on YouTube. Since innovation output measures the final outcome of innovation, we used this index to measure the innovation of countries. The construct is measured as single-item. Finally, financial market trustworthiness and confidence was extracted from the Global Competitiveness Report [26]. The construct has three dimensions: soundness of banks, regulation of securities exchanges, and legal rights index.

## Measurement Model Testing

We used Partial Least Squares (PLS) to validate our research model. We believe that our data is highly robust and reliable because it is collected from valid secondary sources such as the United Nations and the International Telecommunication Union. We used the Smart-PLS version 2.0 software to examine and confirm the reliability and validity of our data before testing the structural model. PLS is appropriate when working with a small sample size [114] which is the case in this study. It is independent of distribution and does not assume true independence of the variables [114, 115]. Furthermore, it is appropriate for the exploratory stage of applying technological determinism theory to the relationship between ICTs and culture and shows great reliability and validity when used with secondary data [116]. Hence, it can handle any possible dependence between variables used in this study. All items loaded significantly on their corresponding constructs as suggested by Gefen and Straub [117] and most items had a high loading on their corresponding constructs which was higher than the cutoff point of .40 suggested by Hulland [118]. ## 5 DISCUSSION

This study has two main findings. First, we find that technology is a driver of cultural change at the national level. Second, this study shows that ICTs act as drivers of national technological advancement. This section first discusses the key findings of the study and then reports its contributions for both research and practice, followed by limitations and future research directions.

## Findings

This study finds that technology drives cultural change. Our findings suggest that the development of ICT infrastructure is associated with more individualism and accordingly, less collectivism. This finding shows that as technology advances, people have higher concerns for themselves and their immediate family and less concerns for collectivities of which they are a member. In other words, technology is changing the relationship between the individual and the society by letting individuals make a more distinction between themselves and their related groups. This effect is probably more prominent in traditionally collectivist cultures like those of Asian countries such as China and South Korea and will eventually decrease the cultural differences between Eastern and Western cultures by shifting Eastern cultures toward higher levels of individualism.

The findings show that ICT infrastructure negatively affects power distance through allowing more widespread access to information for everyone. ICTs have recently been used by Middle Eastern nations to change the structure of power. Since 2009, the reformist movements across the world have significantly used the Internet as a powerful tool to coordinate the collective efforts to conduct political reform. In response to such collective efforts, governments have tried to restrict access to information via the Internet in order to maintain the status quo [121] and a growing number of tech companies are complying with governments’ demands regarding control of information [122, 123]. Censorship is not just about the Internet. There are cases that governments have blocked access to mobile phone networks to prevent access to information especially during civil unrests [124-126]. While governments try to restrict access to information, the introduction of new technologies makes it more difficult for them to control information. Virtual Private Networks (VPN) and anti-filtering software continue to empower people in Middle East and Asia to bypass governmental censorship. Bypassing filters is costly. Hence, many users don’t bother to bypass government filters and use the local equivalents instead. For example, since access to most social

#

media is blocked in China, most Chinese users use Chinese social media such as WeChat and Weibo. To use Weibo, users must register with their real names [127]. Lack of anonymity may affect how openly users express themselves [128]. Because of governmental control of the Internet, the future of free access to information and its effect on nations’ power distance remains unclear.

The main motivation behind Internet censorship can be explained by the theory of IT-culture conflict proposed by Leidner and Kayworth [27]. According to the theory, conflict occurs when the values embedded in a system contradict the IT values of a group. Although governments are aware of the benefits of the Internet and are willing to utilize it, they usually dislike some of the associated features such as free circulation of information and the potential to coordinate collective movements. Leidner and Kayworth [27] suggest that higher levels of difference in the IT values between the champion group and the user group, are associated with higher levels of vision conflict experienced by the user group. Higher levels of vision conflict, in turn, are related to higher potential for change in the group’s IT-values. This is the case that we have recently observed across the world. The conflict of values between the top level and the society has led to severe conflicts and consequently great potential for change.

Power distance is negatively related to democracy [19, 129] and societies with lower levels of power distance are expected to be more democratic. Considering the negative effect of ICTs development on power distance as well as the negative association between power distance and democracy, we can expect the current trend of improvement of ICT infrastructure worldwide can lead to improved levels of democracy globally even though governmental control of the Internet remains a major obstacle in this regard.

In addition to decreasing power distance, ICT infrastructure can also contribute to democratization of human societies through what Welzel and Inglehart [130] call ‘the human empowerment path to democracy’. This approach to democracy usually occurs in three steps. First, by increasing available resources, people would be materially empowered to fight for their rights. Second, people will get mentally empowered to increase their freedom-seeking efforts. Finally, democracy will help people to legally pursue and practice freedom. ICT infrastructure will facilitate human empowerment by increasing information resources available to people. Better ICT infrastructure can also help people grow economically by allowing new business development and cost savings. As a result, ICT infrastructure can be seen as an important democracy-empowerment tool.

#

We did not find support for the hypothesized relationship between technology and uncertainty avoidance. One possible explanation for this observation is the complexity of the interaction between technology and uncertainty avoidance. Although technology helps people to avoid uncertainty, adoption of new technologies exposes individuals to increased levels of uncertainty. It is possible that these effects balance each other out. Furthermore, his study also does not find a significant relationship between technological adoption and long-term orientation. Although evidence suggest that getting accustomed to the instant gratification provided by technology is a cause for short-term orientation, the generational gap between the Millennials and the previous generations might have undermined this relationship. While consumer electronics have been around after the invention of radio in 1920s, Millennials are the only generation that have been a major consumer of technology since their childhood. According to the reverse causation hypothesis, culture can be viewed as the aggregate personality traits of all members of a group [131]. Because childhood and adolescence have a critical role in shaping personality of individuals, Millennials’ early exposure to consumer electronics is a probable determinant of personality as well as cultural differences between them and previous generations. Hence, it is likely that Millennials’ preference for short term orientation [104] is unmatched among the previous generations. As Millennial and post-Millennial generations form a larger proportion of the population, countries with higher levels of technological adoption might move more strongly toward short-term orientation. This needs to be further investigated by future research.

The effect of ICT infrastructure on cultural values of individualism and power distance has implications for a technological view of globalization. The findings suggest that, in addition to social and economic factors, technology is an important driver of cultural convergence shifting different cultures toward more homogeneity at least on certain dimensions. Technology and particularly ICT infrastructure are on the way to constant improvement all over the world. Most countries have experienced sharp growth in the penetration rate of mobile phones in the last decade. The number of Internet users has significantly increased in almost all of the countries in the past decade. Appendix 3 shows the details of change in number of Internet users worldwide. Appendix 4 shows a view of the global ICTs development from 2001 to 2011. The findings of this study imply that as the technology level is improving across the world, cultures across globe are converging on two cultural values: more individualism and less power distance. The effect of technology on power distance, however, is more complicated. As the governments around the

#

world aim to increase their control over the Internet, achieving lower levels of power distance in countries with high levels of governmental control over the Internet may not be an easy task. Furthermore, it is notable that technology is not the single factor shaping cultural change. Economic growth, for example, is a major driver of cultural change [132]. It is also possible that different cultures move in the same direction at similar speeds without converging.

This study also investigates the effect of ICTs on technological development of countries. The findings confirm the role of ICTs as important technological infrastructures that empower technological development. We find that ICTs have a significant positive effect on both technological adoption and technological innovation at the national level. These findings highlight the role of ICTs in national development and illustrate that ICTs not only affect ICT-related economic sectors such as e-commerce, they but also endow other economic sectors and thus require significant attention at the national level.

## Contributions

This study contributes to both theory and practice. From a theoretical point of view, this study has four contributions. First, while most previous studies on the interaction between technology and culture see culture as a constant factor, this study considers culture as a dynamic factor and employs a classic theory to investigate the impact of technology on cultural values. To best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the effect of technology on culture using nationallevel data from several countries. The findings provide scholars with solid empirical evidence regarding the sociocultural impacts of technology.

Second, this study provides empirical evidence on the role of ICTs in national development. Our findings show that, acting as a technological driver, ICT infrastructure positively affects both technological innovation and technological adoption. ICT infrastructure prepares technological advancement with a solid frame for informational access and exchange. ICT infrastructure enables all the individuals across the world to have access to the latest technological findings. Knowledge and access to information is an important input to technological innovation and is a major source of distinction between more-innovative and less-innovative countries [133]. Conversely, ICT infrastructure is a significant determinant of technological adoption. ICT infrastructure facilitates technological adoption in two ways. First, it lets people across the world know about the latest technological advancements and how these technologies can be obtained and used [134]. Second, ICT infrastructure enables easy and mostly free access to online creative products [52]. The current trend of ICT infrastructure growth is promising both more technological innovation and more technological adoption.

Third, most cultural studies focus on a single cultural framework such as Hofstede’s cultural dimensions [e.g., 30, 31]. This study combines a social capital variable, financial market trustworthiness and confidence, with cultural dimensions. We find that technology increases confidence in financial markets through increasing transparency. This finding shows that the social consequences of technology are very extensive and are not limited to the traditional cultural dimensions. Consequently, the academics should extend their vision and have a broader look at socio-cultural variables.

Finally, this study provides evidence that technology is an important driver of convergence among different cultures. While immigration and trade have been previously seen as drivers for cultural convergence [12], our findings suggest that technology can be seen as a new and important driver of cultural convergence. The findings of this study imply that as the technology-level is improving, national cultures are converging on two cultural values: higher individualism and lower power distance.

This study also has implications for practice. First, national policymakers can gain deep insights into how technology affects human societies. The findings can help policymakers improve their understanding of cultural change and assist them in making better policies at the national level. For example, the findings regarding the effect of ICT infrastructure on innovation and technological adoption urges governments to increase investment in ICT infrastructure to improve technological level of their countries.

Second, the findings provide manufacturing and service companies with a better understanding on how technologies created by them affect nations. Such understanding will help corporations better know the requirements of the current world and let them design products and services that better fit people’s needs. For example, considering both the current trend toward more individualism and the positive relationship between individualism and concerns for privacy [135], companies need to put more emphasis on privacy-related issues.

#

Finally, this study can be used by governments, corporations, and global nonprofit organizations (NPOs) such the United Nations to achieve a better understanding of the evolution of societies on a global-scale. Global NPOs need to constantly monitor the evolution of human societies and look for mechanisms to improve it. The findings of this study suggest that ICT infrastructure is an important democracy-empowerment tool. As governments are constantly working to filter the information and to reduce the pace of their people toward democracy, continuous research and development (R&D) on anti-filtering technologies is inevitable. Such R&D should be supported by NPOs and peace-promoting governments. We also need new communication technologies that restrict the censorship power of governments. The Commotion Wireless project, also called “Internet in a Suitcase” [136], funded by the US government is an example of such technologies intended to enable suppressed nations to have free access to information. Investment on such technologies will help the global community to move more rapidly toward worldwide democracy. Considering the important role of ICTs in promoting democracy across the world, perhaps part of military budgets can be dedicated to research on and development of new ICT-related technologies.

## Limitations and Future Directions

Like any other studies, this work has limitations. First, out of more than 200 countries in the world, this study analyzes only 57 of them. This limitation comes from the lack of availability of up-todate cultural data for most countries. Nonetheless, this study does analyze the data related to the largest and most populous countries of the world containing more than 75% of world population. Future studies with a more complete dataset covering more countries may provide better results.

Second, we extracted Hofstede dimensions indirectly using WVS data. This indirect method of data collection may have affected the accuracy of our data. More specifically, the long-term orientation measure suffers from low reliability. Future research can be done upon availability of up-to-date data to give a more accurate view of the effect of technology on national culture and values. Moreover, we investigated the effect of technology on a social capital variable, financial market trustworthiness and confidence. Future research can look at the effect of technology at other important socio-cultural variables to draw a better picture of socio-cultural consequences of technological development.

#

Third, the current study looks at culture and technology of nations at one moment in time. In other words, we look at the absolute values of cultural and technological measures and find their association. Future research can be done in a longitudinal form and look at the change in cultural values during a certain period to provide better insights on how the development of technology is affecting cultural values.

Finally, in this study, we view ICTs as the driving forces behind evolution of social structure and cultural values. Some other cultural studies [e.g., 137, 138-140] suggest a different viewpoint – i.e., societies with higher degree of certain cultural values are technically more innovative than other societies with lower values. Thus, it is noteworthy that an inherent duality relationship exists in technology/culture interaction. Future research could further explore the duality of the relationship between technology and culture.

## 6 CONCLUSION

This study aimed at answering two pertinent questions regarding socio-technological impacts of ICTs. We discuss how the technological imperative shapes societies as well as how certain groups resist the technology-induced values. The study also illustrates that technology remains a significant facilitator of cultural convergence pushing national cultures across the globe to converge on two cultural values: higher individualism and lower power distance. While ICTs keep growing at a rapid speed, moving forward not only requires overcoming technical obstacles it but also demands addressing social and political challenges ahead.
