# DO EARLY WORDS FROM NEW VENTURES PREDICT FUNDRAISING? A COMPARATIVE VIEW OF SOCIAL MEDIA NARRATIVES<sup>1</sup>

Taha Havakhor Associate Professor of Information Systems, Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, QC, CANADA {taha.havakhor@mcgill.ca}

Alireza Golmohammadi Assistant Professor of Marketing, Belk College of Business, University of North Carolina Charlotte, Charlotte, NC, U.S.A {Golmohammadi@uncc.edu}

Rajiv Sabherwal and Dinesh K. Gauri Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AK, U.S.A. {rsabherwal@walton.uark.edu} {dkgauri@uark.edu}

Online equity markets have significantly changed the dynamics of connecting angels and individual equity investors to new ventures that seek early-stage capital. However, for those early-stage investors, information pointing to the success of business-to-business (B2B) new ventures (B2BNVs) is scattered and disconnected. This paper focuses on social media narratives (SMNs) as a source of insight for such investors and proposes that predicting a B2BNV’s likelihood of success requires a comparative view, i.e., a comparison of its SMNs with those of its competitors and customers. We expect that higher (lower) lingual similarity between the SMNs of an early-stage B2BNV and those of its prospective customers (competitors) predict its success. Using a longitudinal panel of 574 B2BNVs resulting in more than 2,700 venture-round observations, we find that a comparative view of a venture’s SMNs can give early-stage investors reliable predictions about the B2BNV’s ability to manage its market presence and its success in later stages. Our models show that a comparative view of SMNs increases the accuracy of predicting a B2BNV’s later-stage fundraising success by an average of 15%. Furthermore, predictive models can reliably point to a successful market presence in later stages, including the landing of customers, the winning of awards and competitions, the receiving of endorsements, the generating of revenue, and the successful patenting of products. Our study contributes to existing literature that focuses on the business impacts of social media by demonstrating the usefulness of comparative linguistics in social media analytics, i.e., comparing the firm’s social media communications to those of its competitors and business customers in the prediction of the entrepreneurial firm’s success.

Keywords: Social media narratives, lingual similarity, fundraising success, new venture, social media analytics

## Introduction

Early-stage equity investment in new ventures—i.e., providing seed money to entrepreneurs who are developing a product or service—has rapidly changed (e.g., Gobry, 2011). Specifically, the prevalence of online equity-investment markets, such as AngelList, SeedInvest, and the Angel Investment Network blog, has broadened opportunities for angels (e.g., Geron, 2019) and other individual equity investors to consider a larger set of investment targets. However, the rising number of investment opportunities makes it difficult for individual investors to garner the information needed for investment decisions. The inherent uncertainty about whether an early-stage venture will achieve later success (e.g., in fundraising and growth) poses additional challenges for early-stage investors, who typically lack the investigative resources of later-stage investors (e.g., investment banks). Specifically, in the early stages of fundraising, it is difficult to predict whether a new venture, once unincubated, will be able to survive in the real world (Martens et al., 2007). This issue is amplified for early-stage investors vetting business-to-business (B2B) new ventures (B2BNVs). To accurately assess a B2BNV, investors need to assess its potential to both draw comparisons with competitors and relate to organizational customers (Jarvenpaa & Tuunainen, 2013; Lusch & Nambisan, 2015). Insights into how investment platforms can enable investors to evaluate B2BNVs, especially in their early stages, would help these platforms as well as the entrepreneurial economy.

We propose that the organizing power of narratives and the predictive power of social media analytics can together offer a solution for these platforms and the early-stage investors they serve. Organizational narratives can connect the dots among the scattered and disconnected information about a firm (Martens et al., 2007). New ventures are increasingly utilizing social media to communicate their organizational narratives (Fischer & Reuber, 2014). Therefore, this study focuses on the predictive power of social media narratives (SMNs), i.e., organizational narratives available over social media. In fact, social media analytics have emerged as valuable business tools for prediction (e.g., Rui et al., 2013; Dewan & Ramaprasad, 2014; Deng et al., 2018).

More specifically, in this study, we examine whether analyzing SMNs can help predict how well a B2BNV will do relative to its competitors and customers. We propose that the answer is “yes,” but it requires departing from current approaches to social media analytics, which largely rely on a focused view of a venture’s own SMNs to predict entrepreneurial success (e.g., Burtch et al., 2013; Jin et al., 2017; Antretter et al., 2019), and thus considering a venture’s own SMNs In isolation. Instead, we argue that a comparative view, i.e., a comparison of a venture’s SMNs with those of its competing and customer firms is needed to predict a B2BNV’s likelihood of success.

We build on prior studies that suggest that both the content, such as topics covered and tone, and the style, such as the complexity of the language used, are critical lingual devices that help express organizational identity. We contend that a higher lingual similarity with the SMNs of prospective customer firms during the early stages of a venture predicts greater congruence between the norms and beliefs of the B2BNV and those of its customer industry. The congruent norms and beliefs help the B2BNV to understand the customer industry and can lead to its products being attuned to customer needs. We further argue that a lower lingual similarity with the SMNs of prospective competitors during the early stages of a B2BNV predicts its potential to differentiate itself from its competitors in later stages. Thus, we expect higher (lower) lingual similarity in the early stages of a B2BNV between its SMNs and those of its prospective customers (competitors) to predict its future success, e.g., raising capital, landing customers, winning awards and competitions, receiving endorsements, generating revenue, and successfully patenting products.

To test these expectations, we compiled an exhaustive dataset on 574 B2BNVs offering IT services or products to industrial customers. We drew upon rich proprietary data about these B2BNVs from “AngelPlat,”<sup>2</sup> a major U.S.-based angel networking platform that connects new ventures, investors, accelerators, and entrepreneurs with one another. It collects information from new ventures and active investors and connects them with each other. To assess each B2BNV’s social media communications, we used the data on ventures’ tweets over multiple fundraising stages. Following empirical evidence that new ventures leverage Twitter as the main channel for organizational communication (e.g., Fischer & Reuber, 2014), we focused specifically on Twitter.<sup>3</sup> Our results show that a comparative view of the venture’s SMNs, before it has a product ready to launch, can give early-stage investors reliable predictions about the B2BNV’s ability to manage its market presence in later stages and its subsequent success. Our predictive models show that considering information obtained from a comparative view of SMNs in the early stages of a venture increases the accuracy of predicting a B2BNV’s later-stage fundraising success by an average of 15%. Furthermore, the predictive models can reliably point to a successful market presence in later stages, indicated by landing customers, winning awards and competitions, receiving endorsements, generating revenue, successful patenting of products, etc.

This study makes important contributions to organizational narrative theories and social media analytics literature. It is one of the first studies to study the value of a comparative view of organizational narratives. By analyzing the content and style of the narratives of B2BNVs—and those of their prospective competitor and customer groups—this study finds that the lingual similarity (dissimilarity) of a B2BNV’s tweets to those of its customers (competitors) in its early stages has a strong association with raised capital in its later stages. Further, B2BNVs that concurrently maintain lingual similarity to their prospective customers’ tweets and dissimilarity to competitors’ tweets, respectively, show superior success in later stages. The comparative view proposed in this study contrasts with the dominantly focused view of organizational narratives in organizational research (e.g., Garud et al., 2014; Bartel & Garud, 2009) and studies on SMNs (e.g., Burtch et al., 2013; Jin et al., 2017). From a practical standpoint, this study demonstrates the value of analyzing a B2BNV’s lingual similarity to its prospective customer and competitor firms in its early stages and shows that investment platforms can generate valuable insights from such analysis. These insights may aid investors’ evaluations of a B2BNV’s capabilities, enabling them to more accurately predict the venture’s success in later stages.

## Theoretical Development

## Organizational Narrative Theory

The organizational narrative theory views communication as a means of organizing beliefs (Brummans et al., 2014; McPhee & Zaug, 2000) and explains that entrepreneurs are “raconteurs” (Roddick, 2000) who both invent and tell the world about who they are and what they do. A substream of this theory, the communicative constitution of organization (CCO) perspective, portrays new ventures as “ongoing and precarious accomplishments realized, experienced, and identified primarily—if not exclusively—in communication processes” (Cooren et al., 2011). The CCO perspective suggests that communications effectively enable a firm to express various aspects of its operations, summarize its goals, and find a rhythm in its interactions with the outside world (e.g., McPhee & Zaug, 2000). This theory connects entrepreneurial narratives and entrepreneurial outcomes, such as legitimacy and success in raising capital, and has been utilized in the broader management literature (e.g., Golant & Silince, 2007; Bartel & Garud, 2009).

## Organizing Power of Entrepreneurial Narratives

The organizational narrative theory suggests that a connection between entrepreneurial narratives and entrepreneurial outcomes exists because entrepreneurial narratives are a source of insight for investors and because the information asymmetry between resource providers and entrepreneurs is exacerbated by the inherent uncertainty of the entrepreneurial process (Shane, 2003; Zott & Huy, 2007). Such information asymmetry exists because information about entrepreneurial opportunities and a venture’s potential to exploit them is often scattered in multiple disconnected locations (Martens et al., 2007). The pressing need to access financial resources motivates entrepreneurs to piece together scattered and disconnected information (Smith & Anderson, 2004) as “related parts of a whole” (Barry & Elmes, 1997, p. 431). Because the entrepreneurs founding a venture often form the stable core of the new venture and often architect the venture through its maturation the early narratives they curate, observe, or approve can give robust cues about the venture’s strategic actions in later stages.

Entrepreneurial narratives facilitate the expression of a venture’s identity—a collage of stable, institution-wide thoughts that influence a firm’s behavior, responses, and actions in its environment (Ocasio et al., 2015). They help the venture to connect to relevant stakeholders and define its boundaries (e.g., McPhee & Zaug, 2000). Thus, a venture’s entrepreneurial narratives provide robust cues about its future actions (Brummans et al., 2014; McPhee & Zaug, 2000).

## A Focused View of Entrepreneurial Narratives

Against this theoretical backdrop, early empirical research in this area has sought to unfold the impacts of formal entrepreneurial narratives (e.g., Golant & Silince, 2007). For instance, Martens et al. (2007) showed that establishing an identity that projects leadership, as expressed in formal preinitial public offering (IPO) prospectuses, is positively associated with post-IPO valuations of 169 high-tech ventures that went public between 1996 and 2000. Less formal narratives, especially SMNs, have recently gained more traction in the literature. For instance, Fischer and Reuber (2011) used an inductive qualitative study to show how SMNs help entrepreneurs enhance their initiatives, formulate their goals, and create new opportunities. Relatedly, Fischer and Reuber (2014) explained how SMNs can reduce uncertainties about new ventures if they are communicated frequently, with a positive tone, and while covering the topics of quality and distinctiveness.

Although primarily developed in the context of crowdfunding, with a few exceptions (Aggarwal et al., 2012; Jin et al., 2017), prior information systems (IS) research on the entrepreneurial SMNs has provided further insight into the association between SMNs and entrepreneurial outcomes. Aggarwal et al. (2012) showed that new ventures with more blogged narratives raise more funds. Similarly, Hong et al., (2018) found that Kickstarter ventures that tweet more receive significantly greater contributions. In addition to the evidence about the impact of the quantity of SMNs (e.g., number of tweets), prior IS research has also unraveled the desirable attributes of SMNs. For instance, Burtch et al. (2013) showed that crowdfunded ventures with more readable pitch stories attract more attention on crowdfunding social media platforms. Also, in a study of over 2,800 technological start-ups listed on Crunchbase, Jin et al. (2017) found the sentiment of start-up tweets to be strongly associated with the funds raised by ventures in the second round of their fundraising cycles.

Overall, prior literature shows the impact of a focused view of SMNs on critical outcomes for ventures (e.g., fundraising success) by unfolding some of their desirable attributes (e.g., readability and positive sentiment of the venture’s own narratives). This focused view is effective in teaching investors more about who the entrepreneurs are and what they do (Martens et al., 2007) but it is not clear whether adopting a focused view of SMNs is conducive to understanding how well the venture can position itself among other firms (i.e., relate to customers and distinguish itself from rivals).

## Positioning Among Other Firms

Relating to customers and distinguishing oneself from rivals is integral to a new venture’s success. First, relating to customers is key to successful value creation, i.e., offering value through products and services because understanding customers helps ventures refine products and services that are of higher value to them (Karpen et al., 2012; O’Cass & Ngo, 2011). Relatedly, distinguishing oneself from rivals is a precursor to value appropriation (Mizik & Jacobson, 2003; Frennea, Han, & Mittal, 2019), i.e., the ability to translate a valuable product or service into higher sales and market gain. The existing strategic management literature (e.g., Mizik & Jacobson, 2003; Cabral et al., 2019) has highlighted the importance of both value creation and value appropriation for firm survival and the attainment of competitive advantage.

If successfully differentiating oneself from rivals and relating to customers in later stages is rewarded by investors, as Navis and Glynn (2011) contend, two pressing questions emerge. Can SMNs organize information that pertains to how well a new venture can achieve these goals? Does a focused view of the new venture’s narratives help extract such organized information? The answer to the first question is affirmative, based on studies showing that entrepreneurial narratives express a venture’s identity, which includes thoughts and beliefs about how the venture situates itself in a broader field (McPhee & Zaug, 2000). But the second question remains mostly unanswered in the entrepreneurial narrative literature.

## A Comparative View of Entrepreneurial Narratives

We can obtain insights about the second question above by drawing upon strategy research on a firm’s positioning in its organizational field. Prior research has suggested that to understand a firm’s positioning (among rivals and customers of B2B firms), we should look beyond the firm and instead focus on its broader field (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Ho et al., 2017). Adopting a comparative view of entrepreneurial narratives, i.e., considering how a venture’s narratives compare to those of its prospective rivals and customers, can better inform investors about the similarity or distinctiveness between the norms held by the venture and other actors in its organizational field. Such a comparative view of entrepreneurial narratives in the early stages of a venture’s development can inform investors about how well it will perform strategic actions in later stages. Achieving distinctiveness from competitors enables competitive gains, whereas having an appeal among potential customer firms can help land customers and garner revenue.

## Lingual Facets to Consider in a Comparative View

For a comparative view of a venture’s narratives, we must first identify lingual facets that are relevant to our inquiry. To do so, we consider the aspects of language in entrepreneurial narratives that prior research has shown influence investors. Initial research on entrepreneurial narratives was conducted on the formal narratives delivered in IPO prospectuses (e.g., Martens et al., 2007), but more recent research has expanded to the less formal narratives on social media platforms, e.g., Twitter (e.g., Fischer & Reuber, 2014; Antretter et al., 2019).

In this body of research, a focused view of social media communications, both in terms of content and style, has been shown to be associated with fundraising success. For example, Fischer & Reuber (2014) explained that topic cues that emphasize quality in social media can reduce uncertainty about a new venture. Topic cues are perhaps the most agreedupon aspects of narratives in terms of their effectiveness in attracting investors, as they relay direct information about entrepreneurial beliefs and likely future actions. Specifically, in comparative linguistics, the attention to topics has been appealing. Shi et al. (2016) compared topics to understand business proximity between a dyad of firms. Also, topics are aspects of content that have defined norms in various institutional settings (e.g., Bentivegna & Marchetti, 2018).

Aside from topics, the tone of words used to deliver topics also carries useful information for investors. Specifically, content that delivers cues about entrepreneurs’ positive psychological capital, as reflected in the positive tone of narratives in

Kickstarter campaigns, has been associated with successful fundraising (Anglin et al., 2018). Thus, tone is an important lingual device for delivering information about the venture’s psychological and emotional capital.

In addition to the content (in terms of topic and tone), the linguistic style, i.e., the readability of the communication, can impact fundraising success (Parhankangas & Renko, 2017; Burtch et al., 2013). Prior studies suggest that the text of entrepreneurial narratives is often scrutinized for its complexity. Moreover, existing research emphasizes the context specificity of the norms of the style used in social media messages in various institutional fields (e.g., Kwon & Sung, 2011; Kim et al., 2018).

Entrepreneurs can use each of these lingual facets to express the venture’s identity (i.e., its thoughts, beliefs, and norms). For instance, in the banking industry, where breaking the traditional norms of distant relationships with customers is a new approach for growth (belief: increasing emotional connections can attract new customers), a B2BNV catering to online banking can demonstrate its attention to this belief by using positive, warm tones in daily narratives. Similarly, in the biotech industry, where the educational disconnection between technologists and natural scientists is considered a hurdle (belief: educational disconnection is a barrier in advancing innovative biotech solutions), a B2BNV offering a tech solution can address this concern by increasing its use of medical and biological writing styles and structures to demonstrate familiarity with relevant materials. Since these lingual facets can help express identity in entrepreneurial narratives, adopting a comparative view of them can offer insights into whether a venture can differentiate from (relate to) prospective competitors (customers).

## B2BNVs and SMNs

The prospective organizational field for B2BNVs includes two discrete worlds. One is the customer world: B2BNVs must understand customer firms’ norms and build strong, dynamic relationships with existing and prospective customers. The B2BNV needs to realize ever-changing market demands and accordingly enhance its products/services. The second world is that of competitors: B2BNVs must understand existing rivals, as well as their aspirations and normative behaviors, in order to sense the dynamics of the supply side of the market and respond as needed to protect their own differentiated positions. While B2BNVs’ realized responses to both customer and competitor sides of the market are either limited in scope or entirely nonexistent in early rounds of fundraising, early-round investors seek information to better evaluate a new venture’s ability to understand these actions and thereby predict its future strategic actions. These strategic actions, which are critical for survival in expansion stages, include distinguishing itself from competitors, producing scaled products or services that are attuned to customer needs, and marketing them effectively. Thus, early-stage investors can benefit from knowing about a B2BNV’s ability to sense the institutional norms of its customer and competing industries.

Social media, with their reach and low costs, present a welcome opportunity for new ventures to communicate with potential investors and stakeholders. If general insights from the identity expression in entrepreneurial narratives hold for communications via social media (e.g., Twitter), then a B2BNV’s communications on such media can inform earlyround investors about an entrepreneur’s norms and beliefs. Also available to such investors is a view into how firms in prospective competitor and customer industries communicate and express their norms and beliefs. This provides an opportunity for investors to compare the B2BNV’s norms and beliefs with those in its prospective competitor and customer groups. Thus, if SMNs reflect the B2BNV’s identity, which in later stages drives its distinctiveness or similarity to its rivals and customers, respectively, a comparative analysis of the SMNs of a B2BNV vis-à-vis those of its prospective rivals and customers could help early-round investors make better decisions.

## Lingual Similarity with Prospective Customers as Reflecting Value-Creating Ability

Strategy literature has highlighted the role of value-creating activities in the profitability and market success of firms (e.g., Porter, 1985). According to this view, one strategic consideration for any firm is to figure out how to create superior value for its customers (Aspara & Tikkanen, 2013). Value creation is even more important for new ventures. Indeed, research shows that value creation through innovation is key to new ventures’ legitimacy and success (Song et al., 2008). B2B firms differ markedly from their business-toconsumer (B2C) counterparts, as they are rarely able to create value in isolation (i.e., closed innovation). In a B2B firm, value creation, as a critical driver of success and growth (e.g., Dotzel & Shankar, 2016), results from collaboration (i.e., coinnovation) with organizational customers (Wagner et al., 2010; Fang et al., 2016). Although value creation also exists in the B2C context (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015), interactive value creation is likely to be more important in the B2B context (Kaufman et al., 2006). In fact, joint innovation between a firm and its organizational customers, wherein both parties integrate their competencies, is an increasingly popular strategy for B2B firms at both dyadic and network levels (Noordhoff et al., 2011). As such, for B2BNVs to succeed, the ability to align with customers and create value is paramount and SMNs can provide clues related to such ability.

The often stable entrepreneurial core (i.e., founders and key partners) that shapes a new venture’s day-to-day narrative in its early stages can enable it to align with customers once it is ready to launch a product/service. Specifically, an entrepreneurial core that understands a venture’s customer industry (as shown by its in-sync social media communication) is better embedded in that industry’s norms and is more capable of creating and delivering value to those customers. Such a core is also more likely to sense changes in that sector and foster the development of a product/service that is more attuned to the customers’ needs after the venture establishes its base. Understanding the norms and beliefs held by a prospective customer group requires a deep understanding of that industry’s lingual structures, jargon, symbols, trends, and other verbal cues. Formal degrees and past experience can indicate general familiarity with an industry but hardly signal the presence of deep understanding. Yet an industry-aligned identity, expressed in SMNs, can provide valuable cues to investors. We do not hold any specific assumptions about whether congruent expressions of identity are intended or unintended, but because identity is co-constructed with the associated lingual devices (Ocasio et al., 2015), entrepreneurial narratives can smoothly manifest the venture’s identity.

All three lingual aspects mentioned earlier are relevant to this discussion. A B2BNV’s use of the same special words as its prospective customers can show an interest in similar topics. A similar positive sentiment (valence) in messaging indicates that the B2BNV is aware of the tone and methods of communication of its customer industry. Finally, the complexity of the language used in narratives across an industry reflects the vastness of the industry’s vocabulary and the bandwidth of its intended audience. Thus, a B2BNV that uses similar levels of complexity as its customer industry demonstrates an understanding of that industry. In sum, the lingual similarity of a B2BNV’s tweets to those of its prospective customer group during its earlier stages is associated with the interaction between that B2BNV and its customers in later stages and its subsequent success in accessing financial resources. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1: The lingual similarity of a B2BNV’s SMNs to those of its prospective customer industry in its early stages is associated with the funds the B2BNV raises in later stages.

## Lingual Dissimilarity with Potential Competitors as Reflecting Value-Appropriating Ability

Although value-creating ability is an important precursor of success, it is not the only path leading to financial profitability and market success. Indeed, research has shown that value appropriation, i.e., a firm’s ability to obtain, maintain, and increase benefit from the value created or access to it, is also an important determinant of a firm’s success. Prior research has also shown that stock markets react favorably to increased emphasis on value appropriation (Mizik & Jacobson, 2003). According to both the resource-based view (e.g., Barney, 1991) and the strategic-positioning perspective (e.g., Porter, 1985), value-appropriation capabilities enable firms to enjoy a stable flow of profit by setting up barriers (i.e., isolating mechanisms) to competition (Aspara & Tikkanen, 2013). One effective value-appropriation strategy is differentiation from competitors, as differentiation increases and thus leads to better current profitability and a higher residual value of the firm (Srinivasan et al., 2009). For example, an increase in the reputation gap between a firm and its competitors increases the firm’s ability to command higher prices than its competitors (Obloj & Capron, 2011). Differentiation is of paramount importance for B2B firms as well. Indeed, one of the crucial tasks of B2B marketers is to signal, through different media, a problem-solving capability superior to that of competitors (Aarikka-Stenroos & Kaakkala, 2012). Indeed, prior research has offered consistent evidence that B2B firms try to differentiate themselves from competitors (e.g., Ulaga & Eggert, 2006). Therefore, the B2BNV’s ability to distinguish itself from its competitors becomes critical to its future success, and SMNs can provide clues related to such abilities.

An entrepreneurial core that makes its voice heard through distinctive messaging shows a deeper understanding of prospective competitors and their norms and beliefs. In turn, an entrepreneurial core with such a deep understanding is better equipped to make sense of the competitors’ nuanced moves and better exploit competitive opportunities. But this deep underlying understanding is rarely achieved serendipitously and is instead rooted in active experiences. Such experiences also help firms understand the industry and infer its subtle lingual and communicative norms so that firms can later create coherent messages to distinguish their final product/service and receive associated recognitions, e.g., win awards.

The distinctiveness of covered topics can reflect an ability to define and ideate a product/service with a core domain of words and tones distinctive from those of rivals. Distinct tone and complexity highlight the fine attention of entrepreneurs to competitive details, a natural entrepreneurial talent to sound differently, or both. Thus, the lingual dissimilarity of a B2BNV’s tweets vis-à-vis those of its prospective competitor group during its early stages is associated with the B2BNV’s ability to distinguish itself from competitors in later stages and its subsequent success in accessing financial resources. Hence, we expect:

H2: The lingual dissimilarity of a B2BNV’s SMNs to those of its prospective competitors in its early stages is associated with the amount of funds the B2BNV raises in later stages.

## Simultaneous Pursuit of Lingual Similarity and Dissimilarity

Prior literature on value creation and value appropriation views them as complementary aspects of a firm’s strategy. Focusing exclusively on value appropriation, such as through differentiation, can endanger the rate at which a firm innovates and thus jeopardize its long-term success (Zott & Amit, 2007). Conversely, pursuing value creation only can decrease a firm’s sharpness in defending its innovation and appropriating its rents (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2004). In addition to these theoretical arguments, empirical research has shown that many firms cannot benefit from their innovations due to a dedicated focus on value creation and overlooking value appropriation (e.g., Golder & Tellis, 1993). Thus, in order to capitalize on the benefits of the value created through innovative products or services, a firm needs to differentiate itself from competitors imitating its innovations. For example, by educating customers about a new product, innovators decrease the risk of market entry for competitors (Lawson et al., 2012). Thus, competition influences the extent to which the firm can capture value. According to the organizational ambidexterity literature (Golder & Tellis, 1993, O’Reilly & Tushman, 2004, Zott & Amit, 2007; Pitelis, 2009), the simultaneous and coordinated pursuit of value-creating and appropriating activities renders higher payoffs than pursuing either one alone.

Based on the above theoretical arguments, a B2BNV pursuing lingual similarity to customers but not lingual dissimilarity to competitors may not convince later-stage investors of its ability to appropriate the value created through successful interaction with customers. The pursuit of value creation implies a reduction in the firm’s ability to defend its innovation in the market and appropriate its rents (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2004). Moreover, empirical research shows that many firms do not benefit from their innovations when overlooking value appropriation (e.g., Golder & Tellis, 1993). Lingual similarity to competitors may indicate a B2BNV’s boundedness in the norms and structures of competitors due to either the attributes of its product/service or the lack of an entrepreneurial ability to be distinguished. This implies that the venture’s gains from attracting customers would also be reachable to its competitors.

Conversely, an exclusive focus on the competition (i.e., exhibiting lingual dissimilarity to competitors without signs of relating to customers) may suggest that the B2BNV does not have the means to survive in the long term, as it may not be able to successfully find the new customers needed for growth. Along similar lines, the sole focus on value appropriation may endanger a firm’s long-term success (Zott & Amit, 2007). Hence, we posit:

H3: The interaction between the lingual similarity of a B2BNV’s SMNs to those of its prospective customers and the lingual dissimilarity of its SMNs to those of its prospective competitors in its early stages is associated with the amount of funds the B2BNV raises in later stages.

## Data and Measures

## Sample

To test the hypotheses, we constructed a panel of B2BNVs that provide IT products/services to other organizations. To obtain information about the B2BNVs’ fundraising, we partnered with a major seed-funding platform, AngelPlat, that provides investor- (or accelerator-) matching services to new ventures. AngelPlat collects information from or about new ventures in each stage of fundraising. It also holds an active database of investors, specifically individual investors and angels who fund early stages. It focuses on early-round investments, but also collects information about the start-ups in their later stages (e.g., Stage A and Stage B rounds) and in the pre-IPO stages.

We identified the B2BNVs present on AngelPlat based on their answers to questions about their product/service, ideal/likely customers (and whether or not they have other businesses as their customers), and potential/existing competitors. Overall, we identified 574 B2BNVs and tracked them from January 2013 to January 2019. During this time, they experienced an average of four rounds of raising capital (around two early, preready-to-launch rounds and two later, post-ready-to-launch rounds), resulting in 2,743 venture-round observations. An average venture in the study’s sample cumulatively raised \$4.8 million in capital, had two co-founders, and employed seven or eight professionals. Around 38% of the venture rounds covered in the study represent early rounds of fundraising and the rest, late rounds. Crowdfunded capital raised in the sample accounted for less than 9% of the total capital raised across the time covered by the study.<sup>4</sup> The period during which we collected Twitter data is from the start date of the focal round, as noted in AngelPlat, until the start date of the next round. This is also the period during which AngelPlat focuses on producing analytical insights based on Twitter communications.<sup>5</sup> On average, each round lasted around 9.5 months. For each year and each round of capital raising, we retrieved the Twitter page content of each venture, which AngelPlat collected contemporaneously. The B2BNVs in our sample cater to manufacturing, retail, transportation and logistics, biotech, pharmaceutical firms, health establishments (e.g., hospitals, medical schools), insurance companies, banks, higher education institutions, and life sciences labs. Appendix A details how we address possible right censorship in the data.

In addition to accessing the venture’s tweets, we collected tweets by the customer group, as well as those by the venture’s competitor group, which are identified in each round of fundraising by AngelPlat. The focal B2BNVs in our sample and their competing and customer firms posted an average of one tweet every 3.28 days, 3.52 days, and 5.31 days, respectively.

## B2BNVs’ Fundraising Success

This study’s main goal is to understand whether similarities between the B2BNVs’ SMNs and those of the customer and competitor industry groups in the early stages of fundraising can help investors better predict fundraising success in later stages, net of other known factors. To identify this predictive value, we took two steps. First, we assessed whether these lingual (dis)similarities in early rounds of fundraising (pre-ready-tolaunch) are associated with the capital raised by a B2BNV in later rounds (post-ready-to-launch) in a reasonably identified explanatory model. The output from this step explains whether a comparative view of a B2BNV’s narratives on social media provides investors with any additional insights that are orthogonal to other observable factors. In the second step, we used the insights from the first step to build a predictive model that used the factors from the explanatory model as inputs to make predictions about the raised capital and whether the venture will be able to land customers, win awards and competitions, receive endorsements, generate revenue, and successfully patent its products in later stages. The second step explains the predictive value of considering a comparative view of a B2BNV’s SMNs.

Conducting the first step, we formed an initial explanatory model of fundraising success of B2BNVs by considering a set of two factors that are well-linked to the success of fundraising in a given round: (1) innovation development factors (i.e., human and social capital) and (2) market advantage factors (business model [BM] and product market [PM] strategy). We then added our measures of lingual similarity to customer and competitor industry groups as additional elements. Appendix B summarizes the measures of all these factors.

## Lingual Similarity to Customers and Competitors

Next, we focused on measuring a B2BNV’s lingual similarities against industry customer and competitor groups as predictive indicators of its future distinctiveness or similarity in its organizational field, which, in turn, helps to attract financial resources. The existing literature on text mining has advanced considerably in recent years. Two notable examples in the IS field that focus on comparative linguistics and most fit our inquiry are the studies done by Johnson et al. (2015) and Shi et al. (2016). While the former study uses textual analytics of online community posts to predict the leadership of individuals in those communities based on the distinctiveness of their language from others, the latter focuses on developing a measure of business proximity between new ventures based on their business descriptions and uses this measure to see if it can predict merger and acquisitions between a pair of proximate ventures.

Johnson et al. (2015) built on a multifaceted view of natural language, breaking it into semantics, syntax, lexicography, and morphology facets, following Bird et al. (2009). Then, the prototypicality (similarity of topics) was considered: positive sentiment (tone) and readability (style) of an individual’s posts to predict their leadership position. We did not find any study using the prototypicality measure to compare tweets, and only found examples of text analytics in terms of measuring positive sentiment (Riloff et al., 2013) and readability (e.g., Flekova et al., 2016) in the context of Twitter. Shi et al. (2016) offer an approach that can complement Johnson et al.’s (2015) method. Shi et al. (2016) show that using a latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA; Blei et al., 2003) approach to identify a vector of topics discussed by new ventures and estimating the cosine similarity between the vector of topics between the two ventures is an effective way of estimating their business proximity.

LDA has also been used in the analytics of Twitter texts by other studies (e.g., Geva et al., 2019). It exclusively relies on a lexicon-based approach and defines K topics, assuming that words used in a corpus are distributed probabilistically across them. LDA, in its regular usage, is a generative, unsupervised tool to find the said probabilistic distributions of words across a set of topics, mostly through Bayesian methods of approximating the hyperparameters of Dirichlet priors assumed over those distributions. LDA produces a vector of weights belonging to each of its generated K topics for each venture’s (i) corpus of tweets $( T _ { i k } = I O , I J , \mathbf k = 1 , \dots , \mathbf K )$ . Shi et al. (2015) used these vectors to estimate the cosine similarity of texts between two ventures. One limitation of this lexicon-based approach is that it may not capture the tone and style in the language as clearly as the positive sentiment and readability features in Johnson et al. (2015).

Prior studies have highlighted the shortcoming of an LDAonly approach in not capturing other important lingual facets (e.g., Lin & He, 2009). A possible solution to getting the best of the two methods is to use normalized values (varying from 0 to 1): the inverse of the absolute positive-sentiment score difference (pairwise sentiment similarity, pw\_PSS) and the inverse of absolute readability-index difference (pairwise readability-index similarity, pw\_RIS) between the focal B2BNV’s corpus of tweets in a given round of fundraising and that of another firm (which is in either customer or competitor group), along with Shi et al.’s (2016) measure of pairwise topic similarity (pw\_TS) between the same pair of B2BNV firms. Therefore, to estimate the lingual similarity to customers (LSC), we first measured the pairwise LSC (between the ith B2BNV and the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ firm in the customer group at time $t ~ ( p w \_ L S C _ { i j t } )$ . We took the average of the normalized values of their pairwise sentiment similarity, pairwise readability similarity, and the value of pairwise topic similarity (Shi et al.’s [2016] measure already generates values between 0 and 1):

$$
p w \_ L S C _ {i j t} = (p w \_ P S S _ {i j t} + p w \_ R I S _ {i j t} + p w \_ T S _ {i j t}) / 3
$$

Next, we defined lingual similarity between the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ B2BNV and its customer industry group at time t (LSC<sub>it</sub>) as the average of all of its pairwise similarities in the customer industry group:

$$
L S C _ {i t} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} (p w \_ L S C _ {i j t}) / m
$$

We followed a similar approach to estimate the lingual similarity to competitors in their own industry (LSO). We first measured the pairwise LSO (between the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ B2BNV and the $k ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ firm in the customer group) at time t (pw\_LSO<sub>ikt</sub>) and defined the lingual similarity between the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ B2BNV and its competitor industry group at time t as the average of all of its pairwise similarities in the competitor industry group. In these calculations, we used a dictionary that identifies the polarity (i.e., weighted positivity and negativity) of English words in online settings (the Affective Norms for English Words [ANEW] dictionary; Center for Research in Psychophysiology, 1999) to measure the positivity of sentiment. We estimated the positivity of tweets by the new venture by summing the polarity weights of the words comprising the tweets divided by the number of words used. Finally, we assessed the readability of a venture’s tweets using the Automated Readability Index (ARI) (Naval Technical Training Command, 1975), which is commonly used in social media analytics (e.g., Burtch et al., 2013). ARI considers the number of characters, words, and sentences in a text corpus:

$$
A R I = 4. 7 1 * \frac {\# C h a r a c t e r s}{\# W o r d s} + 0. 5 0 * \frac {\# W o r d s}{\# S e n t e n c e s} - 2 1. 4 3
$$

Specifically, our document set, as the input to the LDA algorithm, consists of the corpora of tweets made by the B2BNVs in each round of fundraising and the corpora of tweets made by the B2BNVs’ customer and competitor groups during a corresponding time frame (we pooled all the corpora together). <sup>6</sup> An LDA algorithm set at K = 12 topics results in the highest log-likelihood for categorizing our tweet corpora into different topics. Table 1 summarizes these topics, with each topic’s label being agreed upon by the first author and two graduate assistants upon reviewing 100 random tweets from each topic. Table 1 also includes an example tweet for each topic. Further, Table B2 in Appendix B includes illustrations of the topic portfolio, as well as sentiment and ARI scores of tweets by a focal B2BNV, one of its competitors, and one of its potential customers in the same span of time.

The success of new ventures depends on the value of the funds they raise. Therefore, we used raised capital as a good proxy of success for new ventures. From initial conception to commercialization, and eventually public offering, new ventures raise funds from different sources. A new venture might have, for example, two or three rounds of seeding. ## Controls

Along with the visible aspects of a B2BNV’s early BM and PM, as well as its human and social capital, we controlled for the number of actual customers (Client) in a focal round, generated revenue in the round (Revenue), number of competitions or awards won (Awards), number of customer endorsements or recommendations (Endorsement), the ratio of patents won to patents filed (Patents), all of which is information gathered by AngelPlat. Moreover, we controlled for the stage of development (StageCount) in which the funding was raised. We also include dummies for each stage (StageDummy). Further, because fundraising stages can vary in temporal length, we also controlled for the length of each round (RoundLength) in the model.

We controlled for sentiment (Polarity, calculated using the ANEW dictionary), lexical richness of vocabulary (the number of unique words used in tweets), and the complexity of tweets (Readability, calculated using ARI) made by the B2BNV in each round<sup>7</sup> and the volume of tweets (number of tweets in each round). While some lingual controls are used to estimate similarity/dissimilarity, we controlled for their level (i.e., their absolute value) to ensure that their relative similarity to other firms, and not their level, causes the observed effects. We also controlled for the impact of venture age and year (or years, because a fundraising round takes more than a year on some rare occasions) in which the round occurs. Table 3 presents the descriptive statistics, including a comparison (Panel B) of our sample and a comparable (in terms of age) sample of 672 B2BNV IT ventures represented by Crunchbase in a similar time frame to our study but not in our sample. Our sample has ventures with more employees, more funding by angels, and shorter rounds, but the samples are otherwise comparable.

## Analyses

In Step 1 of our dynamic panel specification to evaluate whether lingual (dis)similarities in early rounds of fundraising are associated with the capital a B2BNV raises in later rounds (post-ready-to-launch), we first estimate the following Equation (1) by dynamically predicting Raised\_Capital<sup>8</sup>:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Raised\_Capital } _ {i t} = B _ {1} * L S C _ {i t} + B _ {2} * L S O _ {i t} + B _ {3} L S C _ {i t} * L S O _ {i t} \\ + \gamma^ {*} W _ {i t} + \epsilon_ {i t} + c _ {i}, \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

where i and t denote the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ venture in $t ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ fundraising round. $\epsilon _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ is the error term, $\mathrm { c _ { i } }$ is venture-specific time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity, and $W _ { i t }$ is the vector of control variables. This estimation provides a test of H1, H2, and H3.

## Identification and Estimation.

The goal to make an associative, and later, a predictive interpretation of the coefficients of $L S C _ { i t } , ~ L S O _ { i t } ,$ and $L S C _ { i t } { ^ { * } L S O } _ { i t }$ in Equation (1) guides our identification approach. This choice is informed by the study’s theoretical foundations. Specifically, we estimate Equation (1) for venture rounds in which the venture’s founders disclosed to AngelPlat that they were entering a fundraising period with a product ready to launch. This is congruent with our theoretical expectation that narratives in the early stages are devices to express the B2BNV’s identity and provide cues about its future actions. We also expect that because these actual actions are more tangible to investors, compared to beliefs expressed in the early stages of fundraising, the fundraising gains of being legitimately distinctive occur in rounds with an active organizational field where actual actions happen, and savvy investors can observe and parse those actions. Thus, the choice to use the raised capital in phases with a product ready to launch is logical because it is in those stages that the venture has to enter an active organizational field and produce tangible responses, e.g., attracting customers and getting effective contracts while facing fierce competition from competitors.

In our sample, around 86% of occurrences of finding initial venture capital investors happen in rounds where a product is ready to launch, although the percentage of B2BNVs that successfully find venture capital investors in the first round of having a ready-to-launch product is much lower, 31%. Also, none of the ventures in the pre-ready-to-launch stage reported generating revenue, winning an award or competition, landing a customer, or having a recommendation/endorsement from a customer. Additionally, only 20 had any patents awarded to them in these rounds. This further indicates that tangible evidence of interaction with the organizational field is more limited in the pre-ready-to-launch stage.

Although Equation (1) handles the threat of unobserved timeinvariant heterogeneity by fixed-effects modeling and by dynamic paneling (i.e., the inclusion of Raised\_Capital<sub>it-1</sub> as a covariate), there still remains significant endogeneity threats if Equation (1) is estimated through a regular fixed-effects estimation. First, the contemporaneous values of LSC, LSO, and LSC\*LSO can be simultaneously determined by an unobserved time-varying factor that is visible to savvy institutional investors, such as venture capitalists, in later rounds of funding. Similarly, other significant factors in the model, such as those related to human and social capital and BMs, may be simultaneously identified by unobserved factors. Finally, the inclusion of Raised\_Capital<sub>it-1</sub> automatically violates the condition of exogeneity because the error terms in rounds t-1 and t are most likely correlated, therefore making $\epsilon _ { i t }$ correlated with Raised\_Capital<sub>it-1</sub>. The collection of these threats suggests that an instrumented dynamic panel estimation fits best. Anderson and Hsiao (1982) and Arellano and Bond (1991) provide a family of instrumented dynamic-panel estimations that are executed in two stages. In the first stage, the level equations are transformed, often through first-differencing or forward orthogonal deviations (Roodman, 2009) to remove the fixed effects. Then, either a generalized method of moment-(GMM system, hereafter) or regular-style instruments are used to correct for biases in estimating the transformed equation.

From this family of estimations, we selected those suggested by Arellano and Bover (1995) and Blundell and Bond (1998), who recommend using the deep lags (two lags and deeper) to instrument the transformed equations. LSC, LSO, and LSC\*LSO are instrumented with lags of two and deeper only when those lags are available in the pre-ready-to-launch rounds. Venture rounds without such instruments are dropped.

To avoid overidentification, we instrumented Raised\_Capital<sub>it</sub> <sub>1</sub> and all independent variables other than the controls and dummy variables with only their second lags. Since our panels are short and early observations (i.e., pre-ready-to-launch rounds) essential as instruments for LSC, LSO, and LSC\*LSO, we transformed Equation (1) using a forward orthogonaldeviation technique, which allowed us to drop fewer early observations and avoid heteroscedastic error terms while instrumenting with lagged variables (Roodman, 2009, p. 110). Our sample includes 1,430 post-ready-to-launch rounds, providing 622 observations with both forward lags and instruments in the pre-ready-to-launch rounds. Figure 1 shows the panel structure required to instrument the transformed values of LSC, LSO, and LSC\*LSO with their lagged values.

The deeply lagged variables in Arellano-Bond are not correlated with the unobserved factor, as with fixed-effects removal, as the unremoved heterogeneity is compounded in the error term at time t. It is unlikely that a time-variant unobserved variable at time t would drive the level variables in round t-2 unless the error terms in rounds t and t-2 were serially correlated (in such cases, lags deeper than one should be used as instruments). Arellano-Bond estimators are only reported in the tables when the null hypothesis that error terms are not autocorrelated cannot be rejected. Further, the tables report the difference-in-Hansen test statistics (χ<sup>2</sup>) of the exogeneity of instruments. The tests indicate the null hypothesis that exogeneity cannot be rejected. Before estimating Equation (1), all variables were standardized for meaningful coefficient comparisons and because we included interaction terms in later models.

Model 4.1 in Table 4 presents the results when independently using components of LSC and LSO as covariates. The results show a positive and significant effect of LSC components on raised capital (supporting H1). A negative and significant effect of LSO components on the raised capital in each fundrasing round is also observed (supporting H2). Also, the interactions between components of LSC and LSO are negative and significant (supporting H3). The results indicate that above and beyond the impact of the content of communications via Twitter, the structure and style of the communication (measured through readability) is associated with the fundraising amount. The relatively high correlations among the components of LSC and LSO support using our main composite measures of LSC and LSO (see Table 2) Model 4.2 suggests that LSC is positively and significantly associated with raised capital (0.116, p < 0.001). Moreover, LSO shows a negative and significant association with raised capital (-0.108, p < 0.001). Also, the results indicate a negative and significant interaction for LSO\*LSC (-0.115, p < 0.001), suggesting that lower LSO values increase the positive association between LSC and raised capital. Models 4.3 and 4.4 show the results when tweets are only analyzed for the formal duration of fundraising and show qualitatively similar estimations. In sum, our findings provide strong support for H1, H2, and H3, lending credence to the idea that an early-stage B2BNV’s lingual similarity to customers and competitors is associated with the amount of funds raised in later stages.

Estimating Equation (1) has a limitation: It cannot consider cases where a B2BNV does not reach post-ready-to-launch fundraising stages (e.g., where financial issues bar it from continuing to raise funds) or where the venture’s journey ends in the first post-ready-to-launch round.<sup>9</sup> We conducted an alternative analysis<sup>10</sup> that shows that not only are LSO, LSC, and their interaction in early stages indicative of funds raised in later stages but these lingual similarities and dissimilarities in early rounds are also associated with the chances of a B2BNV getting through to the next round. This is especially important for early-round investors because a mere indication of later-round fundraising success may be insufficient for providing a higher chance of survival to navigate the early stages of fundraising.

## Boundary Conditions

Having established the associations between LSO, LSC, LSO\*LSC, and raised capital in later rounds (Step 1) and before discussing the performance of our predictive models (Step 2), we tested the boundary conditions of these associations. These boundary conditions examine the suitability of our theoretical assumptions.

The first boundary condition arises from whether the comparative view is based on a stable or dynamic entrepreneurial core. In B2BNVs with a dynamic core, the identity held by the entrepreneurial core in early rounds should have a weaker association with the B2BNV’s later responses and thus cannot predict later fundraising success. To examine this boundary condition, we compared the results for Equation (1) in subsamples of observations where the entrepreneurial core (i.e., founders) is (1) the same and (2) different in an early round versus a later round (Models 5.1 and 5.2, Table 5). We found that consistent with the first boundary condition, the associations of LSO, LSC, and their interactions with raised capital in later rounds become significantly weaker when the venture’s entrepreneurial core changes (e.g., when a founder leaves).

The second assumption in our theoretical development regards the greater value of a comparative rather than a focused view of narratives in predicting the raised capital in later rounds. This assumption forms a second boundary condition; thus, we expect that measuring LSO, LSC, and LSO\*LSC based on a subset of tweets that are more conducive to assessing similarity (dissimilarity) with customers (competitors) would have a stronger association with raised capital. Specifically, some topics covered in the tweets, by nature, have a wider reach than the ones specific to the B2BNV. Therefore, we first estimated the associations of LSO, LSC, and LSO\*LSC with raised capital in a model where only tweets on industry-wide topics (tweets with the topic designation of industry-related legal issues, other industry-related issues, social issues) are used to measure the lingual similarities between the focal B2BNV and its referent industry groups. We compared this model (6.3) with one where the lingual similarities between the focal B2BNV and its referent industry groups were measured based on the corpora of tweets with a firm-specific topic designation (6.4): engagement and feedback, talent promotion, product promotion, routine updates/daily news, external news coverage, thanking supporters/customers/stakeholders, and achievements.<sup>11</sup> The comparison shows that the associations between LSO, LSC, LSO\*LSC and raised capital are significantly stronger when tweets with a wider reach are used. This finding is consistent with our second expected boundary condition. Appendix C provides a series of analyses that further test the robustness of our findings with different estimations and alternative measures.

## Predictive Evaluations

As explained earlier, Step 2 of building predictive insights about future success is to use insights from Step 1 to form a predictive model in which factors considered in the explanatory model are used as inputs to predict the raised capital and the venture’s ability to land customers. To do so, we formed four predictive classification tasks with variables used in Equation (2). Tasks 1 and 2 focus on predicting raised capital (a value of 1 if the standardized value of fundraising in that round exceeds 1, 0 otherwise). In Task 1, pre-ready-tolaunch rounds are used to predict raised capital in post-readyto-launch rounds, while in Task 2, round t is used to predict raised capital in round t+1. Tasks 3 and 4 focus on predicting fundraising success (as defined in Equation 2). In Task 3, preready-to-launch rounds are used to predict fundraising in postready-to-launch rounds, while in Task 4, round t is used to predict fundraising in round t+1. For each task, logistic, decision-tree, random-forest, naive Bayesian, and neural network classifiers are used for prediction, with a 60/40 sample split for development and validation. The results (Table 6) show that across the four tasks, predictive models that consider LSO, LSC, and their interaction, compared with models that do not, result in an average accuracy increase of about 15%. This further highlights the predictive value of a comparative view of a B2BNV’s narratives on social media.

## Unfolding the Reason for Better Predictions

We have proposed that lingual similarity to customers and dissimilarity to competitors can underscore a B2BNV’s identity, which is relatable to customers and distinguished among competitors. Such lingual similarity or dissimilarity can, in turn, indicate the quality of actions in the organizational field in later stages. If this contention is correct, lingual similarity (dissimilarity) to customers (competitors) will be able to explain tangible indicators of later success in the organizational field, consisting of customers and competitors. Two critical outcomes for B2BNVs are successfully landing customers and winning industry competitions for their product/service. Landing a new customer, at least in part, shows successful reach and marketing in the customer industry, as well as the ability to beat the competition. Also, winning competitions demonstrates the ability of the B2BNV to distinguish itself from competitors.

To test these contentions, we defined two more predictive tasks with variables used in Equation (2), measured at preready-to-launch rounds used to predict the landing of a new customer (Task 5) and the winning of industry competitions or awards (Task 6) in post-ready-to-launch rounds. The results are also presented in Table 7. The results are noteworthy in two aspects. First, the models that do not consider LSO, LSC, and their interaction perform considerably lower, by an average of 14%, in predicting the landing of customers and the winning of competitions compared with the same models predicting fundraising success. Second, the models considering LSO, LSC, and their interaction perform persistently better, by an average of 3%, in predicting the landing of customers and the winning of competitions compared with the same models predicting fundraising success. ## Discussion

## Theoretical Contributions

This paper makes several theoretical contributions. First, it integrates the insights from organizational narrative theories (Golant & Silince, 2007; Fischer & Rueber, 2011, 2014) and social-media analytics studies (e.g., Kwon & Sung, 2011; Anglin et al., 2018) to examine a comparative view of organizational narratives. Specifically, our results show that above and beyond the known aspects of a B2BNV’s SMNs per the focused view, i.e., readability and sentiment of SMNs, the (dis)similarity of a B2BNV’s SMNs relative to the SMNs of competitors and customers has a strong association with raised capital and success, in terms of either a successful exit in the next round or a successful completion of the next round of fundraising. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to show that such a comparative view of SMNs helps in predicting business success. This view contrasts with and departs from the dominantly focused view of organizational narratives in both organizational research (e.g., Garud et al., 2014; Bartel & Garud, 2009) and studies about SMNs in the IS literature (e.g., Burtch et al., 2013; Jin et al., 2017). Our main theoretical implication is that a comparative view of a B2BNV’s narratives vis-à-vis those of its competitors and customers provides robust insights into how well the B2BNV distinguishes itself from competitors and relates to customers. Such insights cannot be attained by considering the SMNs of a firm in isolation.

Second, we showcase the predictive value of SMNs in explaining a new venture’s success in a later round of fundraising. We do this by considering raised capital as well as success in terms of landing customers, generating revenue, winning competitions and patents, and receiving customer endorsements. Most studies on social media analytics and new-venture fundraising stop at highlighting a contemporaneous association between lingual facets of social media, e.g., its readability, and entrepreneurial success (e.g., Burtch et al., 2013; Hong et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2017), with the view of success being limited to raised capital. This study goes further by showing an association between facets of early-stage communication and success in later stages while considering a broader set of indicators, such as attracting new customers, generating revenue, and successfully protecting the firm’s innovations (through patenting).

Finally, most of the evidence on social media used by new ventures is developed in the context of crowdfunding (Burtch et al., 2013), mainly for ventures with prosocial/public goods (e.g., Hong et al., 2018). By contrast, this study highlights the informative nature of SMNs in the less studied context of B2BNVs, which produce goods and services that are not consumed by the general public. Due to their complex organizational field, including both competitor and customer firms, B2BNVs create more information asymmetries for investors. Thus, we extend the scope of theories on social media and entrepreneurship to contexts with greater information asymmetries.

## Implications for Practice

This study also has several implications for practice. By leveraging the organizing power of language and applying analytics techniques to SMNs, early-stage investors are provided with some easy-to-interpret indicators. We show that a comparative view of a new venture’s daily SMNs that considers both content and style of language can reliably predict successful responses to the organizational field in later stages of fundraising. At a minimum, this predictive input allows venture capital markets, such as SeedInvest, to provide their users with insights that may help them to shortlist promising new ventures. Our work can be equally useful for venture capital firms such as Correlation Ventures that intend to make data-driven investments in a short period of time. We suggest that these venture capital firms (1) identify the competitor and customer groups of a target B2BNV, (2) compare the corpora of SMNs generated by the B2BNV to those generated by the competitor and customer groups, and (3) identify B2BNVs that show lingual similarities to the customer group and lingual dissimilarities to the competitor group.

The study also sheds light on the importance of style in the broader application of comparative linguistics. Past IS research has mostly relied on comparing the content, e.g., the prototypicality measure used by Johnson et al. (2015) and the business-proximity measure based on Shi et al. (2016). This study takes a significant step forward by demonstrating that elements of a message’s structure, such as its readability, can add to content-comparison insights. We show that a multidimensional view of lingual similarity of social media communication, i.e., one that concurrently considers the content as well as the style of communication, better captures informative differences across social media communications. As such, we recommend that venture capital markets and venture capital firms, in assessing the lingual similarities mentioned in Steps 2 and 3, consider the style of SMNs (e.g., readability) in addition to the usual emphasis on content (e.g., topics covered and sentiment of messaging). Our predictive models suggest that combining information from content and style similarities in a composite measure of lingual similarity yields considerable predictive accuracy.

## Limitations

The study’s results should be viewed in light of its limitations. First, this study focuses on B2BNVs for two reasons: (1) their competitive organizational field consists of two disparate worlds, i.e., competitor and customer firms, and is thus more complex than other new ventures (e.g., B2C firms); (2) data about a B2BNV’s customers (but not for other new ventures, such as B2C firms) are available from our data source, AngelPlat. Further research is needed to examine whether the observed consequences of lingual similarity/dissimilarity via social media would apply to other new ventures (e.g., new B2C ventures).

Second, although we control for size, length of fundraising rounds, and the extent to which angels fund B2BNVs in our sample, our sample significantly differs in these aspects from B2BNVs on Crunchbase (Table 4, Panel B). Therefore, caution about the generalizability of our results is warranted and we encourage replication studies using other samples.

Third, this study’s focus is limited to firms’ communications on Twitter, excluding communications through other outlets (including websites and other social media platforms). Whether other outlets, given their different goals and structures, would have a similar impact on investors’ funding decisions needs to be examined.

Finally, our access to various characteristics of the B2BNVs, and to their fundraising rounds, is limited to the data gathered by AngelPlat. However, we find that the scope and depth of the data collected by AngelPlat are on par with that collected by other prominent early-stage investors, such as YCombinator, and the results are robust with respect to a B2BNV’s potential self-selection and potential difference from data reporting fundraising rounds on Crunchbase.<sup>12</sup>

## Conclusion

In sum, predictive analytics is gaining steam in the U.S. venture capital market. For instance, Correlation Ventures is a pioneering venture capital firm<sup>13</sup> that funds new ventures over two weeks using predictive analytics. The idea of using analytics to make venture capital investments is becoming popular for two reasons. First, the traditional means of selecting an investment target are often ineffective, producing success in only 40% of cases.<sup>14</sup> Second, start-ups needing early-stage funding differ from angel investors and venture capitalists in terms of both the speed of investment decisions and the magnitude of investments. Whereas those who are investing often seek opportunities to make large investments and take their time to make decisions, start-ups needing early-stage funding typically seek smaller investments and quicker investment decisions. According to Paul Graham, the co-founder of YCombinator:

The growing disconnect is between VCs, whose current business model requires them to invest large amounts, and a large class of start-ups that need less than they used to. Increasingly, start-ups want a couple hundred thousand dollars, not a couple million.<sup>15</sup>

Against this backdrop, new entrepreneurial marketplaces, such as SeedInvest and Angel Investment Network, are using the power of data archiving and analytics to connect investors to new ventures seeking funding, especially those in early stages. In this paper, we argue and show that the application of comparative analytics to SMNs by new ventures can be a valuable tool in investors’ arsenals, with the information obtained from a comparative view of a venture’s SMNs increasing predictive accuracy of later success by about 15%.

## Sample Construction and Dealing with Right Censorship

It is possible that our sample suffers from right censorship: In this case, either a B2BNV would start a round of fundraising but not finish it, or the study would end before a B2BNV’s round of fundraising finishes. Right censorship, if severely present and left unaccounted for, can bias results (Wooldridge, 2002). To ensure that data does not suffer from right censorship, we used the following approach so that the fundraising status of each B2BNV in its last known round in our data could be identified at all times.

For each round of fundraising, a value for the amount raised was captured and the presence of a next round or an exit from the database was stored in a marker variable for each round’s data. For B2BNVs making a successful exit after a given round t, e.g., due to acquisition, merger, or entering IPO, the given round t after which they announced the successful exit was considered their exit round, and these ventures are assigned a marker of “successful exit,” as the designation of their exit round. AngelPlat also indicates if a venture fails to raise sufficient funds and goes out of business. We marked these ventures with an “unsuccessful exit” marker, and their last known started (but unfinished) round was treated as the exit round for that venture in our data. When such failure data about the last known started (but unfinished) round was missing from AngelPlat itself, we searched other sources, such as Crunchbase, VentureXpert, and the website or social media feed of the ventures to collect data regarding capital raised (Raised\_Capital) and other variables to the extent possible in each year and each round. If the search in alternative resources was unsuccessful, we replaced the missing values of independent variables (other than LSO, LSC, and their interaction) in that round t with the average contemporaneous values (at time t) of that variable in the five nearest ventures to that focal venture, identified based on their propensity scores in the round before the focal round (t-1) (estimated considering all the independent and control variables in the model). In other cases where missing information did not allow us to estimate LSO and LSC (e.g., in cases when AngelPlat could not successfully identify target customer and competitor groups), the observation was dropped from the sample (72 observations). Also, if a matching round did not exist or tweets were not available, that observation was dropped. We imputed 38 control variables across 23 venture-round observations in our sample. When observations with imputed variables were dropped from the sample, the results remained qualitatively unchanged.

To deal with observations that start a round of fundraising but data about the raised capital in that round is missing both from AngelPlat and other public sources, we considered the longest fundraising round in the sample, i.e., 14 months, as the cutoff after which we assumed their fundraising in that round had ended with \$0 raised. We marked these ventures as “unsuccessful exit” and considered the unfinished round with a length of 14 months as its exit round in the data (we did not have any ventures that reappeared after not reporting funds raised for a round).

The 16-month gap between the end of data collection and the data analysis allowed us to ensure that not raising capital in rounds that started close to the end of our data collection was not due to right censorship (i.e., the ending of the study period). Specifically, the latest rounds reported in our sample observations started in January 2019, and we were able to obtain the fundraising information for all those rounds. Also, we marked any venture in which 14 months passed since their last round, which did not start a new round, or which did not have a successful exit, either as reported to AngelPlat or as captured in alternative sources (e.g., Crunchbase, VentureXpert, Twitter, the venture’s website), with an “unsuccessful exit” and considered its last known round its exit round. We did not have any observations that were at the tail end of our timeframe, whose last round ended sooner than 14 months, or who had no rounds or exit information.

The rest of the observations that had an ongoing, active fundraising status are marked “ongoing fundraising” for their last-known status. This process ensured that we did not have any B2BNV with an “unknown” status for its last-known round. We assigned a value of 0 to the raised capital in 98 observations and marked 195 B2BNVs as having an unsuccessful exit, 78 B2BNVs as having a successful exit, and the rest (301) as ongoing. This process ensured that all B2BNVs had a known status if they exited the sample before the study’s window was over and ensure that the fundraising status of B2BNVs starting a round close to the end of our study period was also determined. Model 5.5 in Table 5 uses the exit/not-exit status of each B2NBV as the outcome variable to ensure that the omission of observations due to missing raised capital does not systematically bias the coefficient estimates, specifically due to right censoring.

## Robustness Tests

We examined the sensitivity of our results by estimating the coefficients of Equation (1) through alternative measures of independent and dependent variables (Table C). We started these analyses by using a more sophisticated way of integrating Shi et al.’s (2016) measure of topi similarity with Johnson et al.’s (2015) features of positive sentiment and readability. In our original approach, we estimated pairwise topic similarity using a cosine similarity for the topic-weight matrix of the focal venture and its referent firm (in customer or competitor group). However, the pairwise sentiment and readability similarities were estimated separately using a simple score-differencing approach. This approach still shows predictive promise but a possible shortcoming is that, by nature, we assume orthogonality between the lingual facets of topic sentiment, and readability. Nonetheless, advances in natural language programming suggest that these facets may have interdependent distributions and cannot be assumed as orthogonal.

To assess whether more nuanced measures improve the model’s performance, we followed Lin and He’s (2009) method, which considers textual labels (e.g., sentiment labels) jointly with topics and assigns topic-label weights instead of considering just the topic weights in each document. Such a topic-and-label vector for each B2BNV’s corpus of tweets allowed us to compare the cosine similarity of the weight matrix of tweets between each B2BNV and another firm. This matrix carries both information about the topics and information about other lingual facets, summarized in some labels (i.e., sentiment and readability). To categorize a B2BNV’s corpus of tweets in a round into a lingual label, we used nine combinations of high, medium, and low values of positivity of sentiment of a word (directly extracted from ANEW) and readability of a word (number of characters) as the lingual labels to be integrated with the topic-weight matrix extracted in our original analyses. Any word in our dictionary can be assigned to labels such as neutral-high readability, positive-low readability, etc. We estimated Lin and He’s (2009) three hyperparameters (??, β, and γ) using a Monte Carlo method. Then, we estimated the weights for each topic-label z (TLz, z = 1,…,108 [= 12 topics × 9 labels]) for each B2BNV’s corpus in each round, and for its customers’ (competitors’), and considered the cosine similarity between the topic-label vectors of each pair the pair’s lingual similarity. The lingual similarity between a B2BNV and its referent group (customer or competitor) is the average of its topic-label pairwise similarity with the members of the referent group. Model C1 (Table C) reports these results, which show that the coefficient estimates remain similar to those given in Table 5. Moreover, we ran our predictive models with this more sophisticated measure of lingual similarity and found that the accuracy-rate changes in our five models vary between -1% and +3%. Thus, despite the theoretical improvements made by this nuanced measurement, the models’ predictive accuracy does not change much.

In another alternative approach (Model C2), we tested an even simpler measure of topic similarity. In this model, we present the coefficient estimates when a measure of prototypicality, instead of cosine similarity of the topical matrix, is used to estimate pairwise topic similarities. We followed a similar approach to Johnson et al. (2015) and adopted the statistical language-modeling technique developed by Jurafsky and Martin (2000) and calculated the word entropy between the venture’s tweets and its referent group’s corpus of tweets in each round. We first combined the corpus of tweets belonging to a venture with the corpus of tweets belonging to the referent group (customer or competitor). Then, we followed the three-step approach suggested by Johnson et al. (2015) to estimate the entropy of each sentence in a tweet: (1) estimating the probability of bigrams (combinations of two words) appearing in a corpus, (2) estimating the probability of a sentence appearing in a corpus (by multiplying the probability of all bigrams building the sentence), and (3) normalizing the value obtained in (2) by taking the negative log of its probability and dividing it by the number of words in the sentence. The higher a sentence’s entropy, the lower its prototypicality to the other sentences in a corpus (i.e., the sentence is more unique). We computed the mean of sentence entropy across all sentences comprising the corpus of tweets of a focal venture and used the inverse of the resulting value as a proxy of topic similarity. Model C2 also shows coefficient estimates that are very similar to those obtained in our main analysis. Model C3 presents the results of estimation when trigrams, instead of bigrams used in Model C2, are used to measure prototypicality.

Further, because Twitter affords using hashtags to communicate shared ideas, we measured the number of overlapping hashtags used by a venture and its referent (a customer or competitor) normalized by the number of all hashtags used by both the focal venture and the other firm in the corpus and replace it with our measure of pairwise topic similarity. Although our results (in Model C4) remain qualitatively unchanged from our main analysis, they show smaller effect sizes, which may be due to the limitations of hashtags in communicating shared industry-wide topics. Model C5 presents the results when using an alternative dictionary, AFINN, to evaluate each word’s sentiment. Model C6 shows the estimates when using a Gunning Frequency of Gobbledygook index (see DuBay [2004] for a description) to measure readability, instead of the ARI.

Models C7 and C8 in Table C present the estimates with alternative measures of the dependent variable. While the natural log of the raised capital provides a fair measure of a new venture’s success, depending on the fundraising stage, the average raised capital can be considerably different. Although we controlled for the stage of fundraising in the original estimation, we attempted another way of normalizing raised capital: dividing the natural log of each venture’s raised capital in a stage of fundraising (e.g., Stage A) by the average value of the natural log of raised capital by all the ventures in the sample in the same stage. Model C7 presents the results where this alternative measure was used as the dependent variable.

Also, while Equation (2) estimates success with either an “ongoing” or “successful exit” marker for the round after t, an alternative way of modeling survival is to specifically focus on the hazard of an unsuccessful exit based on lingual similarities observed in the first round of pre-ready-to-launch in our sample. In such an estimation, survival is rightcensored, and failure events are defined by “unsuccessful exit” markers. Using a Cox Proportional Hazard model as a survival analysis, we modeled the following: hi(t) = h0(t) exp(βX), where hi(t) is the hazard of failure (“unsuccessful exit”) for a B2BNV, h0(t) is the baseline hazard of failure in the sample, X is the matrix of explanatory variables used in Equation (20 and measured in the first pre-ready-to-launch round, and t is the hazard time, which is the number of months from the end of the first round in our sample to the time that a venture makes an unsuccessful exit, or the current date, April 2020, with an exit condition defined as the time of reaching a successful exit marker for those ventures that ended up in a successful exit scenario, e.g., becoming acquired or going public. For 544 B2BNVs with a pre-ready-to-launch round completed in our sample, we estimated the Cox regression. Model C8 reports the hazard ratios estimated from this analysis.
