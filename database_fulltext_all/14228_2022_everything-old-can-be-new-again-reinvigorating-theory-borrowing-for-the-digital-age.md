# EVERYTHING OLD CAN BE NEW AGAIN: REINVIGORATING THEORY BORROWING FOR THE DIGITAL AGE<sup>1</sup>

Dan Jiang Department of MIS, College of Business, East Carolina University, Greenville, NC, U.S.A. {jiangd17@ecu.edu}

Lianlian (Dorothy) Jiang Department of Decision & Information Sciences, Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, Houston, TX, U.S.A. {ljiang@central.uh.edu}

Jackie London Jr. Department of Information Systems, Law and Operations, Sellinger School of Business, Loyola University Maryland, Baltimore MD, U.S.A. {jlondon1@loyola.edu}

Varun Grover Department of Information Systems, Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR, U.S.A. {vgrover@uark.edu}

Heshan Sun Management Information Systems Division, Price College of Business, University of Oklahoma, Norman, OK, U.S.A. {sunh@ou.edu}

It has been argued that the theory borrowing practices in IS research have become workmanlike— appropriate and effective, but lacking innovation. This concern is particularly salient at a time when digital phenomena are profoundly transforming society. Therefore, it is legitimate to ask: Are our theory borrowing practices hampering our ability to grapple with revolutionary developments in IS, and if so, what can be done? Through an investigation of the field’s borrowing of transaction cost economics theory, we find extant IS research largely (1) borrows for theory testing within the IS context, (2) develops models that uninspiringly reflect the borrowed theory, and (3) treats the IS as an exogenous actor. In this article, we propose an alternative approach to theory borrowing, inspired by conceptual blending theory. Our approach focuses on the structural nature of IS phenomena and borrowed theories. Such a structure-based approach can reveal correspondence between IS phenomena and unexpected reference theories while also highlighting discrepancies that serve as an opportunity for novel integrations of an information system into the reference theory. We contend that this approach can infuse flexibility into our theory borrowing practices in ways that will increase our capacity for developing innovative explanations of emerging phenomena.

Keywords: Innovative theory borrowing, conceptual blending theory, theorizing in the IS field, digital phenomena, transaction cost economics

## Introduction

Since Peter Keen’s keynote speech at the inaugural ICIS conference, during which he encouraged information systems (IS) researchers to adopt a reference discipline approach to establishing and assessing research quality, theory borrowing has been an important part of the field’s effort to establish itself as a serious repository of scientific knowledge (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015). By borrowing theories from reference disciplines, IS research has achieved respectability and success. In fact, when assessed with standard measures of academic influence such as citation count and impact factor, the field ranks equivalent to or above many traditional management disciplines (Grover, 2012; Grover & Lyytinen, 2015; Jiang et al., 2017; Straub & Anderson, 2010).

However, there is evidence that continuing our current approach toward borrowing is having unintended consequences and our knowledge products often fail to engage with the IS artifact in novel ways. As we will show, the field’s theory borrowing has been uncritical, largely retesting borrowed social, psychological, organizational, or economic theories in the IS context without rendering new theoretical insight or deeply engaging with IS-specific phenomena. The implications of such practices are that we relegate a large portion of our knowledge contribution to external disciplines that have little interest in or concern for the idiosyncrasies of IS phenomena.

The paucity of innovative theorizing is further exacerbated by the emergence of digital phenomena, such as social media, mobile, big data, cloud, and IoT technologies. Often, such platforms offer access to large datasets through APIs and web scraping that tempt researchers to let the data lead. There is some indication that such localized, low-level datasets foster practice-oriented research questions and may unintentionally discourage theoretical abstraction and deeper intellectual engagement as scholars elevate predictive accuracy over conceptual depth (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Grover et al., 2020). Also, recent work indicates that digital phenomena are more fluid and combinatorial than prior instantiations of IS artifacts (Yoo et al., 2010), and researchers may find it challenging to identify reference theories that are similarly elastic. Although such phenomena offer fertile ground for novel theorizing, borrowing practices that seek to retrofit familiar theoretical models may be insufficient to take advantage of such opportunities.

Some prominent voices have recognized the need for improving the field’s borrowing practices, but guidance has been diverse and lacking in specificity. Prior recommendations have called for greater collaboration with outside disciplines (Baskerville & Myers, 2002), greater focus on phenomena (Gregor, 2014), and broadening theory definitions to include problems and solutions (Markus, 2014). Though well-intentioned, such recommendations tend to be abstract and difficult to apply. Also, when researchers have ventured to offer more actionable guidance such as focusing on how research models are contextualized to IS phenomena (Hong et al., 2013; Moeini et al., 2020), the IS artifact tends to remain an exogenous factor and not integral to the theoretical model.

We believe that innovative theorizing in our field is hampered because we tend to borrow theories that are broad and highly congruent with the focal IS phenomenon’s context. Such an approach limits the need for critical engagement with the borrowed theory and increases the likelihood of the IS artifact remaining undertheorized. These practices are problematic because when “fit” between the theory and the phenomenon is prioritized, the opportunities for the theorist to infuse innovation into the theory are necessarily constrained. That is, a familiar framing is unlikely to result in novel insights—although it may be deemed theoretically sound—because the framing is often accompanied by a predetermined network of roles and relationships. Also, because reference disciplines are often agnostic about the theoretical import of the IT artifact, the adoption of agreed-upon models diminishes the peculiar nature of the IS phenomenon.

It is our contention that the status quo regarding the field’s borrowing practices is neither helpful nor productive in describing an increasingly digital landscape that continues to evolve. Emerging digital phenomena are unique in human history, both changing existing relationships—organizational, social, technical, etc.—and introducing new ways of relating. The inherent newness of such phenomena demands fresh perspectives and greater innovation in the theoretical models that explain them. While one could argue that indigenous theorizing (from literature or the creative mind) and big datadriven approaches<sup>2</sup> could address this issue, we advocate for a course adjustment. That is, as we seek new modes of innovative theorizing, let us also correct our posture toward borrowing and seek inspiration from the vast repositories of theoretical knowledge found in other fields.

The management field has devoted some attention to the use of borrowed theories for innovative theorizing. Two main approaches have emerged: the metaphorical approach and the conceptual blending approach. The metaphorical approach leverages comparisons between an organizational concept (e.g., organizations) and a familiar concept (e.g., prisons) to draw novel conclusions about the focal concept (Cornelissen, 2005; Oswick et al., 2011; Tsoukas, 1991). More recent contributions draw on conceptual blending theory (CBT) (Cornelissen & Durand, 2012; Cornelissen, 2005; Oswick et al., 2011) to show how blending two ideas (e.g., theory and phenomena) can yield new ideas (Fauconnier & Turner, 1998). Common to both approaches is the contention that existing ideas can seed novel insights and that those ideas need not be new in toto to be new in situ.

We believe that the fundamental insights of CBT— orthogonality among concepts increases the novelty of the blended concept—can be a source of innovation when applied to the early stages of the theory borrowing process. This has implications for the ways in which researchers identify and formulate research questions, review relevant literature, and select appropriate reference theories. Therefore, in this Issues & Opinions article, we draw on CBT to show how a structurebased<sup>3</sup> perspective on theory building can create opportunities for novelty during the early stages of theory borrowing that will likely facilitate innovative theorizing. Specifically, our thesis is that by acknowledging the structural nature of both the IS phenomenon and the borrowed theory, researchers might avoid models that overemphasize congruence and undertheorize the IS artifact.

In the following sections, we demonstrate the problem through an investigation of the field’s borrowing of transaction cost economics (TCE). Then, we assess various perspectives on innovating with borrowed theory. Next, we highlight the value of a structure-based perspective and suggest how such a perspective introduces novelty into our borrowing practices. We conclude with a discussion of our approach and its implications for various stakeholders.

## Testing Our Premise: Borrowing Transaction Cost Economics

Our premise is comprised of three primary contentions: (1) borrowed theories are selected for “fit” with the phenomenon to be examined, (2) borrowed theories are uncritically adopted and do not change significantly when applied to IS phenomena, and (3) there is little evidence of deeper engagement with the IT artifact, which is usually extrinsic to the borrowed theory. To test our premise, we focus on the field’s borrowing of a single example theory: TCE.<sup>4</sup> We formed a representative sample of 96 research articles from the AIS “Basket of Eight” journals and Management Science. These journals represent the most influential IS journals and collectively provide a representative view of the field’s practices vis-à-vis theory borrowing.

Fit with Phenomena: To understand the extent to which borrowed theories are selected for congruence with the focal IS phenomenon, we coded papers according to a domain classification scheme developed by Vessey et al. (2002). Table 1 indicates that most researchers borrow TCE (80%) to explain inter- and intra-organizational issues such as outsourcing, governance, and competitive strategy. This suggests that IS researchers view TCE as a theory that primarily explains buyer-seller relationship governance. Fewer than 10% of research projects apply TCE to domains that diverge from the field’s dominant view of TCE, with seven projects applying TCE to technology diffusion and one to issues related to knowledge management.

Theory Adaptation: To further investigate the field’s borrowing practices with regard to theory adaptation, we developed a taxonomy that identifies three levels of adaptation: instantiation, modification, and extension (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015). This taxonomy categorizes the degree to which TCE’s constructs (the “what” of theory), relationships (the “how” of theory), and logic (the “why” of theory) are adapted within the who, when, and where (context) of an IS phenomenon (Whetten, 1989). Thus, instantiation refers to testing the theory’s propositions in a new context and involves borrowing one or more components of the theory without modification. Modification is a moderate level of theory adaptation, which engages the theory by contextualizing its constructs to fit the IT context but assumes the borrowed theory’s logic and relationships to be consistent and stable across contexts. Extension is the highest level of theory adaptation and has three major practices that each uniquely alter the underlying logic of the borrowed theory. Table 2 explains each level and its variants and provides an example from our sample of each.

Our analysis reveals that instantiation (Level 1) is the dominant theory borrowing model in the IS field, with 65% of papers mostly preserving the structure and logic of TCE. Such an approach involves researchers using TCE as a template for testable models, with core constructs and relationships (e.g., assets specificity, uncertainty, transaction costs, governance mode) borrowed as-is and tested in the context of IS phenomena.

A smaller portion (35%) of the papers in our sample modify (Level 2) and/or reconceptualize (Level 3) TCE’s structure to reflect the unique nature of the IS phenomenon.<sup>5</sup> In these papers, theory adaptation occurs at the construct level, as we did not find evidence of attempts to change TCE’s configuration (how) or logic (why). Engagement with IT Artifact: In the vast majority (95%) of models examined in our sample, the IT artifact was implicit in the context (e.g., software outsourcing), added as a moderator (e.g., IT investment), studied as a catalyst (e.g., in reducing uncertainty), or incorporated through some minor adaptation of TCE constructs (e.g., asset specificity became software specificity). There were very few cases where IT-specific constructs were developed and implicated in theoretical logic (e.g., explicit coordination, Clemson et al 1993; cospecialization of IT assets, Hann & Weber 1996; IT detachability, Tanriverdi et al., 2007).<sup>6</sup>

In sum, we find that our theory borrowing practices, if accurately represented by our borrowing of TCE, tend to be limited in three distinct but related ways. First, researchers tend toward a narrow conceptualization of theories, restricting their applicability to contexts similar to that of the original theory. Second, researchers tend to adopt theories uncritically. Although these two practices can lead to a cumulative tradition based on a consistent understanding of the theory, they can also lead to path dependencies, inertia, and difficulty in proposing and observing findings that deviate from the majority view. Third, the IT phenomenon is largely treated as exogenous to the theory. These observations are particularly discouraging, given the wide applicability of TCE to a number of new and emerging digital phenomena ranging from participating in the sharing economy to the use of decentralized public ledgers (i.e., blockchain) (Cuypers et al., 2021). In the following sections, we consider several perspectives on the ways in which we might combat those tendencies that limit our ability to extract innovative insights from borrowed theories.

## Innovative Theorizing through Theory Borrowing

The primary goal of the theorizing process is to explain and predict the causes and consequences of some focal phenomenon. In addition, there is an oft-unspoken expectation within the academy that such contributions be new, novel (Oswick et al., 2011), interesting (Davis, 1971), fresh (Oswick et al., 2011), innovative (Straub, 2009), and creative (Weick, 1989). Thus, a fundamental challenge for theory borrowing is to create something new and insightful regarding our phenomena from something old and familiar.

In IS, there is limited guidance for researchers concerned with developing innovative theory through theory borrowing. The guidance that does exist tends to focus on how researchers might develop a more nuanced understanding of IT phenomena by incorporating IT artifacts into borrowed theories (Hong et al., 2013; Moeini et al., 2020). For example, Hong et al. (2013) advocate for a contextualization approach based on Whetten et al. (2009) and propose guidelines for contextualizing IS factors into borrowed theories. This approach often involves reducing the reference theory’s level of abstraction so that either IT contextual factors (e.g., moderators or mediators) might be incorporated, or reference theory constructs might be decomposed into context-specific instantiations that are relevant to the focal phenomenon.

Fortunately, theory borrowing is an issue that concerns many referent fields and two approaches have emerged that offer guidance to those developing innovative theory from borrowed theories. First, the transformative metaphor approach contends that borrowed theories can serve as analogies for emerging phenomena and that novel insights emerge as the theorist contemplates the similarities between borrowed theory and focal phenomenon. The transformative approach begins by distinguishing between a source domain and a target domain (Tsoukas, 1991). The source domain refers to a familiar concept (e.g., memory), while the target domain refers to an organizational concept that researchers want to understand (e.g., organizational decision-making). Researchers then use analogical thinking to identify the correspondence between the source and target (Holyoak & Thagard, 1997). Insights can be drawn from metaphors as researchers interrogate the “deep identities” (i.e., the correspondence between source and target) of the target and source domain and determine “the extent to which knowledge about X’s nature and behavior (i.e., the source domain) can be transferred to the study of Y’s nature and behavior (i.e., the target domain)” (Tsoukas, 1991, p. 573). Ketokivi et al. (2017) expanded these ideas and developed a framework that recasts theory building as an argumentation process and illustrates how analogies can be used to introduce novel explanations or challenge institutionalized perspectives. Also, Accard (2020) extended the concept of deep identity, proposing that transformations may be isomorphic (1-to-1 mapping of everything in X to Y) or homomorphic (retaining only the higher-level structure of the mapping), with homomorphic transformations more likely to yield deeper insight into the target phenomenon.

A second approach—the conceptual blending approach— moves beyond the transformative metaphor approach and argues that many opportunities for innovation can be found in contemplating the dissimilarities between target and source domains (Cornelissen & Durand, 2012; Cornelissen, 2005; Oswick et al., 2011). As such, the conceptual blending approach involves blending two (or more) input mental spaces, i.e., “small conceptual packets constructed as we think and talk, for purposes of local understanding and action” (Fauconnier & Turner, 1998, p. 137) and their organizing structures—i.e., unifying structures that specify the roles conceptual elements play and how they are related. Organizing structures and mental spaces are concepts that originate from cognitive linguistics and are used to describe the makeup of conceptual domains (Sweetser, 1990; Turner, 1991). Each mental space consists of the relevant elements, activities, events, and participants that jointly form the observer’s knowledge about that concept, and these elements are organized by a unifying organizing structure. The blending process begins with the identification of the generic structure—the conceptual overlap between the input spaces—which serves as the initial organizing structure for the blend.<sup>7</sup> Then, discrepant elements are selectively projected into the blended space, effectively blending the input spaces into a new blended concept.

The choices made during the blending process affect the novelty of the resultant blend. Fauconnier and Turner (1998) maintain that blending concepts with discrepant elements or structures requires more cognitive effort and is likely to yield more imaginative insights while blending concepts that are highly congruent is easier but is likely to produce expected or less interesting ideas. For example, to explain the competitive environment, one might describe the competition among market rivals as a heavyweight bout. Such a description suggests a blend of two distinct input spaces: heavyweight boxing and business competition. The boxing input space may suggest multiple elements (e.g., two boxers, a referee, rounds, judges, an audience, announcers, a ring, etc.) and the organizing frame explains how these elements relate (e.g., during rounds, boxers in a ring are trying to knock out their opponent, referees ensure a fair fight, announcers report the action, the audience looks on and cheers). Likewise, the business competition concept is comprised of elements (e.g., businesses, stakeholders, products/services, raw materials, etc.) organized by an overarching structure (e.g., businesses competing for a limited supply of consumers, materials, and suppliers in the same industry). While “competition among parties” forms the basis of a generic structure, the remaining elements and roles are discrepant with no clear corollary. As the blend is elaborated, additional elements and roles are selectively incorporated to draw further insight into the competitive environment: competition for employees (business) among multiple rivals (businesses) is pugilistic (boxing) and comes in waves (boxing).

Several scholars have explored the efficacy of the conceptual blending approach for theory borrowing. Cornelissen (2005) proposes a domain interaction model of metaphors, arguing that there should be initial “inherent structures” for both target and source domains in a metaphor. The commonality between the two organizing structures (i.e., generic structure) serves as the basis of the metaphor because it “provides us with a template for constructing or ‘blending’ correspondences, invites us to make comparisons for a further ‘filling in’ or specification, and directs our attention to unexpected or subtle parallels and analogies” (Cornelissen, 2005, p. 756). Oswick et al. (2011) advocate an approach to blending with borrowed theories that emphasizes differences instead of similarities to inspire original and radical theories. They call for a two-way analogical process of conceptual blending, which involves dissonant thinking, disanalogy, and counterfactual reasoning.

## Limitations of Existing Approaches

While the above-mentioned approaches can help researchers develop better theory borrowing practices, they have important limitations that may lessen their value for the purpose of innovative theorizing with borrowed theories. First, a contextualization approach considers the IS phenomena at a more specific level than is specified by the reference theory. To achieve this, the researcher must incorporate context-specific constructs into the original theoretical framework/model. While useful, such an approach may further exacerbate the problem of IT remaining exogenous to the theory as it encourages the treatment of IT elements as randomly occurring rather than an essential part of the theoretical system. Also, when describing the process of decomposing the theory’s constructs into context-specific constructs, the description seems to emphasize contextspecific operationalization rather than the conceptualization of the construct. Further, this approach is primarily concerned with refining theories for an IS context rather than encouraging innovative theorizing.

A limitation of the transformative metaphor approach is that the resultant insights are limited by the degree of correspondence between the source theory and the target phenomenon. Oswick et al. (2002) argue that the transformative mode operates within the “cognitive comfort zone” of similarity and is therefore limited in its ability to draw out innovative insights into the phenomenon. Also, in the process of theory development, metaphors primarily work before the steps of formal theory building to help the researcher see organizational reality differently (Bacharach, 1989). For instance, imagining the organization as a machine allows one to see its composition as a series of mechanically interconnected parts. This metaphor is heuristic and suggestive but not declarative of a particular organizational phenomenon. Thus, the approaches based on metaphors could be beneficial to studies dedicated to understanding broader IS concepts but would offer limited guidance on specific IS phenomena, particularly digital phenomena that are emergent and unique.

Alternatively, if novelty comes from the structural differences between two input spaces, then a CBT approach could be instructive. However, this process is abstract and reliant upon the researchers’ own intuition and imagination. As Fauconnier and Turner (1998) admit: “projection in a network can occur in different ways and in different directions” and requires “imaginative transformations of elements and structure in integration network” (p. 183). That is, there is no script or “cookbook” offering concrete rules about how to conduct a blend. In the context of theory borrowing, this means that a conceptual blending approach provides no guidance on how to draw novel insights from a blend of a reference theory and a given IT phenomenon.

However, this does not mean that CBT and its core tenets have no value to the IS field. The process of theory borrowing involves many steps, including identifying the phenomenon of interest and research questions, reviewing literature, finding appropriate reference theories, creating models of constructs and relationships, collecting and analyzing data to test the model, etc. While CBT may not provide concrete guidance on creating models of constructs and relationships, the core principle of CBT can be applied to the critical early stages of theory borrowing. Specifically, CBT argues that once a generic structure is established between a phenomenon and a theory, novelty emerges as the researcher decides which structures and elements to integrate into the blend. Leaning toward too much similarity would lead to incremental blending and predictable outcomes while too much discrepancy would lead to nonsense. Thus, the key to achieving innovative blending is to strive for a balance between fit and divergence. Below, we describe the concept of “structure” as we elaborate on this key message.

## A Structure-Based Approach to Theory Borrowing

Our proposed approach helps theorists achieve such a balance by sensitizing researchers to the concept of organizing structure. In describing the import of this concept to theory borrowing, we answer three fundamental questions: (1) How can we identify an organizing structure? (2) How can we identify a generic structure to meet the requirement for blending? (3) How can we address structural differences between an IS phenomenon and a reference theory?

## How Can We Identify the Organizing Structure?

Typically, a theory is an account of some real-world phenomenon abstracted into a network of constructs, relationships, boundaries, and assumptions (Bacharach, 1989). When considered through a conceptual blending lens, such a definition is analogous to an organizing frame in that the what, how, and why of a theory define the roles and relationships that were observed in the original phenomenon. Within our field, when researchers borrow a reference theory, there is an implicit assumption that to accommodate the theory’s organizing structure, the original phenomenon and focal phenomenon must be of the same type (e.g., organizations governing or individuals behaving). However, it is not obviously the case that IS phenomena can or should be typified in such a way. In fact, phenomena emerging from the IT artifact are inherently dynamic and are thus likely to accommodate a variety of roles and relationships, contingent on factors related to the user, context, and purpose, among others. Such conceptual flexibility paired with the felt need for congruence can be problematic, as it creates the temptation to let the borrowed theory supersede in the definitional task rather than allowing the phenomenon to retain its idiosyncratic nature. To avoid this and achieve parity, the researcher must acknowledge that the studied phenomenon need not be conceived granularly but instead can have a similarly abstract organizing structure.<sup>8</sup> Just as theories were once abstracted from an original underlying phenomenon, IS phenomenon can also be abstracted into a network of roles and relations (Tunner & Fauconnier, 1995).

The subjective nature of the process of identifying an organizing structure has two important implications for theory borrowing: (1) the structure of a phenomenon is conceived by the researcher, (2) different organizing structures will elevate certain elements and relationships. First, the process of abstracting the phenomenon frees the theorist from the demands of fidelity. As an abstract narrative, the phenomenon may take on whatever framing the theorist deems appropriate. For example, it is possible to describe IT outsourcing as an adoption process and transactional process, and each has the potential to generate novel insight into the phenomenon. Second, the chosen organizing structure necessarily imposes a different network of roles and relations. Figure 1 illustrates how the researcher-conceived organizing structure differs between the adoption and transactional views of IT outsourcing. In this example, the transactional view implies two actors—buyers and sellers—and the mechanisms dictating their interactions. Conversely, the adoption view implies a single adopter and the artifact to be adopted. Our approach suggests that the elements in any phenomenon can be organized into such a network of elements: some are vital to the structure while others are not. By viewing a phenomenon structurally, we can compare the fundamental semantic differences between the abstracted IS phenomenon and the borrowed theory.<sup>9</sup>

## How Can We Identify a Generic Structure to Meet the Requirement for Blending?

According to Fauconnier and Turner (1998), the generic structure is an abstract network of roles and relationships shared by the organizing structures of the input spaces. It is the existence of such a generic structure that enables the blending of seemingly dissimilar concepts. In fact, Williamson (1985) implies that TCE is applicable to “such apparently unrelated phenomena as employment relation, aspects of regulation, certain nonstandard contracting practices, corporate governance, and even family organization” (p. 13) because they share a common generic structure. Conversely, blends lacking similarity result in nonsense (Fauconnier & Turner, 1998). Hence, it is important that researchers identify the generic structure linking the IT phenomenon and the borrowed theory.

Identifying a generic structure may not be straightforward and may require creative thinking on the part of the researcher. In the example above, if the researcher elects to structure IT outsourcing as an adoption process, it would imply the presence of roles such as an adopter and the objects being adopted. Because adoption is an instance of behavior, the researcher may decide to frame the IT outsourcing phenomenon within the theory of reasoned action (TRA) (Azjen & Fishbein, 1980). Although TRA’s organizing structure is broader than that of IT adoption, the two structures exhibit sufficient conceptual overlap to support blending. However, the overlap may be so extensive that there are too few discrepant elements or roles left to blend, thereby leaving little room to innovate.

Alternatively, Ciborra’s famous hospitality metaphor (Ciborra, 2002; Ciborra & Hanseth, 1998) can serve as a more challenging example. In describing an organization’s attitudes towards IT innovations, Ciborra claims that “it takes courage and a great deal of adaptation for the organization to adopt, or to host, the new, strange technology” (Ciborra, 1996, p. 15).

For Ciborra, such a narrative abstraction of the innovation process looks like the description of hospitality: “behavior that reveals a human effort to cope with the uncertainty and mystery of hosting a ‘stranger’” (Ciborra, 1996, p. 16). Though the adoption of innovations and the practice of hospitality seem to employ two different structures, Ciborra is able to find a generic structure that is common to both: inviting something foreign to your own territory. According to CBT, establishing such a generic space despite apparent incongruence affords many opportunities to develop novel insights.

Such efforts to identify the generic structure make the blending of two concepts possible and can help IS researchers draw more novel insight from our native IS phenomena. First, establishing a generic structure serves to explicitly define the conceptual overlap between the IS phenomenon and the theory. As is illustrated in Ciborra’s hospitality metaphor, the generic structure is the foundation from which the theorist draws insight based on the similarities that exist between the two. For example, a hospitality perspective of the IT adoption process would suggest that organizations need to be aware that the “guest” comes with its own culture and rituals; while guests must respect the host’s traditions, the host must practice improvisation and bricolage as they adapt their routines to accommodate the guest (Ciborra, 2002). In an IT outsourcing context, practitioners might then understand a user’s hesitance as an emotive response to a foreign technology (i.e., this does not look like our technology; this does not work like our technology). Such a perspective would indicate the need for user-centric interventions that nurture hospitality and cultural tolerance rather than designfocused interventions that address the extent to which the technology is useful or easy to use.

Second, establishing a generic structure helps identify concepts and relationships that cannot be explained through a simple metaphor and must therefore be either reconciled through blending or discarded as immaterial to the blend. Identifying such elements forces the theorist to consider issues the theory does not address and creates opportunities to explore the differences between the phenomenon and theory. Finally, the process of identifying the generic structure elevates the theory and the phenomenon to equal levels of importance and prevents an uncritical application of the theory to the phenomena that “locks in” the structure and creates path dependencies.

## How Can We Address Structural Differences Between an IS Phenomenon and a Reference Theory?

As discussed above, it is common for researchers to impose the borrowed theory’s structure on an IS phenomenon without consideration of its idiosyncrasies. While straightforward and easier to justify, such an approach is unlikely to reveal novel insight into phenomena that are outside the well-worn paths established through prior adoptions of the chosen theory. Reference theories almost never involve IS directly, and the structural elements of the borrowed theory are rarely challenged. However, IT elements are almost always new to the original organizing structure, and it is reasonable to believe that the inclusion of such IT elements could trigger changes to the theoretical structure, particularly in the context of digital technologies. Digitalization was defined as a “sociotechnical process of applying digitizing techniques to broader social and institutional contexts that render digital technologies infrastructural” (Tilson et al., 2010, p. 749). Therefore, it is important for IS researchers to be able to comfortably express these divergent roles and relationships.

The thought of dwelling on the discrepancies between a borrowed theory and the IS phenomenon can be daunting but doing so need not be complex and it is likely to yield the most novel insight. Regarding the generic structure, a natural benefit of identifying those elements and roles that are in is that it implicitly identifies the elements, relationships, assumptions, and boundaries that are out. Also, given the foreign nature of borrowed theories, it is likely that essential characteristics of the IS phenomenon will be underrepresented in the generic structure, and thus a rich source of discrepant elements and roles. At times, the discrepancies will be obvious—adopting an IS is quite different from hosting guests!—while at other times, the differences may be subtle. Regardless of the perceived value of incongruous elements and roles, the more phenomenological IT elements are implicated in the theorizing, the more likely it is that the researcher will generate novel insights from the blending. We contend that the extent to which this happens is contingent upon the theorist rather than any characteristics inherent to the IT.

To illustrate this, consider two examples situated in the sharing economy context where an individual is considering adopting the services of a second individual (i.e., two roles united by a single relationship, see Figure 2). The basic phenomena (buyers and sellers) are familiar, but the IT elements have fostered a new crowd-based capitalism. In the first case, illustrated in Figure 2’s structure of adoption, a TRA lens is adopted. Discrepant IT elements are deemed inconsequential and only serve as a catalyst for the roles already existing in the original structure. Hence, the IT constructs simply become instances of the original constructs (e.g., perceived usefulness is an instance of behavioral beliefs).

In the second case, illustrated in Figure 2’s structure of communication, consider how we can explicitly theorize how the review platform (and its IT elements) can influence user behavior. IS researchers who study online reviews tend to use theories from sociology and psychology, such as herd behavior (Goes et al., 2014) and the disconfirmation effect (Ho et al., 2017). Instead, let us consider a media synchronicity theory (MST) lens that has not been used to study online reviews. Communication on a review platform typically involves a one-way exchange with earlier users leaving reviews that are viewed by the later users who then similarly contribute though not necessarily in response to prior users. Though this process is quite different from the typical communication process studied by MST there are indications of the existence of a generic structure between the two. It is reasonable to argue that MST—which explains how information is processed, transmitted, understood, integrated into the cognitive schema of the receiver, and put to use (Dennis et al., 2008)—treats the communication medium as a conduit among participants who communicate as they work together on a task. Likewise, it is possible to contend that the review platform can also be characterized as a communication medium that allows users to work together on a task—e.g., making a purchase decision—albeit asynchronously. That is, prior consumers share their overall consumption experiences in reviews, and subsequent users try to infer product quality from these reviews and then leave their own assessments. In this case, the IT and its ability to facilitate communication among participants is integral to the adoption decision. The models emerging from this framing can implicate important IT constructs into the relationship.

These hypotheticals, though necessarily incomplete, illustrate that if considered early in the theorizing process, discrepant organizing structures can lead to opportunities for novel theorizing. In some cases, IT elements can be simply morphed into roles already existing in the original structure or add nonessential/situational roles to the original organizing structure. In other cases, integrating IT elements alters the original organizing structure by changing or adding roles, relationships, assumptions, and boundaries, thus spurring more innovative theorizing. Much depends on how the theorist chooses to handle such issues.

## Advantages of the Structure-Based Approach to Theorizing

We believe that a CBT-informed structure-based approach to theorizing can inspire greater innovation in our field’s theory borrowing practices. Just as there are many strategies for creative problem-solving (Couger et al., 1993), our admonition to interrogate the structure of borrowed theories and emerging phenomena is one possible mechanism by which scholars might achieve more novel theoretical contributions. In assessing our field’s borrowing of TCE, we identified three practices that discourage, limit, or are opposed to novel theorizing: a fixed view of the theory, a lack of engagement with theory, and a tendency to let the borrowed theory dominate the local IT phenomenon (see Table 3). Below, we discuss each deficiency and offer specific illustrations of the ways in which a structure-based approach might help theorists break from the status quo.

Breaking Theoretical Inertia: For academics, establishing and illustrating coherence between a theoretical frame and a focal IT phenomenon is an essential part of theory building. However, in our field, deference to consensus, lock-in to a theoretical platform, and pressure to publish discourage experimentation; gradually an accepted view emerges of how, when, and why theories fit phenomena. Through repetition, inertia sets in and the dominant view shifts from a proper application of theory to a phenomenon to the proper perspective. A structure-based approach to theory borrowing can break some of the bonds that bind us to a particular perspective and a narrow conceptualization of fit. Although a structure-based approach is concerned with fit—the theory and the phenomenon must have sufficient correspondence to allow for blending—it is less dogmatic in terms of the specificity of that correspondence. The only requirement for fit imposed by our approach is that the theorist be able to identify conceptual overlap between two ideas. Otherwise, the structure-based approach is agnostic regarding the level of abstraction or concreteness required to achieve fit, and a tolerance for fit at higher levels of abstraction will allow for more novel pairings, as scholars of creativity and innovation have shown.

Our analysis shows that IS researchers have an inherently narrow conceptualization of TCE. Theorists can broaden their view of a theory/phenomenon through the progressive abstraction of the organizing frame. Progressive abstraction is a technique that generates alternative descriptions of a concept by employing increasingly generic language until a more helpful or interesting description is achieved (Couger et al., 1993). For example, TCE might be described as a theory that explains how transaction costs affect the governance structure between two companies. Progressively abstracting this description may result in an equivalent but more abstract description such as a theory that explains how obligations affect the relationship between entities. Such an abstract description broadens the set of phenomena that might be said to “fit” TCE.

Increasing Theoretical Engagement: When we train scholars in our field, we encourage them to adopt a critical view of theory, its limits, and its fallibility. However, in practice, there is a tendency to treat the borrowed theory as sacrosanct. Rather than simply applying a “faithful” representation of the theory, a structure-based approach can help researchers pursue defensible departures from theoretical fidelity because such an approach encourages a rigorous interrogation of the theory’s organizing structure. Such an approach offers opportunities to (1) observe and challenge the assumptions and boundaries within which the tenants of a theory hold true, and depart from the network of constructs, relationships, and logic inherited from the borrowed theory; (2) engage in more creative theorizing by identifying generic structures that can be matched with various digital phenomena; and (3) allow the uniqueness of the phenomenon to identify or fill “theoretical gaps” thus leading to greater indigeneity of theorizing in the field.

Our earlier analysis of borrowing practices shows that only 35% of the papers in our sample challenge the structure of

TCE in any way. This could be considered a problem of functional fixedness (Duncker & Lees, 1945): IS researchers, having adopted a particular narrative about TCE, are unable or unwilling to imagine how its core insights might evolve under different boundary conditions and/or assumptions. To address this, interrogative techniques that directly question implicit and explicit assumptions are helpful for reimagining dominant roles and relationships (Osborn, 1953). For example, TCE researchers might pose a series of “what-if” questions: e.g., What if the participants are individuals, what if one participant is an AI, what if assets are modular and partially transferrable? Preventing Theoretical Dominance: A lack of deep engagement with the IT artifact has been widely discussed in the field, somewhat reflected by the dominance of adoption and usage models (e.g., TAM, UTAUT) over a decade ago. While these models were useful in highlighting the psychological and social forces that affect technology adoption, they offered limited insight into how to make an IT usable because the source theories dominated our consideration of local phenomena (e.g., technology adoption). In the same vein, as argued above, most TCE-inspired research studies superficially implicate IS into the proposed research models. The research often downplays the idiosyncratic nature of the IT phenomenon in favor of a more generic depiction of the IT that is easier to study through a TCE lens. Unfortunately, such an approach diminishes the unique value of the IT and the potential novelty of the findings.

As the IT phenomena we study become increasingly diverse, complex, and embedded, a structure-based approach may help researchers highlight the novel characteristics of the IT artifact and the implications they may have for stakeholders. For example, the researcher can enumerate the elements that comprise both the borrowed theory and the focal IT phenomenon and iteratively create forced connections between the incongruous elements. For example, when using TCE to explain issues pertaining to the sharing economy, the researcher may have concepts such as “individual” and “sharing” that have no corollary in TCE. Such morphological techniques assign equal value to the theory and phenomenon and explore both similarities and differences between them, thus allowing for a more nuanced integration of IT artifacts to generate new ideas from seemingly unrelated concepts (Souder & Ziegler, 1977).<sup>10</sup>

## Implications

Theory borrowing is a widespread practice in the IS field. Some may argue that the practice should be discontinued in favor of more “blue ocean” (Straub, 2009) or indigenous theorizing (Kenworthy & Verbeke, 2015). Our position is that there is a wealth of knowledge encapsulated in reference theories that we can leverage to explain IS phenomena. However, our field’s current borrowing practices are too narrow and restrained, particularly when considering emergent and dynamic digital phenomena. In this paper, we propose a structure-based approach to theory borrowing that will bring flexibility into the theorizing process and create new opportunities for innovative theorizing. Our approach is premised on the notion that novel ideas emerge from the blending of seemingly incongruous ideas and we have shown how it is possible to apply the basic principles of conceptual blending to the earlier stages of theory borrowing. As successful innovations require exertions from both innovators and gatekeepers, we will use this section to discuss the implications our approach has for those developing and those evaluating theory.

## Implications for Developers of Theory

Theory development is difficult, and, as we have shown, the tradition of adopting theories from other disciplines almost “as is” has resulted in predictable (and thus less interesting) results. To foster innovation in this process, particularly for emergent digital phenomena, we encourage researchers to reflect on the structural nature of theories and phenomena. Such an exercise can inject creativity by expanding the set of applicable reference theories and by introducing novel insight through the reconciliation of discrepant ideas. To expand the set of applicable reference theories, techniques such as progressive abstraction can be used to imagine a different framing for familiar ideas. Digital phenomena are novel and transformative and can be envisioned in many ways. Thus, imagining new organizing structures is critical to reformulating the research question and changing the set of applicable theories. To introduce novel insight through the reconciliation of discrepant ideas, researchers can use interrogatives to challenge the assumptions and boundaries inherent in most theories and morphological connections to draw novel insights from discrepant ideas. A structure-based approach to theory borrowing complemented with such techniques will give future researchers the discretion to embrace the incongruities between the borrowed theory and the focal phenomenon and implicate more of the uniqueness of the phenomenon into the theory. While researchers have been trained to respect theoretical tradition, we believe that reference theories should not be accepted as immutable. Instead, our approach to theory borrowing will provide researchers with a vocabulary that will help justify their choices as they wield borrowed theories in a way that acknowledges the theory’s explanatory power and our field’s need for flexibility in predicting disrupting and emergent digital phenomena. Further, in the spirit of flexibility being advocated through this approach, borrowing theoretical elements from different reference theories could also be valuable if they could be weaved together in a logically consistent manner to improve our understanding of digital phenomena.

In doctoral programs, the primary aim is pedagogical, with senior scholars seeking to train students in the ontology and epistemology of theory development. Typically, seminar classes rely on seminal works to illustrate what theory is (Bacharach, 1989; Sutton & Staw, 1995), why it is needed (Davis, 1971), what it does, and how to build it (Weick, 1989). Such influential articles may create a sacrosanct view of reference theories where the theory dominates as it is “applied” to the phenomenon. Our approach encourages a loose adherence to such tradition and involves the creative process of thinking about differences: i.e., examining a phenomenon in different ways through different research questions and different generic structures that can engage different theories and the blending of differences. This could infuse seminars with opportunities to discuss not only the what, how, why, and when of the reference theory but also how a shared generic structure allows it to be stretched and applied to phenomena that might otherwise be considered beyond its explanatory power. Abstractions are hard to grasp and harder to teach, but it is our hope such deviations from orthodoxy can stimulate future IS researchers to establish the boundaries of this approach and the corresponding best practices for theory borrowing.

## Implications for Evaluators of Theory

A structure-based perspective on theory borrowing has several important implications for those who evaluate theoretical contributions. Creative works are often evaluated first for adequacy—is the work an acceptable contribution— and second for influence—do others in the field find the work compelling. In our field, the first group is comprised of editors and reviewers who evaluate theoretical work, and the second group is made of the lay members of the field who engage, debate, test and extend theoretical work. Together, these gatekeepers determine which works are published and the extent to which they are integrated into our literature base (e.g., through citations) and influence practice (e.g., through testing). If our field is going to move away from borrowing practices that hew to a narrow, majoritarian view of reference theories, both groups must allow for greater flexibility without relinquishing our high standards for rigor.

First, for journal editors and reviewers, there are two main issues they must grapple with as they evaluate theoretical works leveraging the approach we advocate: (1) How much incongruity is acceptable? (2) What is the appropriate balance between innovation and applicability? Because rigor is often assessed based on fidelity to the borrowed theoretical logic, it has been the practice of the evaluation process to criticize the applicability of the theory based on a violation of boundary conditions or assumptions. A fundamental premise of our structure-based approach is that we need pliable theories to understand pliable digital phenomena, and the balancing act for editors and reviewers is to allow for discretion in theory borrowing as long as the result contributes new logic, constructs, or configurations. Thus, the emphasis should be on whether the arguments are sound, regardless of whether they come from the original logic or the phenomena itself. Stretching theories too far or promoting novelty for novelty’s sake should be recognized and discouraged. On this point, our structure-based perspective is again helpful as it demands adequate explication of the generic space, lest the result be trivial or nonsensical. Reviewers can push authors toward a more defensible framing when adequate overlap is lacking. Ultimately, theories are assessed based on their divergent goals of testability and utility; as a field, we need to calibrate the extent to which the trade-off is acceptable.

Second, our field tends to embrace theories that fit our phenomena. Doing so is straightforward, as we simply need to recast the theory’s logic and constructs to fit our phenomena. However, when we consider examples of the field’s more innovative theorizing, such as media synchronicity theory or “move-to-the-middle” hypotheses, we find that in each case, the IT artifact introduces new elements into the borrowed theory’s organizing structure. As a field, we should be more willing to accept abstract conceptualizations of our phenomena that allow for connections to theories that are peripheral to our domain and yet potentially insightful to our phenomena and their IT elements. Whether through citations or panel discussions, we should reward such risk taking to encourage more of it.

## Conclusion

In this Issues & Opinions piece, we contend that the prevalent approach of theory borrowing results in works that appear rigorous but are often uninteresting. We provide evidence of this based on a large-scale analysis of the use of a reference theory (TCE). We find that our field mostly engages in theory testing within an IS context, develops models that are often uninspiring reflections of the theory, and treats IT as largely exogenous. We then explore remedies proposed in IS and management and find them to have limitations. Inspired by conceptual blending, we propose that the root cause of the issue is the way we initiate our borrowing process. We argue that IS researchers should be mindful of the independent structures that exist prior to engaging in a theory borrowing exercise and recommend that they adopt a borrowing approach that embraces both the similarities and differences between the borrowed theory and the IS phenomenon. It is our opinion that such mindfulness will increase the likelihood of better borrowing practice, greater implication of IT in the theoretical structure, and, consequently, more innovative theories. We are under no illusion that this process is easy or without risk—theorizing is inherently difficult—but it is our opinion that this new path will be more productive than continuing with the old.

Critics of such an approach may argue that it advocates for an “anything goes” approach, undermines our tradition of theorizing, and lacks specificity. To this, we say that our objective is not to offer a prescription on how to deterministically create theory. That seems impossible. Ironically, our field’s dominant theory borrowing practices seem to be more deterministic (or at least scripted) and they do not work well. Here, we aim to challenge the practices and offer a new perspective that injects “disciplined imagination” into the theory borrowing process. We believe that borrowed theories can serve as fruitful sources for innovative ideas about the phenomena we study, and we hope that our perspective can be calibrated through future work as we strive to build from borrowed theories better.

She has also served as a peer reviewer for IS journals such as the Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Information & Management, and Communications of the Association for Information Systems.

Lianlian (Dorothy) Jiang is an assistant professor in the Department of Decision & Information Sciences at the Bauer College of Business, University of Houston. She has published work in Communications of the Association for Information Systems and 3D Research, and in conference proceedings. Her research interests are in the areas of digital platform design & strategy, healthcare IT, human-AI interaction and business analytics using econometrics, natural experiment, lab experiments, and machine learning.

Jackie (Jake) London Jr. is an assistant professor of information systems at Loyola University Maryland. His research has been published in Information Systems Research, Communications of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of Decision Systems, and in conference proceedings. Prior to starting his Ph.D., he spent more than a decade in the information technology industry in various roles, including consultant, system administrator, application developer, and data analyst.

Varun Grover is the George & Boyce Billingsley Endowed Chair and Distinguished Professor of IS at the Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas. He has published over 300 refereed journal articles in IS. Over his 30+ year career, he has consistently been ranked among the top five researchers globally, based on his publications in top journals, citations (>47,000), and h-index (of 98). Thompson Reuters recognized him as one of 100 Highly Cited Scholars globally in all business disciplines and a 2022 Stanford University study ranked him 6th (out of 17,971 authors) in the IS discipline. He is a senior editor for MISQ Executive Quarterly, editor of the Journal of the Association for Information Systems Section on Path Breaking Research, and has served as senior editor for MIS Quarterly (2 terms), the Journal of the Association for Information Systems (3 terms), and The Data Base for Advances in Information Systems. His current work focuses on the impacts of digitalization on individuals and organizations. He is the recipient of numerous awards from USC, Clemson, University of Arkansas, AIS, Academy of Management,

DSI, the OR Society, Anbar, PriceWaterhouse, among others for his research and teaching. He is an AIS Fellow and an AIS LEO recipient for lifetime achievement.

Heshan Sun is a professor of MIS and Richard Van Horn Professor in IT and Analytics, in the Management Information Systems Division, at the University of Oklahoma’s Price College of Business. He received his Ph.D. from Syracuse University. He is interested in how information systems profoundly influence and interact with individuals, organizations, and society. Specifically, his research interests include human-computer interaction, trust and e-commerce, artificial intelligence, and business analytics, among others. His research has been published (or will appear) in prestigious academic journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, Internal Journal of Human-Computer Studies, and Journal of the American Society for Information Science and Technology, among others.
