# Knowledge-acquisitions and post-acquisition innovation performance: a comparative hazards model

Pratim Datta<sup>1</sup> and Yaman Roumani<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Council on Supply Chain and IT Strategy (SCITS), College of Business, Kent State University, Kent, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, Eastern Michigan University, Ypsilanti, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Pratim Datta, PhD Director and Associate Professor A408, College of Business, Kent State University, Kent, OH 44242, U.S.A. Tel: +509-338-5330

## Abstract

Do acquisitions lead to instrumental innovations related to the acquired knowledge? Past arguments on vertical integration espouse how a quest for knowledge drives acquisitions culminating in innovation performance. Using Google and Yahoo as cases-in-point, we examine how facets of acquired innovation knowledge impact post-innovation performance. In particular, the apparently opposing fortunes of Google and Yahoo allow us to investigate the pace of their innovation performance as a hazards model. Results from our investigation highlight Google’s ambidexterity over Yahoo with a swifter, systematic pace of innovation performance – from hastening time to patenting new ideas to the time to releasing new applications from acquisitions. ## Introduction

Most software companies are characterized by hypercompetition and innovation, especially driven by dynamic and complementary relationships among software product markets (Lee et al, 2010). Impelled by a race to innovate and secure intellectual rights and <sup>fi</sup>rst-mover advantages, software companies must constantly seek new ways of acquiring knowledge to build intellectual capital and launch innovative solutions. Consequently, acquisitions become a popular strategy for <sup>fi</sup>rms for appropriating knowledge and differentiating themselves using new ideas and products. Because ‘exclusive reliance on internal sources of knowledge limits a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s innovation performance by creating internal rigidities and learning traps’ (Levinthal & March, 1993, p. 104), ‘a well-planned policy of external acquisition affords technology strategy options that a “go-it-alone” attitude would preclude’ (Capon & Glazer, 1987, p. 6). Beset by market and technological uncertainties, software companies have been particularly active in acquisitions to expand their breadth of offerings and competencies (Prabhu et al, 2005). In 2007, software companies made over 110 acquisitions in the United States alone, amounting to more than US\$2 billion in assets (Mergers and Acquisitions Statistics, 2008).

Although acquisitions remain a popular growth strategy, the role of acquisitions on innovation performance has been ambivalent. On the one hand, importing external knowledge through acquisitions not only increases a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s absorptive capacity and core competencies (Leonard-Barton, 1995)

but also helps establish a continued source of innovation performance (Keeble et al, 1999). On the other hand, it is argued that acquisitions are a ‘placebo’ for innovation performance ( Jensen, 1988) and worse, a source of detriment (Harrison et al, 1991), attrition (Ernst & Vitt, 2000), and/or distraction (Hoskisson & Hitt, 1990).

Acquisitions are costly affairs with uncertainty and high opportunity costs. Uncertainty is particularly acute in acquisitions of software companies. It is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to value a <sup>fi</sup>rm based on a technology that may change or disappear within a few years before securing gains. Technological obsolescence offers no salvage value. Therefore the ‘timeliness’ of gains is an imperative. Because <sup>fi</sup>rms have the choice to forego acquisition expenses to reinvest the money into their core operations, a failure to strategize on acquisitions to reap ‘timely’ gains can negatively weigh on the value of a <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Given the importance of timeliness of capitalizing on acquisitions by releasing requisite products and services, this paper employs the term ‘time to release’ (TTR) as an umbrella term to encompass the launch or release of new products and services related to the acquisition. A Boston Consulting Group (BCG) (2009) survey of worldwide executives found that <sup>fi</sup>rms viewed the length of time in generating great ideas and the time to release (launch) new products and services as impediments. In short, the faster a <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to convert its acquired IP (intellectual property) into commercializable product and service releases, the better it is as a return on acquisition investments. Previous studies on acquisitions and innovation have dealt with the aggregate impact of acquisitions on innovation output (e.g., Hoskisson & Hitt, 1994) and productivity (e.g., Anand & Singh, 1997) or on the impact (e.g., Ahuja & Katila, 2001) and magnitude of knowledge sourcing for innovations (e.g., Prabhu et al, 2005). However, in a competitive environment, the issue of timeliness of output, productivity, and knowledge base gains credence and momentum. While useful strides have been made in assessing the magnitude of postacquisition innovation performance, linking acquisitions to the pace of innovation performance has escaped scrutiny. Moreover, studies have rarely examined innovation performance related to an acquisition. Therefore, a different yet relevant question is: What are the factors contributing towards transforming acquired innovation knowledge into post-acquisition innovation performance?

In this study, the pace of post-acquisition innovation performance is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the duration between an acquisition and related post-acquisition innovation performance. In exploring how acquisitions impact the pace of innovation performance in software companies, we empirically compare all Google and Yahoo acquisitions from their inception till 2008. The choice of Google and Yahoo is particularly suitable for this study. Google and Yahoo are the only two firms whose core competencies are search engines and content aggregation (Microsoft moved to search engines to complement its Windows Operating System and Internet Explorer). Microsoft’s core business logic is its Windows and Windows-platform applications (e.g., MS

Of<sup>fi</sup>ce) rather than dedicated cloud-based and Internetbased services.

Google and Yahoo are two Internet search and aggregation behemoths – quintessential software companies with contrasting fates. Google and Yahoo share similarities that make them useful candidates for empirical comparison based on (i) similar core operating logic focusing on search, aggregation, advertising, and cloud computing as well as (ii) similar acquisition-portfolio competing across similar services such as maps, seach, email, photo aggregation, among others. Therefore, in illustrating our arguments and building our empirical study, we compare Google’s and Yahoo’s entire acquisition portfolio. We test our hypotheses by estimating a hazard model to appropriately capture the impact of acquisitions on the pace of innovation performance. To the best of our knowledge, a systematic study of how acquisitions shape the pace of innovation performance has rarely received scrutiny, especially across two dominant <sup>fi</sup>rms in the same industry.

The paper is organized as follows: The next section explains the importance of acquisitions as a knowledge sourcing and retaining strategy followed by an explanation of innovation performance. The third section on hypotheses development examines characteristics of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm to formulate our hypotheses linking acquisitions to the pace of innovation performance. The fourth section begins with a brief overview of Google and Yahoo acquisitions. Next, we describe the data set, variable operationalization, and the analysis using a hazard function. The <sup>fi</sup>fth section discusses the results. Finally, we conclude with discussing the results, observing contributions, and suggesting avenues for future research.

## Theoretical underpinnings

For the sake of simplicity and consistency in exposition, we use the term ‘acquisition’ to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a business method where the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm assumes complete legal ownership and control of the corporate assets of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm. For the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm, an acquisition strategy has to be grounded in ex-ante planning. Ex-ante planning would allow the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm to have a clear direction and guidance toward valuing the acquisition and a concerted strategy toward ex-post integration of the acquired assets and resources into the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm’s portfolio. Without de<sup>fi</sup>nite ex-ante planning and ex-post resource and asset integration, an acquisition could amount to unwelcome non-value-added costs of redundancy, resource integration challenges and misalignments, among other costs and challenges (e.g., Cools et al, 2004).

In the in<sup>fl</sup>uential essay, The Nature of the Firm, Coase (1937) argues that, as <sup>fi</sup>rms increase in size, there are decreasing returns to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s core functions, necessitating the building of alternative revenue trajectories. Acquisi tions allow the creation of alternative revenue trajectories as the value of the core entrepreneurial function is subject to diminishing returns to scale. Central to acquisitions and innovation performance is the successful transfer and combination of knowledge between acquiring and acquired <sup>fi</sup>rms. When a <sup>fi</sup>rm resolves to seek important knowledge crucial to its strategy and growth, it needs to decide on a deliberate mechanism to secure knowledge. While <sup>fi</sup>rms can choose one of many options, namely strategic alliances, mergers, or acquisitions, acquisitions seem the preferred mode for securing knowledge (e.g., Prabhu et al, 2005), primarily because acquisitions are the only formal contract where the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm owns the knowledge and can leverage it to the best of its ability.

Because an acquisition assumes ownership through vertical or horizontal integration, it creates a new revenue source that complements or supplements revenue streams from core operations. While Cools et al (2004), in analyzing large public U.S. companies over 10 years, found that companies most active in acquisitions performed 29% better in revenues than <sup>fi</sup>rms that made no or few acquisitions, successful conversion of acquired knowledge into innovation requires managing and coordinating of acquired knowledge resources.

From a resource point of view, even though there is an inherent endogeneity and recursive causality where resource/capital surplus can prompt acquisitions and acquisitions can prompt resource/capital surplus, we focus on the latter to underpin our discussion, contending that acquisitions can also occur via forms of debt-leveraged and equityleveraged capital, regardless of immediate capital liquidity.

Therefore, given that an acquisition is a means to sequester knowledge, a thorough examination of knowledge-characteristics of a potential acquisition is a precursor to disciplined postacquisition management and coordination.

The literature in economics and strategy suggests that ex-ante acquisition planning is a key to ex-post innovation performance (Vanhaverbeke et al, 2002). Innovation performance is a measure of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to capitalize on acquisitions to generate intellectual property and launch new products in the market. A Boston Consulting Group Report (2009) on innovations asked 2700 executives around the world the importance of innovation in their companies and how acquisitions play a role in their company’s innovation strategy. The report highlighted the premium <sup>fi</sup>rms placed on innovation performance, with 71% of executives remarking on how acquisitions should drive innovations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, executives noted that acquisition investments allowed them to (i) deploy and guide new innovations to gain access to new markets, and (ii) leverage innovative talents of personnel from the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rms to maintain and grow their competitive advantage. In this strategy of using acquisitions to spur innovation performance, two facets surface: the ability to ‘timely’ generate breakthrough ideas and the ability to ‘timely’ introduce new products/services to the market. The common denominator is a sense of overarching immediacy.

In competitive environments, generating IP via patents is not enough; <sup>fi</sup>rms must be adept at ‘strategically’ speeding the creation of intellectual property and speeding the launching/commercializing of new product and services from by pushing ideas from their IPs to the market. In a study surveying innovation performance of 1900 Danish <sup>fi</sup>rms, Laursen & Foss (2003) used launch of new products to understand innovation performance. Resonating with Schumpeter (1934), <sup>fi</sup>rm efforts must converge on ‘new combinations’ of knowledge to create novel products and services (Ahuja & Katila, 2001). According to Schilling & Hill (1998), new product development and launches (releases) are important factors driving a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s success and failure and ‘most argue that in order to be successful in new product development, a <sup>fi</sup>rm must … minimize time to market’. Therefore, we believe that, together, the complementing facets of idea and IP generation through new patent applications (e.g., Griliches, 1990; Ahuja & Katila, 2001; Prabhu et al, 2005) and new product launches (e.g., Utterback, 1996; Laursen & Foss, 2003) best capture postacquisition innovation performance for the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm.

As companies embark on acquisitions, it must be realized that post-acquisition performance by harnessing knowledge for successful IP generation and product launches often serves as an acid test of a company’s absorptive capacity. Absorptive capacity refers to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to value new knowledge, assimilate, and apply it to further its innovative capabilities (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). Absorptive capacity builds on the premise that <sup>fi</sup>rms with high absorptive capacities will exploit acquired knowledge to better integrate the knowledge to expand its IP portfolio and use the knowledge to engage in timely launches of new products and services for better innovative performance. However, lacking requisite absorptive capacities, companies can acquire knowledge but fail to capitalize the knowledge, impeding knowledge integration for innovation performance. Charles Schwab’s acquisition of U.S. Trust in 2000 is a prime example of failed knowledge transfer from a lack of absorptive capacity. U.S. Trust, in order to protect its high-end client accounts from being garnered by Charles Schwab’s account management, hoarded its client information and operational knowledge. Lacking the absorptive capacity to transfer client information and process knowledge from U.S. Trust, Charles Schwab failed to harness the acquired knowledge to add to its otherwise rich portfolio of innovations and forced Charles Schwab to sell U.S. Trust to Bank of America in 2006. This exempli<sup>fi</sup>es how the absorptive capacity of the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rms is and imperative for post-acquisition innovation performance. In an industry where competitive differentiation is the most stable and enduring strategy, acquisitions have to be strategically managed. To summarize, acquired knowledge has to be absorbed and assimilated to seek competitive advantages from speedy IP generation and product release.

In a world where knowledge is often digitally stored in organizational systems, it is imperative that IS integration serves as a preface to knowledge integration in M&As (Mergers and Acquisitions) (Brown et al, 2003; Dudas & Tobisson, 2007; Seddon et al, 2010). Particularly with technology <sup>fi</sup>rms where knowledge is often embedded in systems and processes, building absorptive capacity from acquisitions requires IS integration. Companies that deftly practice IS integration can increase the absorptive capacity of acquired knowledge by creating a single instance for sharing knowledge. Information systems (IS) are central in the knowledge acquisition and innovation calculus because the process of acquiring, integrating, and sharing knowledge requires a cautious yet deliberate IS integration (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). When a <sup>fi</sup>rm acquires another, the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm’s embedded knowledge often resides in various systems across the organization. In order for the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm to capitalize knowledge acquisitions, organizations must ‘revisit their systems to bring them into alignment with the business needs’ (Mehta & Hirschheim, 2004, p. 143). Without the necessary absorptive capacity to manage change and integrate knowledge embedded in systems and processes, IS integration drops post-M&A (Mehta & Hirschheim, 2004).

In a comparative case study of IS integration of a <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry acquisition in Australia to a previous case study of a similar acquisition in the United States (Brown et al, 2003), Seddon et al (2010) remark on the importance of knowledge transfers and knowledge integration as prerequisites to successful business acquisitions. In fact, given that ‘knowing how IT relates to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s strategic capabilities could provide an effective advantage to business’ (Bhatt, 2000, p. 1355), absorptive capacity of acquired knowledge would rest on the close alignment between the business need and the technical solution (Markus, 2001). This line of thinking is supported by Dudas & Tobisson (2007) who <sup>fi</sup>nd that M&A business integration by knowledge absorption rests on well-aligned IS integration. Therefore, organizations that can streamline the integration of knowledge can effective sequester acquired knowledge and speed the pace of knowledge conversion into IP and commercialization.

## Hypotheses development

In this research, we focus on two broad exigencies critical in shaping the pace of innovation performance in software companies. First, we draw attention to the fact that, in strategizing on potential acquisitions, acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rms need to look closely at the size or quantity of a priori knowledge base of <sup>fi</sup>rms under consideration as an indicator of a posteriori innovation performance. Second, we draw attention to the impact of various facets on the nature and characteristics of the knowledge being acquired on innovation output (Lane & Lubatkin, 1998). As Ahuja & Katila (2001, p. 199) remark, ‘on balance, assessing whether technological acquisitions will have a positive impact on post-acquisition innovation output is likely to depend upon the quantity and nature of knowledge elements that they bring to the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm’. ## Size of the knowledge base of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm

In the context of acquisition, an acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm must evaluate the size of knowledge base of the candidate <sup>fi</sup>rm they plan to acquire. In tracing the cycle of knowledge acquisition and application within <sup>fi</sup>rms, Datta (2007) found that knowledge in <sup>fi</sup>rms is a harbinger of creative ideas and innovations. The author notes that knowledge bases are test-beds for creative pursuits as ‘a result of synthesizing previously unconnected knowledge’, thus mobilizing knowledge for economic returns (ibid, p. 298). Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rms contemplating an acquisition for innovation performance need to pay particular attention to the size of the existing knowledge base of the <sup>fi</sup>rms they plan to acquire.

The size of the knowledge base has a bearing on the pace of innovations and product launches related to the acquisition. Take, for example, the case of Qualcomm’s Mobile WiMAX strategy. Realizing that OFDMA (Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access) will shape the next generation of multi-antennae wireless devices, Qualcomm strategized on acquiring Flarion Technologies because of its existing base of hundreds of OFDMA-related patents. The size of the patent base at Flarion allowed Qualcomm to capitalize on the intellectual capital to swiftly launch successive patents and product standards for Wideband Code Division Multiple Access (W-CDMA), a popular standard for military communications (Yomogita, 2007). Similarly, Apple’s acquisition of NeXT allowed Apple to capitalize on NeXT’s OpenStep interface applications to swiftly issue patents, soon releasing the groundbreaking Mac OS X with its popular docked interface, browser integration in <sup>fi</sup>nder, and the services menu.

The size of the acquired knowledge base allows the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm an opportunity to combine and crosspollinate knowledge to bring new ideas and products to the market. As Ahuja & Katila (2001, p. 200) point out, ‘while a <sup>fi</sup>rm with <sup>fi</sup>ve units of knowledge can generate 10 combinations using two elements at a time, acquiring another <sup>fi</sup>rms with three units of knowledge increases the number of combinations that become available to 28’. An acquisition where the acquired company either lacks an established knowledge base can impede the pace of innovation performance. For these reasons, we expect that:

H1: The larger the size of the initial knowledge base of the acquired firm, the shorter the time to launch ideas and release products directly resulting from the acquisition.

## Nature of the knowledge base of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm

The nature of the knowledge base relates to certain attributes of the knowledge being acquired. After all, an acquired knowledge base is a mixed bag of useful and not so useful attributes that can dampen or enhance the pace of innovations. In a similar vein of absorptive capacity, Vanhaverbeke et al (2002) posit the indigestibility argument to explain how knowledge acquisitions of a particular nature render these assets useless because of the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of integrating these knowledge assets toward useful ends. This study focuses on three characteristics of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm’s knowledge base to investigate the nature of acquired knowledge: radicalness, relatedness, and emphasis of acquired knowledge base.

## Radicalness of the knowledge base of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm

Dewar & Dutton (1986) forwarded the notion of ‘radicalness’ as the distribution of fundamentally different, revolutionary shifts in innovation-related knowledge vis-à-vis an existing knowledge base. Radicalness of knowledge is based on complementarities. A sharp discontinuity from the core design of the product or service is radical; a gradual improvement to the core design of the product or service is incremental. Carlo et al (2012), in examining radical innovations in small software <sup>fi</sup>rms, depict radical innovations are being an extension of the existing product line but a unique and novel complementarity to the ‘base innovation type’. The origin of the innovation is the same but the features and processes that de<sup>fi</sup>ne a radical innovation are a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvement over the base innovation type.

Radical innovations are ‘revolutionary’ while incremental innovations are ‘evolutionary’. A radical innovation maintains the product/service baseline or core but signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant adds to the knowledge-base attributes. The Apple iPod Touch is an example of radical innovation on the base iPod design; it maintains the core attributes of the iPod but adds signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to the knowledge base by replacing the scroll wheel with a touch-capacitance screen and a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different icon-rendition. However, the Apple iPhone is an incremental innovation. An incremental innovation is based on minor improvements and adjustments in innovation-related knowledge to an existing knowledge base. The Apple iPod Video is an example of incremental innovation on the base iPod design, maintaining the scroll wheel but adding a videographics processor and some control enhancements. In short, a radical innovation creates punctuated equilibria (Eldredge & Gould, 1972), a distinct, dichotomous shift or punctuation to the innovation equilibria of incremental (evolutionary) innovations (Abernathy & Clark, 1985). Thus, given that a radical innovation is a discontinuity, radical vs incremental innovations measures are dichotomous (Aboulnasr et al, 2008). Therefore, because a radical innovation increases uncertainty (Abernathy & Clark, 1985), a radical knowledge base is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to absorb and assimilate than an incremental knowledge base.

Given that acquisitions allow adoption and appropriation of knowledge assets of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm, asset-matching plays a crucial role in innovation performance. Assetmismatch can usher resource integration concerns (Ahuja & Katila, 2001).

A radical knowledge base creates an asset-mismatch. Because radical knowledge implies a noticeable departure from existing practices and knowledge utilization, harnessing radical knowledge can be greatly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for the established <sup>fi</sup>rm (Tushman & Anderson, 1986). Unless concerted efforts are taken to harness and manage radical knowledge, radical knowledge can create discontinuities that may be detrimental.

Researchers unequivocally suggest that, even when an organization builds in-house or internal radical knowledge, the organization suffers serious opposition from existing practices. Because of the effort it requires to integrate radical knowledge vs incremental knowledge, there is a greater reluctance toward integrating an acquired <sup>fi</sup>rms’ radical knowledge base, especially when the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm plays the role of an entrenched defender of its own practices.

Compared with a radical knowledge base, there might be less reluctance and effort required integrating an incremental knowledge base. For the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm, it is easier integrating an acquired <sup>fi</sup>rms’ knowledge base that is not a risky or clear departure from existing practices. An acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm is likely to <sup>fi</sup>nd it easier to manage the incremental knowledge base of acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm because of a sense of complementarity. Indeed, integrating an incremental knowledge base to create new ideas and build new products seems a less risky proposition – therefore garnering greater executive support and resources – particularly so when the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm has a history of innovations (re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the size of the acquired knowledge base).

## H2: Acquired incremental knowledge will reduce the time to launch ideas and release products directly resulting from the acquisition more than radical innovation knowledge.

Compounding the radicalness of the acquired innovation knowledge is the size of the acquired ‘radical’ innovation knowledge portfolio. Consider Google acquisition of two companies with radically different innovation knowledge from Google core search competencies. Google’s 2011 acquisition of Motorola Mobility with its large portfolio of mobile telephony hardware patents and Google’s 2014 acquisition of Nest with a large portfolio of smart-home hardware patents were both met with analyst skepticism (Wired, 2014). Analysts have worried about the large and radical innovation knowledge would create problems for Google in strategically integrating the acquired innovations into its portfolio. Therefore, the radicalness of the acquired innovation knowledge is compounded by the ‘size’ of the acquired ‘radical’ innovation knowledge portfolio. A larger innovation knowledge portfolio along with a higher proportion of radical innovation knowledge is more likely to increate integration and assimilation dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, thus delaying the time it takes for the acquiring company to patent ideas and release products from the acquired knowledge base.

Contending that the launch of ideas is generally a precursor to product development and releases, it is important to investigate interaction effects of acquisition characteristics on idea generation instead of product releases. In summary, compared incremental to radical knowledge, if companies acquire <sup>fi</sup>rms with a larger base (size) of radical innovation knowledge, acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm are more likely to spend more time and resources managing and integrating radical knowledge bases from acquired <sup>fi</sup>rms, thus delaying the generation and launch of innovative, patentable ideas. Subsequently, we argue that:

H3: The size of the acquired firm’s knowledge base will moderate the relationship between the radicalness of the knowledge base and the time to launch ideas from that acquired knowledge base. The moderation is such that, acquired companies with a larger radical knowledge base will increase the time for the acquiring company to launch ideas directly resulting from the acquisition more than that of an acquired company with a larger incremental knowledge base.

## Relatedness of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm’s knowledge base

While radicalness of knowledge relies upon the concept of resource complementarity to extend <sup>fi</sup>rm capabilities in new frontiers (e.g., Teece, 1986; Wu et al, 2013), relatedness of knowledge focuses on capitalizing similar, incremental knowledge from within an acquisition for incremental value-addition. Unrelated knowledge is dissimilar – distanced from a company’s core competencies; related knowledge is similar – closer to a company’s core competencies (Rumelt, 1974; Tanriverdi, 2005; Tanriverdi & Venkatraman, 2005).

In his seminal article, Rumelt (1974) considered relatedness based on whether products or services acquired were (i) serving similar markets with similar distribution channels, (ii) using similar technologies, and (iii) exploiting similar R&D. In that vein, unrelated acquisitions are more a diversi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy meant to expand into new markets, services, or value chain within one or more industry verticals. On the one hand, Google’s May 2013 acquisition of Makani, an airborne wind power turbine company, is an example of an unrelated acquisition. On the other hand, Google’s June 2013 acquisition of Waze, a crowdsourced GPS turn-by-turn navigation app that integrates with Google Maps and Google Search, is an example of related acquisition. However, the prototypical Google driverless ‘autonomous car’ with a large GPS module and Google Maps is an unrelated innovation release utilizing economies of scale and diversifying into newer markets. In short, products and services from unrelated knowledge bases are dissimilar and do not rely on the innovation knowledge base of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c product or service. Relatedness of knowledge bases concerns the similarity of their core competencies between the acquiring and the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rms. The relatedness of the knowledge bases between the acquiring and the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rms is an important factor in understanding innovation performance. It is commonly accepted that successful performance of a <sup>fi</sup>rm hinges on restricting activities to cultivating a related and familiar range rather than ‘bold moves into uncharted areas’ (Rumelt, 1974, p. 156) – leading to competence-destroying discontinuities. Competencedestroying discontinuities (Tushman & Anderson, 1986) require paradigm shifts in existing skills and competencies, forcing the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rms to learn new routines and crafts.

Integrating unrelated knowledge is not an easy task. Integrating unrelated knowledge base requires the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm to learn hitherto unknown and unrelated fundamentals of concepts and processes forwarded by the acquired knowledge base as well as recruiting resources (including human resources) to understand, manage, and integrate knowledge bases across the acquiring and acquired <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Related knowledge, on the other hand, allows leveraging existing competencies to establish mutual ‘sub-additive cost synergies’ (Tanriverdi, 2005, p. 313) from where exploiting related knowledge resources lowers joint production costs (Tanriveri, 2005) that rapidly advances the organizational knowledge base.

Over time, an organizational knowledge base is a repository of its capabilities based on sophisticated (and sometimes tacit) crafts – heavily reliant on disciplinary and <sup>fi</sup>rmspeci<sup>fi</sup>c routines (Kogut & Zander, 1992). Because each knowledge base is speci<sup>fi</sup>c rather than generic, grafting or combining knowledge bases consumes considerable resources, making combinations counter-productive when immediacy of innovation performance is a core objective. With a similar portfolio of competencies, the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm can easily assimilate the knowledge of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm without worrying about newfound resource commitments. For related knowledge bases, the knowledge base of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm conveniently supplements the knowledge base of the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm, allowing for focused endeavors in combining knowledge for speedy generation of new ideas and launch new products (Ahuja & Katila, 2001). As Roush in Xconomy (2014) notes ‘when [Google] company was younger, most of its acquisitions related to its core businesses of search, advertising, network infrastructure, and communications. More recently, it’s been colonizing areas with a less obvious connection to search – such as travel, social networking, productivity, logistics, energy, and robotics’ – leading to more concerns about Google being able to successfully integrate the portfolio of acquired ‘unrelated’ patents. Consider, for example, Facebook’s 2011 acquisition of 650 AOL and 750 IBM patents. The AOL patents were based on web search and web and email rankings while the IBM patents were related to hardware and software systems and optimization. Although useful toward future scalability of Facebook’s hardware and software intensive datacenters and ranking algorithms, the acquired knowledge was unrelated to Facebook’s core user posting and ranking competencies. As a result, it took Facebook a few years to integrate the acquired knowledge base into ‘Graph Search’ in 2013.

On the other hand, Facebook’s 2012 acquisition of Instagram, a photo sharing application, allowed Facebook access to a portfolio of related innovation knowledge where Facebook could easily integrate photo sharing into its social networking platform. Therefore, we contend that:

H4: Acquired related knowledge will reduce the time to launch ideas and release products directly resulting from the acquisition more than unrelated innovation knowledge.

## Emphasis of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm’s knowledge base

Another attribute this study uses to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the nature of an acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm’s knowledge base is its emphasis on product or process innovation knowledge. Product innovation knowledge relates to the know-how that a <sup>fi</sup>rm has on building artifacts or prototypes, both radical and incremental. For example, companies like 3M and Intel are renowned for their product innovation knowledge. Process innovation knowledge relates to the know-how a <sup>fi</sup>rm has on creating novel ways of performing an activity, for example, a new mode of work<sup>fl</sup>ow or reengineering existing work<sup>fl</sup>ows to make them ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. Consulting <sup>fi</sup>rms such as Accenture, BCG, Ernst & Young are exemplary for providing process innovation knowledge to clients across various industry verticals.

The acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm’s decision to acquire a <sup>fi</sup>rm with an emphasis on process or product innovation knowledge can have differential effects on the pace of its innovation performance.

Product innovation knowledge is often more concrete while process innovation knowledge is often abstract. While process innovation knowledge is a valuable intellectual property (e.g., Google’s unique page-ranked search indexing), explicating process innovation knowledge as intellectual property is relatively more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult than explicating product innovation knowledge as intellectual property (e.g., Apple’s TrackPad). In fact, the USPTO (United States Patent and Trademark Of<sup>fi</sup>ce) (2009) patent bulletin refers to the relative ease of explaining a product patent over a process patent because ‘patents are more effective for product innovations than for process innovations’ (Guellec & van Pottelsberghe de la Potterie, 2007, p. 68).

Guellec & van Pottelsberghe de la Potterie (2007) further mention how <sup>fi</sup>rms often make deliberate decisions to limit disclosure of competitive processes by not publicizing or commercializing an idea. Even when a <sup>fi</sup>rm can embody a pro<sup>fi</sup>table process innovation as a service, it will delay the launch until it has well-secured competitive advantages from internal use of that process innovation. Arundel & Kabla’s (1998) 10-year study found that large <sup>fi</sup>rms operating in Europe patented 36% of their products compared to 22% of their process patents.

A central factor is that process innovation knowledge is embedded in activities and systems and more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to qualify as a distinct, discrete phenomenon. Process innovations are mainly conducted in the background. Consumers know when Google uses product innovation knowledge to release a new Android version (e.g., Android 4.2 – Jelly Bean). Yet, the same consumers are unaware of when Google uses process innovation knowledge to automatically integrate and streamline its integration process to re<sup>fi</sup>ne its search algorithm that loads results faster. For example, even though Google Instant is a Google product, it requires a server-side software development process innovation that allows streamlined integration of websearch databases and trends into the keyword <sup>fi</sup>eld. While, as a product, Google Instant transparently allows existing users to see results dynamically as they search, the innovations is premised by a streamlined software development and integration process for server-side optimization without a formal media release. In this case, the process innovation knowledge is embedded in internal software development operations and server-side web services as well as continuous quality engineering. The process innovation is based on an internal software-integration process with a backend analytic engine that constantly captures web trends and search histories to allow users to dynamically receive faster and better results, saving 2–5 s per search with more focused predictions. Google Instant, as a product innovation, offers a more discrete timeline although the process innovations that guide software development and integration are less likely to be patented and released as a service.

Therefore, because process innovation releases can be implemented in the background and rarely require promotional engagements, a <sup>fi</sup>rm acquiring a knowledge base that emphasizes process rather than product innovation knowledge will be less likely to act in haste to introduce process innovation ideas to the market or launch services that embody the process innovation. Therefore, we suggest:

H5: Acquired product knowledge will reduce the time to launch ideas and release products directly resulting from the acquisition more than process innovation knowledge.

## Control variables

The paper examines how the size and nature of acquisitions shape the pace of innovation performance. However, this relationship is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by other factors out of our immediate consideration set. First, we control for the country where an acquisition is made. Research suggests that knowledge bases and knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows are often geographically localized (Almeida & Phene, 2004) and offer varying innovation performance. Geographically localized knowledge clusters (e.g., Technology Innovation in the United States; Financial Innovation in the United Kingdom; High-Tech Engineering in Germany and Japan) leverage agglomeration economics and often allow a large labor pool that can help post-acquisition integrate of knowledge bases. For example, Saxenian (2013) examined the dynamics of technology networks in Silicon Valley to point out how smaller companies such as Silicon Graphics and Pyramid Technology were leveraging their geographical presence to create inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm linkages for knowledge-based collaborations. Furthermore, countryspeci<sup>fi</sup>c innovation and patent legislations may differ considerably, making it easier or simpler to hasten innovation output.

Second, we control for the type of knowledge specialization. Firms acquire a knowledge base to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll unmet consumer demand and innovations fail when there is a poor understanding of user and market needs (Hippel, 2005) – propelling a race to achieve differentiation by serving the market with the ‘right’ product. Acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rms must seek the right type of specialized knowledge base to serve the right product. If the acquisition is not strategic, the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm can acquire a specialized knowledge base that may not be useful for its customers. If an acquisition is made without understanding user needs, the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm would <sup>fi</sup>nd little merit in hastening innovations from the acquired knowledge base. If there is a mismatch between the type of specialized knowledge acquired and the market need, the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm would most likely shelve the acquired knowledge instead of seeking <sup>fi</sup>rst-mover innovation advantages. Therefore, we control for the type of knowledge specialization.

## Data sources and validation

In order to perform the survival analysis for Google and Yahoo, we built a data set for each company. The data set was obtained through various sources on the web. Information about the acquisitions was collected from Lexis Nexis Academic database, quarterly reports of Google and Yahoo, Google’s of<sup>fi</sup>cial blog (http://googleblog.blogspot. com/), and Yahoo’s Press Room (http://yahoo.client. shareholder.com/press/). The same sources were used to obtain information regarding companies’ products. As for patents, we used the USPTO (United States Patent and Trademark Of<sup>fi</sup>ce) and Google’s patents search (http:// www.google.com/patents). This database allowed us to gather patent information and <sup>fi</sup>lter the results according to the patents’ assignee, type, status, and date of <sup>fi</sup>ling/ issuing. Our <sup>fi</sup>nal sample size for Google was 63 acquisitions which spanned from 2001 to 2008. As for Yahoo, the same size was 55 acquisitions from 1997 to 2008.

Caution was exercised in scoring the variables assigned to acquisition and innovation performance. Because an acquisition can lead to multiple patents and product/service releases, we focused on only the first patent and product/service release related to the acquisition. The researchers assigned initial scores, albeit independently. For the purposes of mitigating bias and ensuring correct assignment, 46 graduate students from the professional MBA (PMBA) and MS in Accounting programs were randomly allocated into 11 panels. The panels were asked to independently assign scores to each acquisition and innovation performance measure directly corresponding to and resulting from an acquisition. The scoring was based on attribute matching leading to an overall cumulative score. Interim checks ensured convergence in patent and product matching based on a corresponding acquisition based on convergence as well as attribute scores.

The panels researched multiple sources using Lexis Nexis Academic search and well as general trade publications to assign scores. The exercise was optional and constituted 5% of their grade. Once scores were assigned, we checked for group-level inter-coder reliability. The average inter-coder reliability for 11 panels was acceptable with average Krippendorff’s α of 0.867.

## De<sup>fi</sup>nition of measures

This study employ two dependent variables, namely TTR and time to patent (TTP), as multidimensional measures of post-acquisition innovation performance. TTR measured the elapsed time between an acquisition and the release of a product related to the acquisition. Likewise, TTP measured the elapsed time between an acquisition and the <sup>fi</sup>ling of a patent related to the acquisition.

Patents have long been used as a measure of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ innovation performance (Ahuja & Katila, 2001). The usefulness of patents lies in their ability to give the owner the exclusive right to create, use, and sell the patented technology for a time period. Such exclusivity allows companies to exclude competitors from lucrative markets which in return increase <sup>fi</sup>rm’s pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and market share. Simply put, timeliness and novelty of patents can be used as a measure innovation performance. For the purpose of this study, we measured TTP through the elapsed time between an acquisition and the <sup>fi</sup>ling of a patent related to the acquisition. First, we obtained a list of all the acquired companies by Google and Yahoo and the dates of the acquisitions. Second, we used the USPTO (U.S. Patent and Trademark Of<sup>fi</sup>ce) and Google’s patent database to obtain the date of the patent related to the acquisition. Finally we calculated the elapsed number of days between the acquisitions and the release of the related patent. Caution was taken to ensure that the panel chose the very <sup>fi</sup>rst patent release claims related to an acquisition as well as examining prior art references to the acquired company’s patents as few of the many guidelines for correspondence.

Product development and service offering initiatives have also been used as a measure of <sup>fi</sup>rm’s success and innovation performance (Schilling & Hill, 1998). Similar to TTP, we measured TTR through the elapsed time between an acquisition and the release of a product related to the acquisition. Again, we obtained a list of all the acquired companies by Google and Yahoo and the dates of the acquisitions. Then we used our data sources to obtain the date of the product/service related to the acquisition. Finally we calculated the elapsed number of days between the acquisitions and the release of the product/service.

In his Knowledge-in-Motion model, Datta (2007) noted the difference between the generation of creative ideas and the commercialization of those creative ideas into commercial products and services. On the one hand, not all creative ideas that are patented emerge as commercial products and services and often a large proportion of IP is shelved. On the other hand, not all commercial releases rely on patents (e.g., Amazon.com’s initial e-commerce platform release for selling goods online). In short, IP generation (pace measured by TTP) and IP commercialization (pace measured by TTR)

are two discrete events and a company’s post-acquisition innovation performance relies on its ability to harness both.

The following section describes each of independent variables (covariates) used in our study:

RELATEDNESS: Another binary variable which is used to determine whether the knowledge base of the acquired company is related or unrelated to the acquirer. In our data set, we were able to make the distinction by simply referring to the specialty of the acquired company. Simply, any company that specialized in the search engine and content aggregation market was labeled as being related. It is worthwhile to note that our choice of using companies’ specialty as a deciding factor was due to the information available to us at that time (Unrelated = 0, Related = 1).

SIZE\_INITIAL\_KNOWLEDGE\_BASE: The initial number of patents owned by the company being acquired since the company’s initiation until the day of the acquisition. For our data set, we used the USPTO (U.S. Patent and Trademark Of<sup>fi</sup>ce) database in conjunction with Google’s patent search to <sup>fi</sup>nd, triangulate, and con<sup>fi</sup>rm the total number of patents owned by the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm before its date of acquisition.

RADICALNESS: A binary variable that indicates whether a product is an increment (extension) of an existing innovation or a new innovation. In our data set, the researchers were able to draw the distinction by observing the history of all previously launched products and judging whether a similar product existed or not (Incremental = 0, Radical = 1). Given that the basis was ‘prior-art’ citations in patent applications, the consensus was based on objective criteria.

EMPHASIS \_KNOWLEDGE\_BASE: A binary variable that refers to the type of knowledge innovation. This variable can either be a product innovation or a process innovation (Product = 0, Process = 1).

The control variables are operationalized hereunder:

COUNTRY: A binary variable indicating the country of origin of the company being acquired. A value of 1 meant that the company was of a U.S. origin and a 0 otherwise. For our data set, determining the country of origin of the acquired company was achieved through obtaining information the company’s of<sup>fi</sup>cial website. Country was treated as a control variable in our study.

SPECIALIZATION X1, SPECIALIZATION X2: Two nominal variables which indicate the type of product being launched. Following a thorough review of Google’s and Yahoo’s products, we were able to categorize their products into four types based on device-based services vs web-based services: (i) Desktop and Desktop Extensions (device-based), (ii) Mobile Products (device-based), (iii) Web Products (webbased), and (iv) Development and Analytical Products (webbased). In our model, we represented those four specializations types by using a block design as shown in Table 2.

## Data analysis: Cox proportional hazards model

The object of our investigation is con<sup>fi</sup>rming whether and to what extent the acquired knowledge base impacts the ‘time to innovate’ for the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm. Given that the outcome under consideration is a temporal variable (i.e., the time between an acquisition and innovation), survival analysis, particularly Cox regression (Cox, 1972), or proportional hazards regression model seems an appropriate analytical tool. While Cox proportional hazards regression is popular in survival analysis in biostatistics and epidemiology (e.g., Lachin, 2000), there have been few treatments in mainstream IS literature, with the exception of Kauffman et al (2000). Kauffman et al (2000) used (and possibly introduced to the IS discipline) survival analysis to run a logistic hazard model to study the effects of network externalities and other antecedents on the adoption time-lag of electronic banking adoption among branches (refer to Kauffman et al, 2000, for an overview of the method and its treatment).

Cox regression is a part of the survival analysis toolkit. Survival analysis examines the distribution of time between characteristics of the triggering event (predictors or covariates) and discrete event outcomes (survival). In our case, the acquisition is the triggering event embodying characteristics of the acquired knowledge base while the launch of a patent or a product related to the acquisition are the discrete event outcomes. This forms the basis of the hazard function h(t) which assesses the innovation performance a time $t ,$ conditional on survival to that time – based on the covariates of acquisition characteristics.

$$
h (t) = \lim _ {\Delta t \rightarrow 0} \frac {\operatorname* {P r} [ (t \leqslant T <   t + \Delta t) | T \geqslant t ]}{\Delta t}
$$

where T is a random variable representing the survival time with cumulative distribution function $P ( t ) = \operatorname* { P r } ( T \leqslant t )$

In short, building on Cantor (1997), the basic elements used to model and compute and interpret this regression are:

1. The distribution function (cumulative density function), $F ( t ) = \operatorname* { P r } [ T < t ]$ where t ⩾ 0.

2. The survival function, $S ( t ) = 1 { - } F ( t ) .$

3. The probability density function, f(t), where $F ( x ) =$ $_ { - \infty } \int ^ { x } f ( t ) d t$

4. The hazard function is shown as $h ( t ) = f ( t ) / S ( t ) = - S ^ { \prime } ( t ) / S$ (t) where $t \geqslant 0$ and denotes the derivative operation. The hazard function is denoted by $h ( t ) = [ h _ { 0 } \dot { ( { t } ) } ] \ e ^ { b 1 x 1 }$ and dividing both sides by $h _ { 0 } ( t )$ , the hazard ratio $( h ( t ) / h _ { 0 } ( t ) )$ is $e ^ { b 1 x 1 } .$ The hazard ratio is thus a relative hazard calculated as exp(b).

5. The hazard ratio examines the rate of failure at time (t) such that the greater the value of h(t), the greater the hazard. The ratio is the expected change in the hazard when the value of a covariate changes from 0 to 1.

6. The hazard ratio $h ( t )$ for dichotomous variables is 1 when $x = 0$ and $h ( t ) = e ^ { b 1 }$ when $x = 1$

7. The hazards model allows us to generally deduce the following relationships: (i) if $h ( t ) = 1 ( \mathrm { i . e . , } \beta { = } 0 )$ , the covariate does not affect the hazard, (ii) if $h ( t ) { < } 1$ (β is negative), the covariate is associated with decreased hazard (increased survival), and (iii) if $h ( t ) > 1$ (β is positive), the covariate is associated with increased hazard (reduced survival).

While parametric survival analysis entails the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of a parametric model based on an exponential distribution with a log-baseline hazard, the Cox regression model is used instead of parametric survival regression models that leave the baseline hazard function unspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed (Fox, 2002). Cox regression is semi-parametric and covariates are introduced into the model. The hazard ratio is independent of time t and $h _ { i } ( t ) / h _ { i } ( t )$ is constant. The Cox model is proportional hazards regression model can be estimated by partial likelihood methods. Thus,

$$
\frac {h _ {i} (t)}{h _ {i ^ {\prime}} (t)} = \frac {h _ {0} (t) e ^ {\eta_ {i}}}{h _ {0} (t) e ^ {\eta_ {i ^ {\prime}}}} = \frac {e ^ {\eta_ {i}}}{e ^ {\eta_ {i ^ {\prime}}}}
$$

where i and $i ^ { \prime }$ are independent observations.

The proportional hazard assumption is that a unit increase in an additive effect of the covariates will have a multiplicative effect on the hazard for values of $t , e x p ( \beta x )$ Furthermore, we note the importance of scaling (units) of the covariates and therefore all units are consistently measured in terms of the number of days to throughout our regressions.

The Cox regression model assumes that additive changes in a covariate cause multiplicative changes in the hazard function $h ( t , x _ { i } )$ with time t and covariate $x _ { i \cdot }$ The basic proportional hazards model with parameter $\beta _ { i }$ is shown in (1) for three covariates. We note that the covariates may be either continuous or categorical (Cantor, 1997, p. 106). An important aspect is that $h _ { 0 } ( t )$ is independent of $x _ { i \cdot }$ On the one hand, if we reject $\beta _ { i } = 0 ,$ then $x _ { i }$ in<sup>fl</sup>uences the hazard function, on the other hand if $\beta _ { i } > 0$ then the survival time to the event of interest decreases (i.e., hazard increases) as $x _ { i }$ increases; the opposite is the case for $\beta < 0$ (Cantor, 1997).

$$
h (t, x _ {1}, x _ {2}, x _ {3}) = h _ {0} (t) \exp \left[ \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2} + \beta_ {3} x _ {3} \right]\tag{1}
$$

The survival function gives the probability that the survival time (i.e., time to the event of interest) will be at least (t), whereas the hazard function gives information about the risk of a process terminating at time (t) for a process that has continued at least that long. The hazard model assumes proportionality between the underlying hazard function and the log-linear function of the covariates assumption (Cantor, 1997; Lachin, 2000). The hazard ratio is the exponent of the parameter estimate and relates the change in hazard for a marginal unit change.

Our Cox proportional hazards regression considers the following:

1. The independent variables or covariates consist of innovation and <sup>fi</sup>rm characteristics related to an acquisition in time (t) and innovation performances of TTP and TTR thereafter.

2. The dependant variable is a measure of the pace of innovation performance measured by the time in days between an acquisition and TTP and TTR related to that acquisition.

3. The status of the dependent variables of TTP and TTR is a dummy variable depending on whether the observation is terminal (patent applied or product/service launched) or censored (patent not yet applied or product/service not yet launched).

4. The hazard function predicts the degree and the probability that an observation will culminate in a patent and a product/service launch.

5. The regression is semi-parametric and does not concern the baseline hazard or makes assumptions about the nature or shape of the hazard function.

6. The proportionality assumption assumes that changes in the levels of independent variables (particularly dichotomous variables) will produce proportionate changes in the hazard function that is independent of time, that is:

$$
\frac {h (t)}{h _ {0} (t)} = e ^ {(b _ {1} x _ {1} + b _ {2} x _ {2} + \dots b _ {k} x _ {k})}\tag{2}
$$

7. The extension of the Cox regression model with interaction variables can be shown as

$$
h (t) = [ h _ {0} (t) ] e ^ {(b _ {1} x _ {1} + b _ {2} x _ {2} + b _ {3} x _ {1} x _ {2})}\tag{3}
$$

## Results

In the Cox proportional hazards model formulation, a positive parameter estimate (coef<sup>fi</sup>cient) for a covariate refers to a higher hazard or lower survival, that is, a shorter time to patent and launch a product/service directly resulting from each acquisition. For the purposes of our study, we are interested in acquisition-related covariates that contribute to a higher hazard or a lower survival in innovation-related performance. In other words, the higher the parameter estimate, the faster the acquiring company can launch ideas and products directly resulting from an acquisition.

We ran a Cox proportional hazards model on innovation performance (TTP and TTR) for the Google and Yahoo acquisition data sets using a partial maximum likelihood estimation. For this study, all of our regressions were run using the procedure ‘PHREG’ of the statistical software SAS (Cantor, 1997). Further computational details can be found in Venables & Ripley (2002, p. 359) as well as Aalen et al (2008, p. 133). The proportional hazards assumption was checked for each regression as described in Cantor (1997, p. 122). Survival functions were computed using the Kaplan Meier estimates through the SAS procedure ‘LifeTest’ (Cantor, 1997).

It is important to note that in survival analysis studies, the issue of censoring arises when dealing with time dependent variables such as TTR and TTP. Censoring refers to the situation in which the expected event does not occur during the observation period. In our analysis, we had to deal with a special type of censoring called right censoring. Right censoring occurs when a company was acquired and a related product or patent was not released during the time period under consideration (i.e., 1997– 2008 for Yahoo and 2001–2008 for Google).

In interpreting the hazard model, we wish to note these two important facets: (i) a positive coefficient of a covariate corresponds to an increased hazard (or shorter survival) which, in our case, denotes faster times to the launch of ideas and products directly resulting from the acquisition, and (ii) the hazard function is the exponent of the parameter estimate $\beta x ,$ the hazard ratio points to the proportional sensitivity of the hazard (TTP and TTR, in our case) to a marginal change in the predictor (covariate). In that regard, a marginal-change reference is best suited for dichotomous covariates rather than ordinal covariates.

## Discussion

Companies acquire an innovation knowledge portfolio comprising attributes denoted in this study, notably the size and the nature of acquired innovation knowledge, namely incremental/radical, related/unrelated, and product/process.

Size of acquired knowledge on post-acquisition innovation performance Size of the acquired innovation knowledge is an important parameter in tracing innovation performance. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings are intuitive on the premise that a <sup>fi</sup>rm with a portfolio of innovations before acquisition is likely to be well-versed in the rules of idea generation and intellectual property protection. This streamlines the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to hasten the pace of generating patents for the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm, that is, Google. It is likely that companies with patents before their acquisition may have a pipeline of patentable ideas. Google appears to be pro<sup>fi</sup>- cient in capitalizing on the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm’s to speed up the <sup>fi</sup>ling of related patents. Yahoo’s statistically non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>ndings for the same (albeit maintaining the hypothesized positive directionality) appears to suggest that Yahoo’s ability to capitalize a large portfolio of acquired innovation for speeding its patents is unsystematic and often random.

Choices related to the size of the knowledge base of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm or whether the innovation knowledge base focuses on radical or incremental innovations do not seem to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly change the pace and timeliness of the timeto-release. While the size of the acquired knowledge impacts Google time to patent, the covariate does not appear to impact Google’s time to release products/services directly resulting from the acquisition. One explanation could be that because the size of the acquired knowledge is a patent-based measure, a <sup>fi</sup>rm with a history of patents is suggestive of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s best practices in patentability that may not translate into product releases. Moreover, patent <sup>fi</sup>ling may create a sense of complacency in <sup>fi</sup>rms and deter them from pushing forward the patents into product releases. Similarly, ‘patents and other forms of intellectual property protection for upstream discoveries may fortify incentives to undertake risky research projects’ (Heller & Eisenberg, 1998, p. 699) that may be infeasible as product releases in the short term – leading to patent barriers to product development (ibid).

In the case of Yahoo, the size of the acquired knowledge base, the relatedness of the innovation knowledge, or the product or process emphasis of the innovation were not found to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact Yahoo’s pace of release of a new application related to innovation knowledge acquired from an acquisition. This is, once again, worrisome because Yahoo appears to lack proper organizational routines and acquisition management and innovation discipline to create a systematic innovation timeline.

Radicalness of acquired knowledge on post-acquisition innovation performance The hypotheses related to radicalness of acquired knowledge received support only for Google for TTP and only for Yahoo for TTR. TTP for acquired radical innovation knowledge appears to increase the time it takes to patent and release corresponding postacquisition innovation patenting and product/service releases. Intuitively, at the patenting level, a radical innovation patent is less likely to refer to prior art compared with incremental innovations that can cite more prior art. Less prior art citations require more examination and elaboration, therefore increasing the time to takes to <sup>fi</sup>le a patent corresponding to the acquired radical innovation knowledge. In that same vein, a <sup>fi</sup>rm is likely to be more conservative in releasing products/services from acquired radical innovation. Releases based on incremental knowledge can be used to build economies of scale for product line ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies. A radical innovation release can often punctuate economies of scale, requiring a closer and longer examination.

Perhaps this underpins the <sup>fi</sup>ndings related to whether a patent is radical is incremental or radical and the time to release a product. One explanation may be that the radical/incremental emphasis has a perceptible effect of patenting than on product releases – partly because the releases may not be announced or that the feasibilities and because dif<sup>fi</sup>culties surrounding product releases do not change based on whether the innovation is radical or incremental. In the case of Yahoo, apparently, because incremental innovations allow a <sup>fi</sup>rm to build on an installed base of core knowledge, it seems simpler for Yahoo to seek innovations that allow Yahoo to build applications that add new features to its search engine application rather than seek radical application releases. Given that whether Yahoo pursues incremental or radical innovation based on acquired innovation knowledge is the only parameter that has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on Yahoo’s pace of innovation performance. It implies that, if Yahoo seems to have systematic organizational routines to manage incremental innovations to enhance its pace of postacquisition innovation performance, Yahoo might try to emphasize on incremental innovations and seek to acquire <sup>fi</sup>rms with innovation knowledge that can aid incremental innovations.

Relatedness of acquired knowledge on post-acquisition innovation performance Findings for the mechanics of transforming relatedness of acquired innovation knowledge on post-acquisition innovation performance offer contradictory evidence. While the hypothesized directionality is supported for TTP, the directionality is reversed for TTR.

In terms of TTP, <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that, while related innovation knowledge reduces the time to generate patentable ideas, related innovation knowledge may not be the best recipe to increase the pace of new product launches. Such a <sup>fi</sup>nding, albeit unexpected, has some intuitive appeal. A new release, especially, in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of web-based software applications, must be considerably different from its existing line. For example, if Proctor and Gamble adds cavity protection to an existing line of toothpaste, it can launch the innovation as a new product; but the case is different for web-based applications. If Google introduces a contact management tool for its Gmail application, Google will merely integrate it with the existing application (product) instead of heralding it a separate release. If the innovation knowledge is related, it can simply be incorporated within the existing web-product line (as an update) (e.g., the introduction of new Google search mechanisms such as ‘I’m feeling lucky’ or ‘similar pages’ are incorporated into Google Search without the pomp and ceremony of a new product or software release. For web-based applications, a new release needs to be unrelated to an extent that it is viewed as being independent of previous applications. Therefore, as the <sup>fi</sup>nding interestingly suggests, Google must leverage unrelated rather than related innovation knowledge to hasten its releases.

This <sup>fi</sup>nding is interesting, particularly in light of the impact of related acquisition knowledge on the time to generate patentable ideas (previous outcome variable). Of particular interest is the divergent effect of relatedness of acquired innovation knowledge on TTP and TTR for Google’s related innovations. While <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that Google can <sup>fi</sup>le a patent for a related innovation nearly six times faster than an unrelated innovation, it seems to take Google nearly double the time to release products from a related patent than from an unrelated patent. Our explanation to the divergence is the aforementioned dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of launching new applications in technology services, particularly web-based technologies. In non-web-based ‘client’ technologies such as Apple iPhone or Microsoft Windows, there is some software versioning. For example, iPhone’s software releases 3.0 and 3.1 can be considered as speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>rmware products that the client can opt to download and install. Similarly, Windows service packs are often released as speci<sup>fi</sup>c product versions. However, as the application becomes web-based, updates new software releases are incorporated on the server side than on the client side. Therefore, such server-side upgrades, even when they are noticeable, are not released as new applications but directly incorporated into the existing server-side software. For example, Google’s integration of chat (Google Talk) into its Gmail interface and its of<sup>fl</sup>ine accessibility, albeit noticeable improvements akin to new application (product) launches, are never promoted as new application releases such as Gmail 2.0 or Gmail 3.0. Instead, the market (including users) is only aware of

Google’s product releases that are unrelated to its existing application repertoire (e.g., Google Voice, Picasa, Sketchup).

The fact that Google does not release its upgrades as new application releases and integrates them into existing applications is suggestive of the fact that Google is constantly generating new ideas to enhance its existing applications – even when they are on the server-side and not listed as a new, related application releases. It does not preclude Google from protecting the ideas behind the upgrades as patents. Related knowledge centers on Google’s core competencies and allows Google to exploit (e.g., by combining, complementing) related innovation knowledge to arrive at new ideas faster than exploiting unrelated innovation knowledge. Therefore, while unrelated innovation knowledge eases the time to launch new ideas as patents, it impedes the launch of new application releases that are distinctly different than Google’s repertoire of web-based applications.

Product/process emphasis of acquired knowledge on postacquisition innovation performance Patenting post-acquisition process or product innovation knowledge offers a clue into issues related to intellectual property (IP) and its protection. One explanation may be that patenting a process innovation is considerably more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult (and worrisome) than patenting a product innovation. Another explanation may hinge on the possibility that a process patent may divulge competitive information more than a product patent. Given that a patent application does not thwart illegal misappropriation of IP and can merely offer a legal recourse, companies such as Google may not be too keen on applying for process patents in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place. Perhaps, the decreased hazard of process patents may not imply Google’s reticence toward process innovations; rather, the <sup>fi</sup>nding may signify Google’s tendency to not seek process patents to thwart erosion of competitive advantages from internal process innovation.

While the hypothesis received con<sup>fi</sup>rmation, it is interesting to realize the (assumedly) small lag for Google between innovation releases based on acquired product and process innovation knowledge. While lead time incentives for Google are higher for releases product innovation releases, the fact that Google appears to be able to simultaneously release both product and process innovations based on innovation knowledge with, albeit some lag, may be indicative of Google’s absorptive capacity and deft organizational routines (discussed in the next section) related to seeking and managing acquired innovation knowledge.

Taken together, Google appears to be more systematic in its ability to manage acquired innovation knowledge for post-acquisition innovation performance. However, several non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>ndings for Yahoo, albeit supporting hypothesized directionalities, may imply that Yahoo’s post-acquisition innovation management and performance are unsystematic and random without a focused and consistent method to capture and leverage acquired innovation knowledge.

It is a somber and, to a certain extent, a worrisome <sup>fi</sup>nding that may imply Yahoo’s inability to be consistent with generating innovative ideas in a timely manner from its slew of acquisitions. If a company acquires another <sup>fi</sup>rm to gain external knowledge but fails to create an appropriation mechanism that allows it to capitalize on the external knowledge, it may signal to the market the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm’s inability to absorb, combine, create, and protect new ideas for competitive differentiation. In Yahoo’s case, non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in the results while generally maintaining the hypothesized directionality appears to be driven my randomness rather than a concerted innovation strategy. For Yahoo, there seems to be no systematic focus on capitalizing on the acquired knowledge to develop patentable ideas. An unsystematic and random TTP may signal a lack of Yahoo’s ability to establish organizational routines to manage acquired knowledge and seek timely innovative differentiation and <sup>fi</sup>rst-mover advantages. Without a systematic routine to generate patentable ideas on time, acquisitions are likely to end up as sunk costs than as strategic investments.

## Contributions

We close this study by surfacing the contributions and limitations of this research. We began with an inquiry on whether and how acquisitions mattered in examining post-acquisition innovation performance in <sup>fi</sup>rms. The investigation was underpinned and scoped by our own curiosity on how and why two Internet services giants, Google and Yahoo, were sharing different fates and being gauged so differently by popular sentiment.

Although the study focuses on attempting to empirically evidence Google and Yahoo’s post-acquisition innovation performance, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings shed light on managing the dynamics of innovation from acquired innovation knowledge – offering strong implications for research and practice, as noted hereunder.

Central to our argument is the notion that if knowledge from acquisitions does not offer extrinsic and instrumental innovation advantages of increasing the pace of idea generation and application releases, acquisitions may possibly be a poison pill rather than a driver of innovations. The opportunity costs of acquisitions are high as acquisitions preclude <sup>fi</sup>rms from reinvesting in their existing competencies or offering dividends to its shareholders. A failure to assume timely innovations from acquisitions can negatively weigh in on a <sup>fi</sup>rm – from its cash <sup>fl</sup>ows to sustenance. It is interesting to <sup>fi</sup>nd that Google, a younger company than Yahoo, seems to have capitalized on second-mover advantages and out-rivaled Yahoo in its pace of innovation performance.

In regards to coordinating and integrating acquired innovation knowledge, Yahoo does not appear to effectively convert innovation knowledge from acquisitions to hasten its pace of post-acquisition innovation performance.

For example, even 5 years after Yahoo’s acquisition of OddPost, a webmail company, Yahoo does not seem to have appropriated the knowledge as patents or application releases or upgrades. Similar issues abound with Yahoo’s acquisition of MusicMatch and Broadcast.com. In fact, Yahoo’s inability to secure innovative advantages from its acquisitions may have forced Yahoo to reconsider its acquisition strategy. Instances abound: Yahoo’s acquisition of Jumpcut, bought to mimic Google’s acquisition of YouTube, was a duplication of effort with Yahoo Video and Flick and did not lead to related post-acquisition innovation performance. Yahoo has acquired Maven and Blogs but failed to innovate on its video monetization and blog tracking services, respectively; Yahoo recently closed Geocities, costly acquisition in 1999 because it failed to innovate on Geocities business model. Issues with Yahoo’s absorptive capacity may be central to the lack of signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant relationships between Yahoo’s acquisition of innovation knowledge and its ability to drive the pace of Yahoo’s post-acquisition innovation performance. Moreover, Yahoo’s post-acquisition innovation performance appears to be more random than systematic with high standard deviations. Google, on the other hand, appears to have a higher absorptive capacity with a more concerted set of acquisitions that is systematically channeled toward differentiable innovations. Google seems to have acquired the right set of <sup>fi</sup>rms and emphasized speedy post-acquisition innovation performance. Deja was integrated into Google to release Google Groups; Outride was integrated and released as iGoogle; Applied Semantics was released as Google AdSense; Writely into Google Docs, Postini for Gmail spam <sup>fi</sup>ltering, and Keyhole into Google Earth, among many others. In fact, Google’s culture of innovation is palpable in its conduct of releasing a multitude of products as free, beta versions, and the use of Google Labs (labs.google.com) as a showpiece for Google innovations. For example, Google’s \$12.5 Billion acquisition of Motorola Mobility in 2011 has been the company’s main thrust toward exploring the mobile hardware market to complement its Android OS for mobile platforms including 17,000 technology patents and 6800 applications. With the right urgency, Google has already integrated patents and announced an upcoming Motorola Android Device as well as un upcoming Google TV that leverages Motorola Mobility’s hardware and Google Android ecosystem.

Overall <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our study show that Google appears to be better at exploring right sources of acquired innovation knowledge and exploiting acquired knowledge. Google’s exploitation of acquired knowledge in terms of its pace of idea generation is successfully driven by its exploration of (i) innovation knowledge from <sup>fi</sup>rms with an established patent base, (ii) especially when the acquired innovation knowledge is focused on incremental innovations, (iii) the acquired innovation knowledge is aimed at related innovations, and (iv) the acquired innovation knowledge is focused on product innovations.

Beyond the scope of the Google and Yahoo as the two focal <sup>fi</sup>rms that drive the results, <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this postacquisition innovation performance study important pointers for all <sup>fi</sup>rms engaged in M&As.

First, the pace of innovation performance from an acquisition is a fundamental concern. Firms engaged in M&A behavior must create a culture of systematic acquired-innovation knowledge integration. Although innovation, per se, is often random and unsystematic, the process of transforming and leveraging acquired innovation knowledge must be systematic and integrated into the culture of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s operation. After all, a <sup>fi</sup>rm has to understand the opportunity costs of investing in acquisitions vs investing in core functions (Coase, 1937). Without deliberate and systematic integration efforts and mechanism in place, acquisitions can simply result in divestiture from core functions without ensuring alternative revenue trajectories. While <sup>fi</sup>rms can choose one of many options, namely strategic alliances, mergers, or acquisitions, acqui sitions seem the preferred mode for securing knowledge (e.g., Prabhu et al, 2005), primarily because acquisitions are the only formal contract where the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm owns the knowledge and can leverage it to the best of its ability.

The study alludes toward the importance of contemplating alternative knowledge access mechanism to complement a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s core knowledge base. Acquisitions transform the acquired company into a cost center where the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm has to use revenue streams from its core operations to pay for and support the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm’s resources until integration has been successful and the resources can pay for themselves as a pro<sup>fi</sup>t center. Unless the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm has capabilities and well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned SOPs (standard operating procedures) to swiftly coordinate and integrate acquired knowledge resources, uncertainty remains(Cools et al, 2004). However, alternatives offer some strategic respite. Given that <sup>fi</sup>rms have multiple contractual options to complement their knowledge base (Prabhu et al, 2005), for example, strategic alliances, joint ventures, mergers, or acquisitions, it might be prudent for <sup>fi</sup>rms that are unsure of their ability to integrate acquired innovation knowledge to focus their efforts on accessing other forms of complementary innovation knowledge via joint ventures and alliances. Such a strategy might be<sup>fi</sup>t the <sup>fi</sup>rm by allowing it access to information without the liabilities of acquisition investments that can become a sunk cost.

Second, <sup>fi</sup>rms engaged in M&A activities should practice a disciplined innovation culture. A <sup>fi</sup>rm’s innovation culture should be cognizant of its capability to acquire and integrate the innovation knowledge into their own stream of patents and products/services – therefore building its absorptive capacity, that is, its ability to acquire and assimilate resources and assets for effective exploration and exploitation. Google’s singular innovation culture also lends to it absorptive capacity. In order to increase absorptive capacity where a <sup>fi</sup>rm has the dexterity to assimilate various types of acquired knowledge, the <sup>fi</sup>rm has to ascertain that such a culture permeates the organization. Google is famous for allowing employees to spend

20% of their time to work on their own ideas, however radical or unrelated (Washington Post, 2006). According to the Washington Post (2006), ‘Google employees are encouraged to propose wild, ambitious ideas often’ citing Richard Holden, product management director for Google’s AdWords service that ‘If you’re not failing enough, you’re not trying hard enough’. A permeation of such an innovation culture increases the diversity of Google’s own innovation knowledge base that can be leveraged when integrating and absorbing a wide range of acquired innovation knowledge.

In addition to Google’s innovation culture, we argue that two other strategic aspects contribute toward Google’s absorptive capacity in assimilating post-acquisition innovations. First, Google appears to be able to leverage its economies of scope in assimilating acquired of unrelated innovation knowledge. Economies of scale are related to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies arising from producing related and incremental products and services. Economies of scope are related to creating ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies from reusing a bundle of resources in the joint production of multiple products and services. Google’s ability to cross-link services across its products offers it an integrated platform to gain ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies from economies of scope. For example, Google cross-links search, AdWords, mail, maps, and calendar across most of its products, allowing it to capitalize the resource bundle in a more integrated manner. For example, a calendar entry with a location allows it to search for it on Google Maps and prompt advertisements based on the location and/or Calendar item. Second, Google’s restructuring of its strategic focus from search to custom ad-provisioning allows it to better assimilate, integrate, and release products and services under a broader ‘monetizable’ umbrella of operations. Google uses its search competency for advertisement linkages via ‘Ad-Sense’ across all its offering. Regardless of the Google product or service, the ability to integrate custom ad-provisioning allows Google a more uni<sup>fi</sup>ed motive and mechanism to absorb unrelated and radical innovation knowledge, linked together by search-based, custom parsed, personalized advertisements.

Finally, in that vein, it is not only important for <sup>fi</sup>rms to increase their ex-post absorptive capacities and integration capabilities but also understand a priori justi<sup>fi</sup>cations for speci<sup>fi</sup>c acquisitions. Henningsson & Carlsson (2011) remark that post-acquisition IT integration processes are contingent upon the organization status and structure of acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm. The integration mechanics from acquiring a stand-alone <sup>fi</sup>rm (e.g., Google’s acquisition of Motorola Mobility as a division of Motorola) are different than from acquiring a <sup>fi</sup>rm is a part of a multi-business organization (e.g., Yahoo’s acquisition of Ludicorp that developed Flickr).

On the one hand, after Yahoo’s acquisition of Ludicorp, integrating Flickr required considerable carve-out integration costs (Henningssson & Carlsson, 2011) compared with Google’s acquisition of Motorola Mobility. Ludicorp was a multi-business unit with considerable interdependencies.

Flickr was a part of an image-sharing platform for Ludicorp’s massively multiplayer online game called Game Neverending. Carving out Flickr for integration required Yahoo to separate the codebase from the game platform to establish independence while investing in further developments such as tagging and editing as well as major site redesign. Moreover, carving out required migrating content from Canadian servers to U.S. servers with resulting changes subject to different federal regulations (Graham, 2006). Together, resulting interdependencies from acquiring Flickr as a part of a multi-business may have increased integration costs from reduced degree and speed of integration (Bauer & Matzler, 2014).

On the other hand, Google’s acquisition of Motorola Mobility, as a spin-off from Motorola Inc., required integration of a stand-alone <sup>fi</sup>rm (Bradshaw, 2011). Google acquired Motorola Mobility to strengthen Google’s patent portfolio while keeping the mobile hardware and cable and set-top businesses separate with integration requirements. The process allowed Google to (i) reduce complexities and inter-dependency costs by keeping the businesses separate, (ii) integrate Motorola Mobility patents into its patent portfolio while practicing cost-cutting and management restructuring, and (iii) initiate the sale of the cable and set-top divisions to Arris Group in 2013 and the mobile hardware division to Lenovo in 2014. The standalone nature of Motorola Mobility allowed Google to integrate and maintain the Advanced Technologies and Projects unit and 2000 patents while divesting the standalone hardware divisions – aligning innovative complementarities and integration ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies (Bauer & Matzler, 2014).

## Limitations and conclusion

While this study surfaces interesting facets surrounding Google and Yahoo’s pace of post-acquisition innovation performance, we need to acknowledge several limitations of this study. The <sup>fi</sup>rst of the limitations involves the scope and generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings, albeit interesting, are captive to Google and Yahoo only and may not stand up to the scrutiny of extrapolation. Google and Yahoo are behemoths in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of web-based search engines and content aggregation. However, several new <sup>fi</sup>rms (e.g., Wolfram Alpha, Dogpile), metasearch engines (e.g., Blindsearch), and interlopers (e.g., Microsoft Bing) are increasingly making headway, although Google and Yahoo still dominate the market. Take, for example, Amazon.com that has a much more server-heavy architecture vs a company such as Apple that has a client-heavy application-centric architecture. Although both offer services over the cloud, a server-heavy architecture does not enjoy the same fanfare of product and service releases as new ideas are integrated into a remote server without always drawing customer attention. Future research may try to expand this consideration set by examining the contingencies of architecture and emerging players in the online-services market.

In that vein, a further limitation lies in assuming that the core competencies of Google and Yahoo lie in search engines. Google and Yahoo may not essentially share the same placeholder. While both companies heavily relied on enhanced search algorithms with Yahoo! Search and Google, Google was more focused on search and Yahoo on content syndication. However, over time, even Google has become a content integrator with a variety of similar services such as Google Finance (Yahoo! Finance), Picasa (Flickr), and Google News (Yahoo! News). Still, it might be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to compare both companies purely on the basis of web-search evolution given that the difference in their fundamental identities. Moreover, Yahoo and Google share varying evolutionary timelines with Google being a relatively younger <sup>fi</sup>rm. Thus, it would be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to keep in mind these differences when considering the results of this study.

Second, while we predict that relatedness of the knowledge base of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm will enhance innovation performance in terms of faster idea generation and product launches, the prediction comes with a caveat. Related knowledge bases may suffer from some degree of redundancy and reinforcement – thus contributing to marginal innovation quality that might be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to sustain in the long-term. Although the pace of innovation performance was central to scope of our study, future research might <sup>fi</sup>nd it useful to examine the role of related knowledge acquisitions on innovation sustainability as an outcome variable. In addition, while dichotomous classi<sup>fi</sup>cations for innovation knowledge are an established practice in the methodology, future research can increase variable granularity by treating the variables as ordinal or continuous or interval for speci<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Third, the proxies used to assess and operationalize innovation performance may not be accurate indicators. We used patents as the proxy for idea generation and application releases for product/service launches. Although patent and application releases are proxies well established in prior literature, the limitation surrounds the underlying problems of choice. Literature assumes that, in competitive environment, acquisition motives are driven by innovations. However, there could be other motivations for an acquisition. Companies can acquire another <sup>fi</sup>rm to (i) mimic acquisitions of a rival <sup>fi</sup>rm, (ii) preempt a potential acquisition by a rival, (iii) vertical integration, or (iv) its talent pool rather than innovation knowledge. Such motives may dissuade the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm from seeking timely innovation outcomes. While the competitive market for web-based search engines and content aggregation is dominated by innovations, readers should note the limitation that some, if any, of the acquisitions by Google and Yahoo may have been driven by motives other than that of purely gathering innovation knowledge.

On a related note, this study is limited by the scope of the attributes examined herein. The study looks at relatively objective attributes of innovations and does not consider the quality of the acquired <sup>fi</sup>rm or the innovations. Yet, the quality of innovations often plays an important role in innovation performance. For example, in a study of total quality management practices in Australian manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rms, Prajogo & Sohal (2003) <sup>fi</sup>nd a direct and positive relationship between product quality and innovation performance. It would be useful and insightful if future research can surface quality attributes of innovations.

Another limitation of this study is our use of panels as a way to correspond acquisition data to acquisition knowledge attributes. While we assigned cautious checks to for validity and, thereby, ensuring reliability through crosschecks, the issue remains on how well the panels performed their task of accurately gauging innovation knowledge attributes and assigning related innovations to an acquisition. We also took additional steps to cross-validate published sources that were available to assess reliability. Nevertheless, the fact that the panels that gauged the data were not privy to internal information on the companies involved in the acquisition remains a limitation.

A further limitation in the empirical execution is that there may be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant unobserved heterogeneity among the acquisition events that may be correlated with both innovation rate and the explanatory variables, such as the size of the target <sup>fi</sup>rm, the variety of products of the target, the R&D capabilities of the target, the culture difference between the target and the acquiring <sup>fi</sup>rm, or the strategic reason behind the acquisition, to mention a few. Without carefully controlling for all the confounding effect, the estimated marginal effects of the explanatory variables are likely to be biased.

Finally, the moderating role of the size of the acquired innovation knowledge was not used comprehensively across the model. This research argues that the moderating roles of radicalness and size are more important in understanding TTP and TTR. We pay less attention to relatedness and size because it is less common for companies to conduct unrelated diversi<sup>fi</sup>cation than it is for companies to acquire <sup>fi</sup>rms with a radical, albeit related, innovations. In the same vein, we argue that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s knowledge acquisition based on patentability and product releases are generally more skewed toward product-related emphasis, thus making the moderating effect of size of the acquired knowledge emphasis less pronounced. However, we agree that this perspective may not capture the comprehensive acquisition calculus. Future research may <sup>fi</sup>nd it useful to extend the moderating roles of size of the acquired knowledge base across multiple direct measures.

Notwithstanding the endemic scope and aforementioned limitations, this study offers interesting insights into the dynamics of Google and Yahoo’s post-acquisition innovation performance. The novelty of this research lies in the following (i) the study connects innovation performance to corresponding acquisitions, (ii) the study dimensions innovation performance using outcomes of idea generation and new product releases, and (iii) the study examines the temporal pace of innovation performance to establish a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s capability to seek <sup>fi</sup>rst-mover advantages in intellectual property protection and application releases. Furthermore, the study rigorously investigates the anecdotes and trade analysis on the peril and quandary of Yahoo and the promise of Google.
