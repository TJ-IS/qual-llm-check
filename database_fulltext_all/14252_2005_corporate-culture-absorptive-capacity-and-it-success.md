# Corporate culture, absorptive capacity and IT success

Susan J. Harrington <sup>a,\*</sup>, Tor Guimaraes <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Information Systems and Communications, Georgia College & State University, 3690 Sussex Dr. NE Milledgeville, GA 31061-9337, USA

<sup>b</sup> Jesse E. Owen Chair of Excellence, Tennessee Technological University, Cookeville, TN 38505, USA

Received 5 March 2004; received in revised form 22 March 2004; accepted 25 October 2004

## Abstract

This study examines absorptive capacitys role in IT implementation success. Absorptive capacity is the organizations ability to recognize the value of new information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends [Cohen, W. M., & Levinthal, D. A. (1990). Absorptive capacity: A new perspective on learning and innovation. Administrative Science Quarterly, 35(March), 128–152]. Based on previous research, this study proposes a measure of absorptive capacity that includes managerial IT knowledge and communication channels and tests its relationship to the application of new technology in the form of expert systems implementation.

Related to claims about the importance of absorptive capacity are claims that a ‘‘learning culture’’ or ‘‘knowledge-friendly culture’’ is necessary in order for knowledge to be gained and efectively used within the organization. This study examines the type of corporate culture that influences absorptive capacity. The results provide support for absorptive capacitys proposed dimensions and its antecedent of corporate culture that act to influence the implementation of new technologies.

Keywords: Absorptive capacity; Corporate culture; Communication channels; Management knowledge

Recent emphasis by scholars and practitioners on the learning organization and knowledge management has pinpointed the important role that knowledge plays in organizational performance. Coupled with that emphasis are claims that a ‘‘learning culture’’ or ‘‘knowledge-friendly culture’’ is necessary in order for knowledge to be gained and efectively used within the organization (Davenport, DeLong, & Beers, 1998; Harvey, Palmer, & Speier, 1998). Yet little empirical work has fully analyzed the nature of such cultures and organizational knowledge and their relationship to organizational performance, such as when a new IT is implemented within the organization. Meanwhile, two previously proposed constructs: absorptive capacity, which is the ability to recognize the value of new information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends, and corporate culture, which is the pattern of values and ideas that shape human behavior in organizations, likely afect the understanding and attitudes important in the successful application of knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990).

Absorptive capacity has been developed theoretically through an examination of the organizations cognitive structures and prior related knowledge that underlie learning (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). Recently, a review of the literature (Zahra & George, 2002a) revealed two clarifying definitions beyond that provided originally by Cohen & Levinthal (1990): (1) a broad set of skills needed to deal with the tacit component of transferred knowledge and the need to modify this transferred knowledge and (2) the capacity to learn and solve problems. While implied by the original definition, these additional definitions have been used to argue for separating the absorptive capacity construct into two main dimensions: potential absorptive capacity (the capability to acquire and assimilate knowledge) and realized absorptive capacity (the exploitation or use of the knowledge that has been absorbed). Potential absorptive capacity is believed to consist of building the organizations ability to access and share external knowledge, which requires a knowledge-sharing culture (Daghfous, 2004). These dimensions reveal the dynamic aspect of absorptive capacity pertaining to knowledge transfer and creation.

Further, IT management competence, along with an organizations ability to transfer IT knowledge to line management, has been hypothesized as a key element of absorptive capacity related to IT implementation (Bassellier, Reich, & Benbasat, 2001; Boynton, Zmud, & Jacobs, 1994). This thinking appears to be consistent with Cohen & Levinthal (1990)s original theory in which they suggest that absorptive capacity readily applies to adoption and difusion of IT innovations, such as personal computers and software engineering practices that were new to organizations at the time. Yet despite its potential, few IT researchers have studied the absorptive capacity construct, with the notable exception being Boynton et al. (1994).

Meanwhile, culture, through its myths, sagas, and organizational stories, provides ‘‘cognitive maps’’ that help organizational members orient themselves to organizational interactions and technological developments (Argyris & Schon, 1978; Daft & Weick, 1984). Thus absorptive capacity focuses on the ability to implement innovations, whereas corporate culture focuses on the values and norms regarding technology and change. Corporate culture, then, would likely influence absorptive capacity leading to innovation implementation success, including IT implementation success. Converting Cohen & Levinthals (1990) dependent variable of ‘‘applying it to commercial ends’’ into IT terms, IT implementation success would include such dimensions as payback, reliability, enhanced competitive advantage, user satisfaction, and ease of use.

What type of corporate culture is related to absorptive capacity? The current study draws upon the literature on corporate culture, absorptive capacity, innovation, and IT implementation to further examine these constructs and the nature of the corporate culture-to-absorptive capacity-to-IT implementation success relationship. The model under discussion is shown in Fig. 1: what follows are discussions of absorptive capacity, corporate culture, and corporate cultures relationship to absorptive capacity. Hypotheses are then tested using a study of the implementation of an IT, expert systems, within organizations. The results should clarify what specific organizational culture characteristics enhance absorptive capacity and thus IT implementation success, an area where little empirical evidence exists (King et al., 1994).

## 1. Absorptive capacity

Absorptive capacity has been shown to be a promising component in understanding IT management practice and IT use, as well as enhancing the ability to efectively implement new innovations (Boynton et al., 1994). Absorptive capacity is dependent upon prior related knowledge, and knowledge within the organization allows a harvesting and retention of the value of the IT (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Miner, 1994). Possession of related expertise permits the organization to understand and censor new innovations as to their eventual use to the organization. While absorptive capacity is said to be dependent on prior knowledge, Cohen & Levinthals (1990) paradigm clearly shows technical knowledge and R&D as constructs separate from absorptive capacity. Their theory suggests that organizational absorptive capacity depends on the absorptive capacities of its individual members, but it is not simply the sum of the absorptive capacities of its employees. Organizational absorptive capacity is understood by focusing on ‘‘the structure of communication between the external environment and the organization, as well as among the subunits of the organization, and also on the character and distribution of expertise within the organization’’ (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990, p. 132). Thus organizational absorptive capacity is best understood in terms of the structures that allow multiple organizational members to recognize, understand, communicate, assimilate, apply, and exploit diverse knowledge within the organization (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002a).

It is clear then that absorptive capacity includes two major components: shared, collective knowledge within the organization and the use of communication channels across and within the organization. While this proposition has not generally been previously stated, these two components follow from the literature on absorptive capacity, as discussed below.

## 1.1. Managerial IT knowledge

Managerial knowledge can be described as ‘‘a state or fact of knowing’’ with knowing being a condition of ‘‘understanding gained through experience or study; the sum or range of what has been perceived, discovered, or learned’’ that can be applied to the organizations needs (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). Managerial knowledge occurs when the organization captures learning at the individual (and/or group) level and the knowledge is institutionalized into the organization as a whole (Argyris & Schon, 1978). Managerial knowledge is assumed to increase collective rather than individual action (Daft & Weick, 1984).

For experimentation with a new IT to occur, organizations must develop information processing mechanisms capable of detecting trends, events, competitors, markets and technological developments relevant to their survival, and it is assumed that such information converges and is interpreted for the organization at the top management level (Daft & Weick, 1984). Managers must constantly search beyond current competencies and routines, while building on current competencies, to survive competition that may exploit new technologies. Evidence supports the view that management-led, competency-based actions directed toward exploitation of new technologies are efective (Miner, 1994).

Bassellier et al. (2001) argue that technical knowledge, or the ‘‘know-how’’ possessed by the manager, is represented by ‘‘experience’’, developed over time through their participation in IT initiatives. Experience is thus the basis for the development of tacit knowledge, and within a group, shared experience is a mechanism that creates collective tacit knowledge (Leonard & Sensiper, 1998). If knowledge providers and potential acquirers have diferent prior knowledge sets, implementation of new technologies can be slowed (Warner, 2003).

In their study, Boynton et al. (1994) focused on the type of managerial knowledge important to IT implementation. They point out that absorptive capacity does not reside in any single individual but is dependent on the mosaic of activities, interactions, and exchanges among a number of individuals. This mosaic represents the structure of knowledge within an organization, the overlapping extent of this knowledge, and the interactions that occur among individuals (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). Managerial IT knowledge reflects the overlapping know-how of IT and line managers: i.e., it is the knowledge IT managers possess about the business and strategic issues within the firm, as well as the knowledge line managers possess about the potential opportunities resulting from the application of IT within their business domain (Boynton et al., 1994).

Boynton et al. (1994) found support for their hypothesis that managerial IT knowledge influenced the extent of IT use. In a more recent study, Lenox & King (2004) point out that existing literature underemphasizes the role of managers in administering information to organizational subunits and found that managers can directly afect a firms absorptive capacity by providing information to potential adopters in the organization. Therefore, in keeping with Cohen & Levinthals (1990) conceptualization of absorptive capacity, as well as Boynton et al.s (1994) findings, we propose:

H1: The greater the level of managerial IT knowledge, the more likely the successful implementation of a new IT.

## 1.2. Communication channels

Boundary spanning, both inside and outside the organization, is important to skill and knowledge development. Absorptive capacity requires that knowledge of recent technological developments be assimilated into the organization (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). A communication channel is defined as a means by which information is moved from one point to another within the social system (Chakrabarti, Feineman, & Fuentevilla, 1983), and so communication channels are important to absorptive capacity. External bits of information must be mixed with those bits already in use within the organization (Macdonald, 1995). A study of absorptive capacity and technological innovation in the banking industry by Pennings & Harianto (1992) found that an accumulation of IT experiences and interfirm communications (i.e., external communication channels) were components conducive to innovation. After reviewing research from a variety of research streams, Brown & Eisenhardt (1995) concluded that when external communication is frequent, project teams are more likely to develop an absorptive capacity such that they become more eficient at gaining and using the information gathered.

The above discussion suggests that absorptive capacity for new technologies is enhanced when external information is added to IT knowledge already within the organizational context. To allow external technical knowledge to reach organizational members, organizations often hire new employees, access documents describing new developments, encourage personal contacts with knowledgeable individuals outside the organization, and use other external communication channels (e.g., professional meetings, workshops, training) (Zmud, 1983).

Similarly, studies on innovation suggest that project teams access to both external and internal information on IT implementation is important to success (Nilakanta & Scamell, 1990). For example, Guha, Grover, Kettinger, & Teng (1997) found the use of external communication channels to be clearly related to an enhanced learning capacity among three organizations involved in business process change. Rai (1995) found that learning, which occurred through contacts with other programmer/analysts in other organizations, vendor representatives, consultants, and trade publications, enhanced difusion of CASE technologies. When the external communication channel dimension of absorptive capacity was measured by using a co-citation analysis of a firms publication activity, absorptive capacity was shown to have a significant correlation to the firms increase in market-value (Deeds, 2001).

In addition, the existence of a formal internal technical group enhances the transfer of technical knowledge from the outside into the organization (Allen, Tushman, & Lee, 1979; Zmud, 1983). This finding is consistent with Cohen & Levinthals (1990) absorptive capacity theory, which suggests that ‘‘the firms absorptive capacity depends on the individuals who stand either at the interface of the firm and the external environment or at the interface between subunits of the firm.’’ (p. 132). Recipients must be able to understand and exploit the new technology (Szulanski, 1996). Individual expertise with diversity of knowledge within the group increases the prospect that incoming information will relate to what is already known.

Internal communication channels (i.e., the way in which information is moved from one point to another within the organization) linking organizational members should enhance knowledge flow once the idea for a new IT has entered the organization. Based on observing several organizations involved in knowledge management projects, Davenport et al. (1998) suggest that a ‘‘knowledge-friendly’’ culture and multiple channels for knowledge transfer are important for efective knowledge management projects. Such communication channels include regularly scheduled meetings and standard reports, as well as unscheduled meetings and special studies, or what Zmud, Lind, & Young (1990) call group gatherings. Such meetings and reports would be expected to aid the direction, resource commitment and support by an organizations management, thereby facilitating group practices (Zmud, 1983). Lind & Zmud (1991) found that richer communication channels (i.e., those that provide opportunities for immediate feedback, a broader range of message cues, and the capability to ‘‘personalize’’ messages) allowed convergence in mutual understanding between organizational members, thereby efecting innovativeness. Moreover, in situations of high equivocality and high uncertainty, such as in implementing nonroutine technologies or when interdependence across departments occurs, full time integrators, task forces, teams, and group meetings are needed to provide rich information requirements (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Lack of understanding between participants due to lack of interaction, insuficient knowledge sharing, and diferent terminology can lead to missed opportunities in achieving common organizational goals.

In addition, cross-functional experience may also be required to foster a holistic understanding of the organization, whereas lack of communication between depart ments increases the risk of group think (Larsen, 1993). When cross-functional personnel combine their perspectives in a highly interactive, iterative fashion, richer communications and product success can result (Dougherty, 1990). A business units network position or ability to share information with other units may interact with its learning to afect implementation results (Tsai, 2001). Information sharing, participatory decision-making structures, and use of task forces have been found to be related to management knowledge and efectiveness (Boynton et al., 1994; Grover, 1993). When very large projects involving hundreds of people are necessary, an organic network of self-managing teams that facilitate knowledge transfer has been successful (Ayas, 1996). Exchanges of persons and sharing of written reports have been associated with higher absorptive capacity (Mangematin & Nestra, 1999). Thus job rotation, cross-functional teams and task forces, as well as managers as integrators, are structures facilitating absorptive capacity and likely contributing to IT implementation success (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). A call for research has been made on how external knowledge is shared internally, with the suggestion that cross-divisional coordination should be associated with higher absorptive capacity (Van den Bosch, Volberda, & de Boer, 1999).

In sum, we propose that managerial IT knowledge supported by external and internal communication channels constitute an organizations absorptive capacity, and these components should afect the successful implementation of information technologies. Therefore we operationalize absorptive capacitys definition as the managerial IT knowledge and communication channels that enable organizations to recognize the value of new information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends. This operationalization is also consistent with recent research that views absorptive capacity as the capabilities, including the specific routines and tasks, that enable: (a) the evaluation and assimilation of knowledge and (b) the ability to apply the knowledge internally (George, Zahra, Wheatley, & Khan, 2001; Zahra & George, 2002a; Zahra & George, 2002b).

Therefore, communication channels, which are the routines and tasks that transport knowledge from external sources into and across the organization, should enhance IT implementation success:

H2: The greater the use of internal and external communication channels, the more likely the successful implementation of a new IT.

## 2. Corporate culture

Evidence exists that corporate culture shapes absorptive capacity by its efect on both knowledge and communication channels. Because culture reflects the values and norms held by the organization, it plays a role in determining the areas in which the organization is able to learn easily and those in which it is likely to resist changing its policies (Berthoin Antal, Dierkes, & Hahner, 1997). Where an event in the environment is consistent with the organizations existing cultural values, the organization will be more likely to respond more quickly than if the event contradicts long held values (Berthoin Antal et al., 1997). In their development of the concept of absorptive capacity, Cohen & Levinthal (1990) point out that if the event or idea is too distant from the firms expectations, the organization may resist accepting it, resulting in its ‘‘lockout’’ or rejection. More recently, consistency of the change or innovation with the organizations norms and values has been called ‘‘relative absorptive capacity’’ (Lane, Salk, & Lyles, 2001), since the change or innovation should be more successful when consistency exists. Thus the corporate culture with its values is related to absorptive capacity and will influence the desire for and reaction to information that is gathered and transformed through the external and internal communication channels into organizational knowledge.

Similarly, a culture that values both an external focus (i.e., the improvement of its competitive position) and an internal focus (i.e., the maintenance of its sociotechnical system) may maximize its eficient use of innovation (Harvey et al., 1998). The organization must procure information and resources from the outside, but without becoming so outwardly focused that the ability to do its work is jeopardized (Kanter, 1996). Thus both knowledge and communication channels are afected by the value an organization places on meeting the competition or enhancing internal eficiency (i.e., the external or internal focus). In sum, culture will likely influence absorptive capacity through its emphases on flexibility vs. order and the external vs. internal view (see Fig. 1).

As implied by the above, underlying the culture are implicit beliefs about the behaviors and mode of human information processing in the organization that will result in desired outcomes. Quinns (1988) competing values typology of corporate culture is based upon two dimensions of implicit beliefs: (1) spontaneity and flexibility (towards decentralization and diferentiation) vs. predictability and order (towards centralization and integration), and (2) external vs. internal (Quinn, 1988). This taxonomy results in four culture types: developmental (flexible and external), rational (predictable and external), hierarchical (predictable and internal), and group (flexible and internal) that vary along these dimensions (Quinn & Rohrbaugh, 1983). However, it should be noted that ethnographic culture researchers, who believe that understanding culture is best done by immersion in the life of the people under study and who believe that knowledge is fabricated through daily interactions between people, would argue that the competing values framework simplifies the idea of culture to a level that is not culture. Other researchers would argue that the competing values framework corresponds to the collective cognitive representation of value patterns learned over time. These researchers view values as an ‘‘intermediate’’ measure of culture: i.e., an approach that does not deny the existence of deeper levels of culture, such as assumptions and beliefs unique to a culture, but does not rise to the surface level of practices, symbols, and artifacts that are highly concrete (Denison, 1996). Denison (1996) compares and contrasts the research paradigms advocated by ethnographic/anthropological researchers (who tend to be qualitative) with those advocated by Lewinian field theory and psychological researchers (who tend to be quantitative), with the conclusion that the two are overlapping and, at times, merging. Both are based on the values of organizational members. His conclusion is that the two approaches are a matter of interpretation, rather than theoretically diferent (Denison, 1996). Although there are diferent definitions of culture, the common theme throughout culture research has been summarized as follows: an organizations culture can be represented by its values, and there are common value dimensions that are found in most organizations (Howard, 1998).

While not as ‘‘rich’’ as ethnographic studies that study culture in a qualitative way, the competing values framework has been found to be valid for measuring the cultural dimensions that are common across organizations, as well as for evaluating culture types relative to other variables (Howard, 1998). The competing values approach allows managers to understand the values underlying their corporate cultures and to take action to adapt the organization to desired outcomes. Moreover, the measure can be used to examine the compatibility of organizational changes or innovation to the existing value structure of the organization, which is the objective of this study. Thus the competing values framework will be used as the basis of measuring culture in this study, and its four culture types of developmental, rational, group and hierarchical will now be discussed in relation to absorptive capacity.

## 2.1. Competing values related to absorptive capacity

Norms that promote change include risk taking, openness, shared vision, a high expectation for action, and a focus on quality (Kettinger & Grover, 1995). Types of cultures that promote an external focus (i.e., developmental and rational) would be consistent with these norms, as well as with the values of learning and innovation (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Fiol & Lyles, 1985; Grover, 1993). Procedural autonomy, such as may be found in developmental cultures, coupled with multiple milestones, such as may be found in rational cultures, are believed to provide the ideal structural context surrounding innovation projects (Kanter, 1996; Sitkin, 1996). A developmental culture and its values that are consistent with opportunities and incentives for technology experimentation, the external environment, and distributed decision-making, encourage learning (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Fiol & Lyles, 1985; Sitkin, 1996). Meanwhile, monitoring and rewarding completed technology experimentation is consistent with rational cultures, where goal clarification, logical judgment, timely information, and direction setting are assumed to be the means to the end of improved eficiency and productivity. In sum, the external focus culture types of developmental and rational would be conducive to developing absorptive capacity.

A culture and reward system that supports participation and knowledge sharing should also be important in developing absorptive capacity. It has been stated that knowledge sharing across organizations is more of a cultural than a technical issue (Harvey et al., 1998). If an organization is characterized by mistrust and lack of willingness to share information, knowledge sharing and communications will decrease. Therefore, a strong group culture would lead to greater managerial IT knowledge and more open communication channels.

In sum, organizations that emphasize the values of the group, developmental and rational culture dimensions should maximize their absorptive capacity. A culture strong in these three dimensions may lead to increased learning capacity and knowledge-sharing capability (Janz, Wetherbe, Davis, & Noe, 1997; Kettinger & Grover, 1995). Alternately, hierarchical cultures with their emphasis on stability and control are most likely to result in resistance to change and fewer receptors to the environment (Fiol & Lyles, 1985). Other studies have found that those having too strong of a hierarchical culture did not score well in other performance domains, (Quinn & Spreitzer, 1991). Similarly, it has been argued that highly mechanistic structures found in organizations with hierarchical values would be more likely to fail in the implementation of advanced technologies because of the organizations inability to adjust to the new technology (Zammuto & OConnor, 1992). When bureaucracy associated with hierarchical cultures and bureaucratic delays are eliminated, such as in a flatter organization in which managers work directly with lower levels, communication, creative problem solving, and rapid turnaround time for innovation are facilitated (Kanter, 1996). In their study on absorptive capacity, Lane et al. (2001) found that flexibility and adaptability are related to knowledge learned.

It should be noted that the competing values model does not suggest that these four culture dimensions cannot mutually exist in an organization (Quinn, 1988). Businesses are seldom characterized by one pure cultural type, i.e., where one of the dimensions greatly predominates (Yeung et al., 1991). Instead, culture strength has been conceptualized as the degree of intensity of feeling that organization members have about all culture dimensions (Yeung et al., 1991). Some organizations are believed to have strong-comprehensive cultures, with previous studies estimating that between 6% and 25% of organizations have cultures rating highly in all four culture dimensions (Yeung et al., 1991; Zammuto & Krakower, 1991). More recent research (i.e., Buenger, Daft, Conlon, & Austin, 1996) suggests that when managers focus too closely on one set of values, the concern for other sets may decline, resulting in loss of eficiency and short-term profitability. Balancing of values is dificult but important.

In the belief that strong-comprehensive cultures are the most desirable, the competing values framework has been used as an intervention to assist managers in reorganizing and generating action statements that attack weaknesses in some culture dimensions while maintaining strengths in others. For example, the action statements resulting from some interventions have taken account of the need for supporting existing strengths of flexibility and innovation while linking the resulting growth to the internal capacity for quality control and production. In efect, a whole systems approach can result from using the framework as an intervention (Quinn, 1988).

Based on the above discussion, it would be expected that a strong culture for those areas of the organization interested in building absorptive capacity would be one with strong developmental, rational, and group culture dimensions, while ensuring values associated with hierarchical culture dimension are held in check (Quinn & Spreitzer, 1991). We therefore propose that strong corporate cultures that support greater sharing of information (group culture), development of new approaches (developmental culture), and greater productivity and goal-setting (rational culture) would be those which would tend to facilitate absorptive capacity and its hypothesized dimensions of managerial IT knowledge and communication channels. Thus we propose:

H3a: Organizations with stronger levels of the corporate culture dimensions of group, developmental, and rational, while maintaining a lower level of the hierarchical dimension, will have a higher level of managerial IT knowledge than other organizations.

H3b: Organizations with stronger levels of the corporate culture dimensions of group, developmental, and rational, while maintaining a lower level of the hierarchical dimension, will have a greater level of communication channels than other organizations.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Sampling and data collection

It is important to sample organizational members that have knowledge of the IT implementation environment. Among the organizational actors in a position to describe the organizations culture, structures, and expertise surrounding the implementation of an IT, IS managers were selected because of their direct contact with the users and implementers of the IT. Moreover, IS managers would be in a position to know about the managerial IT knowledge involved in the IT implementation and the types of communication channels in use. The IS managers should also be knowledgeable of the results of an implemented IT. Lastly, top management is an important source, if not instigator, of the values (Howard, 1998). Those managers who have a broad view have been found to be the best informants on the environment, available resources, workflow patterns and unit values (Buenger et al., 1996). Therefore, IS managers appear appropriate for this study on IT management knowledge, communication channels, and the culture and have been used in previous absorptive capacity, corporate culture, and IT implementation studies (e.g., Boynton et al., 1994).

The mailing list of a national association of IS managers was used as the sampling frame. The questionnaire was included in a periodic mailing (newsletter, conference announcements, etc.) from the association to its members. One thousand IS managers were randomly selected from the mailing list to receive the questionnaire and the study report. The managers could either return the questionnaire in a prepaid and self-addressed envelope to the association or directly to the researchers. A total of 203 questionnaires were returned within 3 weeks. A reminder postcard was mailed twenty days after the first mailing. In the following 2 weeks, 45 additional questionnaires were received in time to be included in this study for a total of 248. Subsequently, six questionnaires were discarded due to missing or invalid data for a useful response rate of approximately 24%, a rate consistent with many IS studies. Of the 242 respondents, 83 had implemented expert systems, the IT under study, and were able to provide an evaluation of its results at the time of this study, in 1998. Thus a sample size of 83 was achieved.

## 3.2. Sample description

The sample demographics suggest that the preponderance of organizations were manufacturing oriented or multi-industry with a national or international business reach (see Table 1). Approximately 80% of organizations had revenues between 51 and 600 million dollars. The size of the 83 organizations that implemented expert systems ranged from 120 to 11,000 employees, with an average number of employees of 978. Thus our sample and sub sample consist of somewhat larger organizations, and the sub sample of 83 did not appear to be significantly diferent from the larger sample (see Table 1).

## 3.3. Measures

The literature was searched to identify specific measures most appropriate for each construct being studied. As previously mentioned, there is no standard measure for absorptive capacity. Some researchers have operationalized it as ‘‘R&D intensity’’: the ratio of R&D spending divided by sales (Stock, Greis, & Fischer, 2001) or R&D spending (George et al., 2001). Another has operationalized it as a co-citation analysis of a firms scientific publications (Deeds, 2001). However, these simple measures fail to capture the rich multidimensional and structural nature of absorptive capacity conceptualized above. Therefore, items were culled from other instruments, as described in more detail below, and developed further.

A composite measure of absorptive capacity was sought to be consistent with the discussion above and to include managerial IT knowledge and communication channels. ## 3.3.1. Absorptive capacity: Managerial IT knowledge

Boynton et al.s (1994) absorptive capacity instrument consisting of five questions (Appendix A, absorptive capacity questions 11–15) was used to capture the extent to which IS management understood the operations and strategies of other units and the extent to which other units understood the potential of IT. While Boynton et al. (1994) did not claim that absorptive capacity was fully described by their measure, they claimed that it did represent a major component of absorptive capacity.

They argued that absorptive capacity was represented by the conjunction of IT-related and business-related knowledge possessed and exchanged among IT and business unit managers. While their measure focuses on management, we argue that it ignores the structure of communication channels within the organization as included in Cohen & Levinthals (1990) original theory. Therefore we use the Boynton et al. measure but expand it as described in the following section.

## 3.3.2. Absorptive capacity: Communication channels

The discussion above describes communication channels in terms of external (boundary spanning) or internal (transfer of knowledge within the organization). The research described above suggests that external communication channels have included the use of external sources of knowledge (e.g., periodicals), the existence of internal technical expertise, or boundary spanners that can recognize innovations of value, and internal structures that provide the bridge between external knowledge and the organization. Measures used to reflect use of external sources of knowledge were six condensed items from Nilakanta & Scamells (1990) and Chakrabarti et al.s (1983) studies. Respondents were asked to indicate their frequency of use on a 7- point scale, from not used at all to constantly used, of the following: (1) Books: reference, textbooks, handbooks, and professional books; (2) Trade periodicals (e.g., Datamation, Infosystems, Computerworld); (3) Professional journals and reports (e.g., ACM publications, Journal of Systems Management); conference proceedings; (4) Internal people: groups and internal experts; (5) External people: outside consultants and professional organizations; and (6) In-house technical memoranda. In order to represent diversity of external information sources used, these sources were entered as a summed item standardized to a 7-point scale (see Appendix A, absorptive capacity question 1).

Three questions from Grover (1993), Ettlie, Bridges, & OKeefe (1984), & Cohen & Levinthal (1990) were used to assess the availability of technical specialists within the organization who would be able to serve as boundary spanners and aid in the communication about and implementation of the IT (see Appendix A, absorptive capacity questions 2–4).

Communication channels also included questions based on the work of Daft & Lengels (1986) & Boynton et al. (1994) (see Appendix A, absorptive capacity questions 5–10). These questions reflect the use of cross-functional teams, task forces, reports, etc. that would be used to bring knowledge into and share knowledge within the organization.

## 3.3.3. Validity of absorptive capacity measures

The first version of the questionnaire was mailed individually to eight practitioners for review. A few days later the researchers personally interviewed each practitioner to collect any comments regarding the questionnaire content, format, readability, and the ability of target respondents to address the questions. Some of the questions were modified to reflect the suggestions made. The final version was mailed to the 1000 IS managers described above.

## 3.3.4. Corporate culture

Culture was measured by three statements per culture dimension from Quinns (1988) and subsequently Yeung et al.s (1991) culture instrument (see Appendix A, Culture questions 1–12). The statements were factor analyzed using varimax factor analysis (see Table 2). All statements loaded together except those for group culture. Contrary to previous researchers findings and the authors own use of the instrument in another study, the three group culture statements loaded on diferent factors. It may be that larger firms no longer consider their cultures consistent with terms like ‘‘tradition’’, ‘‘family’’, etc. used in the group culture measure.

The nine other culture statements loaded together, indicating that organizations tended to display an overall culture strength, with the dimensions of developmental and rational varying together and the hierarchical dimension varying in the opposite direction. Separate constructs for the individual cultures suggested by Quinn (1988) & Yeung et al. (1991) were not confirmed. The net results of this study suggest that organizations see hierarchical values as opposite and negatively related to an external focus, which is in keeping with the competing values framework. Therefore, our result is not a culture strength per se, but an external-focus culture strength. We therefore computed external-focus culture strength by averaging the nine responses to the individual culture statements without the three group culture statements that did not load on the factor. Hierarchical culture statements were included after being reverse-scored based on the theory described above and because these statements are internal focus and were negatively related to all other culture items. The resulting external-focus culture strength measure displays good reliability, with a Cronbach alpha of 0.94, and good convergent and discriminant validity, as shown by the factor analysis.

## 3.3.5. IT (expert systems) success

This study hypothesized that absorptive capacity would facilitate IT implementation success. To test the hypothesis and the predictive validity of our measure of absorptive capacity, expert systems implementation was selected because it would be expected that a fairly large number of organizations would have had a chance to attempt to implement it and be able to evaluate its results.

Prior research has used a variety of measures of innovation or system success, including user satisfaction, ease of use and perceived benefits of the system, improved decision quality and performance (e.g., Aldag & Power, 1986), and business profitability (e.g., Benbasat & Dexter, 1982). It is widely agreed that the choice of the ‘‘best’’ measure for system success depends on the study objectives (DeLone & McLean, 1992). Alavi & Leidner (1999) suggested the development of meaningful metrics for measuring the value, quality and quantity of knowledge are needed, and to that end, explicit and important aspects of organizational performance, such as customer satisfaction, product/service innovations, time to market, cost savings, competitive positioning, and market shares, should be used as measures to find points where knowledge adds value. In keeping with this recommendation and consistent with the IT under study, our questions focus on whether the IT provided good payback for cost, reliability, improved response, competitive advantage, user satisfaction, and ease of use. Eight questions in total were used to evaluate the results (see Appendix A). All eight items loaded on the same factor in the factor analysis (not shown) and had good reliability with a Cronbach alpha of 0.96.

## 5. Conclusions and implications

Based on previous research, this study conceptualized and provided a measure of absorptive capacity, which is the ability to recognize the value of new information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends. A two-dimensional measure consisting of managerial IT knowledge and communication channels is the result and is consistent with Cohen & Levinthals (1990) emphasis that organizational absorptive capacity is understood by focusing on the structure of communication between the external environment and the organization, as well as among the subunits of the organization. Moreover, the measure is consistent with the recently elaborated nature of absorptive capacity, i.e., the routines and processes related to the acquisition, assimilation, transformation and exploitation of knowledge, as described by Zahra & George (2002a). This study found that both dimensions have a clear relationship to the implementation success of one IT, expert systems, confirming that the absorptive capacity measure has good predictive validity consistent with its definition. Thus a major contribution of this study is a clearer understanding of the measurement and efect of absorptive capacity.

The findings suggest that building absorptive capacity is best envisioned as building overlapping and shared knowledge structures within the organization. Knowledge should be exchanged among IT managers and business unit or line managers, and each link of the communication channel, from gathering information from the external environment to sharing the information through a variety of formal and informal means, should be in place. It is important to establish an external communication channel, i.e., boundary spanners who access a variety of external sources of knowledge and have the technical knowledge to recognize and communicate about ITs. It is also important to establish an internal communication channel, i.e., the cross-functional teams, formal and informal meetings, and formal reports for shared communication.

Our construct of absorptive capacity was related to corporate culture, much as predicted, further suggesting its value as a measurement tool. In particular, a strong externally focused culture accounted for 31% of the variance in the communication channels dimension of absorptive capacity. An organizational culture that is focused externally while limiting bureaucracy (hierarchical values) is most likely to lead to higher absorptive capacity. An external focus means that organizations that value flexibility and the improvement of their competitive position within the environment over the maintenance of their socio-technical system appear to have higher absorptive capacities. The finding of a relationship between the values of an organizations culture and absorptive capacity further adds to the validity of the absorptive capacity measure, since Cohen & Levinthal (1990) advocated that the innovation must be in line with the firms expectations; otherwise the organization may resist accepting it, resulting in its ‘‘lockout’’ or rejection.

## 5.1. Limitations

This study was cross-sectional. A weakness is that corporate culture and absorptive capacity at the beginning point of expert systems implementation was not measured. Therefore the direct causal efect cannot be entirely inferred. However, due to the slowness of cultural and absorptive capacity change, our assumptions with respect to causality appear justifiable.

Another limitation is the use of IS managers as spokespersons for those organizational members implementing expert systems. It is believed that these managers are the best single individuals to judge the culture, managerial IT knowledge, and success of the IT. However, future research may wish to include multiple individuals perceptions of the culture and absorptive capacity surrounding the IT implementation.

Finally, the measure of group culture did not load together or on the other culture dimensions as expected. This is not consistent with previous research, including the authors own research, but, as mentioned, the wording (i.e., tradition, extended family, etc.) of these items may no longer apply in larger organizations. Alternately, this may indicate that the culture measure is possibly subject to impression management in that the respondents are answering in a way so as to gain researcher approval. However, previous research using the measure has not found this problem. Even so, a promising future research area may be to examine the instrument, and the measurement of group culture in particular, for impression management and group cultures applicability to IS.

## 5.2. Implications for research

The absorptive capacity measure developed here would not be useful for all absorptive capacity studies, since it clearly does not capture the items relating to R&D or new product development. However, as a measure for organizational eforts to utilize IT strategically, the measure may prove useful. Given the relative vagueness and lack of attention in the literature to measuring absorptive capacity, our absorptive capacity measure is consistent with Cohen & Levinthals (1990) theory and embodies the structure of communications within the organization. Future researchers may wish to test the appropriateness of the absorptive capacity measure on other innovation or IT implementation eforts.

Researchers also need to investigate the group culture dimension. While some evidence (cf. Zammuto & Krakower, 1991, p. 96) existed prior to this study that larger, independent organizations may score lower or less consistently on group values than on developmental and rational values, the unexpected responses on group values found in this study of generally larger firms suggests diferent views on group cultures may occur depending on size and public vs. private organizations. Terms like ‘‘family’’, ‘‘tradition’’, and ‘‘loyalty’’ may no longer be appropriate terms to describe the group culture dimension within larger organizations. If so, it may also be that diferent culture types influence absorptive capacity diferently in smaller organizations than in the larger organizations investigated here.

Since this research argued for and found that the existence of a separate technical specialist group aided IT implementation, a research question may be whether a small organization can compensate for not having a separate technical specialist group if it is too small to have one, i.e., are there alternative ways to scan for new technologies and share knowledge internally about new technologies, or are smaller organizations going to remain at a disadvantage due to lack of size and diversity of knowledge within the organization? Cohen & Levinthal (1990) state that simply hiring technical expertise, such as in outsourcing or using consultants, will not provide the internal organizational know-how and experience to implement the innovation within the organization.

In addition, the cultural values conducive to absorptive capacity development were those externally focused, i.e, developmental and rational culture types. This finding is somewhat in opposition to some competing values research, which advocates that a high overall culture strength is conducive to organizational performance. Our find ings suggest that a lopsided, externally focused culture is best. Further research is needed to confirm these findings. Use of qualitative culture study approaches may prove fruitful in uncovering the underlying phenomenon and the rich relationships that occur within an organization.

## 5.3. Implications for practice

The study provided insights into the types of activities that management should undertake in order to enhance absorptive capacity. These include extensive use of external and internal communication channels, such as exposing internal groups to external environments and information, hiring the best IT technical specialists possible, encouraging cross-functional teams, group meetings, worker exchange and cross training. The strength of communications channels and managerial IT knowledge in facilitating IT implementation success suggests that training is necessary but not suficient – prior experience and knowledge, and the dissemination of that knowledge, will have an influence on desired organizational change. Managerial IT knowledge consists of knowledge of IT as a competitive weapon and as a way to increase productivity. Its significance as a component of absorptive capacity suggests that management may also wish to look closely at managements underlying assumptions, attitudes and values toward organizational control, group processes and sharing of IT knowledge. Encouraging greater degrees of information sharing, including knowledge of technology use, business strategies, and operations, across business units may enhance the ability of the management team to implement information technologies.

In addition, the shared values reflected by the culture influence the attitudes toward learning and change, and it is these values that must be used as part of top managements eforts to increase absorptive capacity, for it is top management which most influences these values (Miner, 1994). In this study, a culture that enhances absorptive capacity is one that values change, eficiency, and goal setting, and is opposed to formalized, centralized control of organizational processes. Overall, ensuring that the values underlying developmental and rational culture dimensions are strong may facilitate absorptive capacity, whereas an overemphasis on control of internal processes as advocated by a hierarchical culture may thwart absorptive capacity.

In sum, this study supports the view that absorptive capacity is important to IT implementation success. Managerial IT knowledge, coupled with a strong external and internal communication channel, should aid the implementation of IT innovations, resulting in better payback, user satisfaction, reliability and competitive advantage. Organizations can enhance their absorptive capacity by establishing structures that facilitate both the formal and informal cross-functional communication and managerial IT knowledge within the organization. Overlapping and shared knowledge is important in the application of new ITs. Moreover, corporate culture influences absorptive capacity in that corporate culture influences the attitude toward knowledge acquisition and cross-functional learning (Guha et al., 1997). A ‘‘learning organization’’ may be a metaphor for its employees willingness to learn, and an organization cannot force its employees to learn (Macdonald, 1995). A culture that values an outward eye toward the competitive environment, with an emphasis on change and flexibility within an objective-setting environment, should promote the development of knowledge and the use of communication channels to distribute that knowledge. In sum, such a culture should enhance an organizations absorptive capacity and may be what has been described as a ‘‘learning culture’’ or ‘‘knowledge-friendly’’ culture.
