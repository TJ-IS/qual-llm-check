ABSTRACT: Managing electronic trading partner relationships is a key to successful development of an interorganizational systems (IOS) network. Firms often exercise their power and offer reciprocal investments to their trading partners in developing an IOS network. However, limited effort has been made to empirically validate their effects on increasing IOS usage between trading partners. This paper gauges the effects of these two relational factors—power and reciprocal investments—within the context of an electronic data interchange (EDI) network development. Moreover, the role of channel climate in increasing EDI usage is explicated with a particular focus on its determinants and impacts. With insights obtained from social exchange and transaction cost theories, a research model is developed and tested with data collected from 233 suppliers with electronic linkages via EDI with a nationally recognized retailer of home improvement supplies and materials in the United States. The customer’s reciprocal investments in the form of EDI-related support are proven to be effective in increasing EDI volume and diversity. However, power exercised is found to be not effective. Suppliers’ cooperation with the customer, which is influenced by perceived uncertainty, trust, and transaction-specific investments, is found to have strong effects on EDI volume and diversity. Finally, the reciprocal investments are found to be an even more effective strategy when suppliers desire to keep a more cooperative relationship with the customer.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: buyer–seller relationships, electronic data interchange, information technology use, interorganizational relations and cooperation, interorganizational systems.

OVER THE PAST DECADES, FIRMS HAVE USED interorganizational systems (IOS), such as electronic data interchange (EDI), to develop more cooperative and long-lasting relationships with their trading partners at adjacent stages in the value chain [33, 77, 81]. By changing the nature of their relationships, firms have worked to seamlessly integrate their operations with external business partners and, in turn, streamline their cross-company processes [35]. As one example, Chrysler has forged new types of supplier relationships, resulting in an annual savings of more than \$1.7 billion due to faster product cycles and reduced manufacturing costs [22]. Researchers have found that IOS, such as EDI, have played a central role in enabling firms to establish this new trading partner relationship [5, 33, 64]. This form of tightly coupled trading partnership that explicitly leverages capabilities of information technologies (IT) is variously referred to as electronic integration [85], electronic partnership [37], or information partnership [54].

Although EDI has been in use for more than two decades, it still remains the most vibrant IOS technology for business-to-business (B2B) electronic transactions. With the emergence of the Web-based platform, many believed that firms would migrate a significant amount of their trading activities to online B2B exchanges. However, the dot-com bust caused the majority of B2B exchanges to collapse, due in part to a problematic business model. Numerous reports indicate that a majority of B2B electronic transactions are currently exchanged via EDI over a third-party value-added network or the Internet [20, 38, 51, 63, 84]. Although some proponents of EDI have recently moved toward a more open infrastructure such as XML (extensible markup language), organizations are still reluctant to replace their existing EDI systems with XML-based messages for various reasons [91]. Most importantly, although XML has been touted as a more flexible and less expensive successor to EDI, many organizations have realized that establishing an electronic linkage with trading partners using XML still requires considerable costs associated with implementing and maintaining the technology, and redefining processes on each side of the relationship [51, 78]. Whether it be traditional forms of EDI through a third-party value-added network or over the Internet, or more sophisticated data formats such as XML, firms continue to engage in tightly coupled electronic integration.

In the post–dot-com era where IT investments are closely scrutinized for a higher, less-risky return on investment [11], the importance of carefully managing electronic trading partner relationships cannot be underestimated. Generally, IOS projects inherently carry more risk than internal IT projects because managers have less control over their trading partners’ actions [72]. For instance, the reluctance of smaller suppliers to become EDI-capable has been shown to be among the most serious concerns for large customer firms that initiated an EDI network [43, 81]. To this end, large customers often try to influence their suppliers’ decision on EDI adoption. However, many smaller suppliers have implemented EDI in minimal ways simply to meet their customers’ requirements, with the result that functions other than purchasing and billing have not been aggressively implemented. To realize the full benefits of electronic supply chain management programs, firms need to electronically exchange a greater volume and variety of information via EDI with their trading partners [6]. Soliciting trading partner cooperation to migrate more data exchange to the EDI system for economies of scale is an important element in making these systems pay off for firms.

This paper contributes to the extensive literature on EDI-based trading partnership management by addressing two specific issues that have not yet received due attention. First, we examine the effects of two EDI-specific relational factors—power exercised and reciprocal investments—that a customer firm in dyadic customer–supplier relationships often uses to increase the level of dyadic EDI usage with its suppliers. With the exception of Hart and Saunders [37], little effort has been made to examine the adoption and usage of IOS technologies within the context of dyadic interfirm relationships. Furthermore, although much attention has been devoted to understanding organizational adoption and implementation of IOS technologies, and EDI in particular (e.g., [16, 66, 68, 69, 70, 71, 73]), the literature to date has yet to draw these two factors together into a single framework, and provide empirical validation of their effects on increasing dyadic EDI usage within the context of customer–supplier dyadic relationships.

Second, we attempt to explicate the role of the channel climate—cooperative or uncooperative—in the customer–supplier relationship when the customer endeavors to increase EDI usage with its suppliers. With insights obtained from marketing channels research, we first identify certain relational factors, including asset specificity, trust, and uncertainty, which can potentially determine the channel climate between the customer and its suppliers. We then investigate the main effect of the channel climate on EDI usage between the trading partners and its moderating role through the effects of the customer’s strategies of exercising power and offering EDI-related support.

## Theoretical Background

THIS SECTION OFFERS A CONCEPTUAL FOUNDATION for the two types of EDI-specific relational factors—power exercised and reciprocal investments—and the determinants of the transaction channel climate—cooperative or uncooperative—in the customer– supplier interfirm relationship. Social exchange theory and transaction cost economics are carefully reviewed in the following section to construct testable research hypotheses, which are summarized in the research framework depicted in Figure 1.

## Social Exchange Theory

Social exchange theory was originally developed for analyzing exchange mechanisms in interpersonal relationships within a societal environment. Social exchange theorists [23, 42, 52] view the center of the social exchange relationship as the interaction processes between participants, in that a participant’s behavior is potentially influential to others and always invokes some response within the relationship. For this reason, the resultant outcome of a participant’s behavior is dependent upon the responsive behavior by the other participant(s) within the exchange relationship [2].

Although social exchange theory was originally proposed in the context of interpersonal relationships, many of its propositions are well suited for analyzing the interorganizational exchange relationship between two firms. Accordingly, studies in marketing channels research have relied heavily on social exchange theory in building models of the exchange relationship between two adjacent firms in the value chain (e.g., [3, 21, 26]). Viewing a dyadic relationship between two firms as an exchange relationship via interaction processes, they postulate that the outcomes of certain actions taken by a firm are largely dependent on the responses of the other firm in the exchange relationship.

According to social exchange theory, power is regarded as the most important sociological aspect of an interorganizational relationship when one firm needs to influence another’s decisions. Scholars distinguish between two forms of power—potential and exercised [28, 86]. Potential power, often termed as structural power [61], is defined as “the ability of one party (A) to get another party (B) to undertake an activity that B would not normally do” [1, p. 312]. This form of power can be a source of influence over actions of the other party in a dyadic exchange relationship, even if it is not actually exercised or where no attempt to influence is made [28, 36]. For example, Emerson [23] viewed dependency as the greatest source of power, postulating that if party A is highly dependent on party B, B is more powerful.

Power is often exercised with the purpose of having an immediate influence on the other party’s actions. The exercise of power is often referred to as influence strategies [26, 74]. Previous research efforts conceptualized two distinct types of influence strategies used in interfirm relationships—coercive versus noncoercive [27, 30]. Coercive influence strategies refer to a firm’s direct pressure on other firms to solicit their compliance with a certain action by emphasizing the adverse consequences of noncompliance [27]. These strategies typically involve threats, punishment, and the bestowal of aversive consequences. On the other hand, noncoercive influence strategies involve rewards and assistance, and the bestowal of desirable consequences [30, 53].

## Transaction Cost Economics

Transaction cost theory, as originally developed by Williamson [88, 89], posits two traditional polar modes—market and hierarchy—for organizational governance that influence a firm’s choice in coordinating the flow of materials. Market is used to refer to outsourcing activities in which the flow of materials is coordinated by supply and demand forces via external transactions between different firms. Hierarchy, on the other hand, describes in-house production in which the flow is coordinated by means of the managerial decisions made within the firm [56]. More recently, a new governance structure has emerged as an intermediate mechanism between the two traditional extreme modes of market and hierarchy. The new governance mode—termed hybrid by Williamson [90] and relational by Macneil [55]—typically involves tighter links between firms at adjacent stages in the value-adding process, but preserving the ownership autonomy, unlike what occurs in vertical integration. The new hybrid modes have been variously described as value-adding partnerships [47], networks [82], vertical quasi-integration [10], strategic networks [45], and alliances [41]. IT has played a vital role in the formation of these new intermediate governance structures [5, 17]. According to Clemons et al. [18], IT has the ability to reduce not only the coordination cost but also the operation risk and the opportunism risk associated with external transactions, leading to more outsourcing, but with fewer suppliers—what they call the “move to the middle” hypothesis.

Transaction cost theorists assert that the total cost incurred by a firm can be grouped largely into two components—transaction costs and production costs. Transaction costs, often known as coordination costs, are well defined as the costs of “all the information processing necessary to coordinate the work of people and machines that perform the primary processes,” whereas production costs include the costs incurred from “the physical or other primary processes necessary to create and distribute the goods or services being produced” [56, p. 485]. Transaction costs are relatively high when a firm outsources materials from the open market due to the costs associated with searching for and selecting reliable suppliers, as well as negotiating, enforcing, and monitoring trading partner contracts. On the other hand, the production costs are relatively high when the firm decides to produce the materials in-house rather than purchasing from the open market. Gurbaxani and Whang [34] described two distinct types of transaction costs—internal transaction costs incurred from in-house production and external transaction costs incurred from outsourcing from the outside market. The external transaction costs are generally accepted to be higher than the internal transaction costs.

The key premise of transaction cost theory is that, all else equal, firms strive to minimize their total costs by choosing the best organizational governance structures. Transaction cost theorists proposed various determinants of transaction costs that may affect a firm’s decision on governance form choices. They indicated that such determinants as asset specificity and uncertainty contribute to “market failure” in the sense that they make the market mechanism a less efficient way of coordinating the flow of materials [39, 40]. On the other hand, trust is viewed as a critical determinant in establishing a “hybrid” or “relational” mode of governance structure [93].

## Research Model and Hypotheses

MASSETTI AND ZMUD [57] OFFERED A USEFUL FRAMEWORK toward the measurement of EDI usage in organizations by proposing four conceptually distinct dimensions of EDI usage—volume, diversity, breadth, and depth. In accordance with prior studies on dyadic EDI usage in interfirm relationships [37, 83], we particularly focus on the two dimensions of volume and diversity that many firms are concerned with when developing an EDI network with their partners. Within the context of the dyadic interfirm relationship, EDI volume can be described as the extent to which documents exchanged between trading partners are handled by EDI rather than other conventional exchange mechanisms. EDI diversity refers to the extent to which different types of EDI document sets are exchanged between trading partners. As Truman [83] noted, an emphasis may be placed on expanding the exchange volume of a single EDI document type before the implementation of another EDI document type (volume strategy). Firms may also focus on exchanging diverse information with their trading partners by concurrently implementing multiple interrelated EDI document sets with at least one trading partner (diversity strategy).

## EDI-Specific Relational Factors

As described earlier, firms taking new strategic actions often exercise their power to have an immediate influence on the other firms’ actions when the resultant outcome of the actions is dependent upon the responsive behavior by the other firms. Past literature suggests that a firm tends to use stronger and controlling influence strategies where the target firm is less powerful and resists being influenced [86]. However, it should be noted that exercising bargaining power through more coercive influence strategies may decrease positive attitudes toward the influencing firm, which may have an unfavorable effect on building more cooperative and long-lasting interfirm relationships [74]. In fact, the positive link between power asymmetry and use of coercive influence strategies is often not found, especially when the transaction climate is more cooperative between two partners in an interfirm exchange relationship [53].

Recognizing that the success of an IOS network depends largely on their trading partners’ implementation of the IOS technology, some large customer firms with dominant market share have often exerted their bargaining power to influence the IOSrelated decision of their trading partners when initiating an IOS network. Suppliers who were not capable of exchanging trading documents via the IOS network have been often threatened with loss of business. For instance, several large customers such as Sears and Wal-Mart even instituted a mandatory EDI adoption policy to their suppliers, explicitly stating “no EDI, no purchase orders” [24, p. 44]. As another example, Wal-Mart and Target more recently announced their implementation plan for radio-frequency identification (RFID) tags in an effort to streamline their supply chain processes, and began to exert pressure on their suppliers to use RFID tags on the products shipped to the retailers [75, 76].

A majority of prior studies have shown that power exercised by large trading partners has a positive effect on initial adoption [16, 43] and usage [71] of EDI in organizations.

These results are consistent with the theoretical reasoning rooted in social exchange theory described earlier that a firm’s exercising power to other firms for their compliance would have an immediate influence on their actions. However, Hart and Saunders [37] found a strong negative association between power exercised and EDI usage. These conflicting results warrant close reexamination of the effect of power exercised on the EDI-based interfirm network development. Based on the theoretical reasoning and findings reported in a majority of empirical studies, exercising power is expected to have a positive influence on EDI usage between trading partners. Accordingly, we formulate the following hypotheses:

H1a: The greater the power exercised by the customer, the greater the volume of EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship.

H1b: The greater the power exercised by the customer, the greater the diversity of EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship.

Reciprocal investments refer to transaction-specific investments made by one party (A) in an exchange relationship that the other party (B) can consider as safeguarding its investments in transaction-specific assets. According to transaction cost economics [89], along with the perception of party A’s trustworthiness, the reciprocal investments made by party A can serve to counterbalance party B’s transaction-specific investments toward the relationship with party A. In addition, they are viewed as a party’s credible commitment to the relationship. Reciprocal investments signal that the party desires that the exchange relationship continue into the future [94]. The commitment is considered even more credible when a party makes such investments with a small number of partners [5]. As a result, reciprocal investments made by a firm tend to promote a long-term and stable relationship with its business partner in an exchange relationship by encouraging the partner to increase the level of cooperation. For instance, when a customer in an exchange relationship makes reciprocal investments, its suppliers in the exchange relationship may expect more favorable demand from the customer and, in turn, be willing to cooperate to a greater degree with the customer [89].

Specifically, a customer firm establishing an EDI network often provides its suppliers with EDI support in the form of hardware/software support, training, and subsidies. Considering the nature of the exchange relationship and the importance of suppliers, a customer may opt to exercise bargaining power (“stick”), or provide EDIrelated support to its suppliers (“carrot”) when initiating or further developing an EDI-based interfirm network. Based on the theoretical reasoning of transaction cost economics, we claim that the EDI support offered by the customer can serve as reciprocal investments that counterbalance the supplier’s investments required to exchange trading documents using EDI technology with the customer. Accordingly, we formulate the following hypotheses:

H2a: The greater the reciprocal investments provided by the customer, the greater the volume of EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship.

H2b: The greater the reciprocal investments provided by the customer, the greater the diversity of EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship.

## Channel Climate and Its Determinants

In the context of interfirm dyadic relationships, asset specificity can be described as the extent to which the value of a firm’s capital is specific to the relationship with the other firm. The value of transaction-specific assets is significantly lower when employed in alternative uses. Williamson [88] noted that when the difference between the value of transaction-specific investments used in their intended specialized use and that in alternative uses is large, a supplier is “locked into” the transaction with its current customer to a great degree. It is also consistent with the notion of switching cost—suppliers cannot easily switch to another customer due to their investments in highly transaction-specific assets [9].

Since a supplier’s investments in transaction-specific assets make it more costly to switch to another customer, those investments motivate the supplier to preserve its current relationship by cooperating to a greater degree with its current customer. Prior empirical studies support this assertion by finding positive associations between asset specificity and certain aspects of interorganizational cooperation such as contract duration [50], joint actions [41, 49], and quasi-integration [94]. Cooperation in this study is described as the extent to which firms in the customer–supplier interfirm relationship take coordinated actions so as to achieve mutual outcomes or singular outcomes with expected reciprocation over time [3]. Based on the theoretical reasoning and empirical findings, we formulate the following hypothesis:

## H3: The greater the supplier’s asset specificity, the greater the cooperation in the customer–supplier relationship.

Trust, “a firm’s belief that another company will perform actions that will result in positive outcomes for the firm, as well as not take unexpected actions that would result in negative outcomes for the firm” [3, p. 45], is an important aspect of interfirm exchange relationships. Trust can be viewed as one party’s confidence in the reliability and integrity of an exchange partner [62]. According to transaction cost theory [89], trust is a key relational characteristic to building long-term hierarchical relationships between organizations, as it motivates firms to tolerate short-term inequities in the belief that things will “balance out” in the long run. That is, trust can lead the parties within an exchange relationship to believe that short-term inequities would be compensated by mutual benefits over the long-term. The main outcome of trust is to provide one party with an optimistic anticipation of the behavior of another party in an interfirm relationship [36].

Organizations acknowledge the benefits from greater sharing of information with their trading partners. However, they often fear that electronic linkages would potentially result in loss of control of their business operations by opportunistic behavior of their trading partners in the linkage. Hence, the management of relationships through building mutual trust with other organizations should be given attention in the development of IOS linkages between business partners [60, 87]. In this study, we propose an indirect effect of trust on EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship. We argue that perceived trustworthiness of its customer will lead a supplier to have a more cooperative relationship with the customer, which in turn increases the level of electronic coordination via EDI. Accordingly, we formulate the following hypothesis:

H4: The greater the trust perceived by the supplier, the greater the cooperation in the customer–supplier relationship.

Along with asset specificity and trust, uncertainty has frequently appeared as a key variable in prior research in organizations’ decisions on governance structures. Prior theoretical and empirical studies, however, do not provide a consensus on the nature of the relationship between uncertainty and organization governance structures. Organizations faced with an increased level of uncertainty could offer two responses. One is to sacrifice customized design features in favor of more standardized goods or services that are more suitable for market governance. The alternative is to preserve the valued design features and cooperate more with current business partners so as to cope with the increased level of uncertainty [88, 89].

Having recognized inconclusive findings, Sutcliffe and Zaheer [79] offered a useful framework to study the role of uncertainty in organization governance choice by classifying it into three distinct forms—primary uncertainty, competitive uncertainty, and supplier uncertainty. Primary uncertainty “includes the uncertain outcomes of natural events, changes in customers’ tastes and preferences, technological shifts, and other exogenous changes in the broader environment” [79, p. 6]. Competitive uncertainty is defined as “the uncertainty arising from the actions of potential or actual competitors” and supplier uncertainty as “the behavioral uncertainty arising from the (strategic) actions of an exchange partner firm” [79, p. 4].

Similar to the notion of supplier uncertainty, uncertainty in this study refers to the extent to which a supplier’s future transaction relationship with its customer in a dyad is unpredictable primarily due to the actions of the customer. Among the three types of uncertainty, this partnership uncertainty could be particularly important in determining whether a supplier tightens or loosens the exchange relationship with its current customer. We postulate that a supplier facing a higher level of this uncertainty from its current customer will not be motivated to cooperate with the customer, which in turn, contributes to less cooperative trading partnerships in the customer–supplier interfirm relationship. Accordingly, we formulate the following hypothesis:

## H5: The greater the uncertainty perceived by the supplier, the lower the cooperation in the customer–supplier relationship.

We have just described three variables—asset specificity, uncertainty, and trust—as determinants of cooperation in the customer–supplier relationship. Considering that EDI technology has the ability to enhance more effective communication and collaboration between trading partners, it is reasonable to expect that a supplier who desires to keep a more cooperative relationship with a customer would be motivated to increase EDI usage with the customer. Bensaou [8] found that cooperation between automakers and their suppliers is positively associated with the level of IT use between the trading partners in the Japanese automobile industry. With respect to the main effects of cooperation in the interfirm relationship on the level of EDI usage with the customer, we formally construct the following hypotheses:

H6a: The greater the cooperation in the customer–supplier relationship, the greater the volume of EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship.

H6b: The greater the cooperation in the customer–supplier relationship, the greater the diversity of EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship.

A firm’s decision to use influence strategies such as exercising bargaining power or providing reciprocal investments to exchange partners is not made in a vacuum, but affected by the transaction climate with, and the behavior of, the exchange partner [36, 37]. For instance, the extent of expected relationship continuity may be taken into account when a firm decides to exercise power to its exchange partner [53]. Such considerations are generally based on the belief that the effect of influence strategies such as exercising bargaining power and offering reciprocal investments would differ, depending primarily on how a firm’s use of an influence strategy would be responded to by its exchange partners. For instance, use of a specific influence strategy to have an influence on the other firm’s actions may be viewed more favorably, especially to exchange partners who desire to continue their relationship with an influencing firm into the future. Consequently, the exchange partners are expected to be more willing to conform to the influencing firm’s request. Heide [41] empirically demonstrated that a manufacturer takes joint actions to a greater extent with a supplier when the relationship is expected to continue into the future.

The line of reasoning described above can similarly apply to a customer firm that tries to develop its IOS network through exercising power, or offering reciprocal investments (i.e., providing IOS-related support) to its trading partners. In particular, we expect that a customer’s request would be more effective, especially with suppliers who endeavor to keep more cooperative relationships with the customer. Such suppliers would react more favorably to the customer’s request for electronic coordination through IOS such as EDI. In the development of an EDI network between trading partners, a customer’s power was found to be exercised more extensively to suppliers whose commitment toward the relationship with the customer is greater [37]. We expect that suppliers’ cooperation with the customer would have a moderating role through the effects of a customer’s exercising power or reciprocal investments in the form of EDI-related support on the level of EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship. Accordingly, we formally construct the following hypotheses:

H7a: The positive association between power exercised and EDI volume will be enhanced with a higher level of cooperation in the customer–supplier relationship.

H7b: The positive association between power exercised and EDI diversity will be enhanced with a higher level of cooperation in the customer–supplier relationship.

H8a: The positive association between reciprocal investments and EDI volume will be enhanced with a higher level of cooperation in the customer–supplier relationship.

H8b: The positive association between reciprocal investments and EDI diversity will be enhanced with a higher level of cooperation in the customer–supplier relationship.

## Controlling Effects of Other Variables

Effects of several other variables were controlled based on findings in previous empirical studies on EDI adoption and implementation. Control variables incorporated in our study include relative advantage [16, 31, 66, 68, 80], IT infrastructure [16, 31, 32], and transaction volume [44] between the customer and its suppliers. Given that firms are likely to increase the level of EDI use over time [69, 70], we also incorporated years of EDI relationship between the customer and its suppliers as another control variable. All these control variables were expected to have positive effects on the two dimensions of EDI usage between the customer and its suppliers. However, we did not explicitly construct and test hypotheses related to the effects of the control variables, since our focus in this study lies on the theoretical variables described earlier.

While the present study is unique, owing to its focus on the extent of dyadic EDI usage between trading partners with variables from two relatively unexplored theories in the literature, it may be that explanatory variables identified in the present study could somewhat be related to those examined in prior studies on EDI adoption. ## Research Methodology

## Measure Development

WE TESTED THE HYPOTHESES WITH DATA collected from a survey questionnaire administered via mail to multiple key informants within suppliers of a nationally recognized retailer of home improvement supplies and materials in the United States during winter 1999–2000. The retailer started using EDI in 1992, and exchanged six types of EDI documents in the ANSI X.12 standard format with 1,220 suppliers when the survey was carried out (see the survey instrument in the Appendix for the listing of the document types).

We began the instrument development process with a search for prior studies that contained scales for the constructs used in our study. Since existing scales that were proven to be reliable and valid measures were available for most of the constructs, we adapted them for this study. All latent constructs were measured with multiple items on seven-point Likert scales, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The scales for power exercised and reciprocal investments were directly adapted from the multi-item scales used to measured the same constructs in Hart and Saunders [37] and Zaheer and Venkatraman [93], respectively. ## Sample and Data Collection Procedure

Since the retailer did not institute a detailed mandatory policy describing adoption deadlines for each EDI document set, the level of suppliers’ EDI use with the retailer was expected to vary greatly. The EDI manager of the retailer confirmed that its EDI usage varied considerably across the suppliers, ascertaining variances in the main constructs of the study. He also indicated that the retailer often exercised its bargaining power and offered EDI support selectively among the suppliers. Of the suppliers who participate in the retailer’s EDI partnership program, we randomly selected 610. All 610 suppliers were manufacturers. The retailer generally did not purchase products from wholesalers and distributors, but directly from manufacturers.

Having recognized that a single key informant could not precisely answer all questions on the questionnaire, we divided it into two parts, each to be completed by a different employee of each supplier. Two key informants at each supplier, who were most knowledgeable about its EDI program and its transaction relationship with the retailing customer, were asked to complete their parts separately. This multiple informant approach also allowed us to mitigate the common method bias that is prevalent in survey-based empirical research [68]. The first part focused on measuring two dimensions of EDI usage, power exercised, reciprocal investments, relative advantage, IT infrastructure, and the length of EDI relationship between the trading partners. In addition, the second part included questions to measure variables related to transaction channel climate between the trading partners, including asset specificity, uncertainty, trust, cooperation, and transaction volume. The two parts of the questionnaire were mailed to the supplier’s EDI coordinator or information systems (IS) manager, who was asked to respond to the first part of the questionnaire and to forward the second part to another appropriate person in her company. ## Measure Validation and Analysis Results

A PARTIAL LEAST SQUARES (PLS) APPROACH to structural equation modeling using PLS-Graph v. 3.0 was used to validate the measures and test hypotheses in the research framework. The PLS approach appears to be appropriate as a structural equation modeling technique for our study, as it is recommended for testing highly complex predictive research models [48, 92]. Although a series of multiple regression analyses could be used to test the model, the PLS approach was chosen for its ability to account for measurement errors for unobserved constructs and to examine the significance of structural paths simultaneously. It has also been pointed out that, due to the lack of ability to account for measurement errors, researchers are often unable to accurately detect the strength of moderating effects between continuous variables using traditional techniques such as moderated multiple regression [59].

## Measure Validation

We first used the data to evaluate the instrument for various construct validity and reliability properties. The confirmatory factor analysis approach using PLS was employed to assess internal consistency and two types of validity—convergent and discriminant validity. Internal consistency of multi-item scales was assessed by estimating a composite reliability measure [25]. All of the composite reliability measures were greater than the suggested minimum level of 0.70 [65], exhibiting an acceptable level of internal consistency (see Table 4).

All item loadings between an indicator and its posited underlying construct factor were greater than 0.73, and all multi-item constructs had an average variance extracted (AVE) of at least 0.50, adequately demonstrating convergent validity. The square root of AVE for each multi-item construct was compared with the correlations between the construct and other constructs [7, 12]. As shown in Table 5, the square root of the AVE (diagonal elements) was found to be larger than the correlations (offdiagonal elements) between the constructs, adequately demonstrating discriminant validity of the scales.

## Analysis Results

We initially set up and estimated a structural model with only control variables (Model 1). We then added theoretical variables of power exercised, reciprocal investments, cooperation, asset specificity, trust, and uncertainty (Model 2) in order to assess their additional effects on explaining the variance of EDI usage between the trading partners. Finally, our full structural model was estimated by adding interaction terms to test moderating effects of cooperation on EDI usage through EDI-specific relational factors. We followed the product indicator approach proposed by Chin et al. [13] to test the statistical significance of moderating effects under the PLS technique, because the constructs used in the interaction term were operationalized using reflective indicators. The product indicators were created by multiplying each of the three indicators for the moderator variable of cooperation with each of the multiple indicators for the predictor variables of power exercised and reciprocal investments, respectively. Following the recommendation by Chin et al. [13] on producing interaction terms with reflective indicators, the indicators were standardized to minimize multicollinearity due to high correlation between interaction and main effect terms.

## Discussion and Implications

## Findings on EDI-Specific Relational Factors

CONTRARY TO OUR THEORETICAL REASONING, but consistent with the previous empirical finding reported in Hart and Saunders [37], we find a significant negative relationship between power exercised and the level of EDI diversity in the customer– supplier relationship. This unexpected finding can be interpreted as follows: when a supplier’s primary motivation for using EDI with the customer is based on considerable bargaining power exercised by its customer, the supplier actually tends to take a short-term view and exchanges a minimum number of EDI document sets with the customer. Unless given a detailed mandatory policy describing adoption deadlines for each EDI document set, suppliers are likely to believe that they can please their customer by joining the customer’s EDI program with only a minimum number of EDI document sets. In light of this interpretation and prior empirical studies (e.g., [16, 43, 44]) focusing on initial EDI adoption, we conjecture that exercising a power (“stick”) strategy could work best only in the earlier stages of an EDI network development, when increasing the number of participants, or “breadth” [57], is a critical concern. On the other hand, if increasing the level of EDI usage with current EDI trading partners is more important in the later stages of an EDI network development, then exercising power would not be an appropriate strategy. Alternatively, the negative association could also be due to the customer’s choice to exercise power selectively on suppliers that currently exchange a minimum number of EDI document sets. However, as Hart and Saunders [36, 37] argued, the firm’s decision to exercise power is made primarily on the basis of a customer–supplier dependency structure rather than the current level of EDI usage.

Our results do not find a positive association between power exercised and the level of EDI volume in the customer–supplier relationship. Although the customer endeavored to increase the level of both EDI volume and diversity, exercising power was not found to be effective in increasing EDI volume with suppliers. We conjecture that this could be due, in large part, to conflicts with some suppliers’ own EDI strategy that is more concerned with increasing the level of EDI diversity. The strategy of exercising power was not particularly effective in increasing the level of EDI volume with those suppliers, contributing to the finding of no strong associations between power exercised and EDI volume.

With respect to the effects of reciprocal investments, our results find positive associations with both EDI volume and diversity in the customer–supplier relationship. It is in accordance with our prediction that suppliers would increase the level of EDI usage with the customer when they were given greater EDI assistance from the customer such as hardware/software support, education, and training. That is, the customer’s strategy of providing EDI support (“carrot”) was empirically validated to be effective to induce a higher level of electronic coordination through EDI transactions from its suppliers. Taken together, our findings suggest that offering EDI support rather than exercising bargaining power should be further recommended, particularly in the later stage of an EDI network development, when greater emphasis is placed on increasing the usage of EDI with current EDI trading partners.

## Findings on Channel Transaction Climate

As expected, our results show that asset specificity and trust are significantly and positively related to suppliers’ cooperation with the customer. We show that suppliers tend to maintain closer trading relationships with the customer when they make a higher degree of transaction-specific investments toward the relationship with the customer. We also find that suppliers’ perception of the customer’s trustworthiness would lead them to establish more cooperative relationships with the customer. In addition, consistent with our expectation, our results indicate that suppliers’ perceived uncertainty in their future transaction relationship with the customer is significantly and negatively related to their cooperation toward the relationship with the customer.

As expected, our results show that cooperation between the customer and the supplier is significantly and positively related to two dimensions of EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship. Combined with the significant associations between cooperation and three determinants of transaction channel climate—asset specificity, trust, and uncertainty—discussed above, these findings corroborate our assertion that the three relational factors have indirect effects through an intervening variable, cooperation, on the two dimensions of EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship. Furthermore, we find a moderating effect of a supplier’s cooperation on the level of EDI diversity through the customer’s use of reciprocal investments in the form of EDI support. The findings confirms our assertion that offering EDI support should be even more effective when used with suppliers who maintain more cooperative trading partner relationships. In this sense, we suggest that managers attempting to increase EDI usage should consider such cooperative suppliers first when prioritizing their suppliers in providing EDI support.

As a further investigation into the role of the three relational factors of asset specificity, trust, and uncertainty, we evaluated the significance of their direct associations with the two dimensions of dyadic EDI usage. The structural model (Model 2) was modified by adding two direct paths from each of the three factors to EDI volume and EDI diversity, as well as deleting the cooperation construct. The results from the estimation of the modified model suggest that there are no significant direct associations from asset specificity and trust to the two dimensions of EDI usage. Standardized coefficients on the two paths from asset specificity and trust to EDI volume and diversity are not significant at p < 0.05. However, the results indicate that uncertainty has a significant negative association with EDI diversity with a standardized path coefficient of –0.114 $( t = - 1 . 8 2 , p \leq 0 . 0 5 )$ . These findings suggest that uncertainty may have both direct and indirect effects through cooperation on EDI diversity in the customer–supplier relationship. Subsequently, it is recommended that a customer endeavoring to exchange a variety of information through an EDI network should make a greater effort to minimize the level of uncertainty that its suppliers perceive in future trading activities with the customer.

## Limitations and Future Research

READERS SHOULD TAKE SEVERAL LIMITATIONS into consideration when interpreting the results from this study. One limitation is that the cross-sectional design of the study cannot rule out the alternative causal arguments. For example, the positive association between cooperation and EDI usage found in this study could be interpreted in two ways, due to the cross-sectional nature of this study. Contrary to our reasoning, one might argue that a higher level of EDI usage could lead suppliers to cooperate more with the customer. A longitudinal study is desirable to study in greater detail the evolutionary process of EDI network development in the customer–supplier relationship.

Another limitation is that the self-report method used in collecting data through survey questionnaires introduces common-methods bias that may inflate the relationships between independent and dependent variables [67]. However, our survey design, which collected data from multiple informants in each supplier, allowed us to alleviate common-methods bias. Using Harman’s one-factor statistical test [67], we empirically assessed whether common-methods bias is a serious issue in this study. We performed an exploratory factor analysis with all the items used to measure the constructs in our research framework. The result of factor analysis generated neither a single factor nor a general factor. Furthermore, the first factor that emerged accounted for only 23 percent of the variance, suggesting that common-methods bias is not a serious threat in this study.

A third limitation is that our sample consists of suppliers of a single retailing customer. While this sampling approach could allow us to control the effects of confounding factors related to the customer firm characteristics [77], it should be noted that the data collected in this study are limited to customer–supplier dyadic relationships based on only one customer, such that these may not be generalized to dyads based on other customers. Although our approach has been used in other studies carried out within the context of interfirm dyadic relationships (e.g., [15, 19, 37, 77]), this study could have been strengthened by having suppliers of multiple customers in the sample frame.

Finally, our study could have benefited from a more elaborate operationalization of EDI usage. For instance, our measure of EDI volume simply asked for the percentage of trading documents exchanged using EDI, which does not capture the nature of the substitution of non-EDI documents with EDI documents. As precisely pointed out by Hart and Saunders [37], this substitution may not be one for one.

We recommend that future research in a related area should seek to understand the underlying dynamics of a firm’s participation in emerging forms of multilateral IOS networks such as Internet-based B2B markets. Unlike electronic dyads based on EDI that focus on effective communication between two trading partners, multilateral IOS networks may serve as an intermediary between a number of buyers and sellers [14]. Although much research has been done on interfirm collaboration based on dyadic IOS technologies such as EDI, relatively little is known about emerging forms of multilateral IOS networks. As has been done in previous studies on electronic dyads, researchers need to develop a better understanding of firms’ motivation for participating in the new forms of IOS networks and the strategic benefits of joining those networks.

Another potential avenue for future research is to replicate this study in other channel contexts or non-U.S. contexts. For example, whereas this study has focused on strategies used by a downstream channel member (i.e., a retailing customer) with a relatively high level of bargaining power, it should be worth examining influence strategies used by an upstream channel member in developing an IOS network. Moreover, given that configurations governing interorganizational relationships are different between U.S. and non-U.S. contexts [8, 9], it would be valuable to replicate this study in non-U.S. contexts.

## Conclusion

THIS STUDY ADVANCES OUR UNDERSTANDING of the effects of EDI-specific relational factors and the role of transaction channel climate in increasing EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship. With respect to the effects of EDI-specific relational factors, the results indicate that offering reciprocal investments in the form of EDIrelated support is a more effective strategy than exercising power in increasing EDI usage in the customer–supplier relationship. The results also indicate that perceived uncertainty, trust, and transaction-specific investments have strong effects on suppliers’ cooperation with the customer, which in turn increases the level of electronic coordination via EDI with the customer. Furthermore, the study found that offering reciprocal investments is even more effective when suppliers desire to keep a more cooperative relationship with the customer. While we used a mature IOS technology, EDI, as the specific context, we anticipate that the results from this study may be applicable to firms seeking to evolve their EDI networks to emerging IOS technologies such as XML-based messages.
