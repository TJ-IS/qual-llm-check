# Too Big to Cooperate with a Platform? Effects of Loyal Customer Base and Commission Rate on Seller’s Channel Choices

Danyu Liang,<sup>1</sup> Haiyang Feng,<sup>2</sup> Xinxue (Shawn) Qu,<sup>3</sup> Nan Feng,<sup>4</sup> Minqiang Li,<sup>5</sup> <sup>1</sup>College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, China, dyliang@tju.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, China, hyfeng@tju.edu.cn <sup>3</sup>Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame, USA, xqu2@nd.edu <sup>4</sup>College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, China, fengnan@tju.edu.cn <sup>5</sup>College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, China, mqli@tju.edu.cn

## Abstract

Online platforms have become an important channel for sellers to reach potential customers. Although small sellers rely on platforms as their main market channel, large sellers who have direct channels and have loyal customer bases, may not have an incentive to sell on online platforms. We develop a game-theoretic model with an online platform and two sellers that differ in size and sell substitutable products. Comparing equilibria under cooperation and non-cooperation cases, we found that high-value products might be sold at a lower price under non-cooperation, but cooperation between the large seller and the platform can correct this product value-price mismatch. More importantly, the platform’s and large seller’s loyal customer bases and the platform’s commission rate influence the unilateral or joint (with transfer payment) incentive to cooperate. The large seller’s incentive to cooperate can be explained by expanded market coverage or reduced price competition. As its direct channel advantage increases, the large seller’s incentive to cooperate first diminishes, driven by the cannibalization effect resulting from price consistency, but is then enhanced due to the market expansion effect. However, if the smaller seller responds aggressively, both the platform and the large seller may lose their incentives to cooperate as the platform’s loyal customer segment expands. Cooperation, however, can reduce price competition when the commission rate is moderate and the platform’s loyal customer segment is small, thus leading to a win-win-win outcome, benefiting the platform as well as the two sellers.

Keywords: Online Platform, Channel Competition, Channel Cooperation, Loyal Customer Base, Price Consistency, Game Theory

Dorothy E. Leidner was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on October 21, 2022 and underwent three revisions. Nan Feng is the corresponding author.

## 1 Introduction

Online platforms play an important intermediary role in connecting sellers with buyers and facilitating their interactions. Providing services and products through an online platform allows brands or sellers to achieve high market exposure and attract more consumers. In 2023, more than 25 million sellers had stores on eBay, one of the most popular online marketplaces (Lin, 2024). The success of online platforms in recent years has depended heavily on the participation of third-party sellers. For instance, eBay, which has over 17 million sellers that use its platform to sell products, generated more than \$10.2 billion in revenue in 2020 (Djuraskovic, 2022). With the rise of online platforms, not only small sellers but also large ones with established direct selling channels have become willing to join. For instance, in 2012, Best Buy, a leading provider of technology products, services, and solutions, began partnering with eBay to sell products through the giant online marketplace platform.<sup>1</sup> Since then, consumers on eBay have been able to choose between products provided in Best Buy’s official eBay store and substitutable products provided by other sellers. Further, Best Buy’s official eBay store competes with its own direct channels, including both the BestBuy.com website and its brickand-mortar stores.

To sell on an online platform, sellers usually need to pay the platform a commission fee. For instance, Alibaba charges third-party sellers approximately 5% of sales on the Tmall platform (Tmall, 2023), and eBay’s marketplace charges sellers final value fees in the range of 6.35% to 15%, depending on the sale format and product category (eBay, 2022). In practice, some sellers that have direct selling channels and loyal customer bases are capable of independently providing services or products to consumers. In contrast, many small sellers generally have to join an online platform to gain visibility and attract customers. For ease of exposition, in this study, the seller with (without) a direct selling channel is referred to as the large (small) seller. Instead of relying on the platform for visibility, large sellers may choose not to cooperate with the platform or terminate the extant partnership, focusing on selling products or services directly to consumers. For instance, Target, one of the top US retailers, decided to stop selling on eBay in 2018, explaining that making this change would allow it to focus more resources on its digital strategy on Target.com and its stores (Steiner, 2018).

The collapse of cooperation between platforms and large sellers can be driven by intense competition on the platform and sellers’ strategic development of their own channels. Increasingly intense competition with other sellers on the platform may discourage large sellers from cooperating with the platform. In 2021, Urban Outfitters announced the closure of its flagship store on the Tmall platform. This termination of cooperation is attributed to intense competition in Tmall’s marketplace (Yang, 2021). Sellers that have their own direct channels and loyal customer bases have been increasingly reconsidering their partnership with platforms. For example, the luxury brand Coach decided to leave the Tmall platform in 2016 to focus on its own channels (Risley, 2019). Recently, firms efforts to attract more private traffic, which refers to internet users whom a firm can directly contact without paying intermediaries, have drawn significant attention (Hallanan, 2020). Private traffic can be fully managed by the firm, without relying on any platforms or paid channels. More importantly, it is free from the algorithms of large platforms, putting the firm in control of how consumers see their products and helping firms improve consumer retention rates. More firms are seeking to capture private traffic due to the high cost of gaining traffic from platforms and external channels. The increase in attention to private traffic also demonstrates firms’ (especially those with direct channels) willingness to reevaluate their current partnerships with online platforms.

From the perspective of an online platform, hosting more brands or sellers appears to always be profitable, as it can increase product variety and elevate the shopping experience for customers on the platform. Nevertheless, conflict among sellers on the platform is inevitable, and intense competition among sellers that sell substitutable products is likely to reduce not only sellers’ but also the platform’s profits.<sup>2</sup> Hence, for platforms, knowing how to exercise their market power to manage the relationship with large sellers is vital (Yoffie & Kwak, 2006).

The present study aims to answer the following questions: (1) Under what market conditions can cooperation between a large seller with a direct selling channel and an online platform be established? (2) How will the small seller on the platform respond to the large seller’s participation on the online platform? To address these questions, we developed a game-theoretic model based on a simplified yet realistic setting, which includes an online platform with a loyal customer base and two sellers that offer substitutable products: a large seller that has its own direct selling channel and loyal customers, and a small seller that relies on the online platform as its only market channel. In addition to its direct channel, the large seller could cooperate with the platform to set up an additional selling channel. Further, because price consistency is adopted by more and more sellers, it is incorporated into the large seller’s decision-making process when selling through multiple channels. Building upon the market equilibria under the cooperation and non-cooperation cases, we first examine the unilateral incentives of the platform and the large seller to cooperate, followed by the two players’ joint incentive to cooperate under the transfer payment mechanism.

This study extends our understanding of the feasibility of channel partnership and generates certain insights. First, when a large seller sells through its direct channel only, its product offers higher value, and it has to set a lower price than a small seller on the platform if the large seller’s loyal customer base is smaller than the platform’s and the value gap between the two products is sufficiently small. However, such inefficiency of product value and price mismatch can be eliminated under cooperation. Second, cooperation allows a large seller to expand its market exposure by reaching the platform’s loyal customers, and cooperation benefits the platform through increased commission fees, as nonloyal customers choosing to purchase products offered by the large seller can now purchase them on the platform. However, cooperation can also intensify the price competition between the two sellers if the platform’s loyal customer segment is large.

Third, there are two distinctive incentives for a large seller with a direct selling channel to cooperate with the platform, i.e., reduced price competition and expanded market coverage. When the commission rate charged by the platform is moderate, the large seller has an incentive to focus on the direct channel to gain a large profit margin; however, if the platform’s proportion of loyal customers is low, cooperation can alleviate competition if the large seller charges a high product price and treats the platform channel as a showroom. Notably, a large seller with a greater direct channel advantage will be less likely to cooperate in this case. However, if the commission rate is low, the large seller will only prefer to join the platform if the number of customers loyal to the platform is sufficiently high, as it will seek to improve its profitability by reaching a sufficient number of the platform’s loyal customers. However, if the platform has an excessively large loyal segment of customers, this will drive a competing small seller to respond aggressively, leading to intense price competition between the two sellers under cooperation; in such cases, the large seller will not benefit from joining the platform. Nonetheless, a greater direct channel advantage allows the large seller to gain a higher profit margin, making cooperation more appealing.

Fourth, the online platform has a greater unilateral incentive to host large sellers if its own loyal customer segment is small. In addition, the platform will be more willing to cooperate with large sellers that sell high-value products. If the platform’s loyal customer base is sufficiently small, however, the large seller’s incentive to cooperate decreases (or first increases and then decreases) as its product value increases. Finally, cooperation can be established when both the platform and the large seller demonstrate a unilateral incentive to cooperate. Nevertheless, even when a large seller has no incentive to join, cooperation can still be realized by means of transfer payments from the online platform to the large seller. Surprisingly, we found that, for small sellers that sell on the platform only, the participation of a large seller on the platform is not always detrimental. Under certain market conditions, a win-win-win situation, in which the platform and the two sellers are all better off, may occur as an equilibrium. Transfer payments between the platform and the large seller can further expand the feasible region for a win-win-win situation.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the related literature. The model setup is presented in Section 3. Section 4 provides the equilibrium outcomes under non-cooperation and cooperation cases and a comparison of the two sellers market shares and optimal prices. In Section 5, we present the analysis of the platform’s and the large seller’s incentives to cooperate. Section 6 concerns the impact of cooperation on the profits of the three players and presents an extended model that incorporates the price sensitivity of loyal customers. Section 7 concludes with the main findings and managerial insights of this study and provides future research directions.

## 2 Literature Review

This paper is related to three streams of research, namely, the platform economy, channel selection decisions, and cooperation among competing firms. The first related stream involves studies that focus on how platforms attract and manage external firms (e.g., Chen & Guo, 2022; Hagiu et al., 2020; Mantin et al., 2014; Pu et al., 2022). Specifically, Mantin et al. (2014) considered the impact of providing a marketplace platform for thirdparty sellers and found that the presence of a third-party marketplace benefits retailers and customers but hurts manufacturers. The retailer and third-party sellers are assumed to be price takers, whereas, in our study, we considered large and small sellers to be decision makers for their product prices. Hagiu et al. (2020) explored the conditions under which a multiproduct firm can profitably turn itself into a platform by inviting a competing specialized firm to sell products or services on top of its core product. Pu et al. (2022) found that an e-commerce platform can discourage sellers’ quality misrepresentations by increasing the cost of misrepresentation or using a more lenient product return policy. Chen and Guo (2022) showed that sellers can advertise through low-cost new media (e.g., social media, search engine), which motivates retailing platforms to form a partnership with third-party sellers.

The research in this stream also suggests strategies for platform owners to manage the firms on the platform in terms of pricing (e.g., Lin et al., 2011; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2005), direct entry into third-party firms product spaces (e.g., Bar-Gill, 2019; Jiang et al., 2011), business model selection (e.g., Hagiu & Wright, 2015; Tian et al., 2018), and opening up to external firms (e.g., Parker et al., 2017; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2018). Jiang et al. (2011) showed that a platform can strategically learn from the sales records and decide which products are best for direct selling and which ones should be left for third-party firms. Parker and Van Alstyne (2018) found that the platform’s optimal level of openness decreases with intrinsic platform value but increases with developer value, the size of developer and end-user pools, and resource reuse. Platform owners’ decisions and strategies have been empirically found to have a significant influence on third-party firms’ profitability (e.g., Li & Agarwal, 2017; Tiwana, 2015; Wen & Zhu, 2019; Zhu & Liu, 2018). Li and Agarwal (2017) found that the platform’s integration with a first-party firm has a positive spillover effect on a large third-party firm and a negative spillover effect on a small third-party firm. Zhu and Liu (2018) empirically showed that Amazon is more likely to target successful product spaces and that Amazon’s entry discourages affected third-party sellers from subsequently pursuing growth on the platform. Different from existing studies, this paper considers a simplified setting in which an online platform hosts a small seller that competes with a large seller. Both sellers decide on their product prices, and the cooperation decisions of the platform and the large seller are further discussed.

The second stream of related literature concentrates on the channel selection and coordination decisions of sellers. Some studies in this stream have considered sellers’ decisions about whether to introduce a new channel. For example, Chiang et al. (2003) built a model with a supplier and a retailer to analyze a dual-channel supply-chain design and found that the direct channel of the supplier may not always be detrimental to the retailer. Cai (2010) investigated the impact of channel structures on the supplier, retailer, and supply chain in the context of two single-channel and two dual-channel supply chains and showed that adding a new channel might be beneficial to both the supplier and the retailer. Zhang (2009) focused on the competition between brick-andmortar retailers and found that the retailer’s channel and advertising decisions depend on the nature of the product, the retailer’s costs, competitors’ strategies, and the competitiveness of the market. Related literature has focused on the supplier’s coordination with the retailer. Wu et al. (2015) considered whether a manufacturer would refer its loyal customers exclusively to a retailer or non-exclusively to both retailers. David and Adida (2015) identified the incentives of suppliers that sell products through one or more retailers and compete with these retailers through a direct channel. Luo et al. (2021) investigated a manufacturer’s online channel selection (distributed through a retailer and/or its own online channel) and a retailer’s store brand strategy. They showed that when the threat of the online channel is not severe, the retailer would be interested in cooperating.

In addition, with the rise of the platform economy, studies have also examined the decision to join an online platform channel of a seller that already has an established channel. Jiang and He (2021) analyzed the channel strategy in the online travel industry and developed a game-theoretic model to investigate the manufacturer’s optimal choice of low-price guarantee policies and the implications for the manufacturer, retailer, and channel. Ryan et al. (2012) explored market conditions under which the online retailer with its own website would contract with the marketplace platform. Yan et al. (2018) noted that the manufacturer can choose to introduce the online marketplace channel in addition to the reseller channel and showed that as the level of spillovers increases, the manufacturer’s willingness to employ the marketplace channel increases, while the reseller platform’s willingness decreases. Song et al. (2021) characterized the impact of the initial awareness, spillover effect, and commission rate on the retailers’ openness strategy and the third parties’ selling strategies.

Compared to these studies, this paper considers a different setting, in which both the large seller and the platform have their own loyal customer bases, and examines both the platform’s and the large seller’s incentives to cooperate if a small seller may respond aggressively to their cooperation.

Our study is also related to the literature on cooperation among competing firms. Mantena and Saha (2012) studied the effect of technology asymmetry in competition and collaboration between platforms. Kuksov et al. (2017) found that a host store will allow a competitor to advertise in-store for an undifferentiated product when the commission is high enough. Entry deterrence motives have been found to be one reason that online retailers have begun to refer their website visitors to their direct competitors (Zhang et al., 2018). Niu et al. (2019) demonstrated that, when the level of a non-competitive supplier’s production technology is low, the competitive supplier will be willing to maintain its collaboration with the OEM due to weak competition in the component market. Niculescu et al. (2018) studied the technology-opening strategy for an IT-fueled platform on other competitors. They found that when the network effect is moderate, the platform will open the technology to entrants that have a large absorptive capacity. Chen et al. (2019) examined the cooperation and competition of two rival manufacturers and identified the decision region for wholesaling coopetition and license coopetition. Adner et al. (2020) studied the compatibility decisions of two competing platforms and found that incentives to establish one-way compatibility can arise from the difference in profit foci. Dissanayake et al. (2021) showed the incentives of competing participants on crowdsourcing platforms to share knowledge.

The present study differs from the research in this stream in the form of the cooperation analyzed and the decision makers considered. In particular, prior studies have mainly explored the cooperation decisions between two firms with equivalent market positions, whereas this study investigates two firms with nonequivalent positions in terms of whether a large seller should join a platform to achieve cooperation. In addition, this study also takes a third party, that is, a small seller on the platform, into account.

First, previous studies have not explored the cooperation decisions between an online platform and a large seller with a direct selling channel while accounting for the response of an incumbent seller on the platform. Second, three segments of customers are considered in our study: (1) loyal customers who purchase only from the platform, (2) loyal customers who purchase only from the large seller’s direct selling channel, and (3) nonloyal customers who are exposed to both channels. The influence of the size of each seller’s loyal customer base on the cooperation decisions is further investigated under different market conditions. Third, unlike some prior studies (e.g., Ryan et al. 2012) that assume the selling of identical products at different prices through different channels, we follow industry practice and assume that sellers adopt consistent pricing across different channels. Consequently, we found that due to the price consistency, the large seller sometimes chooses to raise its price to derive more profit from its loyal customers at the expense of the demand obtained from the non-loyal segment. Finally, this study belongs to a very limited stream of research examining both unilateral and joint cooperation incentives (see Hagiu et al., 2020), and uniquely considers loyal customers of the platform and the small seller and determines the conditions under which cooperation can arise as equilibrium, with all parties involved, including the platform, large seller, and small seller, being better off.

## 3 Model Setup

We consider a market scenario involving an online platform (M) and two competing sellers: one large seller (L) with its own direct selling channel and one small seller (S) without its own channel. The online platform adopts an agency model and hosts the small seller. In the base non-cooperation case, the two sellers sell substitutable products through the two competing channels. Both the large seller and the online platform have their loyal customer bases. Following previous studies (e.g., Kuksov et al., 2017; Narasimhan, 1988), the loyal customers of the platform or seller do not engage in price comparison or purchase through other channels. The segment of customers who consider purchasing from both the online platform and the large seller’s direct selling channel is referred to as non-loyal customers. In addition to competing for non-loyal customers, when the large seller cooperates with the online platform (i.e., sells on the online platform), it will compete with the small seller for the platform’s loyal customers, but also has to pay commission fees to the platform for products sold through it.

## 5.2 Joint Incentive to Cooperate

Discussions of a unilateral incentive to cooperate are based on the platform’s and the large seller’s independent profit changes under non-cooperation and cooperation cases, while potential transfer payments between platform M and seller L are not incorporated. In practice, platforms can provide subsidies to entice large sellers to join (Parker et al., 2016). For example, in 2023, Tmall launched the Blue Star Program, which offers business rewards and traffic support for brands and merchants that sell high-quality goods.<sup>12</sup> Further, some platforms have partnered with sellers that own direct channels through investment, such as Tmall’s cooperation with Suning and the provision of a \$283 million investment. <sup>13</sup> We refer readers to the existing literature on the structure of transfer payments for more detailed discussions (Sun & Ji, 2022; Ji et al., 2019).

## 6 Summary of Extensions

To demonstrate the robustness of our key findings, we also develop several extension models by relaxing some of the model assumptions, namely, (1) the impact of cooperation between the large seller and the platform on the smaller seller, (2) loyal customers’ purchase decisions being sensitive to product prices, (3) marginal cost differentiation, (4) heterogeneous prices across channels, (5) endogenized market segmentation.

In the first extension model, we analyze and compare the small sellers’ profits in the non-cooperation and cooperation cases, and the results show that the small seller can benefit from cooperation between the large seller and the platform if and only if the platform’s proportion of loyal customers is low and that such cooperation can lead to a win-win-win outcome, benefiting the platform and the two competing sellers. The second extension model takes the price sensitivity of loyal customers into consideration, and the results show that cooperation with and without transfer payments can still be established, with the pattern of the effect of market segment on the cooperation decisions remaining unchanged after taking loyal customers’ price sensitivity into account. The third extension captures the marginal cost differentiation and shows that cooperation between the large seller and the platform would not eliminate but only narrow the price-value mismatch in the market if the marginal cost of the large seller is high. Further, how the market segmentation influences the large seller’s unilateral incentive to cooperate remains unchanged. In the fourth extension, we assume that the large seller charges different prices for the products sold on the platform and for products listed on its own direct channel (relaxing the price consistency assumption). We found that the large seller still has an incentive to cooperate with the platform and that the market segmentation’s influence on the large seller’s motivation to cooperate still holds. The fifth extension model endogenizes the market segmentation and considers a proportion of customers switching channels after cooperation, which would depend on the price gap of a product among different channels. The results show that as loyal customers’ willingness to switch based on the price gap increases, the large seller would be less likely to benefit from cooperation, and its incentive to cooperate would decrease. Further details about these extension models are provided in Appendix B.

## 7 Discussion and Conclusion

Platform-based marketplaces have become one of the main channels for sellers to reach customers. Sellers with or without their own direct selling channels can achieve further market expansion by cooperating with and selling on online platforms. Meanwhile, platforms are better off with such cooperation due to increased commission fees. Although both parties could potentially benefit from such cooperation, there are well-established sellers that have decided to end cooperation with online platforms. This unexpected trend has led to discussions about the conditions under which cooperation between an online platform and a seller that has a direct selling channel can be established and maintained. This study develops a game-theoretic model with two sellers that offer substitutable products, examines both unilateral and joint incentives of the platform and the large seller to cooperate, and investigates the impacts of cooperation on the profits of all related parties in the game.

There are a few important model features captured in this study. The size of the loyal customer base of each agent is incorporated in the decisions made by the platform and the large seller. The price consistency is considered under the cooperation case when a seller sells through multiple channels. In addition, we consider the agency model under which product prices are determined by sellers and the platform charges transaction-based commission fees. The settings of this study led to some important findings. First, a mismatch between product price and value exists in the noncooperation case when both the proportion of the large seller’s loyal customers and the value gap between the two products are small. Once cooperation is established, because of the price consistency across channels, the inefficiency of price and value mismatch is eliminated, so that the high-value product is set at a higher price. Second, we show how the market structure (specifically, the size of each agent’s loyal customer base) and the commission rate determine cooperation decisions. The platform is more willing to invite the large seller to its marketplace when the platform’s loyal customer base is relatively small. For the seller with a direct selling channel, its decision to cooperate with the platform is mainly driven by two incentives: reduced price competition and expanded market share. When the commission rate charged by the platform is moderate, the large seller joins the platform mainly because of the alleviated price competition and the increased profit margin gained through its loyal customer base. When the commission rate charged by the platform is low, the large seller is willing to join the platform because of the significantly expanded market coverage (reaching the platform’s loyal customer base). Third, as the value gap between two sellers’ products expands, the platform is more willing to cooperate with the large seller, but the large seller’s incentive to cooperate decreases—or first increases and then decreases—when the platform’s loyal customer base is small. Fourth, the impact of the large seller’s direct channel advantage on its incentive to cooperate is not monotonic. As its direct channel advantage increases, the large seller’s incentive to cooperate first decreases due to the cannibalization effect of price consistency but then increases, driven by the market expansion effect. Fifth, having identified the market conditions for unilateral incentives of cooperation, we found that cooperation can be established in a larger feasible region under possible transfer payment mechanisms. Even when the large seller has no incentive to join, the platform can provide subsidies to attract it and establish cooperation. Finally, because cooperation between the large seller and the online platform could reduce the price competition between the two sellers under certain conditions, a winwin-win outcome, in which the two sellers and the platform are better off, may arise as an equilibrium. The main results remain robust even when accounting for the price sensitivity of the loyal customers, asymmetric marginal costs, heterogeneous prices across channels, and endogenized market segmentation.

Our research provides some managerial insights to sellers that has a direct selling channel. Joining the online platform could help sellers overcome the dilemma of value-price mismatch. When reaching the platform’s loyal customers under the cooperation case, sellers that sell high-value products can expand their market coverage and/or set high prices due to price consistency. Our analysis underscores the influence of the commission rate and loyal customer bases on the price adjustments of two rival sellers in cooperation and non-cooperation scenarios: A higher commission rate charged by the online platform can drive sellers to raise product prices to pass the cost on to customers and maintain their profit margin, while the platform’s loyal customers can intensify the price competition. Hence, when a large seller joins an online platform, both large and small sellers should raise prices if the platform has a small loyal segment and a high commission rate. Interestingly, with transfer payments between the platform and a large seller, cooperation is more likely to be established when the quality advantage of the large seller’s product is greater.

This study contributes to the literature and generates important insights regarding the influence of loyal customer bases and price match policies on platforms and sellers’ cooperation decisions. It is important that platforms have an accurate estimation of the size of their loyal and non-loyal customer segments when making cooperation decisions. Only when a platform’s loyal customers account for a small proportion of customers will hosting a large seller be profitable. In addition, when a large seller that offers high-value products has no incentive to join, the online platform should provide sufficient transfer payments to lure them to join. More importantly, the platform can adjust the commission rate to influence the intensity of price competition between sellers. If platforms set suitable commission rates, they can benefit from collecting commissions from large sellers and can foster less intense price competition between sellers when cooperation is established. Further, although small sellers could lower a large seller’s incentive to join (e.g., by providing extra services to reduce the product value gap), small sellers should not take such action when the platform’s loyal customer segment is sufficiently small. Joining the platform will change the large sellers’ cost structure, as the platform charges commission fees. Hence, the participation of a large seller on the platform may reduce the price competition between sellers. Thus, small sellers could be better off under cooperation between a large seller and the platform.

This study also points out a few directions for future research. First, the product value is not endogenized for the two sellers. Future research could take product value as a decision variable and reexamine the cooperation decision between an online platform and a seller with a direct selling channel. Second, we assume that both the large and small sellers sell only one product. It might be interesting to investigate their incentives to cooperate when sellers have multiple product lines. Third, in a future direction, one could also examine how an online platform makes cooperation decisions when there is a competing platform in the market. Finally, it would also be interesting to explore why content producers choose to exit digital content platforms (e.g., HBO pulling out of Amazon Prime).

## A12. The Impact of Platform Channel Advantage on the Incentive to Cooperate

The joint incentive to cooperate increases with the platform channel advantage. Corollary A1 shows that, when transfer payments are not available, the platform with a greater channel edge might be less likely to cooperate with the large seller, whereas the incentive of the large seller to join the platform first decreases and then increases. If the platform chooses to provide a transfer payment to the large seller, cooperation is more likely to occur with a large seller that has a strong channel advantage compared to one with a weaker channel advantage.

Corollary A1: As the advantage of seller L’s direct channel becomes greater, the large seller’s incentive to cooperate with the platform first decreases (when $\begin{array} { r } { \theta \leq 1 - \left( \frac { 1 } { \gamma } - \frac { 4 t \alpha } { \gamma ( 3 t + v ) ( 1 - \alpha ) } \right) . } \end{array}$ ) and then increases (when $\theta > 1 -$ $\textstyle { \left( { \frac { 1 } { \gamma } } - { \frac { 4 t \alpha } { \gamma ( 3 t + v ) ( 1 - \alpha ) } } \right) }$

Corollary A1 shows that the impact of direct channel advantage on the cooperation incentives of the large seller is nonmonotonic. As the direct channel advantage increases, both sellers will decrease their prices (Corollary 2), and the large seller will gain a higher demand from the customer segment exposed to both products (Proposition 2). If the large seller possesses a small direct channel advantage $\begin{array} { r } { ( \theta \leq 1 - \bigg ( \frac { 1 } { \gamma } - \frac { 4 t \alpha } { \gamma ( 3 t + v ) ( 1 - \alpha ) } \bigg ) ) } \end{array}$ , it benefits primarily from the increased demand in the customer segment exposed to both products under the cooperation case, especially when the commission fee charged by the platform is high. In this case, as the direct channel advantage increases, the large seller has to significantly lower its product prices to remain competitive, resulting in a significant profit loss from loyal customers. Because seller L’s direct channel advantage is limited in this case, the gain in the customer segment exposed to both products cannot compensate for the loss in the loyal segment, thereby diminishing the large seller’s interest in cooperation. In contrast, if the large seller has a significant direct channel advantage and the commission rate is low, as the direct channel advantage further increases, the large seller can obtain a higher profit margin from customers who are exposed to both products, despite the loss in its loyal customer segment. Therefore, when seller L’s direct channel advantage is sufficiently large $\begin{array} { r } { ( \theta > 1 - \Big ( \frac { 1 } { \gamma } - \frac { 4 t \alpha } { \gamma ( 3 t + v ) ( 1 - \alpha ) } \Big ) } \end{array}$ ), the market expansion effect dominates the cannibalization effect that results from price consistency and motivates the large seller to cooperate with the platform.

From the platform’s perspective, as the large seller’s direct channel advantage becomes greater, one could expect that the platform would be less likely to embrace the large seller’s joining. Figure A1, however, numerically illustrates that, as the direct channel advantage of the large seller increases, the platform’s willingness to cooperate will first increase and then decrease. The reason is as follows. The enhanced direct channel advantage of the large seller can motivate both sellers to reduce their prices under the cooperation case compared to the non-cooperation case (Corollary 2) and lead to increased demand for the large seller $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \frac { \partial d _ { L } ^ { C } } { \partial \theta } > 0 )$ and decreased demand for the small seller $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \frac { \partial d _ { S } ^ { C } } { \partial \theta } < 0 )$ indicating that some customers switch from the low-priced product to the high-priced one. Hence, the platform can benefit from a decrease in its own channel advantage as long as it can derive a large portion of benefit from the switchers, that is, when its channel advantage is relatively large. When its channel advantage is small, however, even though more customers opt to purchase L’s products, the majority of the benefit is derived by the large seller, discouraging the platform from cooperation.

$$

$$
\pi_ {L P} ^ {C} = (\alpha (1 - \varphi) - a p _ {H}) p _ {H} + (\alpha \varphi - a p _ {L}) p _ {L} + \bigl (\theta p _ {L} + (1 - \theta) p _ {L} (1 - \gamma) \bigr) (1 - \alpha) \frac {v - (p _ {L} - p _ {S}) + t}{2 t}.
$$
