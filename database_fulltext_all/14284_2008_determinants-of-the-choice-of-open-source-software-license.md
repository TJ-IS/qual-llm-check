Stal man’s r evol utionar y idea in 1984 on the concept of free software subsequently evolved into the current open source software (OSS) movement [73]. The popularity of this movement is evident on SourceForge.net, a Web site that assists open source projects with communication and coordination. There are more than 100,000 projects registered on this Web site. OSS is a model for software development, which arguably creates better and cheaper software than proprietary development models [66]. Recent developments in the theory of diffusion of technologies with network externalities suggest that OSS is here to stay [10, 71] and the phenomenon of open source and open platform has generated immense interest among academics (e.g., [2, 4, 41, 77]). Many scholars attribute the success of OSS to its less restrictive licenses that avoid inefficiencies of a strong intellectual property regime [24, 53]. Unlike commercial software licenses, which severely restrict software users in terms of how they can use, upgrade, patch, and maintain the software, an OSS license allows users (both developers and end users) to have access to the source code of the software. The OSS license also gives the users the freedom to use the software as they see fit, to modify the software and create derived works, and to redistribute the modified software for free or for profit. Even though the fundamental philosophy behind each open source license is the same, there is considerable heterogeneity in OSS licenses and open source proponents do not agree on the need for any single license [68]. The decision to choose a particular license for an OSS project is important as it affects the success of the project [74]. The OSS license, once chosen, typically does not change over the life of the project [22]. OSS project administrators (who are themselves OSS developers) use the license to signal their intentions to other developers and users about the project. Studies on OSS have also shown that developers choose to participate in open source projects that are aligned with their motivations and attitudes [4, 67]. Given the significance of OSS licenses and developers to any open source project, there is little research in open source literature on understanding OSS licenses from a developer’s perspective. In this research, we develop a framework of the impact of developers on OSS licenses and test the framework empirically using data collected from open source developers.

In order to explain the voluntary participation of developers in open source projects, current research has focused on two major theories—motivational theory, rooted in economics and psychology [44, 46, 67], and attitudinal theory, based on planned behavior and goal-directed behavior [4]. Since the choice of open source license is an individual decision of the developer, we use these theories on motivations and attitudes to investigate how they affect a developer’s preference among OSS licenses. Our research is one of the first empirical efforts on the topic of OSS licenses from a developer’s perspective and can help to improve our understanding of the dynamics of open source projects. In addition to filling an important gap in our understanding of the developer-specific antecedents to OSS licenses, this study integrates the research on the motivations and attitudes of OSS developers and on OSS licenses, by investigating the relationship between the two phenomena. It also validates the importance of motivational and attitudinal constructs in the context of OSS development. Finally, this study advances our knowledge about the impact of individuals motivations and attitudes toward voluntary participation on the key decisions related to the voluntary task.

## Review of Related Research

Ther e ar e two major s tr eams of OSS r es earc h relevant for our study. The first stream relates to the OSS license and its impact on project success. The second stream relates to the motivations and social attitudes of developers who participate in OSS projects. The OSS license, which allows software to be freely modified and redistributed, distinguishes OSS from other types of software [74]. According to the Open Source Initiative (OSI; http://opensource.org), a nonprofit body for reviewing and approving open source licenses, an Open Source Software License is one that complies with the Open Source Definition and goes through the OSI approval process. OSI requires that any OSS license approved by it must provide for all of the following:

•	 Free redistribution: Permit anyone to redistribute the software (at a price or for free) without having to pay a royalty or other fee to the original copyright owner(s).

•	 Source code access: Provide unfettered access to the program source code.

•	 Derivative works: Allow users to modify the software and to create new works based upon it and release these derivative works, under the same terms as the original software or some other license.

•	 No discrimination: Anyone may use the software in any field of endeavor so long as they abide by the OSS license.

In addition, the OSS license must not be specific to a product, must not restrict other software that is distributed along with the licensed software, and must be independent of any unique technology or style of interface. The OSI lists about nine OSS licenses as “popular and widely used or with strong communities” (http://opensource.org/ licenses/category). Many other licenses have been submitted for review and approval by OSI and the list of approved OSS licenses is growing.

Since the license of OSS defines the conditions for using, modifying, updating, and distributing the software, the license can signal the overall utility of the software to potential users. One of the dimensions of variance across OSS licenses is the restrictiveness of the license [74]. Restrictions on OSS licenses affect users’ perception of the likely costs and benefits of software and reduce its perceived usefulness among potential users [74]. However, some studies on open source have shown that OSS licenses can create at least as much total welfare as traditional closed licenses [61]. The existing research on OSS project performance also finds that users’ interest in an OSS project and the project activity levels are affected by the license choice made by that project’s administrators [16, 74]. Some scholars argue that the choice of license may be affected by considerations outside of the utility-maximizing paradigms or by a misunderstanding of the alternative license choices [47]. However, there are very few empirical studies that we are aware of in the area of open source licenses. The study by Lerner and Tirole [47] is a prominent exception, but their paper focuses on the relationship between the license type and OSS project characteristics, such as intended audience, project topic, operating system, and project development stage. Open source developers, in their role as project administrators, influence the choice of licenses for their projects. Also, by placing certain restrictions on how the developers can benefit from their contributions to an open source project, the OSS license can affect the attractiveness of the project to developers. Hence, we believe that the impact of the characteristics of developers cannot be ignored when studying the determinants of OSS licenses.

The motivations of developers to contribute to OSS projects have long fascinated researchers and have been studied in multiple fields, including software engineering, information systems, and economics. Motivation is the psychological process that gives an individual a purpose and direction for his or her behavior [43]. Motivation has also been studied as a predisposition to behave in a purposive manner to achieve specific, unmet needs [13], as an internal drive to fulfill an unsatisfied need [39], and as the will to achieve [7]. In short, motivation is an internal state or condition (sometimes described as a need, desire, or want) that serves to activate or energize behavior and give it direction [42]. In OSS literature, the motivational factors studied include the solving of information technology (IT) problems in day-to-day working [21, 72], reputation and recognition by peers [11, 21, 45, 66], and career opportunities [35, 46]. Unlike working for a commercial software company, participation in OSS projects is voluntary and, hence, developers are assumed to be motivated by other nonmonetary factors as well. Stewart et al. [74] argue that the characteristics of a project must be aligned with the motivations of the developers in order for the project to be successful. One study proposed that the motivations of firms and individual developers are different when choosing a specific OSS license type, with individuals being driven more by their intrinsic motivations than by explicit economic incentives [11]. However, this study only focused on firms that supply OSS products and services and not on individual developers. Bar-Gill and Parchomovsky [6] investigate the rationale behind the choice to publish information in the public domain (e.g., OSS) and patenting (e.g., commercial software). They demonstrate that the strategy to publish information in the public domain is advantageous to the publisher and this strategy promotes sequential innovation. Parker and Van Alstyne [61] show that sufficient external motivation, such as large royalties, network effects, or developer value-added, could make the least restrictive license as a dominant choice for the developer. However, the authors also show that when the developer is motivated by the notion of “free access,” he or she prefers a more restrictive license. With the less restrictive licenses, there is a potential for “hijacking,” whereby some software vendors can make minor changes to OSS with such licenses and commercially exploit the modified software even though the vendor’s contribution to the software is minimal. This risk of “hijacking” could make developers who are against commercial exploitation of OSS to prefer licenses with greater restrictions on redistribution [47].

The choice of OSS license is one of the important decisions made by developers who initiate OSS projects and the licenses chosen do not change over the life of these projects [22]. Also, the OSS license choice has implications for the use of the software created by the project and all the subsequent derivative software and, hence, signals the utility of the software to developers. The limited research on the antecedents of OSS license choice have focused on OSS project characteristics [47] and there is little understanding of the relationship between OSS developers, who are the most important players in the open source process, and the OSS license, which is one of the determinants of open source project success. Our study intends to fill this gap in OSS research by investigating how a developer’s motivations and attitudes affect his or her preferred OSS license.

However, we extend the arguments presented by previous research on developers’ participation in OSS and propose that the motivations and attitudes of the developers impact their preference among the OSS licenses, since the license of an OSS project is an important signal about the utility of the project to developers.

## OSS License Types

The users of OSS typically agree to a licensing arrangement, which is determined by the project initiators or administrators. One of the main characteristics that differentiate various OSS licenses is the degree of restrictions imposed on the ability of the user to redistribute modified version(s) of the software [22]. Lerner and Tirole [47] propose three classes of OSS licenses based on the restrictiveness of redistribution rights—highly restrictive, restrictive, and unrestrictive. Fershtman and Gandal [22] use the three levels of relative restrictiveness (very restrictive, moderately restrictive, and nonrestrictive) in their empirical study. The majority of OSS licenses can be categorized into these three types. In order to use the terminology familiar to OSS developers, we call these three categories Strong-Copyleft, Weak-Copyleft, and Non-Copyleft, respectively. Each license type used in our study is defined below.

## Strong-Copyleft License

This license type requires that once an OSS is licensed by a developer, the subsequent derivative software based on the original must also be licensed similarly. An example in this category is the GNU General Public License (GPL). This category is considered the most restrictive among OSS license types.

## Weak-Copyleft License

This license type requires that once an OSS is licensed by a developer, the subsequent derivative software based on the original must also be licensed similarly. However, the derivative software can be released under a different license under certain conditions. An example in this category is the GNU Lesser General Public License (LGPL). This category is considered as moderately restrictive and allows programs under this license to link with other programs that are not themselves available under any OSS license.

## Non-Copyleft License

This license type specifies that developers are not obliged to inherit the license of the original software for any derivative software, as long as credit for the underlying code is given to copyright holders. An example in this category is Berkeley Software Distribution License (BSD license). This category is the least restrictive among the OSS license types.

## Factors That Affect OSS Licenses

OSS literature has suggested that an individual’s, or a firm’s, motivation may affect the type of license chosen for open source projects [11, 47, 61] though this relationship has not been theoretically or empirically investigated. Studies on open source have used motivational theories, rooted in economics and psychology, to understand a developer’s participation in OSS projects [46, 67]. The motivations identified include the challenge of solving a problem, recognition by peers (status), future employment prospects, and explicit monetary incentives. Developers motivated to participate in an OSS project have to consider the fit between the OSS license and the ability to satisfy their motivational goals through the project, since the OSS license affects the freedom to use, modify, and redistribute the software. Some scholars have used attitudinal theories, such as planned behavior and goal-directed behavior, to explain developers participation in OSS [4]. Theory of planned behavior proposes that one’s behavior is influenced by his or her intention to act and the control he or she perceives over the behavior. Models of goal-directed behavior posit the role of goal-directed emotions of a person in making the person to act or not to act [63]. Based on this notion of behavior, developers whose attitudes favor the “free” nature of the OSS software may prefer licensing options that can best protect the “free” characteristic, even if it means restricting the rights of users to commercially exploit the modified software. Combining the motivational and attitudinal theories used in OSS literature and the consequences of the OSS license for a developer’s personal goals, we propose that a developer is likely to prefer a license type that is best aligned with his or her view of the OSS world and his or her expectations about the types of developers he or she wants in his or her OSS projects. The motivational factors identified in OSS literature can be classified into intrinsic and extrinsic motivations [8]. Extrinsic motivations refer to the external interventions, such as monetary rewards, that influence the behavior of an individual. Intrinsic motivations refer to an individual’s internal conditions, rather than an apparent reward, which drive his or her behavior. Traditionally, economic approach to motivations is based on the external incentives for a person’s behavior (extrinsic motivation), while psychology schools emphasize intrinsic motives where people engage in an activity for its own sake [27].

Open source projects offer opportunities for developers to be creative, to enjoy what they are doing, and to have a sense of accomplishment in their voluntary contributions to OSS projects [67]. In addition, most OSS developers want their projects to attract other OSS developers and users and to maintain high project activity levels, both of which are measures of an OSS project’s success [74]. Key decisions about the projects, such as license, operating system, and programming language, made by developers are influenced by their expectations regarding their projects. Modeling and studying the effects of the different types of motivations that guide the developer’s behavior [57] will help us better understand why certain licenses are preferred over others for OSS projects.

## Intrinsic Motivations

When an activity is performed for its inherent satisfaction rather than for some external rewards or pressure, we say that the person is intrinsically motivated [69]. Activities that allow individuals to express their creativity, to enjoy what they are doing, or to realize a sense of accomplishment are examples of intrinsic motivators. For instance, individuals who adopt learning goals tend to find their subsequent behavior and actions more intrinsically rewarding [59]. The fact that many developers participate in an open source project on a completely voluntary, noncontractual, noncommissioned basis suggests that intrinsic motivations play a significant role in the choices that developers make about the OSS they work on.

Two intrinsic motivations identified in the various studies on open source participation are creative pleasure and challenge [11, 35, 38, 58]. Enjoyment derived from doing a task is affected by the level of intrinsic motivation and has been studied at length (e.g., [17]). Enjoyable activities are found to provide feelings of “creative discovery” [17, p. 181]. These creative pleasures make the activity interesting and the activity is performed for its own sake rather than as a means to an end [69]. A developer who enjoys the creative pleasure of development is likely to prefer OSS projects that do not impede his or her creativity through restrictive licensing terms. Also, when such a developer considers the impact of his or her project’s license on the participation of other users (including developers) in the OSS project, he or she is likely to prefer a less restrictive license type that enables users to enjoy the freedom to make changes to the software. Hence, we hypothesize as follows:

Hypothesis 1a: The developers who perceive greater creative pleasure from participating in OSS projects will prefer less restrictive OSS licenses.

Intrinsic joy from an activity is also enhanced when the participants in that activity are able to overcome a cognitive challenge<sup>1</sup> and resolve a problem [17]. The cognitive challenge offered by the task of software development is, thus, intrinsically motivating and provides the developer with a sense of accomplishment. Challenging problems can be successfully solved by an OSS developer by collaborating with other developers who also enjoy challenges [36, 51]. When developers attracted by problem-solving opportunities contribute to OSS, there is a sense of ownership in the contribution and a desire to limit the number of people who claim the accomplishment. Because lower license restrictions can attract a large number of users, both serious and nonserious, it can also lead to coordination issues and detract the current developers from focusing their efforts on solving the challenge. Existing research shows that restrictive licenses negatively affect the interest of developer participation in OSS projects (e.g., [47]) and are more likely to attract only those developers serious about their participation. Hence, developers motivated by challenge will prefer a more restrictive license type and we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1b: The developers who perceive greater challenge from participating in OSS projects will prefer more restrictive OSS licenses.

## Extrinsic Motivations

Extrinsic motivation stems from the environment external to the task and is usually controlled by some entity other than the person being motivated. Petri [64] defines extrinsic motivation as a motivation driven by external factors such as tangible rewards or pressures. Even though the general philosophy behind open source projects is the “voluntary contribution,” external incentives such as reputation benefits and enhanced career opportunities also attract developers to open source projects [11, 46]. External motivators may provide us insights into the developer’s expectations about his or her project’s acceptance in the open source community and the signal that the developer likes to send to the open source community about this expectation. One of the ways the developer’s expectation is communicated is through the project’s license. In our study, we consider the following two external motivators of status and economic opportunities that have been recognized in prior OSS research [46].

Many developers participate in open source projects to enhance their status in the field through the recognition of their programming skills by peers [11, 21, 45, 66]. Developers contributing to OSS projects frequently get credited for addressing the needs of an unfilled market [21, 72], which increases their status. Many software developers may also participate in open source projects to send a signal about their advanced programming skills to potential employers and improve their career opportunities [46]. Hann et al. [35] found that developers with relatively higher status within the Apache Project had significantly higher wages. In addition, some developers start or participate in an OSS development project because they are paid to do so [38].

Developers motivated by their intention to be recognized in the open source community or by the economic opportunities offered by OSS are more likely to associate themselves with successful open source projects and projects that attract the largest number of users (including other developers). Successful OSS projects and projects with a large user base provide the contributing developers with good exposure to the open source community, which in turn may enhance their visibility and economic opportunities. Because the type of license has an effect on the success of an open source project [74] and on the number of users attracted to the project, developers motivated by external incentives are likely to prefer a less restrictive license for their projects. Hence, we hypothesize as follows:

Hypothesis 2a: The developers who perceive greater status opportunities by participating in OSS projects will prefer less restrictive OSS licenses.

Hypothesis 2b: The developers who perceive greater economic opportunities by participating in OSS projects will prefer less restrictive OSS licenses.

## Developers’ Attitudes

Attitude is a “psychological tendency that is expressed by evaluating an entity with some degree of favor or disfavor” [19, p. 269]. Attitudes are reflected in the preferences of individuals in a given context. Attitudes have been contrasted with preferences, in that people have attitudes toward abstract concepts and express those attitudes in terms of verbal statements of approval or abuse [40]. The open source movement developed from the attitudes of developers that software should remain free to the users and these attitudes may lead to the preference for less restrictive licenses. Developers may see their contributions to open source projects as a way to show that they are generous, fair, and public spirited and as helping to improve their social esteem [9].

The open source movement evolved from the concept of free software and the freedom to modify the software [73]. In addition to the rights to use and modify OSS, users enjoy the right to redistribute any derivative software developed from the original OSS, subject to the OSS license type. Even though open source developers agree on the principle of “freedom,” they might differ in their attitudes about the extent and scope of this freedom for software users to modify and redistribute the modified software. For example, even though a developer may believe that a user should have the freedom to customize software as the user sees fit, the developer may not want the user to freely profit from the intellectual value of the original software. The risk of minimal restrictions on software licenses is that software released under such licenses is prone to unfair exploitation by commercial software vendors [47]. Developers who believe in unconditional rights for software users are more likely to prefer less restrictive OSS licenses. Developers who participate in open source projects may also see the OSS license of the project as signaling this freedom and prefer projects aligned with their beliefs. However, the developers have to differentiate their attitudes toward the endusers’ rights to use or modify the software from their attitudes toward the freedom to redistribute the software. Hence, we hypothesize the following relationships between the developers’ attitudes and license preference:

Hypothesis 3a: The developers who favor greater end-user rights will prefer less restrictive OSS licenses.

Hypothesis 3b: The developers who favor greater software redistribution rights will prefer less restrictive OSS licenses.

Many open source developers believe that OSS can help the society, such as to bridge the digital divide. Projects such as One Laptop per Child (www.laptopgiving.org) that aim to reduce the digital divide by offering cheap computers to kids in economically impoverished areas would hardly be possible without the existence of open standards and free OSS. The attitudes of the developers in seeing their contributions to open source as contributions to society will influence the types of open source projects they participate in. In order for the OSS to be beneficial to society, OSS developers are more likely to choose a license type that will help spread the software without the risk of exploitation by commercial interests. Hence, we hypothesize that the OSS developers’ favorable attitude toward social obligation will lead them to prefer a more restrictive OSS license.

Hypothesis 3c: The developers who view OSS participation as social obligation will prefer more restrictive OSS licenses.

## Control Variables

Control variables are included in our model to account for the contextual differences in a respondent’s OSS license preference. The control variables include the OSS software developer’s geography of residence (i.e., North America, Europe), the developer’s OSS experience (i.e., time spent, number of projects), and the type of the OSS (i.e., utilities, applications). There is little existing research that incorporates the effects of these contextual differences on license choice. A study by Lerner and Tirole [47] investigated the impact of operating system and programming language on license choice, but they did not take into account personal attributes such as developer’s nationality or experience with OSS. Other studies have examined demographic factors with respect to the developers’ participation decision in OSS projects and may help in better understanding their influence on license choice. The insights from these studies include project membership (number of projects joined by a developer) influencing OSS project development methods [52], the skills and experience of developers moderately influencing OSS project quality [1], the preference of Indian programmers for more modular OSS projects compared to their North American or Chinese peers [76], and the OSS platform selection (including operating system) positively affecting software productivity [1]. There are also noteworthy discussions from the business press that indicate, for example, younger developers’ preference for less restrictive licensing [55], Europe’s larger uptake and interest in OSS initiatives compared with that of U.S. firms [3, 60], and an emerging split occurring in the open source developer community between “open source pragmatists and free software idealists” regarding licensing issues [30]. Even though the impact of the control variables on license preference is not discussed in our paper, OSS administrators can assess the effect of these variables on the license type and choose the license that encourages only those developers whose interests are aligned with the project’s goals. For example, if an OSS administrator wishes to attract developers with certain demographic characteristics (e.g., nationality, age group, experience) to his or her project, he or she can then choose the license that appeals to the appropriate demographic profile of interest.

## Measures and Data Collection

The measures for the motivation variables in our model were borrowed from prior literature. The items for the intrinsic motivations of creative pleasure and cognitive challenge were adopted from Ghosh et al. [28], Hackman and Oldham [33], and Herche [37]. Measures for extrinsic motivations were borrowed from Clary et al. [15], Fisher et al. [23], and Ghosh et al. [28]. The measures for attitudes were new since they all relate to the developer’s beliefs about the open source philosophy and redistribution right and we did not come across similar prior empirical studies. All of the independent items were measured on a seven-point Likert scale (“strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”). The license choice is a self-reported measure where the respondents were asked about their preferred OSS license of their projects. The constructs used in our model and the corresponding items are shown in Appendix Table A2. The reversecoded items are indicated appropriately.

We used an online survey to collect data for this study. E-mails of OSS developers were extracted from the open source projects listed at Sourceforge.net.<sup>2</sup> Sourceforge is a free service that maintains a large database of software applications, which are preferably released under an open source license. The Web site is funded by VA Software, a leading provider of software, information, and community support to IT managers and software development professionals. We target open source developers for this survey because we believe that they are the ones who actually make or greatly influence the license decisions of the open source projects they participate in. A pilot study was conducted during the first week of November 2006. The objective of the pilot study was to assess the quality of the instruments with respect to content, scope, and language. About 300 open source developers were contacted with our pilot survey and we received 32 completed responses. The final questionnaire was refined based on an analysis of the pilot survey data and feedback from respondents about the items.

The main survey was conducted during the second and third week of November 2006. From the e-mail list of OSS software developers extracted from Sourceforge, we selected a random set of 2,000 e-mails and sent invitations to the potential respondents to participate in the study (200 e-mails were flagged as undeliverable). We received a total of 241 responses, of which 45 responses were incomplete in one or more questions. Thus, we had a final sample of 196 usable responses for data analysis. Sample size is always an issue with survey-based studies. A general rule of thumb is 50 participants per factor [62]. Furthermore, Guadagnoli and Velicer [32] have shown that solutions with several high-loading marker variables $( > 0 . 8 0 )$ do not require as many cases. In our study, the factor loadings for four constructs were above 0.80, for two constructs were above 0.70, and for one construct was above 0.65. Green [31] also provides a comprehensive overview of the procedures used to determine regression sample sizes. He suggests $N > 5 0 + 8 m$ (where m is the number of independent variables [IVs]) for testing the multiple correlation and $N { > } 1 0 4 + m$ for testing individual predictors. Based on these studies, the sample size of 196 in our study is considered adequate.

## Summary Statistics

An interesting demographic profile of the respondents is that all of them are male. This is not surprising since earlier studies on open source developer communities have encountered similar gender bias in their data samples. For instance, a survey of OSS developers in 2002 found that women do not play a significant role in OSS development [29]. One possible explanation could lie in the fact that female programmers are often rejected implicitly from the software labor market [49]. ## Measurement Properties

Several steps have been taken to evaluate the validity and reliability of the measures in our study. Content validity was qualitatively assessed through a review of OSS literature [58], multiple reviews of the instrument by the authors, two rounds of pretests, and numerous informal discussions with some open source developers. Survey items were adapted from prior studies where possible. Reliability was assessed by computing Cronbach’s alpha values for each of the multi-item measurement variables. Because items measuring attitudes toward open source were not found in current literature, they were newly created for this study and had to be assessed to see if any items needed to be dropped. No item was dropped for the construct attitude toward redistribution rights. Cronbach’s alpha values for the constructs range from 0.656 to 0.944 and are shown in Table 3.

As suggested by Podsakoff et al. [65], several procedures were applied during the data collection stage of our study to minimize the common method bias. In addition, we conduct Harman’s test [65] and examine the correlation matrix [5] to assess the presence of common method bias in our data. Care was taken to keep the questions simple and unambiguous through multiple reviews and a pilot study. Using different response formats for the predictor and criterion variables reduces common method bias. Our survey uses Likert scales to measure the predictors and a discrete choice variable for the OSS license type. Allowing the respondents’ answers to be anonymous and assuring the respondents that there is no right or wrong answer can reduce bias in responses. In our survey, the respondents’ answers are kept anonymous and this anonymity is assured when the survey is introduced. In addition, our survey measures the motivations and attitudes and, hence, there is no right or wrong response for the questions. Finally, the use of online survey and randomized order of the items for each respondent helps us to significantly reduce the common method bias compared to paper-based surveys.

In order to assess the presence of common method bias in our data, we conducted Harman’s single-factor test [65]. According to this test, evidence for significant common method bias exists when the variables in a study are loaded into an exploratory factor analysis and a single factor is found that accounts for all or a majority of the variance in the data. In our study, an exploratory factor analysis of all the variables shows that no single factor explains a majority of the variance in the data. In fact, three factors each explain more than 10 percent of the variance and the maximum variance explained by any one factor is only around 23 percent. We also examined the interconstruct correlation matrix (Table 4) for the presence of any highly correlated variables (r > 0.90) as evidence for common method bias [5]. The values for the correlations in Table 4 show that there is no significant common method bias present in our data.

An important assumption to be satisfied when using multinomial logit models is the IIA (independence of irrelevant alternatives). The IIA assumption holds that the ratio of the probabilities of one choice over another is not influenced systematically by the presence of any third alternative [50, p. 50]. We test for violations of the IIA assumption through both the Hausman test and the Small–Hsiao test. The results of the tests in Tables 6 and 7 show that the null hypotheses (that the odds are independent of other alternatives) could not be rejected. Thus, the IIA assumptions were not violated by our data and the multinomial logit model is appropriate for this study.

## Support for Hypotheses

Our results show that developers motivated by challenge of OSS projects are likely to prefer a Weak-Copyleft license over a Non-Copyleft license, while there is no statistically significant difference in their preference between Strong-Copyleft and Non-Copyleft licenses. Thus, Hypothesis 1b is partially supported. However, creative pleasure does not have a significant effect on the preference of the developer among any of the three license types and Hypothesis 1a regarding creative pleasure is not supported. Developers motivated by status are more likely to prefer a Non-Copyleft license over the moderately restrictive Weak-Copyleft, thus providing partial support for Hypothesis 2a. The economic opportunity motivation of the developers has no significant effect on their preference among the OSS license types. Hence, Hypothesis 2b, regarding the effects of economic motivation, is not supported by our data. Thus, with respect to the motivational factors, the hypotheses regarding challenge and status motivations are partially supported by our data. The hypotheses regarding developer attitudes are supported by the data, except in the case of attitude toward user rights, which has no statistically significant effect on the developers’ preferred OSS license. ## Discussion of Results

## Effect of Developers’ Motivations on OSS Licenses

The average scores on intrinsic motivational factors for the OSS developers in our study are higher than their scores for extrinsic motivational factors (refer to the note for Table 2). These scores show that the developers derive high levels of creative pleasure by participating in OSS projects. However, we do not find from our data that a developer’s creative pleasure from OSS affects his or her OSS license preference. One possible explanation is that OSS developers motivated by the creative pleasure of software development consider their participation in these projects as a rewarding experience in itself. They are less likely to be affected by other users’ interest in the software or the project activity levels, both of which are affected by the license type [16, 74]. We do, however, find that OSS developers who are motivated by the cognitive challenge provided by OSS projects are more likely to prefer a moderately restrictive license. These developers are 72 percent more likely to choose a Weak-Copyleft over Non-Copyleft license. One reason for this result could be that the OSS developer can see the tangible results of his or her work on a challenging OSS project and may want to protect his or her intellectual contribution. A license with the least restrictions will encourage a high number of participants [47], which is likely to dilute the perception of the contributions of existing developers to the project. An implication of this finding is that an OSS project administrator who wants to recruit a limited number of skilled programmers to solve a challenging problem should choose a Strong-Copyleft or Weak-Copyleft license for the OSS. Our result also suggests that OSS projects that develop complex software are more likely to have some level of licensing restrictions in order to attract developers motivated by challenge.

Among the developers motivated by extrinsic factors, those motivated by status (through peer recognition among the OSS developer community) are more likely to prefer the least restrictive Non-Copyleft license. They are 35 percent less likely to choose Weak-Copyleft over Non-Copyleft. In order to improve their status in the OSS community, OSS developers may prefer projects that can attract a large number of users and developers through lesser licensing restrictions; hence, their preference for a Non-Copyleft license over a Weak-Copyleft license. However, the lack of effect of the status motivation on the preference between Strong-Copyleft and Non-Copyleft leads us to believe that developers are indifferent when presented with a strongly contrasting pair of licenses. While a Non-Copyleft license can attract a large number of general users because of its lowest level of restrictions, a restrictive Strong-Copyleft license is likely to attract more serious developers. OSS developers may see both Non-Copyleft and Strong-Copyleft licenses as beneficial in improving their status, but among different types of audiences. A surprising result from our analysis is the lack of effect of economic motivation on OSS license choice. A less restrictive license, such as Non-Copyleft, increases the number of users accessing the software and leads to greater visibility for the developers involved in the project [47]. This visibility should increase the economic opportunities for the developers. However, an analysis of the mean scores for the developers’ motivations and attitudes indicates that developers are less motivated by the economic opportunities from contributing to open source projects than by other factors (see note for Table 2). One possible explanation for this result could be that economic motivators are realized in the short-term whereas the status is achieved in the long term [47]. Because the license is a relatively endur ing characteristic of an OSS project, the developer may be influenced more by the long-term incentive of status when choosing the OSS license than by the short-term incentive of economic opportunities. An implication of this finding could be that when making decisions about project licensing, OSS administrators should focus on signaling the opportunities for challenge and status before considering the offer of explicit economic incentives.

## Effect of Developers’ Attitudes on OSS Licenses

Our results on developer attitudes show that OSS developers who believe in the freedom to redistribute derivative OSS under the license of one’s choice are approximately 72 percent less likely to choose a Strong-Copyleft over a Non-Copyleft license. They are also approximately 57 percent less likely to choose a Weak-Copyleft over a Non-Copyleft license. In short, these developers prefer OSS licenses with lower restrictions. However, developers who believe that participation in OSS is a social obligation are approximately 52 percent more likely to choose Strong-Copyleft over Non-Copyleft and 68 percent more likely to choose a Weak-Copyleft over a Non-Copyleft license. A Non-Copyleft license, in contrast to Strong-Copyleft and Weak-Copyleft licenses, allows developers, including commercial software vendors, to appropriate financial benefits from derivative software without any constraints. Hence, it is not surprising that developers motivated by social obligations prefer a license type that can protect the original redistribution philosophy of the OSS movement. Our results imply that OSS administrators managing projects aimed at helping with social programs, such as bridging the digital divide, would attract more developers if they adopt a Strong-Copyleft or Weak-Copyleft license.

In summary, our results show that the motivation of challenge (problem solving) is associated with developers’ preference for a moderate level of licensing restrictions, while the opportunity for status (through peer recognition) is associated with developers’ preference for minimum level of licensing restrictions. We also find that developers attitude toward the software redistribution rights conflicts with their attitude toward preserving the social benefits of open source, which is reflected in their OSS license preference. When choosing a license, OSS developers are concerned more about the basic open source principles, such as the “right to freely modify and redistribute software” and “spreading of software in the society,” than about the other software rights of the users. Because different OSS licenses reflect these open source principles to different degrees, the developers prefer the license that is closely aligned with their motivations and beliefs in these principles. A surprising result from our study is the lack of significance of the economic opportunity motivation when choosing an OSS license, which implies that OSS managers cannot depend on monetary incentives alone to significantly influence OSS developer behavior.

## Conclusion

The type of l ic ens e of OSS pl ays an important role in the success or failure of the software [74]. For instance, restrictive OSS licenses might discourage other OSS developers to use the OSS in their own projects. At the same time, a restrictive license can help a developer ensure that his or her intellectual work is not used by others to derive unfair advantages. In order to understand why developers prefer certain OSS licenses, it is important to consider the developers’ own motivations and attitudes toward open source. While current research has established strong theoretical support for the relationships between developers’ motivations and attitudes and their participation in OSS projects, a major gap has remained in our understanding of the limits to the “free” philosophy of open source that developers may be willing to accept. In this exploratory research, we have empirically investigated the relationships between the developers’ motivations and attitudes and their preferred OSS license. We examined three types of OSS licenses—Strong-Copyleft, Weak-Copyleft, and Non-Copyleft— and used data collected from OSS developers to examine the relative preference of each type over the others.

An important theoretical contribution of our study is that it partially explains the presence of different OSS licenses, with varying levels of restrictions, and shows that

OSS developers prefer restrictive licenses under certain conditions. By studying the impact of developers’ characteristics on license preference, our research explains the important link between a developer and key open source project decisions and, thus, extends previous research on open source project dynamics that focused mostly on developer participation [47, 48]. While the relationship between open source license and project success is understood in the current literature [74], our research explains the antecedents of this license choice from a developer perspective and helps add to our knowledge of open source success. While the effects of developers’ motivations and attitudes on open source contributions have been well established, our study shows the importance of these developer characteristics in their preference among OSS license types. Further, our results support the theory that developers do not always prefer lower restrictions on OSS. When choosing open source projects, they have to consider the trade-off between their beliefs about “open source” and the consequences of the project decisions based on those beliefs, such as the impact on project activity or success. Further, all statistically significant relationships in our model were in the hypothesized directions, thus supporting our theoretical arguments regarding these relationships. Our study can lead other researchers to further investigate the relationships and their directions and develop a more comprehensive model of OSS licenses. From a practitioner perspective, our results can help OSS project managers understand the impact of their license choice on the participation of developers and users in their project. OSS project managers have to realize that, contrary to their expectations, some level of restriction is preferred by developers who strongly believe in the “social philosophy” of OSS. The managers can choose the right type of OSS license that will attract the developers with the characteristics that the managers desire for their projects.

This study has some limitations. First, we do not consider potential interaction effects between intrinsic and extrinsic motivations in our model. Some scholars have shown that extrinsic motivations have a tendency to crowd out intrinsic motivations, under certain conditions, thereby undermining the contributions of individuals to voluntary work (e.g., [9, 26, 27]). But debate over whether extrinsic rewards undermine intrinsic motivation remains inconclusive. For instance, Deci et al. [18] assert that extrinsic motivational factors tend to have a negative effect on intrinsic motivation. However, Eisenberger et al. [20] disagree strongly, retorting that the conclusions and recommendations of Deci et al. [18] are mistaken, and that more applied research is needed. While not including interactions is a limitation of the study, we feel that the model and findings offer meaningful theory building and advance our understanding in an OSS license choice context. Detailed investigations of the interactions among motivational factors and attitudes supported by stronger theories can be interesting future research opportunities. Another limitation of our study could be the response rate, though we consider it adequate from a statistical significance perspective. The choice of subjects from one community—that is, open source projects registered at Sourceforge—albeit large, may limit the generalization of our results across all OSS projects. This limitation can be addressed by including data from other OSS communities, which can also help increase the sample size.

In future research, we plan to include more categories of OSS licenses to reflect the ever-increasing sophistication of OSS redistribution terms and conditions. Another extension to our study can be the inclusion of feedback from the OSS community and its effects on the developer motivations. For example, feedback seen as competence enhancing can increase subsequent intrinsic and extrinsic motivations [70]. It may be interesting to investigate whether OSS developers link their choice of license type to the subsequent adoption of their open source product and if the feedback affects their motivations or license choice at a later time. A deeper understanding of the economic effects of the different licenses can be another important extension of our study and a useful contribution to OSS research.
