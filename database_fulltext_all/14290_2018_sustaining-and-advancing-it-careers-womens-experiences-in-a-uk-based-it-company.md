# Sustaining and advancing IT careers: Women’s experiences in a UKbased IT company

Gill Kirton<sup>⁎</sup>, Maxine Robertson

School of Business and Management, Queen Mary University of London, United Kingdom

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IT careers Gender Women Under-representation Inequality regimes

## A B S T R A C T

This article contributes to a growing literature on women in IT occupations. Against a national and international context of women’s longstanding and continued under-representation in senior professional roles in IT, our study at organizational level tells the story of women’s career experiences in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c UK-based IT company in relation to its culture, processes and practices. Utilising a concept from the gender literature – Acker’s (2006) ‘inequality regimes’ – the study bridges the gap between the gender and IS literature and feminist theorising in order to shed light on the lack of gender diversity in IT. The article speci<sup>fi</sup>cally shows how components of organi zational inequality regimes, namely, ‘organizing processes’, ‘legitimacy’ and ‘visibility’ of inequalities combine and interact to produce and maintain gender inequality in the IT workplace. The implications of this in the sector more generally are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

This article responds to calls for further research on gender and IT careers (Trauth, 2011). In the context of the growing digital economy, many countries continue to face IT skills shortages, yet globally the industry has been unable to recruit, and more importantly retain and develop the careers of women IT professionals (Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 2007; Reid et al., 2010; Servon and Visser, 2011). This has attracted considerable policy debate particularly regarding questions of women’s career choice and initial entry into IT. Despite various longstanding national campaigns to promote IT education and careers, girls and women remain severely underrepresented in IT-related subjects at school and university (e.g. e-skills 2014). This then naturally limits the numbers entering the industry. Against this macro context and pressing agenda, it is unsurprising, but notable, that issues related to sustaining and advancing women’s careers in IT <sup>fi</sup>rms are less studied phenomena.

However, the high female attrition rate and lack of advancement among those who do enter the industry, suggests that IT employers are more or less wholly failing to address issues surrounding women’s career retention and development (Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths and Moore, 2010; Servon and Visser, 2011). As Trauth (2002) argues, employers are among the many stakeholders that contribute to the social shaping of gender imbalance in the IT profession. Moreover, Armstrong et al. (2012) suggest that the barriers for women advancing in the IT profession have shifted from societal to more organizational. Our research contributes to the debate by focusing on women’s experiences at organizational level.

Employing the concept of inequality regimes developed by Acker (2006) in order to identify barriers to equality in organizations, the article addresses the following research questions: What workplace organizing processes impact on professional women’s capacity to sustain and develop careers in IT? Can organizational inequality regimes change? Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that organizing processes in the IT workplace are shaped by a masculine workplace culture which reinforces women’s under-representation.

Masculinised organizing processes are normalised and therefore appear legitimate, and hence are largely unacknowledged by IT employers. Thus, gender inequalities, in contrast to gendered choices, remain invisible within IT organizations. This perpetuates the status quo and creates a policy vacuum to the detriment of women’s careers. Nevertheless, we also discuss how women can and do demonstrate agentic capacities in their responses to the male dominated and masculinised environment.

We begin the literature review by outlining Acker’s (2006) concept of inequality regimes which we then utilize to organize a brief discussion of extant research on women and the IT workplace, and later to frame the analysis of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. A methods section follows, after which the <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the most pertinent workplace organizing processes in<sup>fl</sup>uencing women’s capacity to sustain and advance their careers in the case study <sup>fi</sup>rm. We conclude by discussing our contribution in relation to Acker’s framework and consider the strategic implications for IT <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## 2. Gendered inequality regimes in the IT profession and workplace

Our primary focus is on the equality argument for gender diversity in IT, as opposed to consumer and demographic (business oriented) arguments (Trauth, 2011). Acker’s (2006) concept of ‘inequality regimes’ is widely used in the gender literature as an analytical and conceptual approach for understanding, rather than merely observing, organizational inequalities (Healy et al., 2011; Wright, 2015). While Acker (2006) acknowledges that organizational inequalities are linked to the politics, history and culture of the surrounding society, workplaces are one important site where inequalities are lived by individuals and as such are worthy of detailed investigation. However, in the gender and IT literature studies of employees in speci<sup>fi</sup>c companies are rare, rather studies are generally at occupational level and o<sup>f</sup>er only glimpses of women’s lived experiences inside IT workplaces.

Acker (2006: 443) begins with a multi-dimensional de<sup>fi</sup>nition of inequality in organizations as: ‘systematic disparities between participants in power and control over goals, resources, and outcomes; workplace decisions such as how to organize work; opportunities for promotion and interesting work; security in employment and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts; pay and other monetary rewards; respect and pleasures in work and work relations . Notably, this broad de<sup>fi</sup>nition of inequality that we adopt here highlights that (for professionals at least) work is not merely about monetary rewards. It is also a social activity central to life, which has implications for the maintenance of inequality regimes insofar as people may be reluctant to protest and challenge organizational inequalities if on balance their job ful<sup>fi</sup>ls at least some of their life goals or if from their relatively subordinate position they fear victimisation for speaking out.

Acker (2006: 443) de<sup>fi</sup>nes ‘inequality regimes’ as ‘interrelated practices, processes, actions and meanings that result in and maintain … inequalities’. Inequality regimes consist of six components (Acker, 2006: 445-55)): (i) bases of inequality (e.g. gender); (ii) shape and degree of inequality (e.g. steepness of hierarchy, degree of occupational gender segregation); (iii) organizing processes that produce inequality (e.g. long working hours, absence of <sup>fl</sup>exible work arrangements, male-gendered networks); (iv) visibility of inequalities (degree of awareness, especially among the senior and powerful); (v) legitimacy of inequalities (whether inequalities are accepted or challenged); (vi) control and compliance of workforce (rules and procedures, rewards and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, extent to which male privilege is taken for granted). According to Acker, it is necessary to study di<sup>f</sup>erent workplaces in order to <sup>fi</sup>nd solutions because these components of inequality regimes vary substantively across workplaces

The concept of inequality regimes provides a bridge between the IS literature and feminist theorising as recommended by Trauth (2011), and a conceptual/analytical path for avoiding the individualised ‘gender-as-variable’ approach, which characterises much gender and IS research (Howcroft and Trauth, 2008; Soe and Yakura, 2008). Acker’s (2006) concept provides the bridge by conceptualising gender rather than as a property of individuals, but as an organizing principle re<sup>fl</sup>ected in workplace hierarchies, policies, processes and routine interactions. In particular, the concept highlights how formal and informal ‘rules’ governing organizational practices, action, behaviour, and interactions combine to produce what are often subtle and elusive barriers to women’s careers. This close examination of organizations is particularly important now that some are claiming that barriers to women’s IT careers are shifting from societal to organizational (Armstrong et al., 2012). While focusing attention on organizations, the concept is also sensitive to individuals’ and groups’ (such as women) power and agency within the organizational setting, the constraints they are faced with both in terms of overcoming and challenging career barriers.

Next we consider previous research about gender and IT around the main components of inequality regimes that we analyse in the <sup>fi</sup>ndings: (i) gender as a basis of inequality and the shape the degree of inequality in the IT profession; (ii) organizing processes producing gender inequality in the IT workplace. Importantly, consideration of visibility and legitimacy (as signi<sup>fi</sup>cant components of inequality regimes) thread through this discussion. The <sup>fi</sup>nal section here discusses whether existing research in IT suggests that inequality regimes can be changed or their e<sup>f</sup>ects lessened, giving consideration to the remaining component of inequality regimes that speaks to an under-researched aspect of why change remains elusive – control and compliance.

## 2.1. Gender: a basis of inequality, and its shape and degree in the IT profession

Data on the gender composition of professional IT occupations highlights that gender can be regarded in Acker’s terms as a basis of inequality in that IT occupations are male dominated and have shown few signs of becoming more gender balanced over time (e.g. e-skills 2014). In terms of degree of inequality, the percentage of women in selected professional IT occupations (where comparable data were available) in the UK and US (the two countries where most studies are located) reveals all occupational categories as strongly gender segregated (Table 1). In terms of shape, in both countries, women are more highly represented in IT management occupations and in newer <sup>fi</sup>elds around web development, rather than in the technical/long established software development. On the one hand, this demonstrates the <sup>fl</sup>uid and changing nature of inequality regimes (Acker, 2006), which some might construe as progress (e.g. women’s growing representation in management occupations). However, the overall gender structure of the IT sector remains entrenched. The most highly valued occupations are still the core technical ones where male domination persists (Roan and Whitehouse, 2007).

Currently, literature tells us little about the shape and degree of gender inequality in speci<sup>fi</sup>c/di<sup>f</sup>erent IT workplaces, although the extent of occupational gender segregation is su<sup>fi</sup>ciently distinctive for us to conjecture that the pattern will be mirrored to at least some degree in most IT companies. Indeed, IT is theorised as socially constructed ‘men’s work’ and the stereotype of the male ‘geek pervades the profession in developed countries at least (Adya, 2008; Crump, et al. 2007; Wentling and Thomas, 2009). Moreover, symbolic as well as statistical male domination exists in the IT workplace (Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 2007).

## 2.2. Organizing processes producing gender inequality in the IT workplace

Interviews and surveys have been the primary means of determining what we know about the organizing processes producing gender inequality in the IT profession. Hence a major knowledge gap is that few studies draw on <sup>fi</sup>eldwork undertaken in speci<sup>fi</sup>c workplaces which has the potential to demonstrate their complexity in terms of the inter-relatedness of organizing processes that mutually reinforce gender inequality. Acker (2006) highlights several organizing practices as salient for producing inequalities and we consider here three of the most relevant to our study: organizing the general requirements of work; recruitment and hiring; informal interactions.

## 2.2.1. Organizing the general requirements of work

With regard to the IT workplace, we hardly need Acker (2006: 448) to be reminded that work is organized on the image of a white man who is totally dedicated to the work and who has no responsibilities for children or family demands’. Indeed, work-family con<sup>fl</sup>ict is a huge debate in the gender and IT literature where the typical requirement is for long working hours, frequent travel and 24-hour availability. These are considered to be a major explanation for female attrition which peaks after around 10 years working in the sector when women often enter the family formation/care-giving phase of the female life-cycle (Armstrong et al., 2007; Griffiths and Moore, 2010: Hewlett et al., 2008: Ouesenberry et al., 2006), While some IT emplovers might offer part-time, flexi-hours or remote working in practice, these flexible work arrangements often result in diminished career development opportunities and consign women to an informal ‘mommy track’ (Panteli et al., 2001; Quesenberry et al., 2006), which can permanently damage career advancement (Panteli et al., 2001). Servon and Visser (2011) suggest that IT employers need to provide a longer career ladder with greater ability to pause in terms of progression without the risk of being written-o<sup>f</sup> by senior management as uncommitted or uninterested in advancement. However, there is little evidence that employers have heeded this advice although we reiterate that there are few studies of speci<sup>fi</sup>c workplaces. Nevertheless, the indications are there that gender inequalities are created in the fundamental construction of the working day and of work obligations’ (Acker (2006: 448)) in the IT workplace. Moreover, while the idealised IT professional (man free of family responsibilities) has these very obvious implications for women with children, it also impacts on all women due to a wider socially constructed view of women as primarily mothers/family care-givers. This view in-<sup>fl</sup>uences hiring and promotion decisions, especially in the case of women of childbearing age (Quesenberry et al., 2006).

## 2.2.2. Recruitment and hiring

Gendered aspects of IT companies’ recruitment and hiring practices, whether external or internal, are seldom discussed in the literature and yet they may help to maintain the organizational inequality regime (Acker, 2006). Servon and Visser (2011) advocate for companies to expand recruitment practices with the aim of including more women applicants. Meanwhile, Soe and Yakura (2008) question the claim that better recruitment practices will prove e<sup>f</sup>ective for organizations wishing to attract more women. They point out that all human resource practices are a<sup>f</sup>ected by the organizational culture and unless this is challenged, women may apply but not be hired which may cause such e<sup>f</sup>orts to back<sup>fi</sup>re either by reinforcing a tendency to blame women or by intensifying cynica resistance. Our express interest – internal ‘hiring’ to projects/teams or senior positions – receives some attention in the literature, although again this is rarely in context of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c company. In her propositional model of factors constraining women’s IT careers,

Ahuja (2002) contends that performance appraisals (where career development and promotion are discussed) might be tailored to a male pattern of career and men might be more likely to be selected for jobs and training likely to advance a career. There is also some empirical evidence that internal ‘hiring’ to projects and teams is also likely to advance a career but often occurs via an ‘old boy network and women sometimes fail to hear about developmental opportunities (Kaminski and Reilly, 2004).

## 2.2.3. Informal interactions

While some recent studies <sup>fi</sup>nd a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant reduction in overt gender discrimination in IT, research continues to highlight the challenges for women of an overarching masculine culture (Armstrong et al., 2012; Crump et al., 2007; Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 2007; Wentling and Thomas, 2009). This produces subtle, if not invisible gender biases towards male competence and negative stereotypes of women (Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 2007; Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths and Moore, 2010; Panteli et al., 2001; Robertson et al., 2001; Quesenberry et al., 2006). De<sup>fi</sup>ned by masculine characteristics such as individualism, competitiveness, results-driven ethos, IT workplaces are perceived as unwelcoming to women (Servon and Visser, 2011; Wentling and Thomas, 2009). In many studies, women in IT report feeling like outsiders, viewed as less capable and less promotable (Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths and Moore, 2010; Hewlett et al., 2008; Michie and Nelson, 2006; Servon and Visser, 2011; Trauth, 2002; Wentling and Thomas, 2009), which might explain why gender di<sup>f</sup>erences in occupational self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy have been found (Michie and Nelson, 2006). Castaño and Webster (2011) go further and argue that IT workplaces are similar to other male dominated social spheres where active organizational undermining of women and undervaluing of their contribution occur. Similarly, in Wentling and Thomas’s (2009) study, women reported that men used derogatory comments and intimidation aiming to ensure they were allocated to the best work assignments and actively excluded women. Nevertheless, gender inequalities are often legitimised by reference to women’s putative caring responsibilities and the tough choice to be made between career and family (Ahuja, 2002).

## 2.3. Can inequality regimes in IT change?

Change e<sup>f</sup>orts need to be multi-pronged and address organizational blockages and failures, rather than focus on individual women’s failings (Acker, 2006). While some scholars claim that companies are or should be addressing work-family con<sup>fl</sup>ict and reassessing related practices that may lead to female attrition (Ahuja, 2002; Wentling and Thomas, 2009), other organizing processes producing gender inequality appear to be neglected. Two areas are <sup>fl</sup>agged in the literature as potentially having the capacity to contribute to positive change: informal networks and mentoring. Firstly, networking is widely seen as essential for career development and therefore an organizational practice that women could potentially bene<sup>fi</sup>t from (Acker, 2006). However, studies observe that women in IT are often excluded from informal organizational networking (Armstrong et al., 2012; Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 2007; Robertson et al., 2001; Quesenberry and Trauth, 2012; Wentling and Thomas, 2009) and that aside from the potential psychologica harm caused by such exclusion (e.g. damage to self-con<sup>fi</sup>dence and self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy), women may be missing opportunities for gaining visibility among powerful organizational <sup>fi</sup>gures, for participating in informal business discussions, and for learning about upcoming career opportunities (Ahuja, 2002; Armstrong et al., 2012; Wentling and Thomas, 2009).

The importance of the availability to women of mentors, and especially female ones, is another recurrent theme in the literature (e.g. Ahuja, 2002; Wentling and Thomas, 2009). Scholars argue that a female-to-female mentoring relationship is of a qualitatively di<sup>f</sup>erent nature and more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in a male dominated <sup>fi</sup>eld because of the unique career advancement challenges that face women in that environment (Soe and Yakura, 2008). Senior female mentors may also introduce women to informal networks and moreover they demonstrate by their very presence that it is possible for women to advance and therefore, they serve as symbolic role models and can be important for sustaining and advancing careers (Adya, 2008; Ahuja, 2002; Trauth et al., 2003). However, by virtue of gender segregation female mentors are often di<sup>fi</sup>cult to <sup>fi</sup>nd in male dominated IT companies (Wentling and Thomas, 2009).

## 2.3.1. Control and compliance

The preceding discussion leads to the question: Why do women in IT appear to either accept the gendered status quo or leave the profession? For Acker (2006), various complex and intersecting ‘controls’ exist which mitigate challenges to inequality and produce compliance among workers. Control mechanisms include direct controls such as bureaucratic rules and various punishments/rewards, as well as indirect controls including restricting information <sup>fl</sup>ows. Internalised controls including belief in the legitimacy of male privilege or the pointlessness of attempting to challenge the way things are, as well as deriving pleasure from the work itself. This resonates with what existing research tells us about how women respond to negative experiences in the IT workplace, Studies show that as a marginalised group, women often silence themselves as a means of survival (Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 2007; Reid et al., 2010); equally they might engage in impression management in attempts to assimilate within the dominant workplace culture (Servon and Visser, 2011). Essentially, many women who survive in the IT workplace appear to accept being, as Trauth (2002) puts it, the odd girl out’ (see also Roan and Whitehouse, 2007), but this may be perceived as a price worth paying for a career that on balance gives many pleasure and satisfaction (Crump et al., 2007; Quesenberry et al., 2006; Wentling and Thomas, 2009).

## 2.4. Summary

To conclude our brief consideration of extant research, there is far more information about the IT occupation than there is about women’s lived experiences of the IT workplace. Nevertheless, research does suggest that there are multiple organizing processes stemming from the overarching masculine culture of the IT environment, which all have the potential to create gendered organizational inequality regimes impairing women’s capacity to sustain and advance their careers (Acker, 2006). Indeed, the IT literature has called for change in organizational culture for some time (Ahuja, 2002; Servon and Visser, 2011). In order to further develop understanding of gender and IT careers, we focus here on women’s experiences of and responses to the inequality regime in one speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>rm.

## 3. The study

## 3.1. Research approach

Our intention as previously highlighted was to avoid the ‘gender-as-variable’ approach seen in much gender and IS research (Adam et al., 2004; Howcroft and Trauth, 2008), not least because our interest is underpinned by our main concern with gender inequality rather than with IT labour shortages and industry performance (Reid et al., 2010). Nevertheless, we share the view articulated by Howcroft and Trauth (2008) that an unequal gender division of labour exists in organizations and that feminine skills and roles are routinely undervalued, which in turn contributes to labour shortages. We started the research from the position that occupational gender segregation (as seen in IT) <sup>fi</sup>rmly establishes gender as a basis of inequality and that it is at least partly responsible for producing and reproducing gender hierarchies and processes in organizations that impede women’s careers (Acker, 2006). The research design was qualitative and exploratory, as we believed that this was the best means of gaining detailed evidence about the gendered dynamics associated with culture, policies and practices in the IT workplace and the impact these have on women sustaining and advancing their careers. Qualitative methods provided an in-depth exploration of the subjective perceptions and everyday lived experiences of women IT professionals in a particular company where we were immersed for the period of the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork. The organizational focus is relatively unusual in this <sup>fi</sup>eld, which typically utilizes a whole-career narrative interview approach or gathers whole-career information via surveys.

## 3.2. Fieldwork

Fieldwork took place in the UK operation of an IT multinational we refer to by the pseudonym ITINTUK. The company is a UK subsidiary (with approximately 10,000 employees),<sup>1</sup> which operates autonomously with its own board of directors, speci<sup>fi</sup>c strategic objectives and employment policies, with minimal intervention from the overseas parent company. ITINTUK responded to a call via a university network of business practitioners to participate in the study. Several factors motivated senior management to endorse participation: (i) IT skills shortages and the need to recruit employees from a larger pool beyond the traditional (i.e. male); (ii) concern about potential damage to reputation as an ‘emplover of choice' that women's under-representation might cause, thereby reducing labour market competitiveness, and (iii) impending application for an industry equality and diversity award. On the latter, a willingness to allow independent researchers to examine its record on gender made this research an attractive proposition.

The organizational gatekeepers were two HR specialists responsible for diversity and engagement who provided an organizational/strategic perspective on women’s careers in the company. We also formally interviewed both and discussed in depth how and why various HR policies and initiatives had been developed and implemented (e.g. training and development; appraisal; performance management; equality and diversity) and what the company aimed to achieve from them.

Fieldwork consisted primarily of semi-structured interviews of around 60 90 min; roundtable discussions with female employees (four sessions each lasting approximately 90 min); interviews with the two female board members; a one-day career development workshop. Participants’ selected characteristics are shown in Table 2. Both male and female participants were currently in management/professional/technical roles, but irrespective of current role, the majority had previous technical experience (e.g. programming, systems analysis, IT support)

Our approach was to interview women and men in order to understand how both interpreted and navigated the workplace culture and how as people/line-managers they applied the various HR policies designed to support professional career development e.g. appraisal, training and development, <sup>fl</sup>exible work arrangements. The fact that we interviewed male managers (including some that women named in interviews as either being perceived as supportive or not of women’s career advancement) a<sup>f</sup>orded us a more holistic view of managerial and gender dynamics in the company.

The workshop entitled, ‘Career development for women in ITINTUK: opportunities and barriers’, was a talking and sharing space. It provided rich data collection and participants also told us that the day was a rare opportunity for them to talk to one another in a ‘safe’ women-only environment where they could voice criticism. The participants were extremely candid, sharing their personal career journeys in IT and views of the company, and o<sup>f</sup>ering suggestions to each other regarding the career obstacles and enablers that they encountered in ITINTUK.

Although we recognise that it is important to mention men’s participation in the wider study, here, we focus predominantly on data from women participants in order to develop our analysis of salient aspects of the inequality regime operating in ITINTUK. Some women had dependent children, some had adult children, and some were childfree. Some currently had <sup>fl</sup>exible work arrangements, which accommodated, or had originally accommodated childrearing whilst most, with or without children, were working full-time and had done so continuously through their careers in ITINTUK.

## 3.3. Procedures

The interviews, roundtables and workshop took place during working hours. Interviewees and roundtable participants were invited to take part by a small number of senior managers covering di<sup>f</sup>erent functions of the business selected by the HR specialists. Interview questions were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from existing literature and from discussions with the HR specialists speci<sup>fi</sup>cally about the most pressing gender concerns/issues facing ITINTUK. Topics included the general characteristics and culture of ITINTUK, experiences and perceptions of HR policies to sustain their careers and advancement, experiences of managing and of being managed in project teams and managing work-life balance. Appendix A provides an outline of the interview themes and sample questions. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Both researchers were present in each of the <sup>fi</sup>rst 10 interviews, which enabled us to debrief after each one, checking for similar and different interpretations of individuals' accounts in order to mitigate interviewer bias. Analysis of the 650 pages of transcribed interview data was carried out using the qualitative research software package NVivo, which enabled thematic organization of the data around the broad aspects of an inequality regime and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of both typical and atypica experiences/views.

Both (female) researchers facilitated the workshop, which consisted of whole and small group discussions. We started and ended the day with researcher-led plenary sessions: at the start of the day, we outlined our research objectives and provided data on women in ITINTUK and the wider IT industry. At the end of the day, we summarised what had been discussed and invited participants’ <sup>fi</sup>na re<sup>fl</sup>ections. Over the course of the day, we posed several broad and open questions, which the participants discussed in small groups, including: what career obstacles do women in the company face? How can women overcome career obstacles? How can ITINTUK support women’s careers? The researchers circulated among participants during small group discussions taking detailed notes throughout the day, and participants summarised their discussions on <sup>fl</sup>ipchart paper. These data were then folded into our thematic analysis.

## 4. Research <sup>fi</sup>ndings

Utilising Acker’s (2006) framework, we begin by establishing gender as a basis of inequality in ITINTUK and brie<sup>fl</sup>y describe the shape and degree of gender inequalities. We then turn to the most salient organizing processes and associated dynamics that constitute the inequality regime, which we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as in<sup>fl</sup>uencing women’s capacity to sustain and advance their careers. In the <sup>fi</sup>nal section, we discuss the prospects for changing and challenging the inequality regime.

## 4.1. Gender profile of the case study company

Women comprised 23% of the total workforce and although their share of managerial/professional/technical pay grades was higher at approximately 25%, vertical gender segregation (male domination of the upper layers of the hierarchy) was still pronounced with women comprising only 11% of senior positions. ITINTUK did not collect data on gender composition of job categories, but most of our research participants, including the HR professionals, believed that women disproportionately occupied non-tech nical roles, especially at senior level, and less prestigious non-technical roles (e.g. there were few female bid managers). In addition to gender segregation, there were other objective indicators of gender inequality: (1) a gender pay gap of 18%; (2) women received lower average bonus payments. Further, there were few signs that the shape and degree of gender inequality would change in the near future. For example, women were less successful in external hiring exercises, and the leadership development programme comprised only 23% women and a talent programme approximately 32% women.

## 4.2. Organizing processes afecting women’s capacity to sustain and advance their careers

## 4.2.1. Organizing the general requirements of work

Among many other feminist authors, Acker (2006) points out that in general work is organized around the image of an archetype man who has no caring responsibilities which allows him to commit fully to his work (e.g. work long hours, travel for work). In ITINTUK full-time working was the prevalent norm: only 18% of female and a tiny 1% of male employees worked part-time and the vast majority of these were in non-professional/non-technical roles. Flexible work arrangements were a strongly gendered option associated with lower level female-typed jobs. Professional employees needed to be available to work whenever and wherever according to project and client demands. ‘Maximum <sup>fl</sup>exibility’ (to suit the needs of the company) was the mantra underpinning the organization of work. One woman explained what this meant:

“I do a lot of work over the phone, it’s messaging, live meeting that sort of thing, and I travel when necessary. I was in the South East last week, and the week before because I was working on a bid where they wanted people to be co-located I was up in Manchester. So yes, you need to be <sup>fl</sup>exible if you want to stay in a job really”.

The above quotation also provides an indication as to why the maximum <sup>fl</sup>exibility discourse was rarely challenged compliance was necessary for those who wanted to keep their jobs. However, most of our female participants were critical of what they saw as a culture of presenteeism whether actual or virtual. The need to be seen around the workplace or to be logged in online all hours in order to demonstrate commitment to ‘maximum <sup>fl</sup>exibility’ was in many women’s view not simply a question of the demands of the work, but as theorised by Acker (2006), it was a male-gendered behavioural norm. Nevertheless, some were prepared to participate in this particular ‘game’ perceiving it to be vital in order to sustain and/or advance their careers.

All the managers we interviewed acknowledged that maximum <sup>fl</sup>exibility posed particular challenges for women given the greater likelihood of their having primary responsibility for the family. However, both men, and women without children or other caring responsibilities, individualised this issue by expressing the view that women carers had to make a choice about whether to prioritise career or family. Concurrently they also externalised the issue as a wider societal one, not one for ITINTUK to consider in policy or practical terms. This overarching assumption about women’s responsibility for caring was therefore perceived as legitimate, and in so doing its impact in reinforcing organizational inequality was rendered invisible (Acker, 2006).

When asked about personal domestic arrangements, male participants often referred to their ‘understanding wives’ to explain how they as fathers, or even as sons with elderly parents, balanced demanding jobs with family. Nevertheless, unlike Wentling and Thomas’s (2009) study, men in ITINTUK often expressed empathy with women’s work-family challenges from their privileged position. However, they did not perceive themselves to have any personal responsibility as managers to attempt to <sup>fi</sup>nd progressive solutions to support women. Thus, the actions of power-holders served to both legitimise and maintain the shape of gender inequality in the company, that is, women’s exclusion from senior roles and the most prestigious projects that could advance careers.

Women with caring responsibilities on the other hand located their challenges <sup>fi</sup>rmly within the workplace culture and linemanagement attitudes and practices rather than in the nature of the work or caregiving per se. One woman who returned to work part-time after maternity leave was advised by her male line-manager to consider looking for a new role because her existing role naturally required ‘maximum <sup>fl</sup>exibility’:

‘My new line-manager said to me that he felt it would be quite di<sup>fi</sup>cult to allocate me on to projects because of my hours of work [three days per week] … but that is not true because there are actually <sup>fi</sup>ve people who work shorter weeks within our team, all women obviously, all got children, and I've worked with them on a number of projects over the last two vears.

Many women participants emphasised the paradox of inaccessibility of <sup>fl</sup>exible work arrangements for professional workers, yet simultaneously the demand for all employees to be <sup>fl</sup>exible with respect to when and where they worked. Even those women working part-time were expected to do unpaid overtime under the auspices of ‘maximum <sup>fl</sup>exibility’:

“Now when I say I worked part-time, I worked 30 h a week, which protected me from working 50 h a week. So I was actually working 37 [full-time hours], being paid for 30, but I didn’t have to work 50 like my colleagues did”.

Further, we encountered some women who had decided to work <sup>fl</sup>exibly when their children were young and had found it very di<sup>fi</sup>cult to subsequently return to full-time or more challenging roles. Once again line-managers invoked the ‘maximum <sup>fl</sup>exibility discourse to exclude such women. Hence, whilst they had sustained their careers, these women felt that development and advancement opportunities were no longer available to them. They were now entrenched in career terms within an informal ‘mommy track’ (Quesenberry et al., 2006) occupying project and managerial roles deemed suitable for women with caring responsibilities, a practice which preserved vertical gender segregation in the company (i.e. the shape and degree of inequality):

About two, three years ago, I started sticking my hand up and saying do you know what, this [role] isn’t actually big enough for me anymore? And I found it very hard to get away from the reputation of ‘no, X is not interested in progressing because she is a mother and she doesn’t need to move on’ … you know, he’s 15 now, but it’s very hard to shake o<sup>f</sup> that tag of a working mother.

## 4.2.2. Informal interaction practices

However, it is important to state that although conceptually the IT workplace is constructed as masculine (Armstrong et al., 2012; Crump et al., 2007; Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 2007; Wentling and Thomas, 2009), this is not to say that all women necessarily feel excluded. Many women in our study were accustomed to being the odd girl out (Trauth, 2002) having interacted with males in masculine domains at school and university as well as in the workplace. In terms of the ITINTUK workplace, some women stated that male colleagues treated them no di<sup>f</sup>erently; they felt accepted as technically equal to male colleagues; they felt that the company valued their skills and competencies. Moreover, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the changes that have occurred in the IT workplace (and elsewhere) there were some, but few, reports of direct sexism (e.g. Armstrong et al., 2012; Wentling and Thomas, 2009). Among the examples conveyed to us in this study, one participant told how she had attended an ITINTUK training course led by an external male trainer who upon her arrival loudly declared, “Oh, we’ve never had a woman before”. Showing no sensitivity to her presence, the trainer proceeded to use sexist and sexual jokes as icebreakers, which made her feel uncomfortable, self-conscious and ‘out of place’ and was not challenged by any participants.

However, more typically, rather than direct sexism, it was subtler interaction processes that served as a constant reminder to women that they were in a masculine domain. The following quotation from a female technical manager exempli<sup>fi</sup>es this. It highlights how being a lone female underscores gender di<sup>f</sup>erence and yet paradoxically in Acker’s terms supports the invisibility of gender inequality:

“Whether it’s because they’re not sure how to deal with you because you are the only female in the team, they’re not sure how you’re going to react especially if it’s the very <sup>fi</sup>rst time they’ve ever had a female on the team, and that could be a lot of the problem. Men are not used to dealing with females in the team and they’re worried that they [women] might, I don’t know, have issues with things and start creating problems because someone said some things or made some funny comments and stu<sup>f</sup>.”

Another aspect of the gender dynamics of informal interactions in the male dominated environment was that being a gender ‘atypical’ manager meant that men lower in the hierarchy did not necessarily accept women’s authority or management style. Consequently, women often faced di<sup>fi</sup>culties in managing men or being a lone female team member as one project manager explained:

‘I have a manager who is very forthright and he and I get on <sup>fi</sup>ne, I’m very forthright, he’s very forthright. He will go into a meeting and say something and that will be <sup>fi</sup>ne. I will go into the meeting and use exactly the same words and everybody would be really upset or take absolutely no notice of me. I <sup>fi</sup>nd that very irritating.

This exempli<sup>fi</sup>es the way that assertiveness in men might be expected and valued behaviour, but that in women it is seen as unacceptable/unwelcome (Soe and Yakura, 2008).

One older senior manager with vast experience in a range of IT jobs and roles used her positional power in the hierarchy to demonstrate to younger male colleagues the legitimacy of female authority. This example is one of the very few highlighting any form of individual resistance to the masculine culture and attempts to debunk the traditionally feminine stereotypes of women that prevailed in ITINTUK:

‘I have actually su<sup>f</sup>ered far more problems with young male graduates who often haven’t had a woman in charge since primary school than I have with long-in-the-tooth greying engineers. There are a couple of male graduates I’ve had to put in their place and I have done things like given them a female line-manager, a female career manager, a female assignment manager

In contrast, some women actually drew on stereotyped notions of gender to describe their interactions with male colleagues. For example, some expressed using mothering skills (such as coaxing male colleagues) to accomplish project work when people (read ‘men’) became intransigent, or utilising ‘feminine charms’ to resolve tensions and disagreements within teams:

“I worked in a team rolling out a new process in the data centre …. This process was going to change a lot of the ways things were done within the data centre …. and there were a lot of men there …. Well, because it was all men, they were sort of like “no, we don’t want to do this” and we got a lot of fractiousness. You can <sup>fi</sup>nd that the feminine side … can help to calm that down. So you

can use your feminine wiles if you like.”

Many women participants also talked about making a conscious e<sup>f</sup>ort to assimilate and to meet the men ‘on their terms’. This mode of interaction is symbolic acknowledgement of the legitimacy of male authority in the IT workplace whilst from a day-to-day working perspective it is a pragmatic strategy to preserve relationships with male colleagues by, for example, making an e<sup>f</sup>ort to join in conversations about sport or participate in banter, generally behaving as if they were ‘one of the lads’. Women referred to themselves as unlike a ‘flufy bunny’ or an easily o<sup>f</sup>ended ‘delicate flower’, again invoking essentialist and mildly pejorative terms to describe women who would not <sup>fi</sup>t into the ITINTUK culture. These women were conscious of being di<sup>f</sup>erent, but they did not want their male colleagues to treat them di<sup>f</sup>erently, and so acted in a way that de-gendered themselves. They disassociated from feminine stereotypes, a further practice that rendered gender inequality invisible to the male majority.

These e<sup>f</sup>orts to assimilate can also be interpreted as an e<sup>f</sup>ort to insulate themselves against potential male hostility to their presence, which previous research has highlighted as a possible survival strategy for women in IT (Hewlett et al., 2008; Servon and Visser, 2011). Indeed, the need to appear to be a strong and con<sup>fi</sup>dent woman was a recurring theme. The following woman provided an example of this from the many women who she had witnessed leave ITINTUK and even leave the IT sector:

If they’re not perhaps strong, not used to working in a very male orientated environment, which IT does seem to be on the technical side of things, yes, you do see them not being very con<sup>fi</sup>dent …. Not all men, but there are a few, that might go and take advantage of that and make the woman feel very uncomfortable, but she wasn’t strong in herself anyway.

The types of interactional processes described by women whereby they felt compelled to attempt to assimilate, de-gender their behaviour, adopt masculine norms and even accept sexism for fear of male reprisals, are all illustrations of the multiple ways that male power and female subordination are maintained in organizations on a day-to-day basis through interaction processes which thereby reproduce the inequality regime (Acker, 2006).

## 4.2.3. Internal ‘hiring’ processes

Acker (2006) talks about recruitment and hiring as an organizing process producing inequality because it <sup>fi</sup>lters out those not deemed suitable for particular roles. Our research highlights that the <sup>fi</sup>ltering processes involved in internal hiring to teams, projects and senior positions produced gender inequality in ITINTUK. Despite the fact that formally gender had no bearing on assignment to teams and projects, many women felt that stereotyped notions of female skills in<sup>fl</sup>uenced managers’ decisions. For example, regardless of status, job title or skills, women were reportedly often assigned to roles that involved ‘nagging the men’ to meet project deadlines. Interviews with male managers also revealed how gender stereotypes of women in<sup>fl</sup>uenced their team sta<sup>fi</sup>ng decisions. Many male managers saw value in supposed female communication skills. One went so far as to state that having a female project manager stopped the men from ‘taking lumps out of each other’ when they disagreed. While in this case a woman’s presence in the team was valued, it was nevertheless self-limiting within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c construction of women s supposed dispositions. Thus, so-called feminine skills end up being a catch-22 for women (c.f. Panteli et al., 2001). These <sup>fi</sup>ndings echo other studies in IT, which have found that while the range of IT jobs might be growing, women occupy areas requiring e<sup>f</sup>ective communication and management skills (Crump et al., 2007).

In addition, the importance of informal networking for internal ‘hiring’ to prestigious projects or senior positions emerged as a major theme con<sup>fi</sup>rming Acker’s (2006) view that exclusionary male networks function as an organizing process negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uencing women’s career advancement. Although the company formally operated open competitions for project assignments, male managers frequently talked about preferring to ‘hire’ people either that they knew and had worked with previously or those referred to them by trusted senior (usually male) colleagues, particularly on high value and therefore prestigious projects. Similarly, in terms of senior promotions one woman’s comment re<sup>fl</sup>ects a typical view:

‘A lot of the more senior management jobs, although it may look like we do everything the right way [formal promotion procedures], a lot of them are done by the old boys’ network of, who you know, whose face <sup>fi</sup>ts and that’s the way people get promoted to the senior roles. If you’re not prepared to play the games or network or whatever it is they do, then you’re not going to get there.’

However, because women perceived themselves to be excluded from the male dominated networks they also believed that they were excluded from many career development opportunities.

## 4.3. Can the organizational inequality regime be changed and challenged?

Acker (2006) contends that inequality regimes can be both changed and challenged. However, e<sup>f</sup>ecting change is complex and involves confronting certain realities: (i) that organizational gender inequality exists; (ii) the role everyday organizing processes play in (re)producing inequalities. Change e<sup>f</sup>orts come from both organizations and challenges from women themselves. Here, we discuss the prospects for change and challenge within ITINTUK focusing on the development opportunities available in the <sup>fi</sup>rm and women’s networking activity.

## 4.3.1. Missed and missing development opportunitie

A major obstacle in ITINTUK was that both HR specialists were opposed to gender initiatives and therefore no one with a specialist background was advocating for a substantive change programme. The only initiative that targeted women speci<sup>fi</sup>cally was a recently introduced mentoring ‘swap shop’, which provided a voluntary virtual space for setting up female mentoring circles and matching female mentors with female mentees. When it was put to the HR specialists that other companies (including some in the IT industry) o<sup>f</sup>er dedicated women’s programmes in recognition of women’s under-representation, they were unconvinced of their utility, arguing that special programmes were patronising, tokenistic and based on old-fashioned notions of gender. Ironically these were all fairly overt aspects of the masculine culture and organizing processes within ITINTUK! They were adamant that talented women had equal access to development programmes. They suggested that their under-representation on company programmes could be explained by the fact that some women were not assertive enough in terms of putting themselves forward, and others were not interested because the programmes were too demanding for women with caring responsibilities. Thus, gender inequality was invisible to them; similar to the majority of senior professionals, and they placed the blame and responsibility for women’s lack of advancement <sup>fi</sup>rmly with women themselves. They themselves were therefore acting to reinforce and legitimise inequality in ITINTUK.

The lived experiences of women participants suggested otherwise. Many mid-career women felt they lacked the essential support of their line-managers and were unclear as to how they could get onto a development programme without this. This had nothing to do with assertiveness. A lack of support could in part be because some male managers also expressed the view that the development programmes were too demanding for women with families. They lamented the fact that women s family roles stood in the way , but they neither took or o<sup>f</sup>ered suggestions for remedial action. Women participants argued that the company needed to do far more to train line-managers around career management in order to develop greater awareness about possible career trajectories that might deviate from the male norm (e.g. especially the idea that some women might want to pause for a period and then resume career development). If this strategy were adopted, they believed that there may be less likelihood of women with caring responsibilities being overlooked in career terms. One example of poor career management processes related to the introduction of a new management accreditation scheme. This initiative had supposedly been publicised throughout the organization, but many of the women who participated in our study were unaware of it which hints at how poor communication <sup>fl</sup>ows can hinder change (Acker, 2006). Others stated that when they had shown an interest, their line-managers had emphasised that they would have to participate outside of working hours (unlike other development programmes) which e<sup>f</sup>ectively ruled the scheme out given their already excessively long

working hours.

In summary, despite rhetorical support for gender equality and acknowledgement of women’s under-representation, there was nothing by way of substantive organizational change e<sup>f</sup>orts occurring. Our study con<sup>fi</sup>rms Acker’s (2006) claim that the legitimacy of inequalities at workplace level (as evidenced by employers apparent lack of will to tackle these) remains robust. Furthermore, it also con<sup>fi</sup>rms that the invisibility of gender inequality in terms of employer awareness and ‘naming’ of it as an organizational one is minimal (Acker, 2006)

## 4.3.2. Tactical networking: challenging gender inequalities

Women have only limited options to break out of a culture of control and compliance that Acker (2006) claims is made possible by both hierarchical organizational power and gender relations. One such strategy is organizational networking. Most of the female participants recognised that informal relationships developed among colleagues via male dominated networks which had the e<sup>f</sup>ect of diminishing career opportunities for women and making the performance appraisal/promotion system unfair. Nevertheless, some women in our study were highly cognisant of the importance of networking for building their careers and they purposefully involved themselves in male dominated networks. This tactical approach to networking rendered these women far more visible, and this they believed meant they were more likely to be chosen for prestigious projects and roles. They discussed how they proactively attempted to get to know ‘the right people’, by for example attending carefully selected after-work events, social or otherwise, and regularly working from the ITINTUK head o<sup>fi</sup>ce, whether they actually needed to or not, in order to come into regular contact with senior male colleagues. Some emphasised how tactical networking had e<sup>f</sup>ectively improved their career development and advancement over time.

Other women were reluctant to engage in this; as it often occurred in male-oriented spaces outside of normal working hours where they felt uncomfortable (i.e. at the pub or at a sport event). Participants were also conscious that networking was time consuming, detracted from work demands and exacerbated the long working hours. Some women also recognised that when women actively engage in male dominated networks in order to gain powerful male patronage they inadvertently reinforce, rather than challenge, male power structures. Nevertheless, tactical networking appeared to have been e<sup>f</sup>ective for some women and served to challenge, albeit indirectly and largely by assimilation, the gendered status quo.

## 5. Discussion

Here we discuss the components of organizational inequality regimes that our study has analysed. Regarding the shape and degree of gender inequality in the case study <sup>fi</sup>rm, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlighted that management level women had sustained IT careers, but onl a few had achieved career advancement to senior levels. Although, none of our female participants planned to leave IT and many expected to remain with ITINTUK, most were critical of the company culture and the formal and informal HR/management processes, which clearly discriminated against women’s advancement to senior roles and also made sustaining careers challenging. We view women s dissatisfaction here as a dimension of gender inequality re<sup>fl</sup>ecting Acker s (2006) broad de<sup>fi</sup>nition, which extends beyond the material (i.e. pay and status) and includes the respect and pleasures derived from work. Practically women had to accept that they would always be viewed as the odd girl out (Trauth, 2002); this was nothing new for them and as such was an accepted cultural norm.

With regard to organizing processes impacting on professional women’s capacity to sustain and develop careers in IT, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm Acker’s (2006) claim that in most organizations work is organized around the image of a man free of caring responsibilities. ITINTUK’s demand for ‘maximum <sup>fl</sup>exibility’ rendered it extremely di<sup>fi</sup>cult for women with caring responsibilities to achieve career advancement. Paradoxically for an IT <sup>fi</sup>rm, women were unable to utilise the potentialities of modern technology (e.g. working remotely) to juggle work and caring successfully in the way that has been suggested should be possible because of this entrenched working norm (e.g. Armstrong et al., 2012). Our study also demonstrates that within masculine cultures all women are tainted by a positioning of them as potential caregivers, or as possessing feminine dispositions which simultaneously legitimates gender inequalit and renders it invisible to male managers and possibly HR professionals (Acker, 2006).

Our study broadens the focus from work-family con<sup>fl</sup>ict to highlight the way in which myriad organizing processes (rather than merely the gender division of household family care) that are inextricably shaped by a masculine workplace culture, such as presenteeism, long hours, lack of female role models and mentors, exclusion from informal networking and development programmes, all interact to in<sup>fl</sup>uence women’s careers negatively. From this perspective, we see how transitioning from merely sustaining a career to career advancement becomes extremely problematic for all women. Moreover, we have highlighted that the workplace culture was highly resistant to change even though at least one of the recommended policy measures (<sup>fl</sup>exible work arrangements) was in place (Servon and Visser, 2011). Notably those women who had <sup>fl</sup>exible work arrangements still had to comply with the maximum <sup>fl</sup>exibility’ mandate and work additional hours with no recognition. Again therefore, they had to assimilate into the dominant work norms sustaining the inequality regime

Why had ITINTUK failed to achieve change? This is where Acker’s (2006) framework provides signi<sup>fi</sup>cant explanatory power. Organizational gender inequality was seen as legitimate by both men and women, insofar as it was viewed either as largely generated by societal factors, not the workplace or was accepted as ‘the way things are’ and as such was rendered invisible. This is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>nding which illuminates why women were poorly represented in senior technical roles and at senior levels more generally in the <sup>fi</sup>rm and could help to explain the persistent lack of women in such roles in IT more generally. However, we demonstrated that there were also elements of both control and compliance at work as revealed through the women’s responses to the environment and the company s policy responses. Women in our study had largely become rather stoic survivors, accepting that they had had at least moderate career success and deriving job satisfaction in the IT industry. Nevertheless, survival involved a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant degree of assimilation into the masculinised culture and accommodation of the gender stereotyping, blatant sexism and subtle discriminatory processes that they encountered. As in other studies (e.g. Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths et al. 2007), assimilation was achieved by remaining silent on gender discrimination and by engaging in impression management strategies (Servon and Visser, 2011) such as tactical networking in order to demonstrate a <sup>fi</sup>t within a male-de<sup>fi</sup>ned culture. Along with their silence, such strategies perpetuated the status quo, both in cultural terms and organizing processes (Gri<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 2007) reinforcing the inequality regime. Our analysis therefore suggests that it is likely that only the most resilient and determined women (in career terms) can sustain and advance careers in IT. However, if the only women who ‘make it’ in the sector are extraordinarily resilient, then male domination at senior levels together with the masculinised culture, will inevitably continue.

With regard to the company’s response, ITINTUK took a highly conservative approach to equality that was underpinned by managers’ reliance on arguments around individual choice and wider societal issues to explain women’s relative absence in technica and senior roles. The company was not prepared to set targets for women’s representation or to develop speci<sup>fi</sup>c programmes for women which previous research has shown can be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial (Panteli, 2012). Fundamentally management were not prepared to confront the organizing processes that contributed to the prevailing gender inequality regime (Acker, 2006).

## 5.1. Strategic implications for IT companies

While gendered organizing processes such as those we identify can be located in wider social relations, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings do have strategic implications for IT <sup>fi</sup>rms. The burden should not be on individual women to overcome career challenges (Soe and Yakura, 2008), rather women's under-representation should be positioned as a problem for the IT industry and for firms (Griffiths et al. 2007). Workplace cultures are notoriously resistant to change and to e<sup>f</sup>ect transformation will likely require bold strategic and organizational measures. Moreover, the gendering of family responsibilities at societal level undoubtedly in<sup>fl</sup>uences women’s capacity to sustain and develop careers in professional IT work as it is currently organized (i.e. around a male norm as we demonstrated here) (Quesenberry et al., 2006). Societal level gender norms around ‘appropriate’ work for women and gender relations in<sup>fl</sup>uence the IT workplace culture. Therefore, companies have to be cognisant of this broader context when examining workplace cultures and developing equality initiatives (Soe and Yakura, 2008), particularly as there is a clear business case for equality in IT given perennia labour and skills shortages.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, IT <sup>fi</sup>rms need to position organizing processes producing gender inequality as illegitimate within the workplace. This can be achieved through robust policies in support of women’s careers (such as mentoring), and particularly for those with caring responsibilities, implemented in such a way as not to damage career advancement (e.g. <sup>fl</sup>exible work arrangements that are genuinely rather than rhetorically accessible to professional women). Research has called for the implementation of work-life balance policies assuming that such policies resolve work-family con<sup>fl</sup>ict (Adya, 2008; Servon and Visser, 2011; Wentling and Thomas, 2009). However, as we have shown, merely creating such policies is clearly insu<sup>fi</sup>cient if managers believe that those who take up <sup>fl</sup>exible work arrangements can be justi<sup>fi</sup>ably and legitimately excluded from career development and advancement. Moreover, where the norm is that all women are viewed as potential caregivers, the rami<sup>fi</sup>cations are far-reaching (Quesenberry et al., 2006)

Secondly, as many researchers have highlighted, the typical masculinised IT workplace culture, and associated formal and informal processes promoting and sustaining that culture, is urgently in need of change for the sake of all women IT professionals, not just those with children (Armstrong et al., 2012; Servon and Visser, 2011). Discursive strategies can play a vital role here in mitigating the e<sup>f</sup>ects of and even changing masculine cultures. For example, workplace culture would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from investment in awareness raising via robust formal diversity training. Senior managers should be tasked with actively championing gender equality and challenging aberrant behaviour among male colleagues. They should model approaches to work and family that demonstrate support for parents. Promotion and access to developmental opportunities should also be based as far as possible on tangible obiective accomplishments that can be demonstrated rather than an over-reliance on subjectivity and undue emphasis being placed on informal behaviours such as presenteeism which naturally disadvantage women in entrenched masculinised cultures.

## 6. Conclusions and implications for research and practice

Gender remains under-researched in IS literature particularly in the workplace context. Utilising Acker’s (2006) concept of inequality regimes, we sought to address this gap and to provide some explanatory power for women’s persistent under-representation in an industry that continues to experience labour shortages and significant attrition of mid-career women. Soe and Yakura (2008: 187) observe that ‘the culture and climate of particular organizations provides the local, proximal context wherein women actually work’. Our study demonstrated its signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly detrimental e<sup>f</sup>ect on women’s career trajectories in one IT organization. However, following Acker (2006) we caution that the organizing processes that produce gender inequality vary across types of organization yet the corollary is that more studies investigating women’s career experiences in particular companies would add to the knowledge base about women’s persistent under-representation in IT.

It is also clear from our analysis that research that argues somewhat blithely for changes in workplace culture underestimates the entrenched and multi-faceted nature of the problem which renders formal and informal practices as legitimate and therefore invisible, ITINTUK had examples of successful women (such as the two executive board members we interviewed) who provided what many managers thought of as role models. Paradoxically their existence highlights how heroic exemplars who succeed despite the culture can contribute to, rather than solve the problem (Soe and Yakura, 2008). Thus, precisely how to achieve cultural transformation in the IT workplace that most research deems necessary remains elusive when the work and the workplace remain highly masculinised and women’s contributions are reduced to purported feminine skills or are largely invisible (Roan and Whitehouse, 2007). Although it is argued that the IT industry now depends on attracting a diversity of employees (Trauth, 2002), it does not seem that women per se are considered a vital element of that diversity or at least not on equal terms with men.

## Appendix A. Interview protocol and sample interview questions

The interviews covered three main themes: (i) career and company experiences; (ii) experiences of teamwork in ITINTUK; (iii) views on gender diversity in the IT workplace. Questions were posed in an open manner and probes were used where interviewees o<sup>f</sup>ered less fulsome responses. Most questions were posed to both women and men.
