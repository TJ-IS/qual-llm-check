# A Benchmarking Model for Management of Knowledge-Intensive Service Delivery Networks

SU DONG, MONICA S. JOHAR, and RAM L. KUMAR

Su Dong is a Ph.D. candidate in information technology in the Belk College of Business Administration, University of North Carolina at Charlotte. He has an undergraduate degree in MIS from the University of Shanghai for Science and Technology, China. His research interests include knowledge management strategies and knowledge sharing in online communities. His research has been accepted by the Workshop on Information Technologies and Systems (WITS) and Workshop on E-Business.

Monica S. Johar is an assistant professor of management information systems at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte. She received a Ph.D. in management science with a concentration in MIS from University of Texas at Dallas. Her research interests include optimal software development methodologies, content delivery systems, knowledge management, and Web personalization. She has several publications in leading IS journals, such as Information Systems Research, and presentations at conferences such as the Workshop on Information Technologies and Systems, Conference on Information Systems and Technology, and Workshop on E-Business.

Ram L. Kumar is professor in management information systems in the Belk College of Business Administration at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte. He received his Ph.D. in information systems from the University of Maryland. He worked for major multinational corporations, such as Fujitsu, before entering the academia. Dr. Kumar’s research has been funded by organizations such as the U.S. Department of Commerce, and firms in the financial services and energy industries. His current research interests include techniques for evaluating and managing portfolios of IT investments, service science, and knowledge management systems. His research has been published in Communications of the ACM, Computers and Operations Research, Decision Sciences, Decision Support Systems, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, International Journal of Production Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, and others. He has advised the U.S. Department of Energy and organizations in the private sector on the evaluation of R&D projects and IT portfolios.

Abs tract: Effective management of information technology (IT) and IT-enabled services is becoming increasingly important due to the growing complexity of their context. These services are often delivered by employees who work at widely dispersed locations and interact with each other to constitute knowledge-intensive service delivery networks (KISDNs). This paper contributes to the effective design and management of KISDNs by presenting a mixed-integer programming model that integrates disparate streams of research. This model facilitates analysis and managerial benchmarking of KISDN performance. It captures how the performance of such networks depends on the interaction between workflow decisions, structure of information flow networks (IFNs), and knowledge management decisions. We propose that knowledge about IFNs and worker competence can be effectively used to make workflow decisions. Our results, based on the study of different IFN archetypes, illustrate practices for effective management of KISDNs. Managers can enhance business value by recognizing existing IFNs, increasing randomness in IFNs, nurturing weak or performative ties depending on the archetype, assigning tasks based on effective worker competence, and selectively delaying assignment of tasks to workers. In addition, our results illustrate the impact of training and network density on KISDN performance.

Key words and phras es : benchmarking, IT services, knowledge-based services, knowledge management, OR models, service delivery, service science.

Organizations increas ingl y us e k nowl ed ge-intens ive information technology (IT) and IT-enabled services delivered from multiple locations. Employees providing such services may be at different locations and interact with each other to form a knowledge-intensive service delivery network (KISDN). We define KISDNs as networks of knowledge workers who use their expertise and professional relationships with other experts in an IT-intensive environment to perform knowledge-intensive service tasks. For example, US Internetworking (USi) (www.USi.com, now owned by AT&T) claims that over 70 percent of its employees have at least one certification (e.g., CISCO networking) and of these, 90 percent have multiple certifications. These employees are located in the United States and India. In addition to IT services, KISDNs include other knowledge-intensive services that are facilitated by sophisticated IT such as some types of management services, financial services, and engineering consulting services. It has been recognized that the real benefits of providing these knowledge-intensive services include expert knowledge and problem-solving capability [63]. Management of such KISDNs is an important, yet underresearched, area with significant potential for information systems (IS) as well as interdisciplinary research.

This research is related to the call for development of a “service science” discipline that integrates perspectives from multiple traditional disciplines such as information science, management science, social science, and IS [4, 13, 29, 56]. Maglio and Spohrer define service science as “the study of service systems which are dynamic value creation co-configurations of resources (people, technology, organizations and information)” [42, p. 18] and suggest that a service system may be considered a basic theoretical construct for service science. KISDNs represent service systems with a significant emphasis on knowledge management in a distributed resource environment, and are described in greater detail in the following sections of this paper. Even though IT and IT-enabled service systems are common, their internal dynamics and organization are not well understood. Prior research in IS [5] and service science [13, 45] has recognized the need to explore the relationship between service science and business value in appropriate contexts, as well as the need to study the relationship between service orientation and knowledge management. Hence, we focus on the following research question:

RQ: How are knowledge diffusion and business value affected by workflow decisions, knowledge management decisions, and organizational information networks in KISDNs?

This research contributes to improved understanding of service systems and contributes to research on service system improvement as described in Demirkan et al. [19]. Maglio and Spohrer describe service systems as “value co-creation configurations of people, technology, value propositions connecting internal and external service systems, and shared information” [42. p. 18]. We present a mixed integer programming model that captures key characteristics of these value co-creating configurations in order to study the above research question. Such modeling can produce a solution that can serve as a managerial benchmark for managing KISDNs. The proposed model considers worker competence, organizational information networks, worker availability, and task characteristics. We propose the use of IT to perform integrated business analytics that combines the above-mentioned factors in support of the service workflow process. The value of such modeling lies in its ability to identify key variables, structure the debate regarding trade-offs that affect the relationship between service orientation and business value, and induce organizational change [40]. This is consistent with the ultimate goal of service science, which has been described as “to apply scientific understanding to advance our ability to design, improve, and scale service systems for business and societal purposes” [42, p. 20].

We also propose a high-quality solution procedure for solving this mixed integer programming model. The proposed solution procedure integrates ideas from prior research on workflow (task assignment), knowledge management, and social network analysis, in the context of service systems. Our computational results based on this procedure provide several interesting insights and business intelligence into the internal organization and dynamics of service systems.

First, we demonstrate the significant additional value that can be generated by facilitating knowledge sharing using organizational information flow networks (IFNs) in conjunction with information regarding worker competence, worker availability, and service tasks. Second, prior research on call centers [23] and IT support [32] typically assumes that service requests are picked from a queue and assigned randomly to available workers. This research, however, illustrates that organizations can benefit from using dynamic assignment. Third, we highlight the impact of different information networks and worker and task characteristics on KISDN performance and discuss how knowledge retrieval and knowledge diffusion are different depending on the type of network structure. A network topology where communication between random workers in the organization is encouraged (random networks) is preferred over other network structures. We also discuss how different types of managerial actions may be appropriate depending on the type of network structure. Fourth, we discuss ways to reduce the performance difference between network topologies by intentionally increasing network density. Fifth, computational results illustrate how worker specialization occurs in a multiskill environment and how the degree of specialization is a function of the network topology, density, and number of skills. Finally, we illustrate how an organization can strategically use worker training to mitigate the effects of network structure differences when altering the network structure might be difficult. In summary, the model and solution procedure proposed in this paper can serve as a managerial benchmarking framework for KISDN management. This paper is likely to be of interest to IS researchers in service science, knowledge management, social networking, and workflow management.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The next section provides the multidisciplinary theoretical background that serves as the foundation for our research. The mathematical model of a KISDN and solution procedure to solve this model is discussed in the third section. Design of the simulation-based experiments is described in the fourth section. Computational results are the focus of the fifth section. A model extension is presented in the sixth section. The seventh section focuses on conclusions, limitations, and future research.

## Theoretical Background

This s ection cons is ts of three parts . First, we position our research relative to existing research on service science and highlight the importance of the KISDN management problem. Second, we review relevant research regarding social networking and knowledge sharing in work processes. Third, we review research on workflow decision making, knowledge management, and business value.

## KISDN and Service Science

Comprehensive reviews of service science from an IS perspective [5] as well as service science research [13, 45] have recognized the importance of knowledge management in service delivery. In particular, the need to conceptualize service delivery as a process with “a focus on dynamic resources such as knowledge and skills” [41, p. 5] has been highlighted. Such a conceptualization with a focus on knowledge and skills of the workforce is lacking in prior research on call centers [23] and IT services [10]. Hence, this research studies KISDNs, which are service systems with knowledge-intensive service tasks and service-level agreements. These tasks require multiple skills and are performed by knowledge workers. These knowledge workers have varying levels of competence in each skill and often require knowledge acquisition from networked co-workers. This research recognizes the dynamic nature of knowledge and skills of workers by allowing them to vary over time during service delivery. In addition, the distributed nature of the workforce and the potential of technology to facilitate knowledge sharing using IFNs are considered. The goal of this research is to better understand how people, technology, organization, and shared information can be brought together for dynamic value co-creation. Such a study is consistent with the service science perspective of studying “how service systems interact and evolve in order to create value” [42, p. 20]. Study of such service systems requires an interdisciplinary perspective [42]. Hence, we review related research from multiple disciplines.

## Social Networking and Knowledge Sharing in Work Processes

IS researchers are increasingly interested in social networking [1]. Consistent with prior research on social networking and organizations, we recognize that “the ability of a firm to be productive depends not only on the talents of its employees but largely on the way in which they interact” [27, p. 2]. In addition, the importance of information flow on productivity of organizations and the differential effect of network structure on information flow has been recognized in economics [60].

The social networking literature uses archetypes in order to understand different types of complex networks. IS research on information networks [34] has also used archetypes in order to study information exchange patterns in organizations. We focus on three IFN archetypes that have been extensively used in the context of organizational networks [9, 13, 21, 25, 34]: random networks (RNs), small-world networks (SNs), and clustered networks (CNs). RNs represent wide dissemination of information to random individuals within the organization. Information flow in CNs, on the other hand, is limited to flow within cliques<sup>1</sup> in the organizations. Information flow in SNs includes flows within cliques and some random flows across cliques. In essence, SNs can be thought of as a combination of clusters and random connections between clusters. Thus, collectively, RNs, SNs, and CNs cover a broad range of network types. Figure 1 illustrates these networks.

Comparison of the performance of RNs, SNs, and CNs has been undertaken in many contexts, including information flow [9], organization redesign [34], and knowledge diffusion [15], to yield context-specific insights. Prior research has indicated that RNs can be commonly found in knowledge-intensive industries [34]. Research on mining e-mail networks indicates the presence of multiple cliques (as in CNs) in some organizations [14, 58]. SNs are extremely common [21] and may also be appropriate in IT-intensive work environments [34, 54]. One or more of these networks may be present in an organization. Analysis of how RNs, CNs, and SNs behave and compare can help managers understand the salient features of these archetypes. In addition, this understanding facilitates effective service system design or improvement.

In studying KISDNs, we recognize that workers could seek help from co-workers in order to improve their competence. Prior research has examined the help-seeking behavior of workers in the context of task completion, and recognized the importance of relationships (ties) between workers [39]. This type of knowledge-sharing behavior is based on the idea of embedded knowledge exchange without immediate reciprocity [35, 39]. We use the term “tie” in order to refer to relationships resulting from sustained information exchange as a result of repeat collaborations. For example, research on mining social networks at Hewlett-Packard used an operational definition of at least 30 e-mail exchanges in two months to define a tie [58]. Another example of the use of ties can be found in IS research on open source software development [54].

Three types of ties between workers—strong, weak, and performative ties—have been identified in prior research [35]. Strong ties occur between workers who know each other directly through organizational relationships that facilitate knowledge sharing. Workers connected by weak ties do not know each other directly but have strong ties with another (intermediate) worker. This intermediate worker plays a bridging role that allows the two workers to get acquainted and share knowledge with each other. Workers may also consult experts who are listed in the company’s internal directory, and with whom they have no strong or weak ties. Consultation with such an expert is referred to as using a performative tie, since the basis for consultation is job performance (expertise) [39]. Help provided by workers is assumed to be a function of how busy they are [22, 44], and involve an efficiency factor [12, 15] that depends on the type of tie. Workers with strong ties are the most efficient and most likely to provide help [6, 26, 38].

Organizations also recognize the value of understanding social networks and factors influencing their formation [25, 49, 55]. There is a growing body of research on mining social networks from different types of organizational data, including e-mail, wikis, and blogs [25, 61], and using social networks in organizations [31]. The basis for formation of these networks could be a variety of organizational factors such as organizational reporting relationships, collocation, project experience, frequency of prior e-mail interactions on a topic [25, 49, 55]. Leading IT service providers such as IBM are building tools to mine social networks from internal and external data and make these social networks available to other applications through application program interfaces (APIs) [25]. “Pass It Along” is a system developed by IBM to help organizations take a collaborative approach to knowledge sharing based on social network information.<sup>2</sup> This technology expands the notion of social networking for employees and learners and includes advanced features such as learning paths, visual mapping, and expertise profiling. This stream of literature is relevant to our research since business intelligence regarding social networks has potential for use in the context of service management.

## Workflow Decisions, Knowledge Management, and Business Value

A classic workflow problem is the assignment of a group of service tasks to a number of knowledge workers in a manner such that each agent is assigned only one task and each task is assigned to one agent. Efficient solution procedures such as the Hungarian method are available for this problem [2]. Several extensions of the basic assignment problem have also been studied [48]. The KISDN management problem consists of making multiple worker-task assignments over a planning horizon. Assignment of these service tasks is based on task characteristics as well as worker competence. Workers are prohibited from carrying out more than one task at the same time, but they often perform multiple tasks over time.

This paper integrates ideas from different streams of knowledge management research that use simulation to study the effectiveness of knowledge-sharing behavior. Levine and Prietula [39] used agent-based simulation to study the impact of the three types of ties between workers in the context of knowledge-sharing behavior in social networks. They illustrated that having some performative ties in an organization improved average task completion times. However, they did not optimize task assignment or consider different types of network topologies. This research is also related to the work of Cowan and Jonard [15], who used simulation to study the impact of different types of network topologies in the context of knowledge diffusion across organizations. Their problem is different from the one studied in this paper in that they did not include optimization of task assignment, or different types of ties between nodes in the network. Shen et al. [53] studied service task assignment in workflow settings and used social network information to assign tasks to groups. However, they did not consider knowledge sharing or network structure. This research studies the workflow of service tasks in the context of knowledge workers. These workers can improve competence by consulting co-workers using ties. We study how different types of IFNs, density of these networks, workforce characteristics, and service task characteristics affect organizational learning, knowledge retrieval, and overall business value.

In our model extension, we consider the value of training. Hence, this research is related to the work of Chen and Edgington [12], who used mathematical modeling and simulation to study how training and learning by doing impact organizational value. They concluded that allowing workers to decide on when to attend training does not maximize organizational returns. Their study assumed that there was no knowledge sharing among co-workers and that learning by doing is relatively inefficient.

The following sections present a model that integrates these different perspectives as they relate to KISDNs.

## Model Development

This s ection d evel ops a mathematical mod el of KISDNs. Such KISDNs can be found in a wide range of service sectors such as management consultancy, design services, computer, and IT-related services [20, 63]. Figure 2 depicts value co-creation in a KISDN. The arrows in the figure illustrate the value co-creation process, starting from the bottom left. This model helps to develop a better understanding of how people, technology, organization, and shared information engage in dynamic value co-creation. Such an understanding facilitates managerial benchmarking of KISDNs.

In such a system, service requests need not be limited to telephone requests and may be routed through other communication channels such as faxes, e-mails, or Web forms [23]. In cases where service requests are routed through multiple levels, our focus is on the higher, more knowledge-intensive levels of support. The bottom-left portion of Figure 2 illustrates that it is not necessary for service tasks to be handled immediately by knowledge workers, though there often is a cost of delay due to factors such as service-level agreement penalties [10, 52]. These knowledge-intensive service tasks vary in terms of task difficulty, required skills, and associated revenue. For example, service tasks related to management consultancy concerning mergers and post-merger integration require distinct skills such as in-depth knowledge of both law and finance [20, 63]. Service tasks related to computer and IT often require skills such as database management and C/C++.

As shown in Figure 2, these service tasks need to be effectively assigned to workers in order to generate business value. The revenue resulting from task completion is based on the skills required, the market revenue for tasks requiring those skills, and task difficulty. Workers in KISDNs vary in terms of their competences in these skills and the organizational networks that they belong to. The time a worker takes to complete a task that requires particular skills could vary due to differences in worker competences [12, 17]. The complexity of these service tasks often requires knowledge workers to share their distinctive capabilities in order to provide unique services [20]. Therefore, it is possible that when workers are assigned to tasks, workers consult other co-workers to complete tasks efficiently [39, 57]. Such competence exchange is an important characteristic of value creation in service systems [42] and is a function of worker properties and the types of ties between workers. As described later, technology can play an important role in facilitating competence exchange. Figure 2 also illustrates that ties can be facilitated by reporting relationships, membership in global teams, and project experience. Some workers in such organizations may remain idle (“sit on the bench”) during any point in time. Organizations may continue to pay out wages to idle workers, thus negatively affecting business value co-creation.

## Model Formulation

Mathematical modeling is a useful tool to understand key variables that describe a problem and their relationships. In addition, mathematical modeling helps understand the relationships between different variables and produces a solution that can serve as a benchmark. This approach is appropriate in the context of service systems such as KIS-DNs when the goal is a better understanding of different factors in the value co-creation process. The model variables described in Table 1 represent the different elements of KISDNs. We model the problem of co-creating value in KISDNs using mixed integer programming, which is appropriate in scenarios where some variables are binary and other are continuous in value. In KISDNs, variables such as assigning a worker to a task are binary and others, such as worker competence, are continuous in value.

We consider a firm managing KISDN with an objective of maximizing business value over a planning horizon (Ph). This planning horizon (time) is divided into a set of discrete assignment periods $t \in \{ 1 , . . . , T \}$ , where t represents the assignment period number. At the start of every assignment period $t ,$ the organization makes task-to-worker assignments based on the number of unassigned service tasks in the system, and the availability and competence of workers. Note that the length or duration of an assignment period (D) represents a context-specific unit of time (1 minute, 5 minutes, 15 minutes, etc.) within which any newly arriving tasks are queued but no assignment decisions are made. Since the skills required by different service requests (tasks) could vary, we assume that there are a total of S skills supported by the firm and a total of M types of tasks in terms of the task properties (skills required by each task type). Every service task belongs to one of these task types. It should be noted that we allow each task type to require more than one skill. In addition, in line with prior research on managing IT service tasks [10, 52], we assume that the arrival rate of tasks follows a Poisson distribution, and that the tasks arriving in each time period are independent of each other. We assume a heterogeneous workforce where workers have varying levels of competence in each of the S skills. This set of competence values is defined as the worker’s knowledge vector. The organization has K workers, and during each assignment a worker may be assigned to a service task, or kept idle (kept on the bench). As discussed earlier, the firm may continue paying out wages to workers even when they are sitting on the bench. In addition, for each unassigned task, the firm incurs a wait-time penalty per unit time.

The firm’s objective is to maximize business value by completing service tasks. The revenue resulting from task completion is based on the skills required, the market revenue for tasks requiring those skills, and how difficult the task is. The cost of completing these tasks is a function of the effective worker competence, which in turn depends on knowledge acquisition from co-workers using the organization’s IFN. The firm also needs to consider the fact that helping a co-worker results in an overhead and reduces the efficiency of the worker providing help. Finally, the firm needs to incorporate the costs associated with workers sitting on the bench and the wait-time penalty from unassigned tasks.

For KISDNs, many of the service task properties (e.g., skills required and their complexity) and worker properties (e.g., competence) outlined in Figure 2 are essentially uncertain in nature. One way to handle this is to use averages to estimate the associated parameters. Although this removes the notion of uncertainty, we believe that uncertainty is a relevant aspect of the firm’s knowledge management problem. Therefore, in order to handle uncertainty more appropriately, we draw different samples of values for each of these parameters from an appropriate distribution. Note that the model formulated in the upcoming section can be interpreted as the knowledge management problem faced by the firm for one such instance or sample path.<sup>3</sup> In the following sections, we begin by briefly discussing each component of the firm’s business value and then present the overall value maximization objective for the firm.

## Task Revenue

Instead of directly choosing the revenue for a task, we developed the revenue function at the skill level first. The revenue for a task is a function of the skills required to complete the task and time that a benchmark worker (of competence 1) in the organization takes to complete the task. Hence, the revenue from task j is given by $R _ { _ j } = \Sigma _ { _ { s = 1 } } ^ { s } B _ { _ s } T R _ { _ { j s } } B M _ { _ { j s } }$ . This billing scheme is consistent with an industry practice of charging a standardized rate for a task based on task complexity [59].

## Costs Associated with Completing Tasks

The total cost associated with completing a task is a product of the time to complete the task and the worker’s wage rate $( W _ { k } )$ . The total time worker k takes to complete a task consists of two components: (1) the time required to complete the task based on worker $k \mathrm { { s } }$ competence, and (2) the overhead incurred by worker k as a result of providing help to co-workers.

Worker k’s competence or expertise in skill type s in assignment period t is given by $W c k _ { s t } \in ( 0 , 4 ]$ , where small values of $W C _ { k s t }$ indicate an expert worker capable of lower task completion times. However, larger values indicate a novice, capable of higher task completion times. Without any consultation, the time taken by worker k to complete task j is a product of worker’s competence in a particular skill and the time required by a benchmark worker (of competence 1) to complete a task $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \Sigma _ { s = 1 } ^ { S } T r _ { j s } B m _ { j s } W c _ { k s t } )$ Once a worker is assigned to a task, he or she can acquire additional knowledge by consulting co-workers. We model the extent of knowledge gained by worker k, as a result of consulting co-worker 1, as depending on: (1) the difference in their competence levels at that point in time $( W c _ { _ { k s t } } - W c _ { _ { l s t } } )$ , and (2) worker l’s willingness to help $( W h _ { l i } )$ A worker’s willingness to help is a function of the worker and the type of tie (strong, weak, or performative) shared by co-workers k and l. Prior organizational research has reported that individuals in organizations prefer using strong ties first (because they are more willing to help), followed by weak ties and performative ties [6, 26, 38]. We allow worker k to gain help from at most one worker in period t in skill s, and denote the total knowledge gained by worker k in period t by $G a _ { _ { k s t } }$ . Finally, worker k’s effective competence in skill s in period t is given by $E c _ { _ { k s t } } ^ { } = W c _ { _ { k s t } } ^ { } - G a _ { _ { k s t } } ^ { }$ . Therefore, the actual time a worker takes to complete a task, after knowledge acquisition, is given by $\Sigma _ { s = 1 } ^ { S } T r _ { j s } B m _ { j s } E c _ { k s t }$ (the Appendix provides the details of the assignment and knowledge acquisition constraints).

While consultation may benefit the worker receiving help, it is possible that a competent worker may be burdened by having to help multiple workers in a given period. Therefore, the organization must take the cost of providing help into account when recommending a worker who can serve as a help source. We model this cost by increasing the time taken by worker k to complete his or her assigned task, when helping other co-workers $( l \in \{ 1 , . . . , K \} , l \neq k )$ . Particularly, this additional time (overhead) in period t is modeled as

$$
\sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \sum_ {l = 1, l \neq k} ^ {K} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {2} H c _ {i} W b _ {k t} H _ {l k s t i}.
$$

Therefore, the total cost of completing tasks over the planning horizon is given by

$$
\sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} W w _ {k} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {t}} A _ {k j t} T r _ {j s} B m _ {j s} E c _ {k s t} + \sum_ {l = 1, l \neq k} ^ {K} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {2} H c _ {i} H _ {l k s t i} W b _ {k t}\right).
$$

We propose that organizations can recommend a help source by combining social network information with competence information. Tools such as IBM’s SOcial Network ARchitecture (SONAR) [25] provide social network information that can be combined with competence information from other available tools such as Microsoft’s Skills Planning und (and) Development (SPUD) [17], Knowledge Interchange Network (KIN), and Tacit Systems EKG (Enterprise Knowledge Gap) [16].

It is important to note that we consider that every time a worker completes a task there is an improvement in the worker's competence (i.e., $W c _ { _ { k s t } }$ decreases), due to consultation. This assumption is consistent with human capital theory [7] and with prior research on knowledge management [12]. Since $W c _ { _ { k s 1 } }$ represents the worker’s competence level at the beginning of the planning horizon, worker k’s updated competence in period t is modeled as

$$
W c _ {k s t} = W c _ {k s 1} - \sum_ {d = 1} ^ {t - 1} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {(t - 1)}} A _ {k j d} T r _ {j s} \kappa G a _ {k s d} W t _ {k j t}.
$$

In summary, our model incorporates four factors that have been recognized in prior research [16] as being important for effective knowledge sharing. These factors are (1) knowing what another person knows, which we model as worker competence $W c _ { k s t } ;$ (2) willingness to engage in problem solving, which we model as $W h _ { \scriptscriptstyle k i } ; ( 3 )$ being able to gain access, which we model by incurring a cost of providing help when a worker is busy; and (4) degree of safety in the relationship, which we model using different values for $W h _ { \scriptscriptstyle k i } ,$ based on the type of tie.

## Cost Associated With Workers Sitting on Bench

When a worker is not competent enough to perform any task or there are no tasks available for him or her to perform, the worker might just have to sit idle for that assignment period. However, the firm could incur a cost for workers sitting on the bench because it may continue to pay out wages to these workers. Therefore, the cost associated with the workers who are kept idle in period t is given by

$$
\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \left(\left(1 - W b _ {k t}\right) - \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {t}} A _ {k j t}\right) W w _ {k} B c.
$$

## The Wait-Time Penalty from Unassigned Tasks

Many IT service requests are time critical and delays in responding to these requests can often result in significant penalties for the firm. To capture this, we introduce a task level wait-time penalty per period $P c \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ . Here, Pc represents the reduction in the task revenue (billing rate) for every time period that the task is kept waiting in the system. Hence, the total wait-time penalty incurred in period t is given by

$$
\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {t}} \left(\left(1 - \sum_ {d = 1} ^ {t} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} A _ {k j d}\right) \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} T r _ {j s} B _ {s}\right) P c.
$$

## Objective Function

Based on the revenue and cost components discussed above, the overall objective function<sup>4</sup> for KISDN optimization problem can be written as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Max} \left(\sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \left(\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {t}} A _ {k j t} R _ {j} - W w _ {k} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {t}} A _ {k j t} T r _ {j s} B m _ {j s} E c _ {k s t} + \sum_ {l = 1, l \neq k} ^ {K} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {2} H c _ {i} H _ {l k s t i} W b _ {k t}\right)\right)\right) \\ - \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \left(1 - W b _ {k t} - \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {t}} A _ {k j t}\right) W w _ {k} B c - \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {t}} \left(\left(1 - \sum_ {d = 1} ^ {t} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} A _ {k j d}\right) \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} T r _ {j s} B _ {s}\right) P c\right). \end{array}
$$

The above mathematical expression can be interpreted as maximizing the following: Total Task Revenue – Total Costs Associated with Completing Tasks (including the overhead of providing help) – Total Bench Cost – Total Wait Time Penalty. See the Appendix for additional details on the constraints.

## Solution Procedure

The complexity<sup>5</sup> of the KISDN optimization problem prohibits realistic problems to be solved in a reasonable amount of time. Hence, we propose a dynamic assignment heuristic (DAH), shown in Figure 3, in order to solve the model discussed above.

If the planning horizon (Ph) was equal to the length or duration of a single period $( { \mathrm { i . e . , } } P h = \Delta$ , implying T = 1), then the maximization problem is similar to an assignment problem with inclusion and exclusion constraints. However, when the planning horizon is divided into multiple periods (T > 1), then this problem can be solved for each period successively. In other words, we first determine the optimal assignment and the optimal payoff in the first period. Next, we set up the problem for the second period. To achieve this we use the assignment information from the first period, and take into account all the new tasks that have arrived and the workers who have become available between periods 1 and 2. In addition, we update the worker’s competences based on the task assignment in the first period. The optimal assignment and payoff for the second period can be obtained by using this information. Similarly, the assignment for the second period then sets up the problem for the third period, and so on. This would essentially be a greedy algorithm with no look-ahead, wherein the emphasis is on maximizing the payoff for only that period. In contrast, the proposed DAH improves over the greedy approach in two ways: (1) it allows for dynamic assignment using a One-Period Look Ahead (OPLA) policy and (2) using suitable approximations, it incorporates the value of learning. In other words, it considers how the worker’s knowledge acquisition in the current period impacts performance in future periods. For details, refer to Figure 3.

## Simulation Design

The compl exity of the probl em precl ud es an anal ytical s ol ution and requires us to use simulation.<sup>6</sup> Other studies in IS have used simulation with synthetic data to provide insights into relationships between key variables. This approach is appropriate when the underlying phenomenon is complex and real-world data is difficult to obtain. Examples of such studies include the value of knowledge management [12], electronic markets [30], the performance of IS teams [47], and security portfolios [33]. The value of our model is primarily to provide generalized insights and facilitate managerial benchmarking for the operation of KISDNs. This section describes the design of simulation experiments, including key parameters and their estimations. Fifty replications of each sample path were used, and average values of system performance measures were calculated. Such an approach using average performance analysis is consistent with prior IS research [12, 30, 33, 47, 52]. Simulations of the KISDN problem were extremely computation intensive. Hence, they were run on a cluster of 160 Intel Xeon CPUs on Dell blade servers using the Red Hat Enterprise Linux operating system. The average time for running each replication of a sample path was 2.4 hours.

## Worker Parameters

We use two worker-related parameters: wage rate per period for a benchmark worker (of competence 1) for skill $s ( W r )$ , and initial competence of worker k in skill s $( W c _ { k s 1 } )$ The wage rate for worker k $( W _ { \boldsymbol { W } _ { k } } )$ is calculated from these as shown in Table 2. These values were chosen to be comparable to the range of values encountered in practice [52].<sup>8</sup> In addition, we assume the bench cost coefficient (Bc) is 1. Consistent with prior research on worker cross-training, a normal distribution of worker competence was assumed [50]. Empirical research on the operation of IT service environments has illustrated the presence of considerable worker heterogeneity in service task completion [32]. Worker heterogeneity refers to the variation in skill levels across workers, for a given number of workers. We chose a range of four for worker competence, based on the Microsoft SPUD project [17], which recognizes four levels of worker competence in each skill. A mean of 1.4 was chosen to allow for a normal distribution of worker competence in the range 0–4. While it is easy to measure worker’s wage rate $( W _ { k } )$ it is more difficult to measure worker’s competence $( W c _ { k s t } )$ . However, as discussed earlier, technology can be used to assess, store, and retrieve information regarding worker competence.

## Task-Related Parameters

The time for a benchmark worker to complete the requirement in skill s for task j is given by $B m _ { _ { j s } } = N ( T t _ { _ s } , \pmb { \sigma } _ { _ s } )$ with values (in minutes) for each type of skill given in Table 2. We assume six types of tasks in terms of their skill requirement. Each task requires two skills and an equal arrival rate ${ ( \lambda _ { _ m } ) }$ for each task type m. Task arrival rates and task times are in the range that are encountered in practice [28, 52].<sup>9</sup> Sen et al. report mean and maximum values for task times in the range of 30 minutes to 8 hours [52], and Hewlett-Packard reports problem resolution times of 4 hours to 10 days [28]. In our simulation, for example, a task requiring skills 1 and 2 has a mean task time of $1 5 \times 1 0 = 1 5 0$ minutes, and a maximum time of 330 minutes. The billing rate for each skill (B ) is calculated based on the wage rate for workers, assuming a profit margin of 50 percent, which is comparable to prior research [52]. Setting a standardized billing rate (B ) for a task based on the skill required is consistent with industry practice [59].

## Knowledge Acquisition Parameters

The willingness to help $( W h _ { k i } )$ , the overhead coefficient $( H c _ { i } )$ , and the knowledge retention coefficient (k) are parameters designed to capture the characteristics of the knowledge acquisition environment. Willingness to help has been extensively researched [11]. This parameter is a function of the type of tie between workers [6, 26, 38]. Cowan and Jonard [15] use values in the range 0.5–1 for strong ties and recognizes that high values close to 1 are unrealistic. The values chosen by us are in this range.

The overhead coefficient captures an individual’s cost of providing help and depends on the type of tie [43]. In our simulations, we assume that the time spent on each help transaction is small, relative to task times.<sup>10</sup> For each help transaction, the values for the overhead coefficient chosen in Table 2 translate to a maximum of 2.5 percent, 7.2 percent, and 12 percent for strong, weak, and performative ties, respectively, for an average task time of 25 periods. Since strong ties are more efficient in terms of communication [37], we assume that the overhead coefficient is the smallest for strong ties, followed by weak and performative ties, in that order. It should be noted that when a worker helps multiple co-workers, the overhead incurred is large. Exact parameter estimation may be difficult. However, the intent is not to be able to estimate these parameters accurately, but to force organizations to think about whether these parameters are low or high and to consider ways to enhance their value. Such an approach is consistent with prior simulation-based knowledge management research [12].

Knowledge retention coefficient forces organizations to think about synergies between tasks performed and is similar to the concept of reuse, which has been used in other contexts such as software engineering [51]. Learning while completing tasks has greater value in scenarios where the knowledge retention ratio is high. We experiment with a range of values for these parameters.

## Network Parameters

A clustered network with 100 nodes (workers) was created by connecting each node with Nd of its nearest neighbors. SNs and RNs were created by disconnecting the link and reconnecting it with probabilities of 0.09 and 1, respectively, using the Watts and Strogatz algorithm [62]. It is important to note that the Watts and Strogatz algorithm maintains the same average number of neighbors even though the network topology changes. The values of Nd used are described in Table 2. We generate 50 different samples (with different connectivity) for each type of network. We simulate the performance of each of these 50 networks, and report the average performance. Technologies such as InFlow (www.orgnet.com/inflow3.html) and IBM SONAR [25] allow organizations to extract the network parameters discussed above, from e-mails, blogs, and other sources.

## Impact of Network Topology and Density

Based on the discussion of IFNs in the second section, we can expect organizations or groups within the organization to have different network structures (topology and density). We expect these IFN archetypes to vary in terms of business value and knowledge diffusion. Understanding these differences can contribute to a better understanding of dynamic value co-creation in service systems such as KISDNs. In order to understand the three networks structures (RN, CN, and SN), we analyze the KISDN performance measures mentioned above for different values of network density (ratio of number of strong ties for each node to the total number of nodes).

We observe that the financial performance (Figure 4a) and operational performance (Figure 4b) increase as networks become denser. In addition, we notice that RNs outperform the other network topologies.<sup>12</sup> However, the difference between RNs and the other two network topologies decreases as network density increases. These results are driven by knowledge-sharing behavior, which in turn depends on network topology and network density. Next, we analyze knowledge-sharing behavior in detail.

As discussed earlier, in our model, the total time a worker spends on a task is a function of his effective competence, which in turn depends on the extent of knowledge acquired from co-workers. The extent of knowledge exchange between two co-workers depends on the type of tie shared and the competence difference between them. Typically, strong ties are the preferred method of consultation since they have a higher willingness to help and the least overhead coefficient [6, 26, 38]. However, the number of strong ties that each worker has is limited. Weak ties have a lower willingness to help, but are greater in number as compared to strong ties. Finally, performative ties allow a worker to connect to any worker in the system, and although they are largest in number, they are the least efficient in terms of both willingness to help and the overhead of providing help. The average number of strong ties per node increases as network density increases. Since strong ties are the most effective means of acquiring knowledge, this accounts for improved financial and operational performance with increase in network density (Figures 4a, 4b). Also, as network density increases to relatively high values, the three network topologies tend to become similar, reducing performance differences between them.

While the number of strong ties is the same across the three network structures, the type of knowledge-sharing behavior invoked by each network structure is very different. This is largely driven by the fact that as workers acquire knowledge from each other to perform tasks, cliques tend to become similar over time (in terms of knowledge vectors of workers). Therefore, the amount of knowledge gained by using strong and weak ties within cliques becomes limited as compared to using same ties from outside the clique, if they exist. In addition, within cliques, there is a high overlap between the strong and weak ties, making weak ties redundant.

## Impact of Cost of Providing Help on Relative Network Performance

As previously discussed, the cost of providing help is an important characteristic of help-seeking behavior [11]. Hence, we study the impact of the cost of providing help on the dynamics of knowledge exchange in KISDNs with different types of ties. In our experiments, the cost of providing help is increased in such a way that the relative cost of providing help via strong, weak, or performative ties is maintained. As the overhead from providing help increases, it is beneficial to acquire knowledge from only those co-workers where the knowledge gain can offset the cost. In such scenarios, workers increasingly tend to use performative ties since strong and weak ties alone may not offer significant knowledge gain. This results in reduced financial performance (Figure 7). In addition, we find that as the cost of providing help decreases, the difference between network structures decreases. Since RNs invoke the most efficient help-sharing behavior, RNs are less sensitive to increase in the cost of providing help, compared to SNs and CNs, in that order.<sup>14</sup>

## Effect of Various Parameters on Assignment Decision Dynamics

Recall that the DAH uses OPLA to make a decision on whether to wait for one period or make an assignment during that time period. We refer to this as the assignment decision. Hence, the time between successive assignments is dynamic and could be one or more time periods. The assignment decision is related to the costs and benefits of keeping tasks waiting or workers idle in the system. As discussed earlier, we assign a wait-time penalty for each time period that a task is waiting in the system. Also, the firm continues to pay out wages to all workers that are kept on bench. The benefit comes from the fact that, in each assignment period, the firm can now choose from a larger pool of unassigned tasks and workers, with varying competences, resulting in improved assignments of tasks to workers. Thus, the net benefit from waiting to make an assignment depends on the number of tasks waiting to be assigned and the magnitude of the wait-time penalty. Hence, we study how the assignment decision depends on various factors, such as worker heterogeneity and network density.

Worker heterogeneity refers to the variation in skill levels across workers for a given number of workers. Recall that the intuition behind delaying assignment is that the firm can choose from a larger pool of unassigned tasks and workers with varying competences. Therefore, when there is significant worker heterogeneity, waiting results in better task-to-worker assignments (in terms of revenue from task completion and future value of learning). This is because there is a greater degree of mismatch between workers’ competence level and the requirement of the arriving tasks. Conversely, for homogeneous workers, it is trivial to see that there is no value of waiting. This is illustrated in Figure 8, where the average number of time periods between assignments for a low value of worker heterogeneity (0.40) is less than that for higher values of worker heterogeneity (0.45).

## Model Extension: Impact of Training on Knowledge Acquisition

As d is cus ed earl ier, the extent of k nowl ed ge exchange, and hence knowledge diffusion, between co-workers depends on the type of network structure. However, it may not be possible to easily alter the IFN of a firm, in order to improve knowledge diffusion. Therefore, we propose an extension to the basic model (outlined in the third section) where a firm can use training as a means to effectively improve the knowledge diffusion process. The firm can provide an opportunity for workers to undertake training and improve competence in one or more skills, in addition to consulting other co-workers. By allowing workers to take training, the firm can mitigate some of the drawbacks associated with SNs and CNs. Specifically, training can be used to strategically ensure that specialized knowledge does not get limited to cliques and that access to knowledge across all workers becomes homogeneous.

The use of training is prevalent in the knowledge management literature. Chen and Edgington [12] discuss two factors affecting knowledge acquisition through training. One factor is the sophistication of the knowledge provided in the training, which determines the maximum gain in competence (for a skill) that a worker can obtain after undergoing training. The other factor is the trainee’s learning rate, which is affected by unique, individual mental models [3]. Hence, the same training may result in different competence gains for different workers. In our model, $M \boldsymbol { c } _ { s t }$ represents the maximum competence level offered by a training session, in skill s in assignment period t. We assume that not all training sessions are equally efficient. In addition, $L r _ { _ { k s } } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ represents the learning rate associated with worker k for a training session in skill s.

In this extension, we allow workers to be engaged in training, in addition to being assigned to service tasks at any point in time. The revenue from task completion, bench-cost due to idle workers, and wait-time penalties due to tasks waiting in the system are calculated similar to the third section. In addition, there are two types of costs associated with training. First is a direct cost, related to the wages paid out to workers when in training. We model this as a product of the worker’s wage rate $( W _ { \boldsymbol { w } _ { k } } )$ and the time required to complete the training $( T l _ { s t } )$ . The second cost is indirect and is related to the fact that assigning a worker to training makes him unavailable for any other task. As a result, the firm might incur additional wait-time penalties on tasks waiting in the system while the worker is in training. This is modeled by adding an additional assignment constraint to the model in the third section. The benefit associated with training comes from the improvement in the worker competence after training. Similar to learning from co-workers (as modeled in the third section), this improved competence allows the worker to complete future tasks more efficiently.<sup>15</sup>

In Figure 9, we show how the competence offered by training affects the firm’s performance. We find that adding training as a means of knowledge acquisition benefits the organization, irrespective of the network structure. It is important to note that CNs benefit the most from offering training (highest percentage increase compared to SNs and RNs) as well as from increasing the level of competence offered by training (highest slope compared to SNs and RNs). This is because training enhances the knowledge diffusion and acquisition process by ensuring that knowledge does not get stuck in cliques in CNs and SNs. Although RNs continue to outperform (not shown), they are least sensitive to the competence offered by training.

## Conclusions

Trends in network ing, gl obal ization, and evol ution of software as a service are increasing the importance of studying KISDNs. In our opinion, managing KISDNs is an important aspect of the emerging discipline of service science, which is of increasing interest to IS researchers. This paper built on prior research by integrating multiple related streams of research (workflow, knowledge management, social networking), in the context of service science. To the best of our knowledge, the model and solution procedure represent the first attempt to systematically analyze an important and complex research question in the context of delivering IT as a service. The value of such analytical modeling lies in the identification and integration of parameters and relationships into a framework that helps to structure the debate on how to manage KISDNs [41]. Such a framework facilitates managerial benchmarking in order to identify value deficiencies and facilitate organizational change. The research presented in this paper views management of KISDNs as a problem at the nexus of workflow management, social networks, and knowledge management. In our opinion, such a conceptualization is of particular interest to IS researchers since extant IS research includes the above-mentioned three research streams.

Mathematical modeling can be a driver of organizational change [40]. In practice, managers could think about model variables and the model in the context of their own organizations and better understand how their organizations function. For example, managers could think about parameter values and the value of the type of task assignment proposed in the model to see how these parameters and task assignments impact their organizational value. The objective function in our model describes the business value goal of a KISDN. Our model solution and simulation results help us understand the optimal solution that serves as a “managerial value benchmark”<sup>16</sup> for organizations.

Such a managerial value benchmark facilitates understanding of value deficiencies and potential organizational transformation toward more effective KISDNs.<sup>17</sup>

To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first to propose how IFN structure information can be combined with worker competence information to improve operational and financial performance of KISDNs. Specifically, we propose the integration of literature and tools for mining social networks [25, 61] and measuring knowledge competence [16, 17] to provide integrated business analytics that can be used for task assignment. Our results also underscore the importance of weak ties in improving organizational performance. Recognizing the importance of weak ties and nurturing them, in our opinion, is an important managerial implication from our results. In addition, our results indicate that managers need to pay attention to different types of ties depending on the information network structure in their organizations. For example, in the case of CNs, which, over time, tend to have similar cliques of knowledge, organizations need to pay special attention to encouraging use of performative ties.

Our results emphasize the need for managers to understand the structure of their IFNs in order to better manage knowledge transfer. Such an understanding is useful in designing KISDNs. Our results indicate that random information flow between individuals is preferred over clustering. Hence, managers could consider job rotation and facilitating communication between key individuals in different parts of the organization as a way of increasing randomness [18, 36]. Complete reorganization to RNs may be expensive or infeasible. We find that improving network density, particularly in the case of CNs, significantly improves knowledge transfer and consequently organizational performance. Creating SNs by means of links between cliques is also desirable, particularly at higher network densities. Also, in cases where it is nontrivial for organizations to change the social network structure, managers should focus on strategically training workers or providing incentives to improve worker’s willingness to help, in order to maximize performance. It is also important for managers who are reorganizing KISDNs to be aware of IFNs in their organization and not disrupt effective network structures in the process [36].

We illustrate that worker specialization resulting from knowledge diffusion depends on the average number of skills required by the tasks. Greater worker specialization occurs in an environment where a task requires only one skill. Our analysis indicated that organizations may benefit from not assigning service tasks immediately (by using dynamic assignment). In other words, when worker heterogeneity is large, waiting to make an assignment is valuable, since it results in higher revenue due to better taskto-worker assignment.

Thus, our results provide several management practices for effective management of KISDNs. Recognizing existing information flow network structures, trying to increase (or not reduce existing) randomness in information flow network structures, nurturing weak or performative ties depending on the archetype, assigning tasks based on effective worker competence, and selectively delaying assignment of tasks to workers can enhance business value. Organizations can also consider training, increased network density, and increased willingness to help in order to mitigate the effect of network structure differences.

The research described in this paper can be further enhanced to improve our understanding of KISDNs, which represent an exciting conceptualization of many IT-intensive service settings. Researchers could enhance the model to incorporate alternative or complementary model elements such as alternative training policies, learning models, worker compensation models,<sup>18</sup> or knowledge depreciation. This paper assumed that the arrival of tasks are independent based on a wide variety of service research [10, 52]. Future research could examine interdependent task arrivals, for example, by extending the unit of analysis in this paper (a single KISDN) to multiple interrelated KISDNs. This extension would be analogous to studying queuing networks [8] and is likely to be more complex and computation intensive. While we concentrate on individual-oriented service tasks, one could consider a team-oriented service scenario. Modeling such a scenario is similar to modeling a project and would depend on the structure of the project and the team. Our focus in this paper has been on maximizing value. However, organizations might be interested in other objectives such as maximizing knowledge sharing for future use. Alternative model formulations to study this are interesting areas of future research. As with any simulation study, the generalizability of our results is limited by our range of experiments. However, as explained in the fourth section, we have justified our parameter choices in a manner consistent with other simulation studies.

The variables identified in mathematical modeling can drive empirical researchers to better understand relationships between key variables in a service context. These variables can also help managers think about what data to collect in order to better understand their own organizations and facilitate managerial benchmarking. In our opinion, improved understanding of the nexus between service workflow management, knowledge management, and social networking is important for service management. This paper will serve as useful initial framework for IS researchers as well as practitioners interested in exploring this nexus or its components in a service science context.

Appendix: Constraints for the KISDN Optimization Problem

Assignment Constraints

$$
\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {t}} A _ {k j t} + W b _ {k t} \leq 1 \quad \forall k \in \{1, \dots , K \}, t \in \{1, \dots , T \}.\tag{A1}
$$

Work er k can be as igned in the current period if and only if worker k is not busy with any tasks.

$$
\sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {t}} \left(A _ {k j t} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} T r _ {j s} B m _ {j s} E c _ {k s t}\right) + \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \left(1 - W b _ {k t} - \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {t}} A _ {k j t}\right)
$$

$$
+ \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \sum_ {l = 1, l \neq k} ^ {K} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {2} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} H c _ {i} H _ {l k s t i} W b _ {k t} \leq T \quad \forall k \in \{1,..., K \},\tag{A2}
$$

Total time spent by a worker on tasks and on the bench cannot exceed T.

$$
\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} A _ {k j t} \leq 1 \quad \forall j \in \left\{1, \dots , N _ {\mathrm{Max}} \right\} \text {and}
$$

$$
\sum_ {j = N _ {t} + 1} ^ {N _ {\mathrm{Max}}} A _ {k j t} = 0 \quad \forall k \in \{1, \dots , K \}, t \in \{1, \dots , T \}.\tag{A3}
$$

A task can only be assigned once and after it arrives in the system.

$$
W t _ {k j t} \left(\sum_ {d = 1} ^ {t - 1} \left(t - d - \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \left(E c _ {k s d} T r _ {s j} B m _ {s j} + \sum_ {r = d} ^ {t - 1} \sum_ {l = 1, l \neq k} ^ {K} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {2} H c _ {l} H _ {l k s r i}\right)\right) A _ {k j d} / T + W t _ {k j (t - 1)}\right) \geq 0
$$

$$
2 W t _ {k j t} \geq \left(\sum_ {d = 1} ^ {t - 1} \left(t - d - \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \left(E c _ {k s d} T r _ {s j} B m _ {s j} + \sum_ {r = d} ^ {t - 1} \sum_ {l = 1, l \neq k} ^ {K} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {2} H c _ {i} H _ {l k s r i}\right)\right) A _ {k j d} / T + W t _ {k j (t - 1)}\right)\tag{A4}
$$

$$
\sum_ {d = 1} ^ {t - 1} A _ {k j d} - W t _ {k j t} \geq 0 \quad \forall k \in \{1, \dots , K \}, j \in \{1, \dots , N _ {\mathrm{Max}} \}, t \in \{2, \dots , T \}.
$$

determines whether worker k has completed task j by period t.

$$
W b _ {k t} = \sum_ {d = 0} ^ {t - 1} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {(t - 1)}} \left(1 - W t _ {k j (t - 1)}\right) A _ {k j d} \quad \forall k \in \{1, \dots , K \}, t \in \{2, \dots , T \}\tag{A5}
$$

determines whether worker k is available in period t.

Knowledge Acquisition Constraints

$$
\sum_ {l = 1, l \neq k} ^ {K} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {2} H _ {k l s t i} \leq 1 \quad \forall k \in \{1,..., K \}, \rho_ {k l i} = 1, s \in \{1,..., S \}, t \in \{1,..., T \}.\tag{A6}
$$

Worker $k$ can get help from only one worker 1 using one type of tie in skill s in period t.

$$
\begin{array}{l l} G a _ {k l s t i} = \big (W c _ {k s t} - W c _ {l s t} \big) W h _ {l i} & \forall k, l \in \big \{1,..., K \big \}, l \neq k, s \in \big \{1,..., S \big \}, \\ & t \in \big \{1,..., T \big \}, i \in \big \{0, 1, 2 \big \}, \rho_ {k l i} = 1 \end{array}
$$

$$
G a _ {k s t} = \sum_ {l = 1, l \neq k} ^ {K} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {2} G a _ {k l s t i} H _ {k l s t i} \quad \forall k \in \{1,..., K \}, s \in \{1,..., S \}, t \in \{1,..., T \}\tag{A7}
$$

$$
E c _ {k s t} = W c _ {k s t} - G a _ {k s t} \quad \forall k \in \{1, \dots , K \}, s \in \{1, \dots , S \}, t \in \{1, \dots , T \}
$$

determines worker k’s effective competence in skill s in period t after consultation.

$$
