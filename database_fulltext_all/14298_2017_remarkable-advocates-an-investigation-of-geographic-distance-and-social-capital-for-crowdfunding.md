## Highlights

This paper answers what kinds of advocates (in terms of geographical distance and social capital) are advantageous to crowdfunding projects. Here we list out five key contributions:

\- This study investigates crowdfunding projects using their advocates.

\- Crowdfunding projects could benefit from leveraging remotely located advocates.

\- Social capital accumulated from advocates' social networking site (SNS) is conducive to the success of the project.

• Findings support interaction effect between geographic distance and social capital.

\- Several practical suggestions for crowdfunding entrepreneurs are provided.

## Abstract:

This study investigates the advocates of crowdfunding projects. Advocates refer to individuals who have invested in a project, and the information of such endorsement is spread through their social networks. By using a combined set of sizable data, this research empirically examines the relationships between two attributes of advocates, namely their geographical distance and social capital, and funding performance. Results reveal that higher funding can be secured with advocates who are (1) of further geographical distance and (2) of higher social capital. Additionally, the relationship between advocates' social capital and funding performance is further enhanced with a lower geographical propinquity.

Keywords: crowdfunding, geographical distance, social capital, advocates, microblog, e-business

## 1 1 Introduction

Entrepreneurship is a creative and thorny endeavor (Steyaert 2007), especially at the very early stage of development of a new company (Ley and Weaven 2011). Entrepreneurs constantly confront various challenges threatening their survival and development. Among many factors, financial capital is a central factor determining entrepreneurial growth (Wiklund and Shepherd 2005). Attracting and retaining talented people cannot be accomplished without money, and innovating products and services cannot proceed without monetary provisions (Brush et al. 2001). For decades, entrepreneurs have acquired financial capital from conventional sources, which

include venture capitalists, angel investors, government institutions, nonprofit organizations (NPOs), and private endowments from family and friends (Davila et al. 2003; Parker 2009; Weerawardena and Sullivan Mort 2006).

In recent years, online crowdfunding, as a form of e-business, has emerged as a viable alternative for entrepreneurs to raise funds, often during the early stage of their entrepreneurship. A recent Forbes article $^{1}$ reported that US34 billion would be crowdfunding in 2015, which surpassed the average amounts, i.e., US30 billion, invested by venture capitalists per year. Through online crowdfunding platforms, entrepreneurs can appeal to the masses (i.e., the crowd) and accrue relatively small momentary tokens from individuals, which cumulatively can be a sizable amount of funding. Enticed by this emerging platform, several studies on crowdfunding have focused on contrasting crowdfunding mechanisms, such as asking people to preorder the product or requesting a fixed amount of money for a share of the future profits (Belleflamme et al. 2014), or discussing their financial regulation (Lehner 2013).

Although seemingly promising, not all crowdfunding projects are able to attract the desired amount of funding (Hollas 2013), probably because migrating fundraising from the physical world to the online setting does not guarantee an easier way to get/raise money; in fact, the paradigm has changed. Crowdfunding platforms allow entrepreneurs to overcome physical boundaries and reach out to the global public (Belleflamme et al. 2014; Giudici et al. 2013; Mollick 2014). To many, this ability means that the appeal for financial support has moved from focusing on a small, identified group of targets (e.g., institutions or agencies) to a wide and probably scattered people who contribute in smaller individual amounts (Kim and Viswanathan 2014). Thus, either the entrepreneur promotes the project himself/herself or engages advocates to help with promotion; obviously, the latter (i.e., tapping the advocates) appears to be a feasible solution. Advocates are individuals who have invested in a project and spread this endorsement through their social networks. Different from the offline circumstance where spatial distance would restrain information proliferation, online information can be rapidly and diffusely propagated across netizens through social media (Rydén et al. 2015; Meijer and Thaens 2013). Lu et al. (2014) argued that social media has a positive impact on promoting crowdfunding projects, i.e., increasing the success rate of fundraising. Several guidelines have been proposed for those fundraisers who expected to leverage social media for their crowdfunding campaigns, such as social media analytics or content analytics (Young 2012). In our case, this process is performed when crowdfunding websites are linked to social media (Zheng et al. 2014), thus spreading the fundraising information through the advocates' social networks. To this end, a leading question is: What kinds of advocates (in terms of geographical distance (GD) and social capital (SC)) are advantageous to crowdfunding projects?

We propose that advocates could differ in their consequences on funding performance. In this study such impact is based on the remoteness of the advocates (e.g., further GD in order to reach out to more people) and the appeal to a large pool of people (i.e., of high SC). The former (offline), that is, the GD, denotes the physical terrestrial spread of the advocates, whereas the latter (online), that is, SC, reflects the ability of an advocate to disseminate project information to people on the Internet (Chen et al. 2012). An empirical study involving a combined set of sizable data from a crowdfunding platform and a microblogging website in China was conducted in a longitudinal manner. The combined dataset contains 442 crowdfunding projects with 6494 funding records that occurred between 2010 and 2013.

This research affords several important contributions to e-business research and managerial practice. First, as discussed earlier, this study investigates crowdfunding projects using their advocates – an angle that reflects an important trait of crowdfunding on the Internet but has yet to gain due attention – instead of the mechanisms and financial regulations. Through this study, we hope to add another dimension to crowdfunding knowledge by considering the advocates’ attributes rooted in the offline context (i.e., GD) and the online context (i.e., online SC). Second, previous studies on entrepreneurship and business ventures suggest that investors may find it difficult to support geographically distant entrepreneurs due to reduced quality of communication, low sharing of knowledge, and poor capability to monitor entrepreneurs (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). Thus, people are traditionally interested in firms that are created by those they know because a trusting relationship exists or the entrepreneurs are geographically closer to them (Chen et al. 2010; Ragozzino and Reuer 2011). This tradition has resulted in an unequal distribution of funding between developed and less-developed regions, which is a problem that the online crowdfunding platform seeks to address (Agrawal et al. 2013). Has online crowdfunding somewhat addressed the enduring problem of GD (or even changed the paradigm)? This research, which explicitly considers the factor of GD, could answer this question by concretely deliberating on whether projects that are more distant from their advocates are able to economically benefit from higher funding. Third, to measure SC, we meticulously traced the projects' advocates, extracted data on their number of followers (operationalized variable for SC) from the largest microblogging website in China, and integrated this set of data with the data obtained from the crowdfunding platform. This approach departs from many studies on SC, which measures the variable by perceptual survey, and highlights the value of secondary data (Zhao et al. 2014). We are able to provide an alternative, objective operationalization of SC and obtain a wider approximation of SC in a crowdfunding project through the specific focus on advocates' SC. Finally, this work contributes to the novel solutions for using social media analytics on secondary data, as in the focal context of crowdfunding (Lim et al. 2013; Zhao et al. 2014). In particular, many previous studies confined themselves to a single platform but paid little attention to unveiling how impetuses from the external environment such as social media influenced crowdfunding activities in the focal platform (Agrawal et al. 2013; Burtch et al. 2013; Mollick 2014). By integrating and probing the social media data of millions of users, we are capable of understanding how online fundraising is influenced by the small fluctuations stemming from important individuals in social media.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. In Section 2, the theory and related hypotheses are developed. In Section 3, the empirical method for validating the hypotheses is described. The results of hypothesis testing are reported in Section 4. Section 5 outlines and discusses a series of post hoc analyses that were conducted to increase the robustness of our findings. In Section 6, we highlight the contributions to research and practice in detail. Lastly, short, concluding comments are provided in Section 7.

## 2 2 Theory and Hypothesis Development

This section develops our theory on crowdfunding projects and their funding performance based on two attributes of the advocates, namely, their GD and SC. The research model is depicted in Fig. 1. Our thesis states that a wider GD between a project and its advocates (Section 2.1) or a project with advocates with higher SC (Section 2.2) could be advantageous to funding performance. Furthermore, these two advocate attributes could complementarily enhance funding performance (Section 2.3).

## 2.1 2.1 Geographical Distance

Previous venture capital literature found that a greater physical distance between a venture capitalist and an entrepreneur could lower the chance that the venture capitalist will invest in the entrepreneur's firm or project (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). Also known as local bias and home bias (Jääskeläinen and Maula 2014), the detrimental effect of GD on investment can be explained in two ways. First, firms are often colocated in close geographical proximity rather than being scattered (e.g., firms situated in an industrial park or several firms with similar businesses located on the same street), not only to benefit from the economies of scale (Whittington et al. 2009) but also to foster interpersonal ties among the firms (Sorenson and Stuart 2001) and information spillover (Owen-Smith and Powell 2004). Entrepreneurs and their potential investors in close geographical proximity can leverage higher forms of communication, such as face-to-face (Daft and Lengel 1986), to reduce information asymmetry between the two parties (Lin et al. 2013) and to foster trust (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). Second, being geographically near allows investors to observe and monitor the activities of focal entrepreneurs (Lerner 1995).

For example, the capacity to monitor entrepreneurial activities is an important investment consideration of venture capitalists (Ernst-Ludwig Von 1995). In a similar vein, entrepreneurs can more easily mobilize their resources by being physically near (Stuart and Sorenson 2003). The benefits of close geographical proximity may be significantly diminished in online crowdfunding platforms (Petersen and Rajan 2002). Entrepreneurs do not need to be geographically active to use the Internet to perform fundraising projects, appeal for funding from the public, and share information on the progress of the project (Belleflamme et al. 2015). Online discussion boards and embedded social media functions support fundraisers' ability to directly exchange information with potential project advocates (Xu et al. 2012). In particular, various social media, including conventional social networking sites (SNSs) such as Facebook or social platforms embedded with strong mobility such as Twitter or Instagram, have proved to be an effective tool to overcome spatial distance for information exchange (Schwartz and Halegoua 2015). In our context, computer-mediated communication, i.e., online crowdfunding platforms and social media, assists project advocates in obtaining more information on crowdfunding projects, reducing perceived uncertainty (Adjei et al. 2010), and developing interpersonal relationships (Ou et al. 2014).

The deduction so far only indicates that fundraising initiatives are not constrained by GD because of migration from the off-line to online platform. The success of a crowdfunding project, that is, the amount of funding sourced, requires aspects beyond this advantage. Specifically, a crowdfunding project needs to gain sufficient attention and recognition to encourage the public to contribute. Geographical proximity could reduce entrepreneurs' reach for wider and untapped potential. Thus, if entrepreneurs can leverage their advocates' social networks, they could feasibly bridge social circles. Establishing direct and indirect social ties could reduce the effect of local bias (Jääskeläinen and Maula 2014). In addition, gaining attention from a wider pool of people through the advocates' social networks implies that the quality of fundraising projects is recognized in a wider area. Restricting a project to a small social circle, such as people who are likely to have a personal relationship with the entrepreneur, including family and friends (Agrawal et al. 2013), may not be sufficiently convincing to infer project quality.

Hence, we propose the following:

H1: The GD between a crowdfunding project and its advocates is positively related to the funding performance as reflected by the amount of funds solicited.

## 2.2 2.2 Social Capital

The entrepreneurship literature has many studies that consider SC and social network (Casson and Della Giusta 2007; Greve and Salaff 2003). SC, the resources embedded within social contacts of individuals (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998), influences access to information and financial support. Existing attempts to reveal the relationship between social networks and fundraising activities mainly focus on the SC of entrepreneurs. For example, Colombo et al. (2015) compared internal and external social networks and observed that SC in the crowdfunding community (i.e., internal social network) can attract advocates interested in the project. Additionally, Mollick (2014) found that the external network size of entrepreneurs significantly improved the probability of project success on the crowdfunding platform. Regarding peer-to-peer lending websites, Freedman and Jin (2014) found that the social ties of borrowers

were associated with the probability of loan success and interest rates. Findings from these studies suggest that entrepreneurs could solicit more funding by leveraging contacts in their social network. By contrast, the SC of project advocates is largely ignored in the previous literature.

Advocates can tap their social networks to promote and support their projects, and thus increase the chance of funding success. To this end, the amount of SC that can be accumulated online influences the success of the focal crowdfunding campaign. Previous literature indicated that SC could be reflected by an individual's number of followers in social media (Aral et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2014; Zhang 2012; Zheng et al. 2014). For instance, a reseller could leverage the SC embedded in social media, i.e., the fans of its Facebook page, to spread advertising or promotional information to those followers' social circles (Goh et al. 2013). Such a spreading model that goes viral was also found to benefit NPOs. In particular, because of limited resources and capacity, such NPOs had to leverage their stakeholders' SC in Twitter for sharing information and building dialogic relationship (Lovejoy and Saxton 2012). Such evidence indicates that social media is not only a broadcasting tool for publishing information but also a valuable means of diffusing information through penetrating the multilevel social ties. To this end, in our context, the followers of a project advocate not only serve as message receivers but also as extended bridging nodes connecting them to other social circles. In particular, three benefits are often cited, namely, (1) referrals, (2) access, and (3) timing (Burt 1992; Sorenson and Stuart 2001). (1) Referrals represent the information associated with a particular group of people and add value to the information (Trusov et al. 2009). When project advocates with high

SC distribute information on a crowdfunding project, followers are more likely to perceive that the information has high credibility and will reduce negative concerns about the project. Giving credit to the information sent by friends on social media is human nature. At the same time, project advocates with high SC will benefit from a higher probability of persuading more people to invest. (2) Access refers to the way people obtain valuable information (Borgatti and Cross 2003). People gather together because of common interests, and such behavior is denoted by the concept of homophily (Aral et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2014). The followers of an individual who is also an advocate of a crowdfunding project are more likely to share similar viewpoints on the project. Therefore, they are more likely to be receptive to fundraising requests. (3) Timing refers to information that can be quickly accessed. Through project advocates with high SC, fundraising requests for a crowdfunding project can be more quickly propagated to a wider pool of people. Such information dissemination speed is instrumental to the success of a fundraising initiative.

In light of the abovementioned arguments, we posit:

H2: SC, as reflected by the number of followers of a crowdfunding project's advocates, is positively related to funding performance as reflected by the amount of funds solicited.

## 2.3 2.3 Moderating Role of GD on SC and Funding Performance

As posited in H2, projects advocated by those with high SC (i.e., high number of followers) are likely to yield higher funding performance. H3 argues that such a relationship is further enhanced when the advocates are at greater GD from the project. Our thesis for this argument states that distant advocates may aid the extended reach of project advertisement through their social

networks that are also probably further from the project's place of registration (i.e., from the entrepreneur's place of residence). In other words, compared with advocates in close geographical proximity to a project, those with greater GD from the project are more likely to reach out to people who are geographically far but are socially linked.

Great GD between project advocates and entrepreneurs implies that the project information has been distributed into a relatively new area, where people are less likely to be familiar with it. Indeed, sociology suggests that the probability of two individuals having a connection declines with geographic distance (Dahl and Sorenson 2007).

Studies have found the existence of spatially mediated knowledge spillovers (Audretsch and Feldman 1996), which indicates that spillovers tend to be localized (Bottazzi and Peri 2003). Furthermore, Ganesan et al. (2005) show that nonlocal knowledge flows lead to a high level of innovation because great GD is related to new attention, which reflects that information or knowledge flows into a new area where new resources could be used to enhance current activities. This flow would imply that project advocates who are geographically distant have a greater ability to attract attention for fundraising projects; thus, the utility of their SC is enhanced.

In addition to this contribution, the research on knowledge transfer shows that distant locations often possess novel knowledge and creative solutions (Ambos and Ambos 2009; Tallman and Phene 2007). Global crowdfunding projects may contain more novel information for advocates and their followers, which has been identified as an important determinant of the crowdfunding project process. People would like to support crowdfunding projects that have a high level of

novelty, relevance, and comprehensibility (Schulz et al. 2015). Thus, an increase in distance may fulfill such needs of followers of advocates, and hence, they are more likely to invest. Thus, we posit:

H3: GD enhances the relationship between crowdfunding project advocates' SC and funding performance.

## 3 3 Method

As a computational social science study (Chang et al. 2014), we collected our empirical data by integrating project data from Demohour.com and the advocates' social network data from Weibo.com; together they constitute a sizable secondary data. This integration is consistent with the suggestion of “combining data from different sources” (Fan et al. 2015, p. 31) in big data research. Demohour.com is one of the largest crowdfunding platforms in China. As a Kickstarter-like crowdfunding site, Demohour.com supports 10 major categories of fundraising projects, including technology, design, publishing, movies, animation, music, photography, food, games, and others. On this platform, entrepreneurs initiate fundraising projects by creating a page describing a project and then inviting project advocates to fund. If the money pledged to a project is equal to or larger than the target amount, then the fundraising project is successful, and the money is cashed in by the project owner; otherwise, the fundraising project is unsuccessful, and all pledges are void (Colombo et al. 2015). We collected project data from this platform in the winter of 2013. Projects that occurred between 2010 and 2013 were observed. Our dataset contains 442 fundraising projects and 6494 daily fund records. The descriptions of the focal variables are shown in Table 1. Three sets of variables are used, namely, project-related variables entrepreneur-related variables, and focal variables (i.e., independent and dependent variables). Project-related and entrepreneur-related variables capture the information on the 442 projects and the related entrepreneurs (i.e., project fundraisers), whereas the independent and dependent variables are time-variant variables regarding the 6494 daily investment records. Demohour.com encourages their users to link their social media accounts with their crowdfunding account, and Weibo $^{2}$ , as a leading Twitter-like social media in China, was found to dominate such linked social media accounts in our data sample (refer to Fig. 2 for an illustration). In particular, 87.93% of the registered users in Demohour.com were found to link their Weibo accounts as the sole social media identity. A histogram depicting the distribution across the connected social media platforms is shown in Fig. 3. When a connection is made, entrepreneurs and project advocates share the crowdfunding project information directly on the social media website (i.e., Weibo). For instance, project advocates share about their supported projects with their followers on Weibo.

## 3.1 3.1 Focal Independent and Dependent Variables

The independent and dependent variables are measured based on information about timing and funding of each project collected on a daily basis. The panel data are captured by recording the daily fund records of the projects. Time is counted as the number of days since the fundraising projects were initiated.

Geographical Distance. As the explanatory variable, GD depicts the distances between project advocates and fundraising projects. The locations of fundraising projects are accessed from

project information, whereas the locations of project advocates are obtained from their Weibo accounts. A focal project receives funds from n project advocates at the focal date, and m of them link their Weibo accounts with Demohour.com. The GD is calculated by adding the distances between project location and the location of the m project advocates:

$$
G D _ {j t} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} D i s _ {i j t},
$$

where $Dis_{ijt}$ denotes the distance between the $i^{th}$ project advocate for focal project $j$ on day $t$ . Social Capital. In this study, SC is operationalized as the number of followers of an advocate in the social network (Hofer and Aubert 2013; Li et al. 2015; Zheng et al. 2014). Similar to GD, the number of followers is measured using the project advocates' Weibo information. Similar to the calculation of GD, that of the number of followers of $m$ project advocates takes the sum as the indicator of the number of followers for a project on a focal day:

$$
S C _ {j t} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} F o l l o w e r _ {i j t},
$$

where $Follower_{ijt}$ denotes the number of followers of the $i^{th}$ project advocate for focal project j on day t.

Funding Amount. As the dependent variable, funding amount describes the amount of money that the focal project receives on the focal day. The control variable – fund amount from non-Weibo project advocates – is calculated because project advocates are classified into two groups, namely, the groups with and without Weibo information. This variable measures the amount of funding received by the focal project on the focal day from project advocates without a Weibo account.

## 3.2 3.2 Project-related and Entrepreneur-related Variables

We controlled for variations across projects as well as the entrepreneurs who initiated the projects. The project-related variables that are controlled for include the following: (1) the target budget, that is, the amount of money that a fundraising project seeks to collect; (2) the project description, that is, word count, video count, and image count; and (3) the project category. The entrepreneur-related variables include gender of entrepreneur, platform (Demohour.com)

follower number, Weibo follower number, and valuable follower number. The platform follower number, Weibo follower number, and valuable follower number represent the entrepreneur's own social network. The platform follower number shows the number of followers of an entrepreneur on Demohour.com, whereas the Weibo follower number counts the number of followers of an entrepreneur on Weibo. The valuable follower number evaluates the number of followers of the focal entrepreneurs who have funded at least one project on Demohour.com.

Descriptive statistics are given in Table 2, and the correlations between variables are shown in Table 3. The table shows that, on average, the fundraising projects attempt to reach CNY 16,432, while they receive CNY 503 each day. The average aggregated GD $^{3}$ is 3451 kilometers, and the average SC (represented by follower number) is 7881.

## 4 4 Data Analysis

The primary objective of this study is to examine whether two advocate factors, namely, GD and SC, influence the funding secured by crowdfunding projects. The base equation can be written as

## follows:

$$
y _ {j t} = \beta_ {1} G D _ {j t - 1} + \beta_ {2} S C _ {j t - 1} + \beta_ {3} G D _ {j t - 1} \times S C _ {j t - 1} + \gamma_ {1} X _ {j t - 1}
$$

In this model, $y_{jt}$ represents the amount of funding that project j receives during its $t^{th}$ day. The independent variables, $GD_{jt-1}$ and $SC_{jt-1}$ are the GD and SC, respectively, of the focal project j on the $t-1^{th}$ day. $X_{jt-1}$ includes several control variables. First, the cumulative amount of funding at day t-1 is controlled. As noted in previous studies on crowdfunding (Kim and Viswanathan 2014), the herding effect in crowdfunding platforms may influence later investment. This variable shows that previous advocates' collective evaluations of projects are an indicator of herding momentum. Second, the amount of investment from non-Weibo advocates at the $t-1^{th}$ day is also controlled. Some of the advocates are users of the social networking website, whereas others are not. Apparently, advocates without Weibo accounts neither help to distribute the information of the crowdfunding projects nor promote the projects in the social media platform. Our model controls for this variable. Third, another concern is found in the existence of correlated unobservables that lead to the dependency of advocates across time. As time passes, an increasing number of people may know of the crowdfunding platform and invest in projects. We include the date of funding as another control variable to mitigate this effect. Fixed effect models will be used to capture unobserved heterogeneity across projects. Random effect models will also be used to check the reliability of our findings. The results of the fixed effect models are shown in Table 4, and the results of the random effect models are depicted in Table 5.

In each case, a baseline model including only control variables is estimated. In Model 1, only the control variables are included in the analysis. Subsequently, the independent variables are added to the second set of analyses. In Model 2, the effect of the independent variables is estimated with 1-day lag effect. In Models 3–5, the control variables are also considered in the analysis with different time lags. Specifically, Model 4 tests the effect of GD and SC with a 2-day lag effect, whereas Model 5 tests their effects with a 3-day lag effect. Finally, the interaction term is further included in the third set of analyses, as indicated in Models 6–8.

Hypothesis 1 posits that the GD of project advocates improves funding performance. We thus expect the path coefficient of GD to be positive and statistically significant. We find support for Hypothesis 1. In the fixed effect models (Models 3–5 in Table 4), the GD significantly improves the amount of funding received 1 day later ( $\beta = 0.063, p < 0.001$ ), 2 days later ( $\beta = 0.049, p < 0.01$ ), and 3 days later ( $\beta = 0.034, p < 0.05$ ). Similar findings are found in the random effect models, wherein the attributes of projects and project leaders are also considered. As can be seen in Models 3–5 in Table 5, the GD consistently improves the amount of funding ( $\beta = 0.054, p < 0.001; \beta = 0.043, p < 0.01; \beta = 0.029, p < 0.05$ ).

Hypothesis 2 suggests that the SC of project advocates improves funding performance. We thus expect the path coefficient of SC to be positive and statistically significant. We find support for this hypothesis when predicting the amount of received funding. In the fixed effect models (Models 3–5 in Table 4), SC significantly improves the dependent variable (i.e., received funding) 1 day later ( $\beta = 0.075, p < 0.001$ ), 2 days later ( $\beta = 0.058, p < 0.001$ ), and 3 days later ( $\beta = 0.044, p < 0.01$ ). Analysis of the random effect models shows similar findings. As shown in Models 3–5 in Table 5, SC has a positive and significant relationship with the amount of funding ( $\beta = 0.080, p < 0.001; \beta = 0.065, p < 0.001; \beta = 0.056, p < 0.001$ ).

Hypothesis 3 proposes the moderation effect between GD and SC. We find strong support for this hypothesis asserting that GD amplifies the effect of SC on funding amount. The interaction term of GD and SC is created and then included in the analysis model. In the fixed effect models (Models 6–8 in Table 4), the interaction term significantly improves the dependent variable (i.e., received funding) 1 day later ( $\beta = 0.040, p < 0.001$ ), 2 days later ( $\beta = 0.030, p < 0.001$ ), and 3 days later ( $\beta = 0.023, p < 0.01$ ). In the random effect models (Models 6–8 in Table 5), the interaction is also significantly related to funding amount ( $\beta = 0.038, p < 0.01; \beta = 0.029, p < 0.01; \beta = 0.022, p < 0.001$ ). The simple slope analysis in Fig. 4 shows that when GD is high, the effect of SC on competitive advantage is stronger.

## 5 5 Robustness Check

Post hoc robustness checks of the findings obtained above were also conducted.

First, the funding number was used to replace the amount of funding as the dependent variable. Funding number counts the number of project advocates that fund the focal project on a focal day. A negative binomial estimation provides a better fit for data count because the dependent variable is a counted value. The results are shown in Table 6. The results did not change qualitatively from the original model specifications, as we also found support for all the three hypotheses.

Second, we replace the dependent variable with fund amount purely from Weibo users, considering that those advocates with Weibo accounts have a higher likelihood to be influenced by our suggested factors. Thus, we reestimate the impact of GD and SC on the amount of a project's funding from Weibo users with fixed effect models. In this regard, the cumulative fund amount from Weibo users is also controlled in the models. As reported in Table 7, the proposed hypotheses are supported by the analysis results. In particular, it is found that GD, SC, and the interaction term also have positive impact on the fund amount from Weibo users, which is consistent with the results shown in the main analysis.

Third, as our research was contextualized in China, GD may be potentially biased because of unbalanced regional economic development. In China, people who are located far from each other may still feel mutually close when their cities have a similar economic level. One example is that people from Beijing and Shanghai may feel socially close to each other because these two cities are regarded as the most advanced cities in Mainland China. In this regard, the GD between the fundraiser and the advocate may not regulate the fundraising activities as the two parties, i.e., fundraiser and advocate, are likely to be socially close, which will challenge our results. To reconcile such concern and better verification of the role of GD in our research context, we use the variable representing the touted “social distance” to replace the GD as a robustness check. The following steps are adopted to estimate the social distance among advocates. First, the locations of advocates are classified into three levels (i.e., high, middle, and low) based on the human development index (HDI) $^{4}$ , which evaluates the development level of cities. Second, we used the Blau index (Blau 1977) to measure the social distance among advocates. The Blau index is defined as:

$$
B I _ {j t} = 1 - \sum_ {w = 1} ^ {S} p _ {j t w} ^ {2}
$$

where $p_{jtw}$ is a/the proportion of advocates' HDI level (high HDI, middle HDI, or low HDI) in level w and s is the number of levels (i.e., s = 3), j denotes the $j^{th}$ project, and t denotes day t. The Blau index is widely used for measuring diversity in category heterogeneity studies (Wahid 2012 Harrison and Klein 2007). The Blau index ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 shows advocates are from the same HDI level, while 1 indicates people are from diverse levels. In particular, we estimate whether social distance can well represent the GD in our research context. The regression result shows that social distance significantly influences the GD (Coef. = 14248.65, Std. Err = 580.83, and p value < 0.001).

We run two sets of analyses with this new variable as reported in Tables 8 and 9. The GD is replaced with social distance in these models. The dependent variable in Table 8 is the fund amount, while the dependent variable in Table 9 is the fund amount from Weibo users. The results are consistent with those in the original model specifications, as we also found support for all the three hypotheses. A slight difference is found in the results estimated with 3-day lag. A plausible explanation is that the crowdfunding projects have to gain popularity within a relatively short term. Fundraising activities before 3 days may have limited impact on the crowdfunding performance.

Taken together, these results suggest that our proposed hypotheses are supported by the empirical findings. Getting advocates who are from large socially/geographically distant areas and have strong SC contribute to improving the fund performance of crowdfunding projects.

## 6 6 Discussion

This study presents a holistic investigation of the impact of advocates' GD and SC on the performance of Internet fundraising, that is, crowdfunding. By using a large-scale dataset composed of two data sources in China, we unveil several interesting findings.
First, different from the conventional viewpoints accentuating the beneficial role of geographical proximity on investment appeals, our findings revealed that such detrimental constraints on fundraising resulting from GD gradually vanished with the emergence of Internet services, that is, the crowdfunding mechanism and SNSs. Conversely, GD can still improve funding performance of crowdfunding projects because of the expansion of individual social circles.

Second, motivated by the literature on social media, this study is the first empirical study to examine ways to leverage an individual advocate's SC from his/her online contacts to acquire financial capital. It was reported that the chance of success of crowdfunding campaigns could be increased by 30% by linking the crowdfunding campaign to social media platforms such as Facebook or LinkedIn $^{5}$ . In addition, several rules of thumb, such as the ratio (20%) of shared crowdfunding information to overall content in social media or the suggested number of bloggers (15–25) for advertising crowdfunding campaigns, were given to show that social media can be leveraged as a tool for promoting a crowdfunding project $^{6}$ . Nevertheless, little empirical evidence is found in the current literature to articulate how social media empowers crowdfunding activities. To this end, this study serves as a pioneering work unveiling such underlying mechanisms. Our findings offer results consistent with those of previous studies arguing that

online SC could be regarded as an important resource conducive to task accomplishment, such as a marketing campaign (in e-commerce research) or knowledge sharing (in knowledge management research; Goh et al. 2013; Yates and Paquette 2011).

Third, the moderating effect of the two abovementioned constructs, namely, GD and SC, was also revealed, suggesting that the initiators of crowdfunding projects manage to attract more distant advocates without the loss of SC. That is, the two variables are complementary in nature. Our study also contributes to existing social network analytics, which are regarded as an important part of big data analytics (Chen et al. 2012). Our study enriches network analytics by considering more attributes of the nodes in the network. We investigate the impact of two characteristics of advocates, who serve as the nodes in the funding network, namely their geographic distance and their SC. SC represents an online feature of the nodes (i.e., advocates); geographic distance shows an off-line feature of the nodes. This extension shows that offline attributes in a social network could help to develop complete understanding of online activities (Tinati et al. 2014).

## 6.1 6.1 Contributions to Theory

By characterizing the features of crowdfunding, this study affords several important contributions to reconcile the conventional dilemmas in entrepreneurship, especially early-stage fundraising performance. By contextualizing crowdfunding, we can reinterpret the influence of geographic distance and SC on fundraising performance from three facets.

First, previous studies indicated that the GD between project advocates and entrepreneurs would constrain investment because of the deficient exchange of information (Stuart and Sorenson

2003). Investors are more willing to support geographically adjacent entrepreneurs for various reasons such as benefits from knowledge spillover (Owen-Smith and Powell 2004), richness of interpersonal communication (Daft and Lengel 1986), and low post-investment monitoring cost (Lerner 1995). However, our results indicate that crowdfunding could effectively remove such geographical constraints on fundraising by leveraging remotely located advocates. To this end, project advocates do financially support geographically distant entrepreneurial projects. This may result from the mitigation of geographically local bias. In particular, social circles can be expanded by connecting indirect social ties to direct ones (Jääskeläinen and Maula 2014), which was found to be an effective way to remove the local bias. To this end, projects with more geographically distant advocates will spread to a wider pool of people, which is conducive to the fundraising performance of a focal project.

Second, previous studies have unveiled that the SC accumulated from social media, such as microblog followers (e.g., Twitter) or fans of an SNS (e.g., Facebook page), was very important for online activities such as promoting digital marketing campaigns (Goh et al. 2013) or online knowledge sharing (Yates and Paquette 2011). Even so, leveraging the capital residing in social media to accumulate financial capital for entrepreneurial activities is still rarely prevalent in current studies. By drawing on the findings from both offline fundraising and social media studies (Aral et al. 2009; Burt 1992; Sorenson and Stuart 2001; Wang et al. 2014; Zhang 2012; Zheng et al. 2014), our empirical results indicate that SC accumulated from an advocate's social media could promote the success of a crowdfunding project. Our finding extended the points in previous literature that only accentuated the role of the number of advocates in crowdfunding

performance (Mollick 2014). An alternative explanation is that the information about a project backed by those possessing stronger SC can be diffused to more people, which increases the probability of attracting more advocates. In other words, SC was converted to financial capital in terms of information diffusion.

Third, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first empirical study to examine the role of the interaction between GD and SC on crowdfunding dynamics. The evidence supports our third hypothesis stating that GD will enhance the causality between SC and fundraising performance. The spillover effect is more prone to localized areas because of spatially mediated knowledge spillover. In other words, information will be confined to a certain area within the geographical proximity between advocates and entrepreneurs, which is not conducive to fundraising from the touted crowds. Thus, a large distance between advocates and entrepreneurs could effectively restrain the adverse effect on fundraising performance caused by the spillover effect. In addition, individuals tend to back those projects novel to them, and geographically distant projects can provide such novelty. Thus, with the same level of SC, advocates can help their crowdfunding projects gain more financial support when such projects are geographically distant.

## 6.2 6.2 Contributions to Practice

In addition to the theoretical contributions, salient contributions to practice are provided by the findings of this study. For those entrepreneurs seeking financial support from crowdfunding, two suggestions can be drawn from our findings. First, the SC of entrepreneurs is crucial. In particular, entrepreneurs' contacts in social media would be the most important advocates for their projects. In addition, such interpersonal connection will cause a snowball effect through

social media. This phenomenon is very akin to word-of-mouth advertising. A point to note here is that we do not suggest that practitioners be selective in the choice of advocates, but they should be aware of who among the set of advocates are more advantageous for the fundraising expedition.

Second, entrepreneurs should consider the role of geographical proximity. Individuals are found to be more willing to back those entrepreneurs from geographically distant areas. To this end, we suggest that entrepreneurs should use the information asymmetry caused by GD to promote their novel ideas for fundraising.

Our findings also offer several salient suggestions for improving the overall quality of service on crowdfunding platforms. For example, considering the vital role of social media in promoting crowdfunding, service operators should and could design certain reward policies to encourage individuals to use their social media to advertise projects. Boosting fundraising efficiency is conducive to attracting more potential projects, which in turn will increase their market share. In addition, the operators could design a recommendation mechanism based on GD. Different from the conventional wisdom stating that nearby services or products are always recommended to individuals, especially in mobile apps, our results indicate that individuals preferred geographically distant projects. Thus, our findings could help operators select sound assessment criteria for designing the recommendation agents for crowdfunding services. Finally, crowdfunding initiators are encouraged to spread their information to certain social media groups that should not only have members with considerable SC but also serve geographically distant communities or areas, though all other advocates should not be ignored.

## 6.3 6.3 Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research

This study can be regarded as a nascent empirical investigation that unveils the underlying factors influencing individual behaviors in online crowdfunding platforms, a marketplace that is still evolving. To this end, new and interesting user behaviors may be triggered by some emerging mechanisms in crowdfunding services, which have not yet been examined in this study. Such limitations will provide great opportunities for future investigations.

First, in reward-based crowdfunding, individuals are offered various reward options based on extent of investment. To this end, the nature of such rewards may influence project advocates' psychological impetus for placing or forgoing their investment. Unfortunately, our panel data fall short of providing us the opportunity to understand the psychological effects. Future research is recommended to apply pluralist research methods to investigate the project advocates' behaviors from a more comprehensive perspective. In addition, the extent of advocates' effort to share information with their online social circles should be investigated, which cannot be precisely measured because of the limitations of our dataset.

Second, previous studies on business ventures argued that the reputations of entrepreneurs would significantly influence the investment decision of project advocates (Shane and Cable 2002). However, in consideration of the fact that limited knowledge about the subsequent development of those backed projects can be found because crowdfunding is fairly recent, the impact of entrepreneurs' reputations on investors' decisions cannot be answered in this study. With the further growth of crowdfunding, future scholars are encouraged to study the role of reputations on fundraising.

Lastly, future studies are also encouraged to further explore the cultural factors influencing individual behaviors, because previous studies on entrepreneurship and finance indicated that venture capitalists with distinct cultural backgrounds would make significantly different investment decisions (Li and Zahra 2012). For instance, Asian venture capitalists pay more attention to the private relationship, or Guanxi, in lieu of objective facts such as accounting information when making investment decisions (Bruton and Ahlstrom 2003). Thus, multiple datasets should be collected for conducting bicultural crowdfunding studies.

## 7 7 Conclusion

Complementing previous studies that focused on the mechanisms or financial regulations of crowdfunding platforms or projects, this study investigated the impact of advocates' characteristics on fundraising performance. In particular, two explicit and significant dimensions representing offline and online contexts, namely, GD and online SC were examined to understand fundraising performance in a crowdfunding platform. Considering that crowdfunding offers a distinctively alternative option for entrepreneurs to raise funds, great opportunities are offered for future work. We hope that the findings on crowdfunding from this study can provide salient insights for both researchers and practitioners engaging in Internet financial investment.
