# Adoption of open source software in organizations: A socio-cognitive perspective

## a b s t r a c t

Open source software (OSS) is an important trend in the information technology adoption landscape. It has received considerable attention in the scientific literature, but mostly in the professional press. In fact, there is much debate over its actual commercial and organizational value. Since the public discourse accompanying an IT may influence adoption decisions, it is important to consider IT specialists’ perceptions of the discourse on OSS. In this study, we investigated the relationship between IT specialists’ profiles, IT specialists’ reception of the public discourse on OSS, and their organizations’ receptivity to OSS. Drawing on the socio-cognitive perspective of IT innovation adoption and the organizing vision theory, a survey of 271 IT specialists was conducted to examine these issues. Our results indicate that a majority of IT specialists in our sample are rather neutral about the OSS concept conveyed in the public discourse. However, our sample also comprises respondents with more extreme perceptions who can be classified as either supporters or detractors. Our results indicate that detractors have more years of experience but have been less exposed to OSS than supporters, and that IT specialists’ perceptions of the OSS concept are positively associated with their organizations’ openness to OSS adoption and, to a lesser extent, with the existence of an organizational policy that favors OSS adoption. Altogether, our findings provide strong support for the organizing vision theory and the idea that the popularity of an IT innovation concept favors the adoption of the material IT innovation in organizations. By providing a preliminary test of a nomological network of IT specialists’ perceptions of the OSS concept, our study offers insights as to why organizations may or may not take OSS into account in their software procurement decisions.

## 1. Introduction

Software procurement decisions are not as simple as make-or-buy anymore. Organizations now have numerous procurement options to choose from such as renting software applications from application service providers or obtaining open source software (OSS) from open source communities (Campbell-Kelly, 2008). OSS, which is the topic of this study, is a contemporary phenomenon and an important trend in the information technology (IT) adoption landscape (Fitzgerald, 2006;

Karahanna and Watson, 2006). Without a doubt, OSS represents a growing share of the software market and is increasingly being considered alongside traditional, commercial software (Driver, 2010).

According to many specialists (e.g., Fitzgerald, 2006; Oh and Jeon, 2007), OSS will continue to play a leading role in a wave of profound, radical change that is sweeping through the IT world. However, the rate of OSS adoption by organizations is still low in many regions, particularly in North America (Actuate, 2008). Despite the promises of this relatively new software procurement mode, many organizations have not yet developed policies and procedures for adopting OSS and a call for research on specific aspects that predispose organizations toward leading or lagging the adoption curve has been made by Niederman et al. (2006b). We thus concur that OSS represents an emerging business reality that deserves more attention from researchers (Boudreau et al., 2008; Fitzgerald, 2006; Olive, 2008).

In this paper, we define OSS as software developed in a public collaborative manner, for any application (e.g., operating system, word processor, digital imaging system), and available under a license allowing free source code accessibility, reuse, modification and redistribution by users (Fontana, 2010; Wang, 2009). OSS is often used interchangeably with other designations such as free software and free open-source software (FOSS). For clarity purposes, it is widely acknowledged that FOSS is an inclusive term that covers both free software and OSS and that despite describing comparable software development models, free software and OSS have differing underlying cultures and philosophies. Indeed, free software focuses on the philosophical freedom it gives to user communities, whereas open source software focuses on the perceived strengths of its peer-to-peer development model (Feller et al., 2005).

OSS is not like other new IT and is not just a new IT (Lee and Cole, 2003; von Hippel and von Krogh, 2003; Sawhney and Prandelli, 2000). OSS represents a phenomenon that is mind bending for many IT specialists for two main reasons, namely, the type of licensing and the type of development process that transform the rules of software procurement. First, OSS comes with a new type of license governing how the software can be used. The license associated with OSS guarantees rights to users and protects the interests of its developers who believe that their software should be freely available to all (Wybo and Bernier, 2007). In contrast, the goal of proprietary software licenses is to protect the intellectual property and financial interests of software vendors (Nelson et al., 2006; Wybo and Bernier, 2007). The main feature of the license associated with OSS is that users have the right to use the software as they wish – for example, they can access the source code and can legally copy the software –, while the main feature of a proprietary software license is that it must remain the exclusive property of its designer (de Laat, 2005). A software license describes how its author believes rights and duties should be distributed between himself and users (Douglas, 2011). IT specialists are used to the terms of proprietary software licenses and understand how rights and duties are distributed between their organization and the proprietary software authors. This is not yet the case with OSS licenses for many IT specialists, which creates uncertainty towards OSS adoption. Even lawyers struggle to understand OSS licensing and to advise organizations about its adoption (Fontana, 2010).

Second, as OSS licenses grant the right to access the source code, this has created some typical OSS development practices (von Hippel and von Krogh, 2003). In fact, OSS benefit from the collaborative effort of a large number of developers, dispersed across organizational and geographical boundaries, forming Internet-based development communities (Lee and Cole, 2003; Sawhney and Prandelli, 2000; von Hippel and von Krogh, 2003). These communities consist of freelance developers who volunteer and/or developers working in organizations that pay them to contribute to OSS projects (Lindman et al., 2011; Onetti and Verma, 2009). This type of software development changes the way organizations acquire software and related services such as training and support. This makes OSS different from any other new IT. In fact, OSS can be downloaded over the Internet and used at no charge in any organization (Lundell et al., 2011). OSS based on large development communities are usually of good quality and accompanied by professional service offerings (Lundell et al., 2011; Ven et al., 2008). However, OSS service organizations are sometimes too small to service large customers (Feller et al., 2008). Less popular OSS based on smaller and less active communities may be of poor quality and often suffer from a lack of professional service offerings (Chengalur-Smith et al., 2010; Lundell et al., 2011). In such cases, the IT department must rely on in-house IT specialists to support the OSS in their organization (Lundell et al., 2011). Most IT specialists are used to a turnkey software acquisition process that systematically involves a vendor to which the organization pays license fees for the right to use the software for which the quality is often a given. They are also used to finding professional services easily for all the software found in the organization. OSS thus requires unprecedented efforts on their part in terms of evaluation of the impacts from the OSS development and service networks (Ebart, 2008; Lundell et al., 2011).

Swanson and Ramiller (1997) coined the term ‘‘organizing vision’’ to encompass all information and opinions available in the environment about a new technology and its applications within organizations. The existence of an organizing vision for OSS has recently been acknowledged by researchers in the IS field (i.e., Lindman et al., 2010; Marsan and Paré, 2008; Wang, 2009; Wang and Swanson, 2008). The number of available OSS applications grows dramatically from year to year (Fitzgerald, 2006), and many researchers suggested that OSS can be of strategic value to firms by allowing faster adoption of technology, increased innovation, reduced costs, and reduced risk of vendor lock-in (Bonaccorsi and Rossi, 2006; Hedgebeth, 2007; Lundell et al., 2011; Morgan and Finnegan, 2005; Wurster et al., 2011). Despite the rising enthusiasm, such suggestions often come with doubts (Gartner Group, 2005; Ven et al., 2008). OSS covers a broad and complex domain with a wide variety of stakeholders, impacts, influences, and changing conditions (Niederman et al., 2006a; Al Marzouq et al., 2005; Fitzgerald and Feller, 2001; Ven et al., 2008). Hence, the lack of reliable information on the concept of OSS has been cited as a major barrier to its adoption in organizations (e.g., Paré et al., 2009; Ven et al., 2008).

IT specialists are an important target audience for the OSS’s OV, since historically most OSSs have been used for back-end rather than front-end systems (Behlendorf, 1999; Evans, 2002). In general, OSS is seen as better suited to technical systems than business applications (Brydon and Vining, 2008), even though this situation is changing, as OSS is increasingly developed and selected for business applications (Wurster et al., 2011). IT specialists are thus among the best individuals to identify and interpret the concept of OSS, but they may nevertheless find this task challenging. Indeed, when an IT differs markedly from what is generally known and accepted in the profession, IT specialists need to expend great efforts in order to ascribe meaning – Gioia and Thomas (1996) and Weick (1993) describe this as ‘‘sensemaking’’ – to the new technology (Swanson, 2010). OSS is no different since about half of IT specialists see themselves as new to this type of software, the adoption of which is hindered by the general lack of internal and external expertise (Gartner Group, 2006; Goode, 2005; Paré et al., 2009).

In short OSS represents a complex and radically new phenomenon that creates a lot of confusion and conflicting claims in the public discourse. There is unusually high uncertainty about the very criteria on which to base OSS adoption decisions (Lundell et al., 2011; Ven et al., 2008). The concept of OSS thus definitely presents an unprecedented set of challenges for IT specialists (Karahanna and Watson, 2006). The success of IT specialists in organizations and their role in IT investment decisions is closely tied to their understanding of emerging phenomena such as OSS (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997; Yang and Ashley, 2006). In turn, such decisions may have a significant impact on the IT function’s and, ultimately, the organization’s overall success and strategic position (Hedgebeth, 2007; Li, 2009; Lim et al., 2012; Lundell et al., 2011; Wurster et al., 2011). With the proliferation of OSS, some observers urge organizations to adopt formal policies regarding the adoption of this type of software (e.g., Wurster, 2008; Wurster et al., 2011).

In light of the above, the objectives of the present study are threefold: 1) to investigate how IT specialists perceive the OSS’s OV; 2) to examine if these perceptions differ across IT specialists; and 3) to explore the link between IT specialists perceptions and their organizations’ receptivity to OSS adoption. Our study thus offers insights as to why organizations may or may not seize the strategic and operational opportunities commonly associated with OSS adoption. The next section of this paper introduces the conceptual foundations and the study’s underlying framework. This is followed by a presentation of our research methodology and results, and by a discussion of our study’s contributions for both practice and research.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. IT innovation adoption

The majority of prior research on IT innovation adoption has been done within what Fichman (2004) calls the dominant paradigm. According to this author, this paradigm is ‘‘typified by the desire to explain innovation using economic-rationalistic models, whereby organizations that have greater innovation-related needs and abilities are expected to exhibit a greater quantity of innovation’’ (p. 315). The adoption of OSS in organizations has also been examined in accordance with this dominant view. For instance, Bonaccorsi and Rossi (2006) suggested that firms take part in the OSS movement mainly for economic and technological reasons. For their part, Ven and Verelst (2008) found cost and reliability to be the most frequently cited reasons by firms for adopting OSS. As a final example, West and Dedrick (2005) observed that organizations OSS adoption decisions are made on pragmatic grounds, i.e., based on characteristics of the software.

The economic-rationalistic perspective has yielded several insights on how potential IT adopters can more effectively evaluate innovations and manage the process of assimilating them. It is however reaching the “point of diminishing returns" in providing additional opportunities for highly influential research, and, hence, future work stepping outside this paradigm is required (Fichman, 2004). In line with Fichman’s call for a more original and integrative approach to the study of IT adoption, Wang (2009) introduced two theoretical perspectives that are concerned with the factors and processes in the social environment of the focal innovating organizations. First, empirical studies from the socio-relational perspective have investigated the influence of interorganizational relationships on firms’ innovation behaviors. For example, Strang and Macy’s (2001) work on social contagion reveals that innovations are more likely to be imitated when earlier adopters are perceived to be more successful, prominent, and similar to the focal organization. Second, the socio-cognitive perspective considers external organizations such as software vendors, consulting firms, and business partners, as important sources that allow focal organizations to understand the innovations they intend to adopt. This perspective, which informs the present study, implies that an IT innovation not only exists in its material form. It is first and foremost considered an innovation concept whose adoption and diffusion can be theorized, legitimized, and mobilized through the public discourse about the innova: tion itself. In this line of thought, Strang and Meyer (1993) introduced the idea of separating the concept (what they call the oretical argument) from the material (what they call practice) when studying diffusion of innovations in organizations:

‘‘The theorization of innovative practices expands their diffusion potential [by] simplify[ing] and abstract[ing] their properties and specify[ing] and explain[ing] the outcomes they produce. Compelling theoretical arguments may diffuse very rapidly themselves.’’ (pp. 497–498)

According to Wang (2009), the notion of innovation concept is applicable to all IT innovations. The claim is that each innovation concept carries an organizing vision about the IT and its applications within organizations (Swanson and Ramiller 1997). Because OSS comprises various types of software applications, the socio-cognitive perspective allows us to examine organizations’ openness to OSS through the public discourse surrounding the innovation concept rather than its perceived properties (material innovation) which vary from one particular OSS application to another. In other words, we argue that the adoption of any specific OSS in an organization first depends on the diffusion of the OSS concept. Last, the socio-cognitive perspective allows us to consider the highly controversial nature of the OSS concept which can only be captured in the the oretical argument of the innovation.

## 2.2. Organizing vision

An organization’s decision to adopt a software or system is usually preceded by a phase where the intent is to reduce the uncertainties surrounding the innovation by acquiring information, mainly through mass media (Rogers, 2003; Swanson, 2002). Such information is a determining factor in the IT adoption decisions in organizations (Jeyaraj et al., 2006). This information is part of what Swanson and Ramiller (1997, 2004) call the OV, which is defined as the discourse of a community made up of different parties – such as consultants and adopters – interested in diffusing an IT innovation and its application in organizations. This community creates and maintains this discourse. An OV serves three main functions: (1) it interprets the IT’s presence and its objective while reducing the associated uncertainties; (2) it supports the IT’s legitimacy by developing the underlying justification; and (3) it encourages entrepreneurial and market forces to support the actual develop ment of the technology (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997).

IS researchers have been using the OV theory since it was first proposed by Swanson and Ramiller (1997). Most of these studies focused on determining what had influenced the emergence and development of various OVs. Empirical evidence shows that various stakeholders, such as vendors and consultants, who have an interest in the diffusion of given IT, invest resources to develop OVs, but that various events (such as the Y2 K problem, which was solved in part by integrated application packages) have also driven the development of OVs (e.g., Currie, 2004; Kaganer et al., 2010; Klecun-Dabrowska and Cornford, 2002; Wang, 2001, 2005; Wang and Ramiller, 2009; Wang and Swanson, 2007, 2008).

## 2.3. Reception of an organizing vision

Ramiller and Swanson (2003) developed and used the concept of the reception of an OV at the individual level, This notion is the cornerstone of the present study. ‘‘Reception of an OV’’ refers to individuals’ opinions or perceptions of the innovation concept (Ramiller and Swanson, 2003). Originally, the OV construct was developed to compare IT managers’ perceptions of several nonspecific IT-based innovations such as computer-aided software engineering (CASE), electronic commerce, and client–server technology. Like these innovations, OSS is more than a particular instantiation of IT; it represents a generic innovation that comprises various IT artifacts and technological families, from applications and platforms to interfaces and standards. The inherent complexity underlying generic innovations such as OSS makes the investigation of the reception of the OV even more relevant.

Fig. 1. Conceptual framework.

As shown in Fig. 1, the reception of an OV has four key dimensions (Ramiller and Swanson, 2003) at the center of our conceptual framework. The first is interpretability, which refers to the degree to which an individual finds representations of the OV in public discourse intelligible and informative. It therefore represents the clarity, coherence and richness of the discourse. Second, plausibility refers to the degree to which an individual finds that representations of the OV in public discourse are free of distortions, misunderstandings, exaggerations and inappropriate assertions. Third, discontinuity refers to the degree to which an individual finds that the OV presents an innovation that requires a conceptual shift (conceptual discontinuity) or poses unprecedented implementation challenges (structural discontinuity). Discontinuity therefore represents the extent to which the OV implies concepts and implementation challenges that are different from those in the OVs associated with other, competing or complementary IT. Finally, importance refers to the degree to which an individual finds the public discourse influential or as having clear value, often simply through the fact that it exists. This dimension has three sub-dimensions: the desirability, acceptability, and attractiveness of the OV.<sup>3</sup> First, desirability represents the degree to which an individual finds that the public discourse demonstrates that the associated IT is desirable for better organizational performance. Second, acceptability refers to the degree to which an individual finds that the public discourse shows that the associated IT is widely accepted by organizations. Last, attractiveness represents the degree to which an individual finds that the public discourse attracts market attention.

Ramiller and Swanson (2003) suggest that, depending on an individual’s perception of each of the abovementioned dimensions, his or her overall reception of a given OV may be negative (i.e., detractor), neutral, or favorable (i.e., supporter). They were able to identify neutrals as well as supporters and detractors for client–server, but it was not possible to identify detractors or supporters for electronic commerce and CASE at survey time. This type of finding is crucial since an OV’s legitimacy is ultimately reflected in how the OV is received by IT specialists and works its way into their practices in organizations (Ramiller and Swanson, 2003). The reception thus becomes the vehicle for the OV’s persuasive power regarding the adoption of the IT with which it is associated. The wider stance towards the OV influences the development of an organization’s own stance toward the innovation and its organizational applications (Ramiller and Swanson, 2003). Identifiable groups of supporters and detractors may play a key role in this. The presence of a large group of supporters is likely to be linked with wide adoption and diffusion of the IT with which it is associated. Conversely, the presence of an influential group of detractors for a particular OV might be linked with limited or no adoption. In sum, supporters or detractors may have the dominant voice, providing leadership for the evolving direction of the community’s view towards the OV (Ramiller and Swanson, 2003). A high level of confrontation between identifiable supporters and detractors may also put a halt to adoption and diffusion in the community (Wang, 2009).

## 2.4. Antecedents and consequences of IT specialists’ reception of OVs

According to Ramiller and Swanson (2003), an OV plays an important role in shaping practitioners’ thinking about the opportunities and threats that may be associated with a given innovation concept, and in motivating the organizational actions towards it. These authors suggest that, depending on an individual’s perception of each of the four dimensions, his or her overall reception of a given OV may be negative, neutral or favorable. This provides a conceptual schema for exploring the role of factors shaping the IT specialists’ opinions associated with the OV of an IT (Ramiller and Swanson, 2003). A key contribution of our study is therefore to explore the factors that influence IT specialists’ perceptions of the public discourse associated with OSS.

In this regard, Bureau (2006) highlighted the importance of considering that individuals with various profiles are likely to perceive an OV differently. As shown in Fig. 1, we took this idea one step further by proposing that IT specialists’ professional profile and prior experience with OSS will influence how they perceive the public discourse about OSS. For one thing, we considered three variables that characterize IT specialists’ position and responsibilities. More precisely, we posit that IT specialists with more vears of IT experience, who actively participate in IT monitoring activities, and who participate in IT investment decisions are more likely to be exposed to OSS's OV and, hence, might be more receptive or critical of the public discourse surrounding OSS than those with less experience, and who do not participate in IT monitoring activities and IT investment decisions. Furthermore, we argue that IT specialists who have had less exposure to or interactions with OSS will be more neutral about the concept of OSS than those who have concrete experience with OSS either through their active participation in the OSS community or in tangible OSS projects in organizations. This is based on Ramiller and Swanson’s (2003) argument that active engagement with an IT may allow individuals to develop their own stance towards this innovation’s OV. Based on Lundell et al.’s (2007) results, it is likely that concrete experience with OSS development communities and tangible OSS projects allow IT specialists to learn about its peculiarities. This learning may help them to better grasp the OV for OSS.

The OV concept also invites attention to its effects on organizations and their members (Ramiller and Swanson, 2003). Research has shown that IT specialists have a certain influence on the organizational adoption of OSS (e.g., Alexy and Henkel, 2007; Dedrick and West, 2008; Ven and Verelst, 2006). In this light, we believe that such influence may happen through their perception of OSS’s OV. In fact, the success of IT specialists is largely dependent upon their knowledge of emerging technol ogies and the choices associated with their introduction in organizations. Consequently. IT specialists are often the first to take an interest in OVs within organizations (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997; Yang and Ashley, 2006). It thus appears both important and relevant that the link between IT specialists’ perception of OSS’s OV and their organizations’ receptivity and openness towards OSS adoption be explored. Interestingly, even though OSS represents a key ingredient of the contemporary IT landscape, the topic of organizational adoption of OSS is yet to be deeply or widely investigated (Aksulu and Wade, 2010; Alexy and Henkel, 2007; Hauge et al., 2010). The present study takes a step in that direction by adopting a sociocognitive perspective of IT innovation adoption.

## 3. Methodology

In order to provide answers to our research questions, a cross-sectional survey was conducted. The questionnaire was designed to capture information about IT specialists’ views with regard to the public discourse surrounding OSS (their reception of the OSS’s OV), their professional profile and experience with OSS (antecedents), and the openness and receptivity of their organization to OSS adoption (consequences). The survey was followed by two focus groups in which a total of 40 IT directors and executives discussed our results and improved on our interpretation.

## 3.1. Survey measures

Consistent with the conceptual framework shown in Fig. 1, the survey questions covered 13 variables. The measure for the OSS’ OV reception construct was a combination of Ramiller and Swanson’s (2003) original scale and a few newly-developed items. The four dimensions of the construct, namely importance, interpretability, discontinuity and plausibility, were measured with 9, 9, 6 and 4 items, respectively. In light of Bureau’s (2006) study, we included a series of items that allowed us to draw the professional profile of our respondents, namely the total number of years of IT experience as well as the extent of participation in technology monitoring activities and IT investment decision making within their organization. We also included three items derived from Lundell et al. (2007) and Ven and Verelst (2008) in order to capture IT specialists’ experience with OSS, namely: experience with projects involving OSS, number of projects involving OSS and active participation in the OSS community. In order to investigate the relationship between the reception of OSS’s OV and organizational receptivity to OSS adoption, we integrated items derived from Alexy and Henkel (2007), Dedrick and West (2008), Kendall et al. (2001) and Tung and Rieck (2005), namely: organizational openness towards OSS, existence of an organizational policy about OSS adoption and general orientation of the organizational policy about OSS adoption. The focus of our study is on organizational adoption of OSS for internal use. Thus, we consider OSS adoption in some but not all of the possible forms suggested by Hauge et al. (2010), that is, we only refer to deploying OSS products or integrating OSS components for internal use. Other forms of OSS adoption, i.e., involvement in existing OSS development communities, providing an OSS product, and using OSS development practices, are not considered because they do not necessarily imply organizational adoption of an OSS for internal use. Lastly, demographic data were obtained for gender, age and industry.

## 3.2. Data collection

Following the recommendations of Boudreau et al. (2001) and Straub et al. (2004), the initial version of the questionnaire was pre-tested with six IT specialists. The participants completed the questionnaire and gave their opinion about its instructions, its length, and the phrasing of the items. The pre-test resulted in only minor editing changes. The revised questionnaire, with a cover letter indicating the purpose and importance of the study, was sent to the 1090 Quebec members of the Canadian Information Processing Society (CIPS) in November 2008. This sample was chosen because CIPS membership represents a wide variety of IT jobs and organizations. A total of 271 completed questionnaires were returned within an 8-week period, for a response rate of 25%.

Given the response rate it was deemed necessary to determine how representative the sample was of CIPS members in Quebec. In early 2009, the professional association reported that 78% of its members were men, whereas 88% of respondents in our sample were men (see Table 1). Thirty-five percent of CIPS members in Quebec were in the 40–49 years range (largest group), compared with 39% in our sample. Lastly, 12% of CIPS members in Quebec worked as CIOs (versus 14% in our sample), 34% as IT directors as in our sample, 9% as business process or systems analysts (7% in our sample), and 10% as IT consultants (versus 17% in our sample). These results suggest that our sample is representative of the population under study.

Table 1 also reveals that our respondents had 20 years of IT experience, on average, and worked in various industries such as finance and banking, education, manufacturing, government, retailing, and software. Furthermore, 16% of our respondents actively participated in the OSS community, and 69% had experience with projects involving OSS. The average respondent had worked on five OSS projects. Overall, 69% of our respondents were active in technology monitoring and 55% participated in their organization’s decision-making process on IT investments. Lastly, only 25% (59) of our respondents worked in organizations that had a policy about OSS adoption. Of those, 69% worked in organizations where this policy favored OSS adoption. This result is in line with a recent Georgia Institute of Technology survey which indicates that Canadian organizations rank 28th among 32 countries on openness to OSS adoption.<sup>4</sup>

## 4. Results

## 4.1. How do IT professionals perceive OSS’s OV?

The results for this question, shown in Table 5, indicate that IT specialists perceive the OSS’s OV as both important (desirable) and discontinuous, but relatively less plausible and interpretable. IT specialists in our sample represent a relatively neutral group with all means close to the middle point and small standard deviations. Table 5 also presents the number of respondents that were classified as detractors, neutrals, and supporters for each of the four dimensions. Our results clearly indicate the presence of the three groups in our sample, whereas neutrals represent the largest group on all four dimensions. Interestingly, no significant difference was found based on gender, age and current industry between detractors and supporters for any of the constructs in our study.

## 4.2. Does the perception of the OSS OV differ across IT professionals?

For our second research question, findings in Table 6 first indicate that the number of years of IT experience do not allow us to differentiate between detractors and supporters on any of the four dimensions. Furthermore, we observed that among the IT specialists who participate in IT investment decisions, there were more detractors (i.e., those who find OSS’s OV more discontinuous) than supporters, whereas there were more supporters among IT specialists who do not participate in their organization’s IT investment decisions. Our results also reveal that active participation in the OSS community allows discriminating between detractors and supporters on all four dimensions. As expected, we observed that, among the IT specialists who actively participated in this community, there were more supporters (i.e., individuals who find OSS’s OV more desirable and plausible and less discontinuous and uncertain) than detractors. On the other hand, among IT specialists who did not actively participate in the OSS community, there were more detractors, that is, they found the OSS’s OV to be less desirable and plausible and much more discontinuous and uncertain than their counterparts. Experience with projects involving OSS also helped separate detractors from supporters on the desirability and plausibility dimensions. Among the IT specialists with experience in such projects, there were more supporters (i.e., individuals who find OSS’s OV more desirable and plausible) than detractors. Lastly, the number of projects involving OSS allowed us to differentiate between detractors and supporters on the desirability, interpretability, and plausibility dimensions, as supporters had participated in more pro jects of this type than detractors.

## 4.3. Are IT specialists’ perceptions of OSS’s public discourse linked to their organizations’ openness to OSS?

The results associated with our last research question are presented in Table 7. First, the four dimensions associated with the reception of OSS’s OV allowed us to differentiate organizations with much and little openness towards OSS adoption. Indeed, desirability and plausibility scores were significantly higher for respondents from organizations that were more open to OSS compared to those in organizations that were less open to adopting this type of software. As expected, we observed the opposite result for the interpretability (uncertainty) and discontinuity dimensions. Our results also indicate that respondents working in organizations that have a policy about OSS adoption scored higher on the desirability dimension than those from organizations without an OSS policy. Finally, respondents from organizations where a policy in favor of OSS adoption existed found the reception of the OSS OV more desirable and, to some extent, more interpretable and plausible than respondents from organizations where the policy opposed the adoption of OSS.

## 4.4. Summary of key findings

Fig. 2 synthesizes our key findings. In short, we observed that the desirability of OSS’s OV is the only dimension that is associated with all three antecedents characterizing IT specialists’ experience with OSS as well as the three consequences characterizing organizational receptivity to OSS adoption. This is also the dimension that has the most significant links in our research model. The discontinuity of OSS’s OV is the dimension with the smallest number of significant relations in our model. The other dimensions, namely plausibility and interpretability, have a relatively moderate significance in the proposed research model.

In line with our second objective, Fig. 2 shows that IT specialists’ perception of the public discourse on OSS is much less tied to characteristics of their professional profile than to their experience with OSS. More specifically, active participation in the OSS community is crucial. Our results indicate that there are more detractors among IT specialists who do not participate in the OSS community. What’s more, detractors in our sample had participated in fewer projects involving OSS than supporters. Detractors first perceive the public discourse associated with OSS as less desirable and plausible than do supporters. In our focus groups, these individuals explained that the public discourse does not demonstrate that OSS can help enhance organizational performance. On the contrary, they hear that it may harm the organization because ‘‘OSS is less reliable,’’ ‘‘there are more security holes in OSS." “it is more difficult to get professional support for OSS when troubles arise" and “the total cost of ownership is way higher for OSS’’ compared to other software. They also explained that OSS is just another ‘‘hype’’ that keeps people talking but that it will probably ‘‘vanish sooner than later’’. For these reasons, they are still skeptical regarding the desirability and plausibility of the public discourse associated with OSS. This is in line with Wang and Swanson (2008) who, among others, recommend that public discourses present more stories of successful adoptions of the associated ITs in order to ease sensemaking about them

Second, detractors in our sample perceive the public discourse associated with OSS as more conceptually challenging and difficult to interpret than do supporters. These results are in line with Lundell et al. (2007) who found active participants in the OSS community to have extended knowledge about the OSS phenomenon. One could argue that taking part in the OSS community and participating in OSS projects results in greater familiarity with the jargon associated with OSS, allowing IT specialists to better understand the related public discourse. Among the participants in our focus groups, those who had participated in projects involving OSS also found the public discourse easier to interpret than those who had not. The participant with the least experience with OSS stated that, for him, the public discourse around the OSS concept was ‘‘very confusing [. . .] in general; everything that is being published [about OSS] is so confusing,’’ (Director, IT department, Insurance firm) while one of the more experienced participants said that there was ‘‘considerable work to be done to demystify OSS for those who have a poor grasp of the OSS phenomenon’’ (CIO, Loyalty Management firm).

Interestingly, our results indicated that detractors in our sample had worked on fewer OSS projects even though they had slightly more years of IT experience than did supporters. One possible explanation may be the conservatism and habits developed by individuals who have spent many years implementing, supporting, and managing proprietary software, exclusively or almost exclusively. Professional experience creates certain habits which may result in a disinterest in alternatives or new approaches or even an aversion to change (Lehmann and Norman, 2005; Sullivan, 2000). One of our focus group participants talked about ‘‘old timers’ paradigms that will have to evolve with the changing world of IT’’ (CIO, Engineering and construction firm). For their part. Paré et al. (2oo9) have shown that the conservative nature of IT decision makers is a barrier to OSS adoption in health care organizations.

In line with our third objective, we observed that IT specialists’ reception of OSS’ OV is associated with their organizations receptivity to OSS adoption. More precisely, our findings indicate that IT specialists who perceive the public discourse about the concept of OSS to be easy to interpret, desirable. less discontinuous and more plausible, work in organizations that are open to OSS adoption, In addition, our results reveal that desirability of the public discourse is associated with the presence of a formal policy on OSS adoption. Furthermore, IT specialists who find the public discourse to be less desirable, interpretable and plausible work for organizations where the existing policy does not support OSS adoption or where there is no such policy. Only 59 of our respondents reported working in an organization where a formal policy about OSS adoption existed. About 30% of these respondents indicated that this policy was against the adoption of OSS. Altogether, these results are aligned with the OV theory which posits that the public discourse about IT innovations can facilitate or hinder their adoption in organizations. They also provide support for the existence of some fundamental roles associated with an OV, namely (1) reducing the uncertainties associated with OSS by offering an interpretation of its intricacies or specificities, and (2) supporting the legitimacy of OSS by proposing a clear rationale or justification. As per Swanson and Ramiller, our findings suggest that, in the case of OSS, IT specialists may be the way by which the public discourse enters organizations and shapes organizational openness to OSS adoption. However, further research efforts are required to investigate the potential institutionalization of IT specialists’ practices and discourse in this area.

Finally, our results reveal that the perception of the degree to which the discourse on OSS demonstrates that it can enhance organizational performance (i.e., the desirability of the concept of OSS) is relatively important. In fact, it is the most significant dimension regarding organizational openness to OSS adoption. This is in line with Kaganer et al. (2010) and Wang and Swanson (2008) who stress the importance of having stories of successful adoptions in the public discourse surrounding IT-based innovations if they are to be more widely acknowledged as viable solutions and diffused among organizations. At a broader level, this finding supports the idea that the popularity of an innovation concept favors the adoption of the material innovation in organizations.

## 5.1. Limitations and implications for research and practice

Our study has the usual limitations associated with exploratory and cross-sectional designs, and some care must be exercised when interpreting these results, as we cannot infer directionality. Our study was conducted in a Canadian setting and, hence our results may not be generalizable to other regions. European countries have been shown to be more open to OSS than North-American regions. Future research is thus required to investigate the impacts of this difference. Furthermore, the measurement instrument for the reception of OSS’s OV still needs refining and further validation. Our results suggest that some items used to measure the dimensions of the construct were inadequate. A few of these items were negative formulations, which, according to Barnett (2000) and Weems and Onwuegbuzie (2001), may adversely affect reliability, Future research should aim at further developing and validating the measurement instrument so as to increase confidence in study results and allow for a reconciliation of results which would contribute to furthering our understanding of the role of professionals’ perception of organizing visions in organizational adoption of new innovations.

Despite its limitations, our study makes key theoretical contributions and opens interesting future research avenues. First, our study is one of the very few attempts to adopt a socio-cognitive approach (Wang, 2009) to explain IT innovation adoption at the organizational level. Put simply. this theoretical perspective posits that an innovation concept can be theorized and legitimized through the community discourse about this innovation. It also conveys the idea that the popularity of the innovation concept aids the adoption of the material innovation. In line with this idea, our data suggest that the large amount of neutral IT specialists about the concept of OSS is related to the low rate of OSS adoption in organizations. Second, this study is the first attempt to situate the IT specialists’ OV reception construct in a nomological network. The results obtained reveal interesting patterns of relationships that warrant deeper investigation to further the contribution to the OV theory. Of utmost importance, future research is needed to explore the direction of the relationships we observed. We found relationships between IT specialists’ reception of the OSS concept and their organizations’ openness to such applications and relationships between IT specialists’ reception of the OSS OV and their professional profile and interactions with OSS. Future studies may explore how and why IT specialists’ professional profile and interactions with OSS influence their reception of OSS’s OV, or vice versa. Ramiller and Swanson (2003) argue that IT specialists’ perception of an innovation concept works its way into their organization and we indeed found a relationship. This raises the question of the discourse of the inevitability (Leonardi, 2008) that can result from detractors or supporters’ ideologies. Longitudinal studies are required to investigate this process. In addition, the existence of organizations with detractors or supporters among their ranks calls for further research efforts, as strong proponents and opponents’ assessment may be biased in favor of or against the concept of OSS. We believe such positioning can be detrimental to organizations by preventing them from objectively assessing potential business solutions.

It also appears important to further examine the large ‘‘neutral’’ group that was found. A question of utmost importance with regard to this group refers to its underlying meaning. What does neutrality imply? Are these IT specialists really ‘‘neutral’’ in the sense that they are neither detractors nor supporters but may join either group on a case basis or are they simply ignorant or not sufficiently knowledgeable to form an opinion about OSS? In other words, did they select the middle point on the Likert scales because of their ambiguity, indifference or ignorance with regard to the concept of OSS? We argue that this is a question of utmost importance both for research and practice, as organizations rely on their IT specialists to make strategic decisions and researchers ought to inform practice. Last, the implications for organizations of having (or not) a formal policy on OSS adoption needs to be examined further. More specifically, we need to know why so few organizations have adopted such a policy. What are the advantages and disadvantages of having a policy on OSS adoption? What motivated the creation of a policy in favor of or against OSS adoption or any other innovation?

Our results have also several practical implications. First, this study contributes to the minimal sustained literature on why some innovation concepts become highly popular and others do not. For one thing, our findings indicate that the lack of convincing arguments in the public discourse on the advantages of adopting OSS prevents greater interest in the concept of OSS among IT specialists and their organizations. The public discourse on OSS therefore needs more stories of successful adoptions. Other researchers have made this recommendation for a variety of IT innovation concepts (e.g., Kaganer et al., 2010; Wang and Swanson, 2008). Practitioners are also in need of a public discourse that is intelligible and informative. Paré et al. (2009) posit that the lack of reliable information on OSS represents a barrier to its adoption in health care organizations. The task of clarifying and adding to the public discourse on OSS needs to be taken up by all parties with an interest in OSS diffusion and, in particular, by the suppliers of OSS products and services. They need to make adjustments to the discourse so that IT specialists can more easily make sense of the concept of OSS and assist their organization in adoption decisions.

This study also makes a significant practical contribution by identifying associations between antecedents and consequences of IT specialists’ perceptions of OSS. Of utmost importance, our results clearly indicate that IT specialists who are exposed to OSS are more likely to be supporters, but, most importantly, they find the OV to be easier to interpret than their counterparts who are not acquainted with OSS. Some organizations allow their IT specialists to actively participate in the OSS community during working hours (Agerfalk and Fitzgerald, 2008; Lundell et al., 2006; von Krogh and Spaeth, 2007). Such organizations can then benefit from OSS applications that are best suited to their needs (Lee and Mirchandani, 2010; von Krogh and Spaeth, 2007). Furthermore, this type of participation can improve the retention rate for IT employees (Guzman and Stanton, 20o9), which, given the shortage of qualified labor in IT. is a significant advantage, Demand will grow for IT specialists who understand the OSS discourse and are skilled at developing, implementing and managing such applications (Agerfalk and Fitzgerald, 2008).

Our study also provides organizations with insights about the professional profile of IT specialists who are either supporters or detractors of the concept of OSS. In the course of their careers, detractors have been less exposed to OSS than those who believe that OSS can be of value. The detractors seem to be characterized by the conservatism and habits they have developed over many years spent implementing, supporting and managing proprietary software. Professional experience leads to habits that are often difficult to change (Lehmann and Norman, 2005; Sullivan, 2000), and conservatism may prevent organizations from even considering OSS (Paré et al., 2009). On the other hand, supporters may be influenced by all the hype surrounding OSS, which, according to Swanson and Ramiller (2004), is often the case with emerging IT. It is crucial for organizations to take these issues into account, as they most likely play an important role in their organizations’ adoption decisions. For instance, IT specialists who participate in organizations' IT investment decisions have a tendency to believe that OSS represents a structural discontinuity, i.e., it poses unprecedented implementation challenges. This implies that they could prevent organizations from identifying opportunities to adopt specific OSS that could be beneficial and even of strategic value to their firm (Hedgebeth, 2007). Our results echoed the European Commission Vice-President for the Digital Agenda who, in a speech at the Open Forum Europe 2010 Summit, said: ‘‘[Many organizations] have found themselves unintentionally locked into proprietary technology for decades. After a certain point that original choice becomes so ingrained that alternatives risk being systematically ignored. no matter what the potential benefits" (Kroes. 2010)

Lastly, our results indicate that very few respondents in our sample worked in organizations where a formal OSS policy had been established. This result is aligned with a recent report by Gartner Group which revealed that more than half of the 547 CIOs from 11 countries surveyed in summer 2010 had adopted OSS as part of their IT strategy, but only one-third of the responding organizations had a formal OSS policy in place (Wurster et al., 2011). Such a policy is nevertheless important to outline why using OSS might be beneficial and the circumstances under which it makes sense to adopt it; the policy sets consistent organizational standards and proper procedures for OSS adoption and use (Cohn and Spiegel, 2011; Wurster et al., 2011). Because it forces stakeholders to consider OSS in IT acquisition decisions, such a policy may be instrumental in realizing better adoption decisions. This is especially true for organizations where there are strong detractors defending their position in IT acquisition decisions. Detractors can contribute to a loss of competitive advantage by decreasing OSS usage in the organization (Hedgebeth, 2007). The existence of an OSS policy also shows investors, partners, customers and other stakeholders that the organization has actively acknowledged and addressed potential risks and benefits associated with this type of software (Cohn and Spiegel, 2011). We further believe that such a policy is equally important for organizations where IT specialists are ‘‘neutral’’ with regard to the OSS OV, as this neutrality may be a symptom of a lack of information or even of a lack of interest in the phenomenon. Regardless of the reason, both cases can be detrimental to the organization that leans on its IT specialists for technological opportunity scanning.

## 6. Conclusion

The increasingly commercial nature of OSS products, underscored by Fitzgerald (2006), is generating growing interest in this type of software. However, the rate of adoption of OSS by organizations is still low, particularly in North America. A better understanding of the OSS concept as an emerging business reality is thus needed to enable researchers to further develop this knowledge base (Boudreau et al., 2008; Fitzgerald, 2006; Olive, 2008; von Krogh and Spaeth, 2007). By drawing on a socio-cognitive perspective of IT innovation adoption in organizations and providing a preliminary test of a nomological network of IT specialists’ perceptions of the concept of OSS, the present study takes a first step in this direction. Our findings offer several insights as to why organizations may or may not take OSS into account in their software procurement decisions and why overlooking OSS may prevent them from achieving operational and strategic advantages. Our findings have also triggered many important questions which warrant further research efforts in this area.
