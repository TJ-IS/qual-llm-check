## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Reporting guidelines Survey Replicability

## A B S T R A C T

As a research method, the survey is known to be subject to various types of biases. Nevertheless, a survey is often the most direct and cost-efective way to solicit people's opinions. We emphasize that, regardless of the data collection method, it is impossible to remove all potential biases in a research setting. Aside from adhering to research design best practice, authors are responsible for providing suficient transparency in the reporting of their work to enhance replicability and to allow others to evaluate the validity of their research. In this paper, we develop a reporting guideline for IS survey research. Researchers conducting IS survey research can use this guide as a checklist when they prepare their manuscripts, and peer reviewers can use it to evaluate research quality and the suficiency of reporting. We hope that similar guidelines can be developed for other IS research methods and that their use and endorsement by research outlets can motivate researchers to pay greater attention to research design and data quality.

## 1. Introduction

No useful results can be drawn from analysis of poor-quality data. However, in general, there is a lack of focus on data quality in IS research, which can eventually lead to replicability problems [1]. We agree with Marsden and Pingry [1] that, as researchers, our empirical research must address data quality issues and provide the information necessary to assess the seven data quality dimensions: what, when, where, how, who, which, and why. In the following, we further argue that reporting guidelines can be a useful tool for authors and peer reviewers to ensure that a manuscript has adequately addressed these data quality issues. Reporting guidelines have been widely adopted in the health research community, and we believe their adoption in IS can improve the credibility of our research in the long run.

Although many factors afect replicability [2], one way to improve replicability is to provide suficient transparency in the reporting of research work [3]. Transparent and adequate reporting increases the usability of the research by allowing others to evaluate potential biases, consider replication, and identify potential contextual/moderating factors in meta-analyses or systematic reviews [4]. Researchers who receive public funding for their research have an obligation to minimize the waste of scarce resources from producing incomplete or unusable reports [5,6].

The objective of this paper is to develop a reporting guideline for IS survey research. The survey method is known to be subject to several types of biases, such as common method variance [7] and social desirability bias [8]. Some research contexts are even prone to respondent dishonesty, especially those that involve asking sensitive questions on topics such as smoking, drug use, and criminal behavior [9,10]. Despite its many shortcomings, the survey method has its strengths. When there is a need to access a large population or when the variables of interest reside in the human mind (e.g., job satisfaction and attitude toward a public policy), self-reports are often the most cost-efective data collection method. To minimize potential biases, best practice for conducting survey research should be followed [11]. In addition, the adherence to best practice should be demonstrated through adequate reporting to allow the determination of any potential confounding factors that might afect findings.

Peer reviews are the first line of defense against poor-quality publications. Our reporting guideline will provide a useful tool for peer reviewers to identify missing reporting items in a manuscript. The use of the reporting guideline by researchers also encourages consideration of data quality in the early stages of research design. In health research, there are reporting guidelines for diferent research methods, such as CONSORT for randomized trials [12], STROBE for observational studies [13], and PRISMA for systematic reviews [14]. We hope this paper serves as a good starting point for further discussion on the use of reporting guidelines for diferent data collection methods in IS.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Sections 2 and 3 de scribe our search for existing guidelines on survey research and the development of a consolidated list of reporting items. Section 4 describes three contextual factors specific to IS that need to be reported to aid the determination of any potential confounding factors that might arise from the research setting. Section 5 explains how the developed guideline can be used, and finally, Section 6 summarizes the paper and identifies future research opportunities.

## 2. Methodology

Reporting guidelines can be relevant to any research discipline. Therefore, we started our search using Google on January 11, 2019, with the keywords “reporting guidelines for survey research” (without quotation marks). The search results were reviewed one by one in the order of their ranking. A paper was short-listed if it was judged to likely contain reporting guidelines for survey research. This judgment was subjective and was based on the title and the summary text presented underneath on the search results pages. We excluded papers with a title or summary text that suggested a focus on reporting guidelines for specific research methods other than surveys, for example, case studie or controlled experiments. As we went through the results, it was ob vious that they were ranked by relevance. We stopped the search when 50 or more consecutive search results were considered irrelevant. This search yielded 37 non-duplicated potential papers.

The same process was repeated for Google Scholar. The same key words were used. The title and the summary text underneath a search result were again used to determine if the paper likely contained reporting guidelines for survey research. We stopped the search when 50 or more consecutive search results were considered irrelevant. The Google Scholar search added 18 non-duplicated potential papers to the 37 that were identified from the previous Google search.

To ensure comprehensiveness, on January 14, 2019, we performed the same search on more databases in the following order: Science Direct, Web of Science, Academic Search Premier, and Business Source Premier. Diferent databases ofer diferent types of search. For Science Direct, Academic Search Premier, and Business Source Premier, we used the full text search; for Web of Science, we used the topic search. Again, we evaluated each search result one by one according to the search ranking using the title and/or the summary text (depending on availability). We stopped searching in a database when 50 or more consecutive search results were considered irrelevant. The Science Direct search yielded 17 additional, potentially relevant papers that were not duplicated from the previous searches. The Web of Science search added 9 potentially relevant papers. The Academic Search Premier search returned just 6 results; of these, only 2 were found potentially relevant, but both had already been identified from the previous searches. The small number of search results returned is probably because only publications between 2008 and 2012 were available in our library for this database. So, no papers were added from the Academic Search Premier search. The Business Source Premier search yielded 1 additional, potentially relevant paper.

Overall, our searches yielded 82 potentially relevant papers. They included 67 journal articles, 4 book chapters, 2 conference papers, 8 non-peer reviewed guidelines published by research institutions, and 1 unpublished working paper. Fig. 1 summarizes our search process.

We then went through each of these 82 articles and identified 21 that provided guidelines specifically for reporting survey research. When we went through these articles, we added 3 referenced guidelines that had not been previously identified from our searches. In total, we identified 24 articles that had reporting guidelines for survey research. These articles are listed in Appendix A.

## 3. Consolidated list of reporting items

Bennett et al. [15] provided a table showing the reporting items recommended by four diferent guidelines ([16–19]). To organize our data, we extended this table by adding additional reporting items suggested by the other 19 guidelines. We also recorded the reporting items recommended by each guideline. Because diferent articles recommended reporting items at diferent levels of specificity, we ended up with 217 distinct reporting items, many of which are very specific and mentioned by few articles. Six of the items were specific to demonstrating the quality of momentary, self-report data in behavioral medicine, such as training provided to respondents (patients) and evi dence that respondents complied with the stipulated assessment procedures; these items were removed from the list because they are not particularly relevant to IS.

To consolidate the list, two of the authors first independently assigned each item to diferent sections according to the recommended article structure and supplementary materials in the “Guide for Authors” in Decision Support Systems [1]. The percentage of agreement was 85.78%, indicating good inter-rater reliability. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Next, two of the authors grouped together repetitive or similar items in the list. Consensus was reached through discussion and consultation of the original articles. The consolidated list is provided in Table 1 (excluding the reporting items highlighted in gray, i.e., user characteristics, organizational characteristics, and technology characteristics). To preserve some of the details from our literature search, we provide a description of each reporting item in Appendix B.

In the following, we explain how each reporting item can be used to enhance our understanding of the survey data quality in terms of the 7 Ws. The mapping provided in Table 1 is based on our subjective interpretation of the 7 Ws in [1]:

What provides an explanation of exactly what is captured in the data. The research summary provided in the abstract (item #1), the definition of constructs (item #4), the unit of analysis (and relating the instrument to the unit of analysis) (items #5, #14), the research design (including the description of the technology under study and the organizational or market characteristics) (items #7, #8, #9), and the acknowledgment of special circumstances (item #41) help us understand the theoretical constructs that the data are supposed to measure and the contextual factors that might afect the data. When preparing the collected data for analysis, unusual data patterns such as outliers (items #28) and severe missing data in some of the constructs (items #27) might suggest potential measurement bias or quality problems related to the instrument. Validity analysis (item #33) helps confirm that the data are truly measuring constructs they are supposed to measure. Summary statistics (item #32) and the testing of statistical assumptions (item #35) help us understand the statistical properties of the data. Discussion of any potential biases (item #34) helps the reader evaluate the quality of the data. All these reporting items contribute to our understanding of nature of the data collected.

## 4. Reporting items specific to IS survey research

In IS, many of the users' perceptions, attitudes, intentions, and be haviors are afected by external or contextual factors [20] such as:

• User characteristics (e.g., [21,22]).

• Technology characteristics (e.g., [21,23]).

Organizational/market characteristics (e.g., [24,25]).

Therefore, we also added these factors to the consolidated list. In total, Table 1 consists of 44 items categorized under 9 main sections (i.e., abstract, introduction, theory, data and methods, results, discussion, acknowledgment, appendix, research data).

We emphasize that diferent authors prefer diferent article struc tures. As such, we note that a reporting item can be placed under a diferent section than that suggested in Table 1. However, it is important that all of the items (where applicable) should be addressed in the paper or accompanying supplementary materials. The list is not exhaustive and diferent contextual factors that can afect data validity might arise in diferent research settings. It is the author's responsibility to report any potential confounding factors that (s)he can think of.

## 5. Recommendations

The reporting guideline (comprehensive version in Appendix B) developed in this short paper can be used as a checklist by survey researchers as they prepare their manuscript for submission to IS journals. In fact, submission of a checklist of reporting items is recommended or compulsory in various health research journals, such as Annals of Family Medicine, Journal of Public Health, and Journal of Global Health.

Peer reviewers can use this checklist to ensure that methodological rigor is demonstrated in the reporting of research design and findings. However, it is important to note that data quality can be afected by a host of contextual factors that are unique to each research setting. The guiding principle for the peer reviewer is whether suficient information is provided to allow other researchers to replicate the study and to evaluate the quality of the research. If the peer reviewer thinks that anything is unclear in the reporting of the research, (s)he is re sponsible for requesting further information from the authors.

This paper serves as a starting point for further discussion on the need to develop similar guidelines for diferent data collection methods, and for journals and the IS community to endorse such reporting guidelines for future publications.

## 6. Conclusion

Although the survey method is subject to many biases, the literature has suggested diferent ways to minimize and manage these biases, including statistical tools for detecting and correcting potential biases (e.g., [26,27]), assurances of anonymity [9], and the use of randomized response technique to encourage truthful response [10]. We believe that survey research can produce reliable data through careful research design and implementation. We also argue that a key to establishing research credibility for surveys is to demonstrate methodological rigo through transparent and adequate reporting. A reporting guideline has thus been developed to provide researchers and peer reviewers a tool to help assess methodological rigor.

Endorsement of reporting guidelines by academic associations and journals is not yet common in IS. For data collection methods that are new or unique to IS, such as big data generated from various information systems, the development of a reporting guideline might be more time consuming and involve various experts in the research method in a Delphi study [28]. However, we believe that in the long run such endorsement will increase the credibility of IS research by improving data quality and replicability.

Besides reporting guidelines, in the future we might also consider data and analysis code sharing practices (e.g., [29,30]), already adopted by other disciplines, that require authors to deposit data and related analysis codes in an online repository and share them with at least the reviewers and editors. These practices can increase transparency, provide evidence of data quality, improve research replicability, and enable a wider utilization of the collected data.
