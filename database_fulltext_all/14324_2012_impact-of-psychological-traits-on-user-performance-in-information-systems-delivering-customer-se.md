# Impact of psychological traits on user performance in information systems delivering customer service: IS management perspective

Md. Dulal Hossain <sup>a,1</sup>, Junghoon Moon <sup>b,</sup>⁎, Jae-woong Yun <sup>b</sup>, Young Chan Choe <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Institute of Computer Science, Atomic Energy Research Establishment, Bangladesh Atomic Energy Commission, Savar, Dhaka, Bangladesh

## a r t i c l e i n f o

## a b s t r a c t

Information systems-assisted customer service delivery has emerged as a strategic imperative that is increasingly tied to information systems resources, organizational capabilities, and work environment in organizational information systems management. Despite the potential of information systems, extensive diversity exists in the task performance within organizations which provokes the factors of psychological traits that affect performance. Drawing upon information systems success literature–contextual theories of organizational creativity, and behavioral and human psychology in an organizational environment–this paper develops a model to assess user performance in an organizational IS in providing customer services. Here, hypotheses have been developed in regard to the interaction of system satisfaction and organizational work environments with the user's psychological traits and how these interactions in<sup>fl</sup>uence their performance. The model has been tested using survey data collected from a total of 314 respondents, regarding the users of a call center information system employed in a large organization. Results from the partial least squares analyses support the proposed model and highlight the important role of psychological traits in mediating the effect of systems, and organizational environment on performance. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also shed new light on the technological and organizational factors associated with the user performance. The implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings for research and practice are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The performance enhancement of information systems (IS) users within an organization is a necessary step to achieve quality customer service and the organization's competitive advantage [58]. Today's service-based economy faces major challenges in assessing and managing the performance of the employees (users) involved in a human in-the-loop systems [21]. In particular, call centers have become a core part of the service industry, therefore require interactions between the users and customers. Hence, the provision of quality customer service has emerged as a strategic imperative for most organizations, and this is highly related to IS resources and organizational capabilities [68]. Quality customer service is not only the most important factor in achieving customer satisfaction, but is also the principal criterion for measuring the competitiveness of the customer service process [89]. Similarly, the increased emphasis on customer service has emerged as a key motivation in prioritizing the call center IS, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the general recognition of the essential role that IT plays in this process [29].

Despite the business value creation of information systems [35], extensive diversity exists in task performance within competing organizations [73]. Particularly, in the context of call centers, IS management faces criticisms from callers about the user's (operators') capability in effectively processing customer requests [3]. There are also complaints from the users regarding the management and work environment [82]. Furthermore, in recent years, the media has paraded call centers as prime examples of stressful work environments. These issues raised crucial challenges for managers of the workforce [44]. As a result, the operational challenges faced by call center managers have become more complicated [2]. These issues have provoked the consideration on how psychological traits may impact user performance with IS of organizations in providing customer services, especially in the context of call centers [27]. However, academic studies regarding the call center IS management and performance are currently limited in both number and scope, especially in the <sup>fi</sup>elds of organizational IS work environment and human psychological traits.

Behavioral scientists have a long history and impressive track record of using psychology for improving human performance and productivity, quality of work life satisfaction, and organizational effectiveness [27]. The literature regarding human resource management and strategy identi<sup>fi</sup>es organizational work environment as a critical non-IT resource in determining the user performance in providing service [68]. While a number of studies have already emphasized the critical role of IS in customer service [29], however, empirical research examining the link between IS and the user performance is still lacking. Previous studies on call centers have focused on issues such as queuing models [45], optimization problems [50], multi-skill call centers [1], work organization, work station layout, work postures, physical factors in the work environment, and ergonomics [10,83]. However, there remains a further need to examine the impact of user behaviors on the operations and service quality in depth [2]. Therefore, empirical analyses based on historical data will be extremely valuable in providing insight into these questions [2]. Yet, the framework on the organizational work environment and the impact of human psychological behavior on performance enhancement are still lacking in this regard. Furthermore, regardless of some psychometrically sound instruments that are available for assessing perceptions of organizational environments and human psychology in general, very few scholars have attempted to quantitatively assess the work environment for the user performance of organizational IS [5].

In this light, to improve the explanatory values of the factors associated with user performance in organizational IS and work environment, this paper developed a model drawing upon IS success literature, contextual theories of organizational creativity, behavior, and human factor psychology in an organizational work environment. This model will speci<sup>fi</sup>cally explain how the user's psychological traits mediate the impacts of the systems and organizational environment on work life satisfaction and performance. It also focuses on identifying the factors that can be generalized across organizational IS and environments regarding the in-<sup>fl</sup>uence of psychological traits on the user performance. The hypothesized model has been tested using a total of 314 survey data points collected from call center operators of a large organization. Research questions addressed in this paper focus on (1) the impacts of psychological traits on user performance in an organizational IS which provides customer service and (2) the technological and organizational factors that shape user performance in an organizational IS which provides customer service.

By addressing these research questions, this paper contributes to the IS success literature by providing empirical evidences regarding the impacts of psychological traits on performance, as well as by isolating a parsimonious set of factors that are theoretically grounded to affect user performance in organizational IS in providing customer services. Thereby, this study contributes to the development and management of motivational propensities in organizational IS management for human resources, implying the substantial returns that may be yielded by investing in the development of psychological traits, beyond those of the other traditional forms of capital investment.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, a theoretical framework will be developed. Followed by the framework, a research model and hypothesis will be constructed in Section 3. Sections 4 and 5 will describe the research method, the procedure of data analysis and the results of the model tested. Finally, in Sections 6 and 7, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research will be discussed, as well as the theoretical and managerial implications, limitations, and future research directions, followed by the conclusions.

## 2. Theoretical framework

Our theoretical framework was found upon these three elements: IS success literature, contextual theories of organizational creativity and behavior, and human psychological traits in an organizational work environment. Perceptual measures of IS success have been employed extensively over the years, with essential reliance on the system satisfaction as a surrogate measure of IS success [30]. Again, IS researchers have recently scrutinized phenomena such as users, the user competence, and the IT artifact as the core of system usage [15]. Based on this, we argue that the system satisfaction retains its in<sup>fl</sup>uence throughout the life cycles of IS.

Recent contextual theories of organizational creativity and innovation have attempted to identify work environments that are related to user activities [5]. Previous studies have found that call center works tend to be intense in physical and emotional sense, as they involve performance pressure, close surveillance, and limited autonomy [83]. Bohle and his colleagues (2011) found a relationship between work intensity and work life con<sup>fl</sup>icts in relation to the satisfaction level of individuals [13]. Work life con<sup>fl</sup>icts associated with higher levels of both chronic fatigue and psychological symptoms have also been supported by the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of a previous study in the healthcare sector [13]. Therefore, environmental factors, regardless of its strengths, have no effect on the behavior of an organization itself, unless it <sup>fi</sup>rst affects the behaviors of human agents directly involved with the IS within the organization [51]. Thus, we argue that environmental factors affect the performance of key organizational IS users throughout the industry. The literature on organizational behavior, especially, “the study and application of positively-oriented human resource strengths and psychological capacities,” suggests that the psychological capacities can be developed, measured, and effectively managed to enhance the user performance [53]. Particularly, some positive traits of human psychological capacities such as hope [77], optimism [71], resiliency [57], and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy [62] are found to have a positive impact on work-related performance and satisfaction on an individual level [53]. Therefore, we argue that the system satisfaction and organizational environment affect the user performance after being mediated by their psychological traits; hence, our theoretical framework is grounded on this proposition. We developed a conceptual model (Fig. 1) that is grounded on IS-assisted customer services, organizational environments, and user psychological traits in the context of call centers.

## 2.1. IS satisfaction and performance

System satisfaction is one of the criterion variables [54], and most widely used measure in assessing the IS success [25,26]. Furthermore, it has been predominantly critical in the IS implementation as well [7]. The fundamental argument behind this approach is that high levels of system satisfaction lead to high levels of user performance [46]. The reciprocal relationship between system satisfaction and system use has been touted as one of the key constructs representing the IS success [31,32]. Further, in the Garrity and Sanders Model of IS success, system satisfaction has been an antecedent of individual/organizational in<sup>fl</sup>uences that can re<sup>fl</sup>ect individual/organizational performance [31,32]. DeLone and McLean made an explicit distinction between the information aspects and the systemic features as determinants of satisfaction in their highly cited model [26]. In fact, in their model, net bene<sup>fi</sup>t has been used as the primary concept to measure the performance [31,32]. Traditionally, system satisfaction has been measured indirectly through information quality, system quality, service quality [8,67,87], as well as through the equitable need ful<sup>fi</sup>llment (work performance, relatedness, and self-development ful<sup>fi</sup>llment) [7]. Furthermore, previous studies validated user system satisfaction as a function of performance [7], thus revealing that an effective system and perception about lower user effort leads to increased user satisfaction [33]. Therefore, these comprehensive reports suggest that the user system satisfaction is highly relevant in understanding the user performance in organizational IS.

## 2.2. Organizational IS environment and performance

The organizational work environment has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a critical non-IT resource that determines the customer service performance [68]. In this context, the work environment refers to the employees' perceptions of the practices, procedures, and behaviors that are expected, supported, and rewarded in regard to customer services [72]. Such a work environment is perceived directly or indirectly by employees and thus it is assumed to be a major force in<sup>fl</sup>uencing their behavior [38]. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition of work environment has been used to refer to a broad class of organizational and perceptual variables that re<sup>fl</sup>ect individual organizational interactions, which affect individual behaviors in organizations [70]. Hoyt and Gerloff subsequently made a distinction between the psychological and the organizational climates [37] by dividing the work climate into two parts according to the individual and the organizational viewpoints. This concept re<sup>fl</sup>ects shared individual perceptions of the work environment and also shows in<sup>fl</sup>uences like technology and demographics.

The most in<sup>fl</sup>uential theoretical paradigms that have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced researchers in the last decade include Amabile's development and validation of the new instrument, KEYS (Assessing the Climate for Creativity), which assess the perceptions of organizational work environments [5]. The author proposed three broad organizational factors through a componential model of creative activities and innovation in organizations [4]. These factors included speci<sup>fi</sup>c elements, such as organizational encouragement, organizational motivation, team support, and management practices. Management practices refer to the allowance of freedom or autonomy in the conduct of work, the provision of challenging and interesting work, the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of clear overall strategic goals, and the formation of work teams, and leadership practices re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the organizational context.

Work in call centers is demanding, stressful, and very labor-intensive [34], which leads the employees to both burnout and turnover [2]. Furthermore, it has been characterized as a newer phase of Tayloristic scienti<sup>fi</sup>c management (a system of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c management, advocated by Fred W. Taylor, which analyzed and synthesized work<sup>fl</sup>ows [81]), where management control is elevated to new levels through the joint use of target settings and monitoring of both the quantitative and qualitative aspects of performance [10]. For this reason, managers tend to make an effort to improve the effectiveness of interactions between the operators and customers through organizational encouragement, coordinated teamwork, proper training, and performance evaluation.

IS is resource-intensive, thus it requires substantial material and managerial resources [19]. One survey from a call center compensation noted that the call center employees felt that they have very little respect from their supervisors and that they experienced role con-<sup>fl</sup>icts due to inconsistencies between the job performance expectations and the performance evaluation criteria [84]. Call center leaders must be careful in managing the key trade-offs between customer services and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [2]. Planning and controlling the employees, together interact with other human resource practices thus jointly they in<sup>fl</sup>uence the performance outcomes [2]. Therefore, leadership at all levels requires meta-structured action to de<sup>fi</sup>ne institutional norms and values regarding the structure of actions related to the technology [19]. As a result, supervisory leadership can modify prevailing structures, introduce complementary measures to facilitate the system use, and reinforce norms to encourage the use [48]. These extended literatures suggest that organizational encouragement, team support, workload pressure, and leadership are the organizational contextual factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence user performance.

## 2.3. Psychological traits and performance

Psychological traits in this context refer to the extent where individuals tend to be hopeful, optimistic, resilient, and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacious [64], representing some of the positive traits of human psychology. Hence, we term these traits as Extended Positive Psychological Traits (EPPT) and focus on hope [77], optimism [71], resiliency [57], and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy [62]; as these have positive impacts on work-related performance and satisfaction at an individual-level [53]. Every call center manager is acutely aware that phone conversations between customers and operators are interactions between human beings. This suggests that the psychological issues associated with operator's experience can have a major impact on both the customer satisfaction level and the overall system performance [2]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in recent years, there has been considerable discussion and emphasis on positive psychology and positive applications of psychological science [22]. Such works that focus on organizational issues are well positioned to frame the issues and the research in a positive manner [16], thereby realizing some of the products and potentials of applied positive psychology [6]. Research efforts are underway to understand how to best enhance and bene<sup>fi</sup>t from workforce diversity, cross-cultural collaboration and communication, new models of leadership development, and new work arrangements like telecommuting [17]. These efforts are typically focused on how to best maximize human performance in workplaces [27].

Several empirical studies in call centers have examined the impacts of work and job design on employees [18], psychological contact [18], and psychological well-being, including intrinsic and extrinsic job satisfactions [34]. Psychological traits have been studied with regard to their measurements and relationships with performance and satisfaction [52,53]. Furthermore, many CEOs demonstrate positive psychological traits, including leadership and performance [64]. However, little has been known in relation to the psychological traits or their impacts on performance in IS within an organizational context.

We theorize that EPPT are state-like [53]—individual experiences of particular time, events, or contexts which may bring stability over some time but are expected to be less stable in terms of personality traits of core self-evaluations. Therefore, state-like traits are subjected to change and development [53], unlike the enduring, stable, and cross-situational individual tendencies to experience such capacities [64]. Therefore, the EPPT of this study are considered as “state-like;” for they are less stable and more open to change and development compared to the “trait-like” constructs, such as the Big Five personality dimensions. However, it is important to note that the state-like traits are not of the momentary states. Furthermore, we chose hope, optimism, resiliency, and selfef<sup>fi</sup>cacy because previous theories and researches suggest that these capacities are positively related to outcomes such as job performance and problem solving [64], as well as playing important roles in leadership development [52].

Both prior researches and theories on hope, optimism, resilience, and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy support the notion that these capacities can be developed [53]. Snyder provided evidence that hope can be developed or increased, and has published the state-hope scale to measure the levels of hope [77]. More recently, Carver and Scheier discussed strategies to develop optimism; while Shifren and Hooker demonstrated its situational measurement [53]. Experimental studies have shown that the positive emotions enhance resilience in the face of negative events, thus re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the state-like qualities of these capacities [53]. Hence, self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy has a strong positive relationship with work-related performance [11].

Consistent with the theoretical arguments made in the earlier research, we also theorize that EPPT [53] are second-order constructs composed of the dimensions that are related to the components mentioned above. There are also some preliminary evidences to suggest that when these four constructs are combined together to form a higher-order structure, the result can be considered as state-like, hence may be developable [53]. Luthans et al. showed that this composited factor may be a better predictor of performance and satisfaction than the four individual facets [53].

## 2.4. Work life satisfaction and performance

The effects of organizational work on work life satisfaction describe the affective beliefs concerning the overall status of one's life [70]. The potential in<sup>fl</sup>uences of work environment on work life satisfaction are considered in terms of the effects, mediated by the perceived quality of work life in regard to the person-changing versus the environmentchanging effects [70]. A sustainable organizational environment provides a meaningful life experience to its employees, as well as a positive work-life balance [43]. The quality of work life has pervasive consequences on an individual's sense of worth and personal control [55]. One's level of work satisfaction can spill over to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the behaviors in the other spheres of their life, which in return can impact the general life satisfaction [70]. In other words, the quality of work life affects both the job-related and non-job-related social psychological outcomes. Furthermore, the socio-technical approaches the IS in terms of its impact on people's feelings, physical needs, and psychological states. The systems in a given environment can also cause psychological harm and may impair the intrinsic maintenance needed by workers, such as psychological and cognitive or higher order needs [31]. Alternatively, IS in such an environment could also enhance the individual's quality of work by providing an avenue to enhance individual performance, and thus sharpen the worker's skill through greater autonomy, as well as it increases social interactions [31]. This implies that the work life satisfaction can in<sup>fl</sup>uence values such as the perceived organizational environment impact of IS use; where individual utility is understood as of a psychological consequence [31].

## 3. Research model and hypothesis

The aforementioned literature supports our theoretical proposition that the user psychological traits mediate the effects of system satisfaction and organizational environment on their performance. Building on this proposition, we have developed a research model (Fig. 2) and proposed hypotheses grounded on the context of a call center IS's user performance.

## 3.1. System context: user's effort to use

User's effort to use is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which users exercise additional efforts to restore any information from the system if they feel restricted by it [86]. A number of behavioral studies have relied on the ‘effort–accuracy framework of cognition’ to investigate the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial impact of the systems on reducing the user's effort expended by themselves and on increasing their decision quality (accuracy) [36,86]. According to the effort–accuracy framework of cognition proposed by Payne, the primary objectives of a decision maker are to maximize accuracy (decision quality) and minimize the effort. Furthermore, recent literature on decision aids has also examined the impact on the user decision quality and effort [80,86].

System constraints can invoke psychological reactance [14], thus producing user dissatisfaction that may result in negative performance outcomes. Applying the reactance theory to the context of call center systems, we contend that individuals use the systems to facilitate their decisions in delivering customer service, rather than being restricted by the system query processes that they do not prefer to apply. Thus, together with the user's effort and the system satisfaction, we hypothesize the following:

H1a. User's effort to use has a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on system satisfaction.

H1b. User's effort to use has a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user performance.

## 3.1.1. IS department service quality

IS department service quality provides relational and technologica contexts in which the employees interpret behaviors and events linked to the system satisfaction. The role of service quality in shaping user cognitions and behaviors has received signi<sup>fi</sup>cant attention, and has become an important dimension of IS success given the importance of IS support in the organizational environment in which customer service is crucial [26]. Service quality is a form of attitude, as well as a component of long-term overall satisfaction [61]. Extant research in this area shows that proper implementation of such service quality features may increase user satisfaction [61]. If services provided by an organization meet a user's needs, higher user satisfaction may result [61]. As a consequence, the role of service quality has become critical to organizational success [49]. Therefore, we believe that service quality from an IS department plays a key role in shaping the user cognitions, actions, and behaviors in call centers with respect to its day-to-day usage applications. Hence, this leads to the following hypotheses:

Fig. 2. Research model.

H1c. IS department service quality has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on system satisfaction.

H1e. IS department service quality has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on organizational encouragement.

H1f. IS department service quality has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on team support.

In the case of ERP adoption, service quality is an important factor during the post-implementation stages, especially the IS support in the form of extended technical assistance, emergency maintenance, updates, and special user training [78]. Previous study has shown that service quality reduces user's effort in their decision making even in online situations [86]. According to Heskett, Sasser, and Schlesinger, internal service quality leads to employee satisfaction, which then increases employee loyalty and productivity. In a call center environment, this internal IS department service quality encourages the users to perceive time pressure as a necessary concomitant of importance and urgency. This may add challenges to their work and therefore help them develop intrinsic motivations [4]. Hence, service quality in turn, reduces workload pressure. This leads to the following hypotheses:

H1d. IS department service quality has a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user's effort to use.

H1g. IS department service quality has a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on workload pressure.

## 3.1.2. System satisfaction

System satisfaction refers to the degree of user satisfaction with the systems [67]. Furthermore, it also refers to their overall affective and cognitive evaluations of the pleasurable level of consumption-related ful<sup>fi</sup>llment experienced with IS [7]. In this context, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned the system satisfaction as a degree of user satisfaction with the systems including their affective and cognitive evaluations of the system experience with the call center IS. Four items have been selected as relevant measures to examine system satisfaction: being contented, pleased, delighted, and satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, based on Oliver [60], as these items have been widely used to measure the level of system satisfaction [7,41,59]. Numerous studies have advocated that IS failures are due to the psychological and organizational issues, rather than the technological issues [7,31]. Hence, examining the user's effort to use and the service quality shape the system satisfaction, thereby in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the user performance. Call center operators encounter major psychosocial pressures, including high work intensity and undesirable working hours [13]. A highly restrictive system can invoke psychological reactance [14], resulting in user dissatisfactions, which affect psychological traits when otherwise could have had a positive impact on work-related performance [53]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the system satisfaction positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences EPPT.

H2a. System satisfaction has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user extended positive psychological traits.

System satisfaction can have a positive impact on task performance and decision-making in delivery of information to customers within the context of Assimilation Theory; high levels of usability are related to improved task performance [32]. System satisfaction, as an advanced organizer, should enhance work and decision-making performance. Poor system design has been cited as a key element in a number of high pro<sup>fi</sup>le IS site failures. On the other hand, work life satisfaction recognizes and focuses on the human component of systems' work environment, as it is drawn from the socio-technical viewpoint [70]. Particularly, this concept attempts to measure whether the organizational work environment supports the social and intellectual needs, and/or psychological requirements of individuals in the context of job-related activities [31]. Thus, it is implied that the systems also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the individual's work life in the given environment. System satisfaction in<sup>fl</sup>uences values such as the perceived organizational impact of IS use [32]. It has been hypothesized, therefore, that system satisfaction positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences both the work life satisfaction and the overall performance.

H2b. System satisfaction has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user work life satisfaction.

H2c. System satisfaction has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user performance.

## 3.2. Organizational environment context

## 3.2.1. Supportive supervisory leadership

A good supervisor serves as a good work model; sets appropriate goals, supports the team, values individual contributions, and shows con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the team [5]. Several studies have focused on the roles of direct supervisors, particularly in the areas of (1) goal clarity [9], (2) interactions between the supervisor and its subordinates [42], and (3) support for a team's work and ideas [24]. Therefore, supportive supervisory leadership develops hope among the call center employees through determining goals and planning the ways to meet these. Previous studies showed that the supportive and facilitative leadership accounts for the extrinsic and intrinsic motivations [85], as well as the employee creativity [75], which in turn may develop optimism, resiliency and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy among them.

Finally, supportive supervisory leadership can play a role in regulating the pace of call center services by establishing usage goals, targets as well as having impacts on individuals through meeting their socio-technical needs within their organizational work domain of life [70]. Throughout the life cycle of call center IS, without strong ongoing leadership, it is dif-<sup>fi</sup>cult, if not impossible, for the users to experience association with the organization's missions and strategic goals, as well as to allocate valuable resources to support IS initiatives, to overcome inertial routines, and to establish new method for daily IS use. With this in mind, we posit the fol lowing hypotheses:

H3a. Supportive supervisory leadership has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user extended positive psychological traits.

H3b. Supportive supervisory leadership has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user work life satisfaction.

H3c. Supportive supervisory leadership has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on organizational encouragement.

H3d. Supportive supervisory leadership has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on team support.

Again, in the context of call center systems, supervisory management can establish a context, where the IS actions and behaviors affect the meaning, commitment, and political support through participation, by articulating goal clarity and establishing a strategic work plan to achieve the organizational business mission. Hence this leadership may change the user perception of time pressure as mentioned above, thus developing intrinsic motivation for work [4]. Hence, supportive supervisory leadership may reduce the workload pressure, which leads to the following hypotheses:

H3e. Supportive supervisory leadership has a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on workload pressure.

## 3.2.2. Organizational encouragement

An organizational culture is one that encourages employee activities through the fair, constructive judgment of ideas, reward and recognition for creative work, mechanisms for developing new ideas and active <sup>fl</sup>ows of these ideas, as well as a shared vision of the organization's goals [5]. High organizational encouragement supports approval, esteem, and social identity, thus producing expectation that the superior performance and extra-role behavior will be recognized and rewarded. On the basis of the norm of reciprocity, organizational encouragement would strengthen the affective commitment to the organization, hence increasing the efforts made on its behalf [28]. People are motivated to maintain positive states of person–environment <sup>fi</sup>t and avoid negative states [69]; they are motivated to anticipate in ways that will result in a good balance between themselves and their environment [56], thereby encouraging people to be ef<sup>fi</sup>cacious, hopeful, and resilient. Furthermore, workload pressure in call centers is largely accounted by job-related psychological strain [79]. However, the positive traits of hope, optimism, resiliency and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy can be achieved through organizational encouragement, resulting in positive work feelings, thus enhancing performance [47]. Both the organizational encouragement or support and the work life satisfaction might create a sense of obligation for employees to repay the organization [28], thus resulting in performance improvement based on work life satisfaction and psychological traits. As such, we posit the following hypotheses:

H4a. Organizational encouragement has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user work life satisfaction.

H4b. Organizational encouragement has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user extended positive psychological traits.

## 3.2.3. Team support

Team support refers to a diversely skilled team where the members communicate well, are open to new ideas, constructively challenge the other's work, trust and help one another, and feel committed to the their given work [5]. However, call center operators feel tension partially through the control and commitment of measurement systems [2]. This <sup>fi</sup>nding illustrates the ways in which team supportiveness, caring, and nurturance interact to form a ‘humane’ and ‘understanding’ workplace [47]. Previous researchers examined the relationships between IS team performance and team cohesion, as well as the team member involvement [40], their personal characteristics, and also the interactions among team members in empowering them to achieve their goals [39]. Furthermore, mutual openness between team members to ideas and information sharing may facilitate creative activities by exposing individuals to a greater variety of unusual ideas. Such exposure has been demonstrated to have progressive impact on positive thinking [63], thereby encouraging people to be ef<sup>fi</sup>cacious, hopeful, optimistic, and resilient. A study regarding pediatric nurses suggested that the perceptions on team supports in<sup>fl</sup>uenced their overall quality of life satisfaction [23]. With this in mind, we posit the following hypotheses:

H4c. Team support has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user work life satisfaction.

H4d. Team support has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user extended positive psychological traits.

## 3.2.4. Workload pressure

Several studies have highlighted the harmful consequences of workload pressure or work overload. According to Wilkes et al., work overloads and time constraints were the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributors to work stress among community nurses. This situation was accompanied by the general physiological, psychological, and behavioral stress symptoms [88], which then affected the positive psychological traits of the workers affected. Call center workers made complaints over the routine work, high information processing load and stress [79]. Empirical studies regarding call centers have demonstrated the impacts of work and job design on employee satisfaction [18]. The psychological context of call center operators was found to affect the attitudes and behaviors of the employees with a higher relational orientation in general to their work, leading them to feel more satis-<sup>fi</sup>ed and thus to present more civic virtue behaviors [18]. The psychological effects of extended performance of computer–telephone interactive tasks have also attracted increasing concern with regard to the adverse health impacts [79]. Therefore, excessive workload pressure is expected to undermine performance [4] by hindering the positive thinking [63], thereby discouraging people to be ef<sup>fi</sup>cacious, hopeful, optimistic, and resilient; especially if the time pressure is perceived to be imposed by organizational work environment [4], which may further degrade work life satisfaction. The aforementioned studies regarding pediatric nurses suggested that the perceptions upon workload in<sup>fl</sup>uenced negatively on worker quality of life satisfaction [23]. As such, we posit the following hypotheses:

H4e. Workload pressure has a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user work life satisfaction.

H4f. Workload pressure has a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user extended positive psychological traits.

## 3.3. Extended positive psychological traits

The studied psychological traits are aimed at performance improvement; however, employees with more positive psychological status may also be more contented with their work life satisfaction. Luthans' study presented a relationship between psychological traits and job satisfaction [53]. In particular, in terms of optimism, he demonstrated a relationship between organizational performance and satisfaction in a call center context [84]. Optimists perceived lower levels of job stress and fewer work/non-work related con<sup>fl</sup>icts. As a result, optimism was found to moderate performance and result in higher levels of job satisfaction [84]. On the other hand, work experiences were shown to have an impact on self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy [74], which also exhibited a positive relationship with work performance [11]. In general, employees who displayed higher levels of hope were found to be more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. Furthermore, Luthans showed that increase in satisfaction may occur when hope is accompanied by optimism, self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy or the resilience, which help to respond favorably to setbacks. According to Rice et al., the analysis on how the characteristics of works may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perceived quality of life has stressed individual psychology by linking the perceived quality of life to the more general idea of human health and well-being. The author also found that perceived quality of life indicates something important about an individual's psychosocial status. On the basis of this theoretical foundation for psychological traits, we derive the following hypotheses:

H5a. User extended positive psychological traits have a positive in-<sup>fl</sup>uence on user performance.

H5b. User extended positive psychological traits have a positive in-<sup>fl</sup>uence on work life satisfaction.

## 3.3.1. Work life satisfaction

Work life satisfaction is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the assessment of the impact that the call center IS has on individual lives within their organizational work domain [70]. Hence, this idea assesses the potential in<sup>fl</sup>uences of work environment within the system usage (i.e., how well the IS work environment supports the basic social and maintenance needs of workers) [31,70]. Work life satisfaction measures an individual's response affective to a system's work environment in terms of its effects on quality and job satisfaction [31]. Therefore, some measures attempt to ascertain the amount of support an individual receives in job-related activities within the organizational environment. In a study by Rice et al., a similar concept of work life satisfaction was derived as the “in<sup>fl</sup>uence” on perceived quality of life, with a variable “psychological state, or feeling—and therefore a cognition—of pleasure, happiness, well-being, or satisfaction” [70]. Systems and organizational work areas provide greater control through autonomy. A secure environment should therefore produce positive feelings regarding the system, leading to job ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and pleasure [65], which are then interpreted as work life satisfaction. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H6. Work life satisfaction has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user performance.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Instrument development

This study used a survey method to test the model that has been developed throughout this paper. All measures for the hypotheses were developed through successive stages of literature review, theoretical modeling, and re<sup>fi</sup>nements. Since the targeted organizations are situated in South Korea, the English questionnaires were translated into Korean, and a panel of experts in the MIS research area examined the facial validities of the items. Some modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to the existing scale were made to suit the context of the call center IS management. Finally, we again translated the questionnaires to English. Overall, <sup>fi</sup>fty-one measurement items were developed, all of which were posed as questions on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. All constructs and measures are listed in Appendix A.

## 4.2. Measure for performance

Actual performance evaluations were gathered independently, where the performance measures were based on objective data and managerial user ratings. Performance was evaluated by managers on a four-dimension scale based on recorded conversations between employees and customers. Among these four dimensions, one was referred to the service performance (40%), one to kindness in the voices of the customers (40%), one to customer claims (10%), and the remaining to working performance of the employees (laziness or illness) (10%). The performance evaluations for each sample were obtained at times corresponding to the execution of the survey questionnaires. Ultimately, in average 13 months of evaluation data for each category were collected and used for the analysis. Finally, the four-dimension scale was used as the formative indicator of performance.

## 4.3. Measures for independent variables

All of the exogenous constructs in the model were operationalized to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the constructs. This study pursued multistep processes to obtain professional reviews of the survey instrument following its initial creation. We received feedbacks regarding the instructions, speci<sup>fi</sup>c items used for constructs, and the clarity of the wording. First, three faculty members that had extensive experience in survey development examined the instruments. In the next stage, six call center supervisors inspected them to comment on the clarity of the questions and instructions. Finally, 51 call center operators from various call center organizations participated in the pilot study, and their feedback was used in each stage to revise and re<sup>fi</sup>ne the instruments.

## 4.4. Data collection

We investigated the user performance in the organizational IS with regard to the call center IS management. In order to develop a suitable sampling frame, samples were collected from call center organizations situated in South Korea. The reason behind choosing the speci<sup>fi</sup>c ‘Korean’ context is due to its brand popularities, and the size of organizations (volume of customer calls=1 billion/year, total employees=2900, 2800 of which are service representatives) located at six different locations, which may provide a fruitful environment for the operation of service representatives' performance.

The questionnaires were distributed to call center operators along with the purpose statements of this investigation. There are a total of 50 organizational call centers operating in South Korea, each containing 40 to 50 employees. Out of 50, this study sample included 40 call centers. Respondents were requested to complete the survey within 20 days, and reminder phone calls were made two weeks after the initial distribution of the questionnaires. A total of 364 responses were received, indicating a response rate of approximately 42.82%. This response rate is comparable to those of the other survey studies of a similar type and scale [19]. A total of 314 responses were utilized for the analysis, excluding 50 with missing values, which then led to a response rate of 86.26%. The sample covers a wide range of geographical and cultural diversities and a broad range of organization sizes with different service delivery requirements, and therefore is suitable for evaluating speci<sup>fi</sup>c customer services and productivity improvements achieved through adherence to the organizations business missions.

## 5.1.2. Non-response bias

In order to evaluate the non-response bias, respondents were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three different groups based on the time when they returned the survey. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were used to evaluate whether the later respondents systematically differed from the earlier respondents in regard to the number of employees in the call center. ## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

The next step in our data analysis involved examining the signi<sup>fi</sup>- cance and strength of each hypothesized effect. Fig. 3 and Table 4 present the estimates obtained from the PLS analysis. The model shows approximately 23.8% variance in the performance and 44.8% variance in the work life satisfaction.

These results provided evidence for the hypothesized mediating role of psychological traits between systems, organizational environment, and performance. As shown in Fig. 3 and Table 4, the user's effort to use (UEU)-system satisfaction (SSF) and UEU–performance links were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, contributing as an evidence for Hypotheses H1a and H1b. Again, the IS department service quality (SQL)–SSF and SSF–work life satisfaction links were substantial, presenting as an evidence for Hypotheses H1c and H2b. The SSF–performance link, including the WLS–performance link, however, was found to be insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, thereby not supporting H2c, which suggested the mediating role of WLS between SSF and performance. Thus, this mediation is spurious if SSF is not significantly related to performance in the absence of WLS. Therefore, to test the independent effect of SSF on the performance, we removed WLS and conducted another PLS analysis. The results of the new analysis showed that the SSF–performance link was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (β=0.122; pb0.05) only when WLS was excluded from the model, supporting the arguments that SSF could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the performance, although its effect is mediated by WLS. Fig. 3 also shows that the SQL–organizational encouragement (OGE) relationship, SQL–team support (TSP), and the SQL–work load pressure (WLP) link are all signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings support Hypotheses H1e, H1f, and H1g, indicating that the IS department service quality in<sup>fl</sup>uences the organizational environment.

## 6.1. Theoretical contributions

The roles of systems and the organizational environment in discretely affecting user performance has been well discussed in the IS and organizational literatures. However, how the integrated context of systems and organizational environment affect users' psychological traits and performance are less understood. Therefore, by addressing these research questions, this paper contributes to the IS success literature by providing empirical evidence in relation to the impacts of psychological traits on performance, as well as by isolating a parsimonious set of theoretically grounded factors that affect the performance within the organizational IS. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings can improve the explanatory value of IS success in terms of user performance by incorporating the theories from human psychology to IS research. Hence, the following four key aspects of this study signify our contribution to the theory of IS success.

First is the focus on human psychological traits in the context of the IS within organizations. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings further extend the work of Luthans et al. [53] from psychological research to performance in the context of IS in providing customer services.

Secondly, this study integrates the systems and environmental factors, as well as the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of users' psychological traits on performance into one combined model, thus reconciling the in<sup>fl</sup>uences that are rarely studied together. In this study, therefore, we demonstrated the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the systems and at least some of the environmental factors that manifest their impact on the organizational behavior through the psychological traits of users and how these traits relate to their performance.

Next, by composing users' psychological traits as higher-order statelike constructs (hope, ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, optimism, and resilience), we have demonstrated that such a composite construct helps to enrich the understanding of predictive powers of performance, satisfaction, and IS success phenomena.

Finally, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship that exists between the positive psychological traits and the work life satisfaction implies that the narrower and more conceptually linked traits, such as hope, optimism, resiliency, and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, hold substantial capacity for understanding the roots of work life quality and the satisfaction. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings strengthen the relevance of applying psychological principles and researches to enhance work life quality, as well as the organizational effectiveness across a wide range of diverse organizational settings [27]. Hence, we discovered that, while the systems in<sup>fl</sup>uence the psychological traits of users, and thus their behaviors, organizational encouragement and supportive supervisory leadership may directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the user behavior; these results hold signi<sup>fi</sup>cant potential for explaining the roots of supportive supervisory leadership behaviors in organizational contexts.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

Many of the study <sup>fi</sup>ndings offer guidance for management and IT practices. However, here the mediating role of the users' psychologica traits clearly highlights the importance of user roles in enhancing their performance in call center IS. Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the effects of users' psychological traits are mediated through the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of work life satisfaction on performance. Conversely, if users' psychological traits get weakened, their performance is likely to suffer accordingly.

Therefore, the management should be well aware of the importance of improving the level of users' psychological traits. Hopeful, optimistic, ef<sup>fi</sup>cacious, and resilient employees may be more likely to “weathe the storm” compared to their counterparts with weaker/lower psycho logical traits/capital in organizations, which are mostly dynamic in their environmental contexts today. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have enhanced the argument made by Luthans et al. [53] that <sup>fi</sup>nancial, human, and social capitals are necessary investments; however, they may be no longer suf <sup>fi</sup>cient in organizations that provide customer services [53]. Thus, the en hancement of psychological traits in turn improves psychologica empowerment of employees, as it is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a process to enhance the sense of self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy among the members as well as a personal sense of control in the workplace. This psychological empowerment then strengthens employee commitment to an organization. Hence the psychological attachment of workers to their organization has been found to have positive relations with a variety of desirable work out comes, including job satisfaction, motivation, and performance [20] Therefore, managers and leaders of organizations associated with cus tomer service should seek to cultivate psychological capacities of employees, such as hope, optimism, ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and resiliency in every member of the organization. Furthermore, research has also suggested that these traits are adiustable to significant interventions [76]. The po tential malleabilities of hope, optimism, ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and resiliency mean that call centers may be able to improve their performance by enhancing these features in the operators. In fact, several researchers have devel oped guidelines for improving hope, optimism, ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and resiliency [53,76]. Particularly, high involvement management practices, such as selective hiring and extensive training, job designs that include individ ual discretion and allow for ongoing learning, as well as the incentives such as training, security, high pay levels, and lastly the trust building performance measurement systems are required; these are also charac terized as a commitment strategy [12]. Therefore, investment in the psychological traits may yield substantial returns beyond the other traditional forms of capital investment. Further, psychological principles have been incorporated into technological systems through the engi neering psychology, a discipline that refers to how the employees inter act with technological systems. While engineering psychology draws on the basic principles of ergonomics and focuses on how to design systems for the workplace, the combination of ergonomic principles and the sys tems perspective help to develop more effective interfaces for employee and IS [27]. This is to maximize productivity by minimizing operator dis comfort, fatigue, and stress. Hence, the present study discovered that the development of psychological traits in human resources (operators) ca maximize performance in the context of the organizational IS. As such, organizational management should implement strategies to invest in improving the users' psychological traits, through engineering psychology that have been tailored to <sup>fi</sup>t the design of the systems.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings that the organizational environmental factors strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uence performance is important for the call center systems management. In the same vein, since the organizational encouragement and the leadership are also found to be effective in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing user performance, we suggest that the organizational management should encourage the supervisors and the employees to exchange information in a professional sense. From this perspective, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings convey the implications for the development and management of worker motivational propensities in the organizational systems management. Thus, it should be of great interest for organizational policy makers to provide quality customer service and achieve organizational competitive advantage.

Finally, our study also offers implications for organizational policymakers. Supportive supervisory leadership can establish a context where the system actions and behaviors take up meaning, and illustrate the importance of leadership as a driving force in the value of organizational IS businesses. Therefore, in addition to investment in and development of users' key psychological traits, another application would be to focus on the link between the psychological traits of leaders and their impact on the followers. Such information allows decision makers to be more corrective in carrying out actions in their current operations, as well as to develop strategies for addressing problems and focus on the creation of leadership at different levels of the organization.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

In this section, we would like to present some limitations and unanswered questions of this study, and raise some future research directions. First, self-reported data were collected, which is a general concern for method biases resulting from multiple sources, such as consistency motif and social desirability [66]. However, our dependent variable data were collected directly from managerial records independent from the survey responses, thus allowing us to contend that the method is unlikely to be of a serious concern for this study.

There were also some limitations to the sampling approach. Our study utilized a cross-sectional survey design and measured user performance through the average 13 month overall performance index. This design was suitable for addressing our questions of interest, though a longitudinal research could have generated more insights on how different systems as well as the organizational and psychological traits could factor in to change and interact over time to affect user performance. Again, we did not have an avenue to collect data from a random sample of organizations (all of our data were collected from operators from a single call center service provider), therefore, we were limited in access to widely generalize our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

A follow-up study would have data collected from a random sample of call centers that have implemented the IS from multiple vendors, as well as the centers located in different geographic locations. The comparison of the results would most certainly shed some light on the call center IS success in the context of various systems and organizations. Future research with different sampling strategies and extended investigation on the organizations of greater diversity will be useful in generalizing our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Thus, the generalizability of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings beyond the call center IS management would need to be empirically veri<sup>fi</sup>ed; although, to the degree that it bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, threats and adjustments exist for all new technologies, hence, the results would be similar in the other IS within the organizational contexts.

## 7. Conclusion

Drawing upon IS success literature, contextual theories of organizational creativity, behavior, and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of human psychological traits in an organizational environment, this paper developed and tested an IS success model in terms of performance in the context of an organizational information system. The theoretical framework combined the independent contributions of two well established streams in the literature—studies that explain the impact of human psychological traits on performance, and those that explain the effects of systems and organizational environment on performance. Furthermore, this paper con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the systems and the environments are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factors in the IS success by highlighting the importance of positive psychological traits in facilitating the IS performance. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings will have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant practical implications for the organizational management for information systems, as well as for the streamlined business mission. Finally, our theoretical perspective and managerial <sup>fi</sup>ndings will stimulate researchers to investigate the IS success in a wide variety of settings using multiple streams of literature. Hence, it will contribute them further to create a cumulative body of evidence that can advance knowledge regarding the performance in the information systems within organizations.
