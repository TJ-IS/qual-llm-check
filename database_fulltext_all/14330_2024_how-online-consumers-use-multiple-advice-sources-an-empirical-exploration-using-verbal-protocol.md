# How Online Consumers Use Multiple Advice Sources: An Empirical Exploration Using Verbal Protocol Analysis

Hongki Kim,<sup>1</sup> Izak Benbasat<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Clemson University, USA, hongkik@clemson.edu <sup>2</sup>University of British Columbia, Canada, izak.benbasat@sauder.ubc.ca

## Abstract

With more online stores providing recommendations and reviews simultaneously from multiple advice sources—such as recommendation agents, other consumers, and experts—consumers are facing the challenge of deciding how to use wide-ranging and possibly conflicting sets of information to improve their product selection. Although previous studies have investigated the classical decision-making strategies used in preferential choice problems, most of these are not directly applicable to multiple advice source environments. In addition, since most of these studies have mainly focused on a variance model rather than a process model, they cannot fully explain how decision makers reach their product selection decisions. To shed light on the processes that online consumers use in making product selections when using multiple advice sources, this study: (1) explores if, how, and when consumers use consistency as a part of their decision-making strategies through developing process models; (2) identifies the consistency strategies utilized when using multiple advice sources; and (3) proposes a new consistency-based decision-making model. Through concurrent verbal protocol analysis, we identified four consistency strategies and found that the use of consistency strategies increases decision quality more than traditional nonconsistency strategies. Our findings are triangulated through the theoretical lens of cognitive dissonance theory, information search process model, and confirmation bias for rigorous validation. We also describe the theoretical and practical implications of our findings.

Keywords: Multiple Advice Sources, Consistency-Based Decision-Making, Consistency Strategy, Verbal Protocol Analysis, Decision-Making Performance

René Riedl was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on May 6, 2021 and underwent three revisions.

## 1 Introduction

Understanding the decision-making strategies utilized by online consumers in selecting products is one of the key areas of interest to information systems (IS) and ecommerce researchers. Strategy in this context refers to the mental processes involved in information acquisition, selection, utilization, and judgment for effective and efficient decisions in the construction of preferences between alternatives (i.e., preferential choice problems) (Payne et al., 1993). By identifying such strategies, IS scholars have provided theoretical foundations for developing decision aids to support online consumers (Todd & Benbasat, 1992, 1994). To support online consumers’ product selection, some online stores provide advice from multiple sources, such as recommendation agents (RAs), <sup>1</sup> consumers, and experts. For instance, Amazon.com provides both

RAs’ and consumers’ opinions. Likewise, Cnet.com, a third-party electronic product review website, makes advice available from experts and consumers.

Although more information would appear to be desirable, the availability of potentially diverse and divergent advice from multiple sources increases the number of products a consumer needs to examine to make a better decision. Furthermore, this advice may present differing opinions that could undermine consumers’ confidence in the quality of the products being assessed. Faced with multiple advice sources, consumers may make cognitively costly mistakes in how they choose products. Consequently, consumers need to develop and rely on various decision-making strategies to simplify their information processing to adaptively cope with this complexity (Bettman et al., 1998; Butler & Peppard, 1998; Liu & Goodhue, 2012; Lynch Jr et al., 1993; Simon, 1990).

In the last three decades, two streams of research, namely, the internal-attribute and the externalevaluation-oriented perspectives, have been employed in investigating consumers’ decision-making strategies. In the internal-attribute-oriented perspective, researchers have investigated the preferential choice problem in terms of the product attributes that represent a set of alternatives through inherently given values (Bettman et al., 1998; Payne et al., 1993). In the external-evaluation-oriented perspective, researchers have investigated online feedback and advice mechanisms that support the reduction of uncertainties in online shopping. While the internal-attribute-oriented perspective focuses on product attributes that are the given inherent values representing an alternative, the external-evaluation-oriented perspective uses externally generated online reviews, recommendations, and ratings that represent others’ opinions about an alternative. Classical decision-making strategies utilizing inherently given product attributes are not directly applicable to fully explaining online consumers’ utilization of multiple sources when multiple or possibly conflicting external evaluations are available. Thus, in this study, we look beyond the classical decision-making strategies to explore how online consumers reach decisions when they have access to multiple advice sources for making purchasing decisions.

Previous studies have extended the understanding of the impact of external evaluations from multiple advice sources on the behavior of online consumers (Baum & Spann, 2014; Li et al., 2010; Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019; Soll & Larrick, 2009; Xu et al., 2020; Yaniv, 2004; Yaniv et al., 2009). For example, Yaniv et al. (2009) theorized that people tend to underestimate the informative value of independently formed opinions if they appear to conflict with one another while having higher confidence in them when there is consensus among them. Moreover, decision makers tend to discount dissenters and give greater weight to their own opinion than to other people’s opinions (Yaniv, 2004). Soll and Larick (2009) found that people tend to use two basic strategies when revising estimates: (1) choosing between the two estimates or (2) averaging them. They observed that the averaging strategy is the more effective strategy, though people tend to favor one strategy over the other.

However, most online product selection studies to date have investigated only a single source (e.g., either RAs, consumers, or experts) and/or a single type of advice (e.g., either reviews or recommendations). Moreover, they have not directly demonstrated when and how people utilize these heuristic cues in improving decision-making performance across the decisionmaking process. A heuristic is a strategy that ignores part of the available information, with the goal of making decisions more quickly, frugally, and/or accurately than more complex methods (Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier. 2011). Decision-making is a sequence of adaptive mental operations using diverse heuristic cues contingent on an individual’s cognitive capability, task complexity, and social context (Bettman et al., 1998). Most prior studies have mainly focused on studying variance models—which are models that explain the variability of a dependent variable based on its correlation with one or more independent variables— rather than process models, which are models that explain the occurrence of an outcome by identifying the sequence of events preceding it (Shaw & Jarvenpaa, 1997). As the process model is a complementary representation of the variance model for understanding diverse perspectives on phenomena, several IS scholars (Huber et al., 2013; Markus & Robey, 1988; Shaw & Jarvenpaa, 1997) have called for further uses of the process modeling.

Despite such encouragement, most IS research has been dominated by variance models (Shaw & Jarvenpaa, 1997), leaving many questions unanswered. Some of these questions in the domain of multiple advice sources are: (1) When and how is the advice from multiple sources used across the information search processes? (2) How do consumers simplify the cognitive complexity of choosing among products by using multiple advice sources? (3) What are the impacts of consistency (or inconsistency) among advice sources across the information search processes? Consistency refers to the consumer’s perception that there is agreement among multiple advice sources concerning product quality.<sup>2</sup> Thus, by developing process models as complementary representations for understanding online consumers’ use of multiple advice sources across the information search processes, this study attempts to fill these knowledge gaps.

Given the current early state of knowledge on online consumers’ utilization of multiple advice sources simultaneously, it is more appropriate to conduct an exploratory study, which could shed more light on online consumers’ new decision-making strategies than a theory-driven explanatory study. Thus, this study uses verbal protocol analysis (Ericsson & Simon, 1993) to pursue two main objectives: (1) to explore if, how, and when consumers use consistency among multiple advice sources as a part of their decision-making strategy, and (2) to identify consistency-based strategies as process models. Todd and Benbasat (1987) state that verbal protocol analysis is the most appropriate approach to delineate entire decision-making processes with a perspective of consistency-based decision-making, given the exploratory approach taken in this study.

## 2 Literature Review and Theoretical Foundations

## 2.1 Consumer Decision-Making Strategies

Over the decades, a vast body of behavioral decision theories has identified a variety of decision-making strategies. In response to the limited working memory and computational capabilities of rational individuals, scholars have theorized the cost-benefit trade-offs made in selecting a strategy (Bettman et al., 1998; Butler & Peppard, 1998; Liu & Goodhue, 2012; Lynch Jr et al., 1988; Payne et al., 1993; Simon, 1990). While less heuristic strategies are more accurate, they also require more cognitive effort. Therefore, in general, people try to reduce this cognitive effort by relying more on heuristic strategies using simple cognitive cues.

## 2.1.1 Internal-Attribute-Oriented Perspective

The internal-attribute-oriented perspective investigates the preferential choice problem in terms of product attributes that represent an alternative through inherently given values (Bettman et al., 1998). A group of strategies uses more comprehensive processes that use all product attributes. For example, the weighted adding strategy (WADD) sums each product attribute’s weighted value, representing its subjective importance. Although WADD is considered to be a more comprehensive strategy than the others delineated below, it requires more processing capacity. The equal weight strategy (EQW) is a more simplified approach that sums all product attribute values without considering their subjective importance.

Another group of strategies employs a more heuristic process that focuses on one or a few product attributes. Using the lexicographic strategy (LEX), a consumer selects a product with the highest value of the most important product attributes. The elimination-byaspects strategy (EBA) eliminates alternatives below cut-off values assigned for its most important product attributes. This process continues by iterating comparisons of the next important product attributes until a single product remains. Although EBA eliminates alternatives by sequentially processing each product attribute, the satisficing strategy (SAT) sequentially processes each product that appears in the list. That is, if one product does not meet any predetermined cut-off value in all of its attributes, it is dropped from the list and the next product is evaluated. Because each strategy has its strengths and weaknesses, a consumer uses combinations of these strategies contingent on the characteristics of a task (Bettman et al., 1998).

## 2.1.2 External-Evaluation-Oriented Perspective

The internal-attribute-oriented perspective uses product attributes that are inherently given values that represent an alternative. In contrast, the externalevaluation-oriented perspective uses online reviews and ratings that bestow externally generated values representing others’ opinions about an alternative and can be influenced by these others. Online consumers’ utilization of online product recommendations and reviews has received significant attention in the IS literature (Bansal & Voyer, 2000; Benlian et al., 2012; Duhan et al., 1997; East et al., 2008; Hu et al., 2008; Jha & Shah, 2019; Kumar et al. 2006; Park & Lee, 2008; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006; Wang & Benbasat, 2009).

When there are multiple advice sources, there is a good possibility that external evaluations of an alternative may differ among these sources (e.g., differences in ranking position in a recommendation or rating scores in reviews). Thus, consumers are likely to face such conflicts in selecting products. Further, due to the diverse and complementary characteristics (e.g., expertise, benevolence, and preference-matching) of each source (Xu et al., 2020), consumers might want to use multiple sources. They can build a more comprehensive and reliable understanding of products and validate the recommendations and reviews available from any one source. Because experts have high levels of product knowledge, their recommendations and reviews represent in-depth and comprehensive details of product performance (Xu et al., 2020). Consumers’ recommendations and reviews can reflect their experience and satisfaction gained from product use. RAs provide recommendations that match each consumer’s elicited product attribute preferences (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007). Moreover, any recommendation or review source might provide deceptive recommendations or reviews benefiting certain online stores or manufacturers (Kumar et al., 2018; Pfeiffer & Benbasat, 2012; Xiao & Benbasat, 2011; Xu et al., 2020). Hence, to validate the faithfulness and value of reviews or recommendations from a given source, it is advisable for consumers to use multiple sources instead of relying on just one (Xiao & Benbasat, 2015).

## 2.2 Advice-Taking Literature

Several studies outside of the IS literature have investigated how people integrate others’ opinions in making decisions. Advice-taking has been investigated for two decades to improve the understanding of how people integrate others’ opinions in decision-making. As discussed above, example, Yaniv et al. (2009) theorized that people tend to underestimate the informative value of conflicting opinions while having greater confidence when there is consensus among them. Also, while decision makers tend to discount dissenters and give greater weight to their own opinions (i.e., egocentric mode of processing) (Yaniv, 2004), people can improve their accuracy by engaging in a less egocentric mode of processing advisory opinions. Harries et al. (2004) found that individuals use discounting strategies when they encounter extreme opinions and found that it is easier to reinforce discounting strategies with feedback but more difficult to counteract such default strategies (Harries et al., 2004).

A few studies have investigated the impact of quantitative summarization of diverse opinions on individuals’ decision-making. For example, Coba et al. (2019) investigated how different summarizations of rating distributions (i.e., in the form of a number of ratings, mean, variance, skewness, bimodality, or rating origin) impact users’ decision-making. Soll and Larick (2009) found that people tend to use two basic strategies when revising estimates: choosing between two estimates or averaging them. They found that the averaging strategy is more effective but people tend to favor one strategy over the other.

## 2.3 Information Search Process Model

The information process of consumers has been interpreted as a series of activities, defined as “the activities people engage in when searching for, selecting, purchasing, using, evaluating, and disposing of products and services to satisfy their needs and desires” (Baum & Spann, 2014, p. 107). According to the traditional information processing model, these activities comprise five stages: problem recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase, and post-purchase behavior (Butler & Peppard, 1998). Although online consumers’ purchasing processes may differ from the traditional model, extant research has revealed that three major stages in the purchasing process—information search, evaluation of alternatives, and purchasing—are inevitable in any context (Karimi et al., 2010). Previous studies have thus focused on the information search process as a key aspect of the online consumers’ purchasing process and proposed its substages (Johnson et al., 2004; Karimi et al., 2010; Kuhlthau, 1991; Li et al., 2010; Sproule & Archer, 2000). For example, focusing on the role of decision aids in facilitating the information search process, Li et al. (2010) proposed screening as a key component in the information search process, which is defined as “the process of delineating attribute levels and filtering alternatives that fail to meet the criteria” (p. 3), and proposed three substages: pre-screening, screening, and post-screening.

Assuming a limited capacity of cognitive resources and the potential for information when screening alternatives, prior studies have viewed consumers as “cognitive misers” (Lynch Jr. et al., 1988; Roberts & Nedungadi, 1995). Consumers tend to reduce cognitive complexity in decision-making and focus on the processes by which the consideration set is formed and used by consumers for subsequent purchase operations (Lynch Jr. et al., 1988; Roberts & Nedungadi, 1995). The consideration set is defined as the set of products that consumers possess in working memory on a particular purchase occasion (Roberts & Nedungadi, 1995) and is considered to be dependent on the perceptual and memorial processes used by the consumer within the decision-making context (Alba & Chattopadhyay, 1985).

Therefore, fundamental motivations for examining product-relevant information before and after building a consideration set could be varied because of the changes in cognitive load and consumers predetermined attitudes when building a consideration set. Extant research has examined two different modes of information processing—exploratory and confirmatory information-seeking (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 1996; Lee et al., 2011; Nickerson, 1998; Richard & Chandra, 2005). Exploratory informationseeking refers to the behavior of acquiring consumption-relevant information to fulfill consumers’ cognitive stimulation needs out of curiosity (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 1996). Confirmatory information-seeking refers to consumers’ behavior of seeking and overvaluing consumption-relevant information supporting predetermined beliefs to defend their beliefs (Nickerson, 1998). In an online shopping context, exploratory information-seeking has been found in online consumers’ browsing activities in the information search process (Richard & Chandra, 2005). Confirmatory information-seeking has been suggested as a motivational perspective determining consumers’ choice and preference for a certain wordof-mouth source (Lee et al., 2011).

In accordance with extant research, this study proposes the exploration, formulation, and collection stages. The exploration stage refers to a process in which consumers build an overall understanding of a product to decide whether further exploration is warranted. The formulation stage refers to a process in which consumers build a consideration set—namely, the set of products that they find attractive and want to keep in mind for further evaluation in the process of making a final decision (Roberts & Nedungadi, 1995). The collection stage refers to a process in which consumers make an effort to build an in-depth understanding of a product in the consideration set for the product selection decision. We consider the general sequence of the information search process as going forward from “exploration” to “collection,” with “building a consideration set” (or the formulation stage) constituting the transition point between them. In the initial stage of the information search process, consumers are motivated to fulfill their cognitive stimulation needs out of curiosity and engage in exploratory information-seeking. When building a consideration set, they also have predetermined attitudes on considerations because they are adding products worthy of an in-depth examination. Predetermined beliefs about considerations activate the motivation to seek and overvalue information supporting their beliefs. Moreover, since building considerations alleviates information overload, they may have cognitive resources available to process more concrete signals rather than relying on more abstract signals. Thus, building a consideration set comprises a border distinguishing these two motivational perspectives (i.e., exploration and collection) in the purchasing process, which then influences the roles and effects of advice-taking in the decision-making process.

While previous studies of decision-making strategies and advice-taking have explored how people use diverse information and opinions in making decisions, how people use that information and opinions across diverse information search processes has not yet been explored. For example, in the early stage of the information search process, people may underestimate the informative value of conflicting opinions while having more confidence when there is consensus (Yaniv et al., 2009). However, in the later stages of the information search process, people may discount dissenters and give greater weight to their own opinions (Yaniv, 2004) as they build their consideration set. This egocentric mode of processing advisory opinions may lead to more biased decisionmaking and decrease decision-making performance (Harries et al. 2004).

## 2.4 Cognitive Dissonance Theory

Cognitive dissonance theory (Festinger, 1957) postulates that relevant but conflicting cognitions create an aversive motivational state that makes people form and/or change their cognitive system of beliefs to maintain a state of consonance by looking for more cognitive consistency (Gawronski, 2012; Harmon-Jones & Harmon-Jones, 2007). This cognitive consonance concept follows key findings in the advice-taking literature, such as the egocentric mode of processing and the tendency to discount extreme opinions. Therefore, we can apply it to explore how people use multiple advice sources across diverse information search processes.

For example, the cognitive consonance concept can be applied in our study’s context for (1) identifying products that deserve to be further explored, (2) preventing presumably overlooked information (i.e., misunderstandings), and (3) validating one’s beliefs (Gawronski, 2012; Hoch & Ha, 1986; Koller & Salzberger, 2007; Lee et al., 2011; Nickerson, 1998; Pfeiffer & Benbasat, 2012; Quine & Ullian, 1978). First, cognitive consonance can be applied as (1) a heuristic to identify those limited sets of products that are worthy of consideration (Festinger, 1957; Gawronski, 2012; Harmon-Jones & Harmon-Jones, 2007). According to the theory of limited cognitive capacity (Bettman et al., 1998; Chewning Jr & Harrell, 1990; Lang, 2000), people are constrained in fully processing all available information when assessing performance, especially under conditions of high cognitive loads. Second, to prevent the potential misassessment of products, cognitive consonance can be applied to (2) compare recommendations and reviews from multiple sources (Gawronski, 2012; Quine & Ullian, 1978). Suppose a consumer is considering a product that is not in conformity with the recommendations and/or reviews of other sources or a product that has been ignored by the consumer but is highly rated by other sources. In that case, the consumer will likely deliberate this product further to identify and minimize misassessment. Third, consumers can apply cognitive consonance to (3) validate their beliefs by seeking and overvaluing information that confirms their beliefs while avoiding and devaluing disconfirming information. If consumers and other sources are consistent in their product quality assessments, consumers will be more certain about their beliefs about and their understanding of a product. This cognitive bias has been investigated in decision distortion theories (Nickerson, 1998). Among various cognitive biases, confirmation bias—the tendency to search for, interpret, or recall information in a way that confirms one’s beliefs—provides a theoretical lens to understand the egocentric mode of processing and the tendency to discount extreme opinions (Harries et al. 2004; Yaniv 2004).

## 2.5 Developing an Analytic Framework: A Preliminary Process Model

In response to the limited working memory and computational capabilities of rational individuals, scholars have investigated how people use limited cognitive resources in the decision-making process. People generally reduce this cognitive effort by relying on more heuristic strategies using simple cognitive cues.

While one type of decision-making strategy mainly relies on product attributes that are inherently given values and represent alternatives, another type relies more on online reviews and ratings that bestow externally generated values representing others’ opinions about an alternative and can be influenced by these opinions. As discussed above, previous studies on advice-taking and decision-making strategies have shown that people have more confidence when there is consensus among independently formed opinions and that people tend to devalue conflicting opinions (Harries et al. 2004; Yaniv et al. 2009).

The information search process model provides a general overview of decision-making processes, allowing us to explore and identify how consistency among multiple advice sources can be utilized. By tracing and comparing the outcomes of product selection decision-making, this study explores whether different consistency strategies can influence consumers’ decisions. Hence, to explore and identify consistency strategies, this study derives online consumers’ key activities (i.e., events) based on the information search process model to propose a preliminary process model (shown in Figure 1) as an analytic framework to identify consistency-based decision-making. In accordance with the advice-taking literature and cognitive dissonance theory, after accessing advice sources, people may examine alternatives and actively or inactively identify consistency between advice sources as a heuristic cue. People may then use the cue to decide whether to explore the product further or add it to a consideration set to prevent any overlooked information or to validate their decisions by following the egocentric mode of processing and confirmation bias. The final decisionmaking event will be purchasing a product. To trace and capture these decision-making processes under the information search process model, this study applied five events (i.e., accessing an advice source, examining a product, identifying consistency, adding to a consideration set, and purchasing a product) in the preliminary framework. As the information search process model consists of exploration, formulation, and collection stages, which represent a general sequence of information search stages but not a strictly linear process (Kuhlthau, 1991), the sequence of events (i.e., accessing advice source, examining a product, and identifying consistency) in the exploration and collection stages is a somewhat recursive rather than strictly linear process.

According to the advice-taking literature, confirmation bias literature, and cognitive dissonance theory, a decision maker can use consistency as a cue (1) to identify products that deserve to be further explored (i.e., discounting extreme opinions), (2) to prevent presumably overlooked information (i.e., more confidence in the consensus), and (3) to validate one’s decision by pursuing cognitive consistency (i.e., egocentric mode of processing). Hence, in the exploration stage, online consumers will examine a product by accessing advice sources to build an overall understanding of a product for further exploration. In examining a product, a decision maker will proactively or reactively identify consistency among advice sources and use consistency as a strategic cue in their decision making process. For example, in the formulation stage, the decision maker will proactively or reactively use consistency as a cue (1) to find an attractive product that they would like to add to the consideration set for further exploration and (2) to prevent presumably overlooked information by building more confidence in the consensus among opinions and discounting an extreme opinion. Suppose the product has already been added to the consideration set. In that case, the decision maker will proactively or reactively use consistency (3) to support and validate their decision to add the product to the consideration set as an egocentric mode of processing. Therefore, after adding a product to a consideration set in the collection stage, the decision maker can build an in-depth understanding of the product by proactively or reactively identifying consistency and reexamining products by accessing advice sources. Consequently, online consumers will respond to consistency by building a consideration set and/or making a purchasing decision.

Our preliminary analytic framework provides a general overview of the information search process. It allows us to explore and identify how recommendation consistency and review consistency are utilized across information search processes. Moreover, by tracing and comparing the outcomes of product selection decisionmaking, this study explores the role of consistency at different stages in the consumer decision process. Specifically, by connecting decision-making strategies, advice-taking theories, cognitive dissonance theory, and cognitive bias literature in the information processing model, we postulate that online consumers’ decisionmaking strategies in using multiple advice sources can be classified based on (1) how to identify consistency among multiple advice sources, (2) how to process the consistency across information search processes, and (3) how to respond to the consistency in building a consideration set and in making a decision across information search processes.

Thus, this study postulates that consistency among multiple sources is embedded in consumers’ information search processes and utilized as a part of their decision-making strategies, which consequently influence behaviors associated with product selection.

Overall, the preliminary process model provides an analytical framework as a general overview of the information search process and allows us to explore and identify how consistency can be used across the decisionmaking process. While this framework is not a definite final theory, it represents a starting point for further exploration of consistency-based decision-making.

## 3 Process Tracing Method

## 3.1 Protocol Analysis: Concurrent Verbalization for Data Collection

In their efforts to understand why and how changes occur in the decision-making process, IS researchers have examined changes in dependent variables through variance models. These examinations have used deductive and confirmatory approaches based on systematically varying the independent variables. In contrast, process models represent the occurrence of an outcome by identifying the sequence of preceding events (Shaw & Jarvenpaa, 1997). That is, process models represent temporal-and-sequential relationships as key mechanisms of research domains in which one event occurs after another event. Therefore, they show how certain combinations of events, in a particular sequence, lead to certain outcomes. Each event in a process model is necessary but not sufficient to cause the outcome (Seddon, 1997). The temporal-and-sequential relationships can be examined through an inductive and exploratory approach with process tracing methods (Todd & Benbasat, 1987). Many disciplines consider process tracing methods to be effective methodologies to observe and access processes occurring between the onset of a stimulus and a response to it (Ericsson & Simon, 1984; Russo et al., 1989). In summary, using a process tracing approach could offer a comprehensive means of evaluating and understanding the decisionmaking process that allows for the extraction of the appropriate design information and the evaluation of the IT artifact (Todd & Benbasat, 1987).

Over the past decades, a number of diverse protocol analysis techniques have been developed and continuously enhanced: for example, (1) eyemovement tracking (Just & Carpenter, 1976; Lohse & Johnson, 1996; Russo, 1978a, 1978b; Russo & Rosen, 1975); (2) information display boards (Arch et al., 1978; Payne, 1976; Wilkins, 1965); (3) computerized process tracing, such as Mouselab (Payne et al., 1993), ISLab (Cook & Swain 1993), ISCube (Tabatabai, 1998), and MouseTrace (Jasper & Shapiro, 2002); (4) phased narrowing (Jasper & Levin, 2001; Levin & Jasper, 1995); (5) active information search (Huber et al., 1997; Williamson et al., 2000); and (6) verbal protocols (Ericsson & Simon, 1984; Newell & Simon,

1972; Nisbett & Wilson, 1977). Among these varieties of process tracing methods, verbal protocols (i.e., protocol analysis of the thought processes of a decision maker by using verbal cues) have been considered as a method to access the stages of a decision maker’s information processing. Questions that might be asked include what information is examined, how the manipulations conducted on the input stimulus are processed, and what evaluations or assessments are made by the problem solver, which represent major interests in IS studies (Ericsson & Simon, 1984; Todd & Benbasat 1987).

In addition, because most decision-aid systems are implemented and interact with users in online shopping stores, video clips recording users’ activities on an experimental website provide access to additional insights available from nonverbal cues. Therefore, protocol analysis recording of verbal and nonverbal cues during experimental tasks has been used in IS research (Burton-Jones & Meso, 2006; Ericsson & Simon, 1984; Kim et al., 2000).

Verbal protocol analysis comprises retrospective and concurrent verbalization (Bouwman et al., 1987; Ericsson & Simon, 1984; Todd & Benbasat, 1987). The retrospective verbalization method acquires verbal cues from the long-term memory of problem solvers by asking them to “recall their processes” after a specific problem-solving task. A concurrent verbalization method gives simultaneous access to thought processes by asking problem solvers to “talk aloud” while performing the task. Given the different roles of short-term and long-term memory, retrospective verbalization could be distorted when problem solvers try to rationalize their behavior. It could even fail to represent concrete and detailed information that was not internalized in long-term memory but processed only in short-term memory. However, retrospective verbalization is a less obtrusive approach than concurrent verbalization and could interfere with an ongoing problem-solving process. Given the strengths and weaknesses of each method, retrospective verbalization has been recommended for less sensitive and less complicated tasks, whereas concurrent verbalization is recommended for more sensitive and more complicated tasks. In addition, to prevent intrusiveness, which could alter a problem-solving process, Ericsson and Simon (1984) suggested a less obtrusive manner called “talk aloud.” In “talk aloud,” a problem solver is asked to speak based on the content of short-term memory alone and without the use of a recoding process. That is, researchers should not directly push problem solvers to explain why they are performing concurrent verbalization, and the main contents of the task should not be pictorial representations requiring a recoding process for understanding (Ericsson & Simon, 1984; Todd & Benbasat, 1987).

Under the concurrent verbalization approach, the participants verbalize the thought processes underlying their decisions. The recorded audio is transcribed, segmented, and encoded to obtain a trace of the decision-making process. While other protocol analysis techniques (e.g., eye tracking, mouse trace, etc.) have been developed and enhanced, these approaches have limitations in directly accessing an individual’s thought process. To observe how and why individuals access certain information in making a decision and to identify decision-making processes and strategies, we need to access in-depth decision-making processes (e.g., how to use information, why access the information, etc.) rather than just information accessed during the decision-making process.

Due to the strength of verbal protocol analysis for accessing the decision-making process, in the decision-making literature, concurrent verbalization has been used to investigate the individual’s decisionmaking process. Sanderson (1990) applied verbal protocol analysis to explore the decision-making involved in process control and transportation problems. Ball et al. (1998) explored the strategies used by people to solve resource-allocation problems and identified the solve-and-schedule (SAS) strategy with concurrent verbal protocol analysis. Crawford et al. (1999) used verbal protocol analysis to investigate the working process of industrial schedulers. Wezel and Jorna (2009) explored the shunting operation decision-making process at the Netherlands Railways. And Gavirneni and Isen (2010) explored inventory decision-making problems and identified the newsvendor decision makers’ thought process. With these studies as the methodological background, the current study adapts verbal protocol analysis to an individual’s decision-making process in the multipleadvice-source context.

Because examining diverse advice from multiple sources under potential information overload requires substantial time and cognitive resources, it is critical to concurrently access the problem-solving process during a purchasing decision. If the purchasing process is captured after the task is completed, the consumer might consciously or unconsciously recreate a distorted memory or rationalize both the purchasing process and the decision. Thus, to explore and capture the information and strategies used for product selection, this exploratory study uses a concurrent verbalization protocol analysis (i.e., “talk aloud”) in a lab-experiment context instead of a retrospective verbalization protocol analysis.

## 3.2 Experimental Design

## 3.2.1 Design of the Online Store

In the laboratory study we conducted, an online store that was developed specifically for this study was used as its platform. We used two product categories—a laptop and a digital camera—to investigate high and low product knowledge and ensure the generalizability of our findings. <sup>3</sup> To enhance mundane realism (i.e., shaping the similarity of experimental events to real experience), we selected laptops and digital cameras sold on Amazon.com, a highly popular online store.

The online product recommendations and reviews from consumers and experts were adopted from real product recommendations and reviews from Amazon.com and Cnet.com, a well-known third-party website featuring professional reviews of electronic devices. This study screened for products with at least 10 reviews from consumers on Amazon.com and at least one review from experts on Cnet.com to provide realistic and valid reviews from both consumers and experts. We thus built two databases containing 64 laptops and 64 digital cameras, respectively, sold on Amazon.com. We added product attributes and reviews to these databases. To control the amount of information in the reviews from consumers and experts, we restricted the reviews to an average of 250 words each, which is the average word count of reviews on Amazon.com.<sup>4</sup>

A recommendation agent (RA) that generates fit scores from the product preferences elicited by each participant was developed based on WADD, which is known to deliver better decision quality than other strategies (Bettman et al., 1998; Xu et al., 2020). To receive recommendations from the RA, participants first provided a preference value and an importance score for each attribute (see Figure 2 above). Using this input, the RA generated a fit score <sup>5</sup> for each product in the database and provided recommendations from the highest to lowest fit score.

As an exploratory study, the presentation format of recommendations and reviews was adopted from Amazon. Recommendations from up to three sources could be viewed separately in the same format on the same screen to allow participants to compare recommendations from different sources (the RA, consumers, and experts) and prevent the effects of different interface designs (see Figure 3).

Participants could utilize the advice sources at their discretion by requesting information (i.e., clicking a source name to see its recommendations and reviews) from any or all of these sources. Participants could select the number of advice sources and the ratings and reviews for each product. They could access a single product, multiple ones, or all the products. For the chosen product, they could read expert or consumer reviews only or both types. To prevent any effects from the order in which recommendation sources were displayed, we randomized the placement of the three sources on the screen. Each recommendation source provided its top five recommendations based on ratings and fit scores in the form of tables (from highest to lowest score), including a product picture, product attributes, and hyperlinks to consumers’ and experts reviews. When the participants clicked on the hyperlinks of reviews from each source, a pop-up screen containing a rating score and comments from consumers or experts appeared on display (see Figure 4). Participants could navigate freely between reviews by clicking hyperlinks. If participants wanted to see all products sold in the online store, they could choose the “all products” section. ## 3.2.2 Manipulation of Experimental Treatments

This study relied on two constructs—recommendation consistency and review consistency—to explore how online consumers use the similarities and/or differences of the ranking position in the recommendation and rating score in the review. Recommendation consistency was operationalized as a binary variable representing whether the product is ranked among the top five recommendations by any two or more of the sources (see additional details below). Review consistency was operationalized as a continuous variable representing the differences in rating scores (1-5) in the reviews between experts and consumers;<sup>6</sup> the smaller the difference, the higher the review consistency of the product.

To capture the potential impact of diverse combinations of similarities and/or differences among the recommendation sources on the decision-making process, we generated four recommendation consistency conditions. These conditions contained (1) two products common to both the RA and expert recommendations, (2) two products common to both the RA and consumer recommendations, (3) two products common to both the expert and consumer recommendations, and (4) no products common to any two sources.<sup>7</sup> In Table 1, we describe how the recommendation consistency conditions were implemented. Hence, a 4 (consistency) × 2 (product knowledge) factorial design with two between-subject factors was used to represent a variety of realistic contexts (see Table 2).<sup>8</sup>

The experimental procedures were as follows. First, prequestionnaires for perceived task involvement and product knowledge were administered to control for confounding effects. Next, participants were trained to “talk aloud”—verbalizing every thought in their mind as if they were talking to themselves—using two standard training tasks (Ericsson & Simon, 1984). After participants demonstrated that they fully understood how to “talk aloud,” they were instructed on using the online store interface (e.g., eliciting personal preferences on product attributes, hyperlinks to read reviews from experts and consumers, and hyperlinks to access each advice source and all products tab). Then, participants watched a short video clip providing a “talk aloud” example that used equivalent interfaces. To prevent a learning effect from the video clip, it did not contain verbalization of any consistency or any decision-making strategies. After participants confirmed their understanding of “talk aloud” procedure and the online store interface, the main experimental task was administered. Participants were asked to select the best laptop or digital camera based on their interests. All of the verbalizations and activities during the main task were recorded. After finishing the task, participants completed post-questionnaires for perceived recommendation consistency, perceived deception, and demographic information (see Appendix A).

## 3.4 Coding Schema Development and Coding Procedures

Verbal cues from concurrent verbalizations during the main task were the major source of data. Nonverbal cues from video clips that presented participants’ activities on the experimental website provided supplementary data for more comprehensive and complete tracing (Rist, 1989). We developed a coding schema based on advice from Boyatzis (1998) and Ericsson and Simon (1984). We used an episode— small, self-contained phases of highly organized activity (Newell & Simon, 1972)—as the unit of the verbal protocol in developing the coding schema.

We focused on three major decision-making processes—information search, evaluation of alternatives, and selection decision—as the basic framework for guiding the initial coding schema (Karimi et al., 2010). Chi (1997) suggested that a coding schema can be developed based on the topic domain or the research questions. Based on this suggestion, to capture and trace the roles of recommendation and review consistency in making product selection decisions, we developed substages of each process by categorizing concurrent verbalizations from two pretests with 20 participants (see Figure 5).

Developing a coding schema is an inductive and iterative process involving multiple rounds with different data sets (Strauss et al., 1997). To improve objectivity and validate the completeness and accuracy of the coding schema, two coders—the first author and a graduate student who had investigated online consumer decision-making processes—classified the verbal protocols for information search, evaluation of alternatives, and product selection. Then, they classified the verbal protocols representing the utilization of recommendations, reviews, and consistency in each process. The coding schema was updated when the two coders found an additional category relevant to the task but not included in the initial coding schema. Another coder—the second author, who has investigated online consumer behavior and decision-making for over four decades—validated the completeness and accuracy of the updated coding schema. These processes were iterated in five separate rounds. Because no new categories arose after the first three coding rounds, we concluded that theoretical saturation had been reached (Strauss & Corbin, 1994).

We confirmed our coding schema after the fifth round; at that point, all three coders agreed that all the taskrelevant verbal protocols could be classified in the coding schema. There were no other relevant coding categories for understanding the product selection processes. The final coding schema, with descriptions and examples, is listed in Table 4.

The 64 transcripts and video clips from the participants were provided to coders to conduct the final coding for analysis. Three coders were engaged in the coding process to improve the objectivity and reliability of the coding. Two of the coders were graduate students interested in protocol analysis and the product selection process who were not involved in the earlier coding development process. The third was the first author.

The coding procedure was performed in four stages: (1) instruction, (2) main coding, (3) feedback, and (4) supplementary coding. In the instruction stage, the first author briefly explained to the other two coders the general theoretical background of the product selection process and the coding schema as a framework for them to understand the substages of the process. After the coders demonstrated that they fully understood the coding schema, they were instructed on how to assign verbal protocols in a transcript to categories in the coding schema by referencing a video clip of the transcript. Then, the coders were provided with sample-coding tasks to check their understanding. After two sample coding tasks, all coders were able to distinguish and assign verbal protocols to the categories of the coding schema. We used transcripts and video clips from pretests during instruction to ensure independent coding and prevent a learning effect from transcripts and video clips. After instruction in the coding schema was completed, all the transcripts and video clips of the 64 participants were sent to each coder for the next stage: main coding.

In the main coding stage, the coders were asked not to discuss any issues regarding the coding process or associated topics to ensure independent coding. The main coding process took around three weeks. We calculated the intercoder reliability score (Krippendorff, 2018) to validate the objectivity, reproducibility, and reliability of the coding done by the three independent coders. Because the calculated reliability score (0.83) exceeded the recommended cut-off value (0.70), we concluded that the reliability of the coding was assured.

In the feedback stage, the three coders met to discuss the completeness and accuracy of the coding schema. They agreed that they could not find any instance of a verbal protocol that was relevant but did not fit in the coding schema or a verbal protocol that represented multiple coding categories. Thus, we concluded that the validity and theoretical saturation of the coding schema had been demonstrated. Lastly, in the supplementary coding stage, to improve the traceability of participants reviews and recommendation processing of each product, the first author marked an information source and a product name on each verbal protocol assigned to the coding schema. Thus, through such coding procedures, this study was able to trace which product and source were selected during the product selection process and when and how participants processed recommendations and reviews from a specific source.

## 4 Main Analysis: Consistency Strategies

To explore how online consumers cope with similar and/or different external evaluations from multiple sources, we identified when and how participants identified consistency. Examining consistency is inferred when a participant identifies a product present in two recommendation sources <sup>12</sup> and/or identifies agreement between expert and consumer reviews of the same product by examining them.

All the verbalizations from 64 participants were categorized in accordance with the coding schema. To analyze an individual’s decision-making process, we segmented verbalizations into sentences. Each sentence was categorized in a coding schema and organized chronologically. For simplicity, we grouped adjacent sentences categorized in the same coding category. This procedure identified not only whether individuals used consistency but also when and how they used specific decision-making processes (see additional details in Appendix B).

Protocol analysis shows that consumers (1) access advice sources simultaneously or sequentially, (2) examine the consistency proactively or reactively, and (3) utilize the consistency before or after adding a product to their consideration set. Based on this, we identified four consistency-based strategies (see Table 5). A consideration set refers to products that consumers find attractive and wish to keep in mind for further evaluation during the decision-making process (Roberts & Nedungadi, 1995). While 81% of participants examined consistency, the other 19% of participants used classical decision-making strategies, mainly heuristics (e.g., EBA, SAT), without paying attention to consistency (see Table 6).

To improve the objectivity and reliability of the classification of the consistency strategies, three coders were engaged in the classification process. Two of the coders were graduate students who were not involved in the earlier coding processes. The third was the first author. The procedure followed the earlier coding process in assigning verbal protocols to categories in the coding schema. The coders were asked to assign each participant’s decision-making process to one of five strategy categories—four consistency strategies and one nonconsistency strategy. Because the calculated reliability score (0.79) exceeded the recommended cut-off value (0.70) (Krippendorff, 2018), we concluded that the reliability of the classification had been demonstrated (see Step 4 in Figure 5).

## 4.1 Identifying Consistency Strategies

## 4.1.1 Seeking Consistency Strategy

In the process of making their product selection decisions, some participants simultaneously accessed all advice sources and proactively looked for consistency before examining any alternatives in detail. They started with the intent of “seeking” consistency to identify a product that deserved to be further examined. In the seeking consistency strategy, participants began by simultaneously accessing all three advice sources and looking for any products featured in multiple sources. Upon finding a product, the features (attributes) of that product were scrutinized. If they were found to be satisfactory, the product was added to a consideration set for further and more focused evaluation (see Figure 6 above).

For example, Participant No. 12 used a seeking consistency strategy. After disclosing her preferences, she first clicked on all three sources (i.e., RA, experts, and consumers). Without first examining any alternatives in detail, she searched for any products present in all three sources. As soon as she found two such products, she started examining their attributes. Because one of the two products fit her needs, she added it to her consideration set. After using a seeking consistency strategy, she examined all other alternatives from top to bottom by checking product attributes and reviews. After adding two more alternatives to her consideration set and comparing them to the product present in multiple recommendations identified at the beginning, she decided to choose that product as her final choice. Her key verbalizations are described in Table 7. Another example involving Participant No. 51 is presented in Table 8.

## 4.1.2 Anchoring Consistency Strategy

A group of participants also proactively examined consistency but only after sequentially accessing an advice source and examining its alternatives, and after adding a few alternatives to a consideration set. In the anchoring consistency strategy, participants proactively accessed recommendations from other sources to seek products they had already added to their consideration set (i.e., consistent) based on examining alternatives from a given source. If they found such a product, they used consistency to support their beliefs about the product and became more confident in the choice(s) they had already made. Otherwise, they continued examining other products (see Figure 7). For example, Participant No. 27 used an anchoring strategy. After providing preferences, she first clicked on the RA. After examining the recommendations of the RA, she added two products to her consideration set after first examining the experts’ and consumers’ reviews. Then, she clicked on all other sources, as well as experts’ and consumers’ reviews, and checked whether those other sources also recommended these two products. Because she was assigned to the condition in which the RA and consumer recommendations had two products in common, one of her choices was recommended by a consumer. After identifying recommendation consistency, she decided on the product they had in common as her final choice without further examination of the other alternatives. Although this participant did not examine other alternatives exclusively recommended from other sources, some other participants using the anchoring strategy examined other alternatives after identifying recommendation consistency. ## 4.1.3 Deliberating Consistency Strategy

A deliberating consistency strategy is a consumer’s reactive attempt to identify and assess overlooked or presumably misappraised information about a newly identified product present in multiple recommendations that was previously examined but not added to a consideration set. Participants using a deliberating consistency strategy started their information search process without an initial intention of identifying products present in multiple sources. Therefore, they sequentially accessed advice sources and examined alternatives. However, when they realized that they had identified a product that they had seen or examined earlier from another source (but not previously added to their consideration set), they now deliberated on this product mentioned by multiple sources (i.e., reexamined product information). This deliberation was an effort to minimize their potential misappraisal of a product by exploring whether they had previously overlooked product information<sup>13</sup> (see Figure 8).

For example, Participant No. 3 (Table 8) used a deliberating consistency strategy. After her preferences were elicited, she first clicked on the RA. After examining the recommendations, she added an alternative to her consideration set. As the second source, she chose “experts” and examined the alternatives from the top down. Because she was assigned to the condition in which there were two products common to both the RA and expert recommendations, there were two alternatives. However, none of them had been added to her consideration set. During her examination of alternatives from top to bottom, she realized there was a product common to both the RA and expert recommendations. As soon as she realized this recommendation consistency, she carefully deliberated on this alternative and added it to her consideration set.

## 4.1.4 Validating Consistency Strategy

In the validating consistency strategy, after sequentially accessing advice sources, participants reactively identified that another source had also recommended a product that, based on a previous source, they had already added to their consideration set. Therefore, they stopped the examination of the other products in the consideration set and focused instead on this product featured in multiple sources and engaged in searching for more indepth information about it. In other words, although participants had intended to examine other products in their consideration sets in search of a better-quality selection, they interpreted the identification of consistency of a given product as a cue to validate their choice of the product that was already in their consideration set (see Figure 9).

For example, Participant No. 34 used the validating consistency strategy. After providing preferences, she began with expert recommendations. After examining them, she added three alternatives to her consideration set. Next, she chose RA and examined the alternatives there from top to bottom. In the middle of this examination, she realized that one of the alternatives in her consideration set was also recommended by RA. Rather than continue examining other alternatives in the consideration set, she engaged in an in-depth examination of the product common to these two sources and validated her choice in adding it to the consideration set. After examining the other sources, she chose this product as her final choice. Key verbalizations are described in Table 13. Another example of Participant No. 64 is presented in Table 14.

## 4.2 Consistency-Based Decision-Making Model

Through concurrent verbal protocol analysis, this study identified four consistency strategies represented by four process models that complement and enrich the variance models typically utilized to investigate online consumers’ decision-making in IS research (Sabherwal & Robey, 1995; Todd & Benbasat, 1987). To provide a general overview of the information search process in which consistent product recommendation triggers a user’s cognitive process of consistency among advice sources, which in turn determines the user’s behavioral reaction across the decision-making process, this study proposes a consistency-based decision-making model (see Figure 10).

The model shows that consumers can access advice sources simultaneously or sequentially to perceive consistency among multiple advice sources. Moreover, consumers proactively or reactively examine consistency. In identifying consistency, our protocol analysis shows three consistency strategies, called seeking, anchoring, and deliberating. These three consistency strategies show how and when online consumers use consistency in decision-making processes. After perceiving consistency, consumers can use consistency as a cue that validates their behavioral reaction to consistency, such as adding the consistent product to a consideration set and/or purchasing the product. This validating consistency strategy shows how consumers simplify the cognitive complexity of choosing among products in multiple advice sources by adding the product that is featured consistently to their consideration set; it also reveals the impact of consistency among multiple advice sources on decision-making processes. Our consistency-based decision-making model could potentially lead to an update of the classical variance model of consumer decision-making strategies.

## 4.3 Theoretical Perspectives for Triangulation

Although this study identifies consistency strategies across decision-making processes, our explorations need to be interpreted and supported by analysis through a theoretical lens. Behavioral decision theories have shown that conflicting cognitions (i.e., cognitive dissonance) are a major determinant of avoiding and/or accepting potentially biased information. Moreover, the information search process is a major part of a decision-making strategy to extend the state of knowledge of a particular product (Butler & Peppard, 1998; Johnson et al., 2004; Karimi et al., 2010; Klein, 1998; Kuhlthau, 1991; Li et al., 2010; Sproule & Archer, 2000). Hence, to triangulate and validate our understanding of consistency strategies, this study uses confirmation bias (Nickerson, 1998), with cognitive dissonance theory and the information search process model for further triangulations. A summary of consistency strategies with theoretical triangulations is presented in Table 15.

## 4.3.1 Cognitive Dissonance Theory and the Information Search Process Model

In accordance with cognitive dissonance theory and the information search process model, consistency strategies can be conceptualized according to whether consistency is used in the exploration or the collection stage to reduce cognitive dissonance. For example, one group of people might use consistency to improve their understanding of a product in order to make a decision about whether they should engage in further exploration of the product and potentially add it to their consideration set, while another group might use consistency as evidence to supports their in-depth understanding of a product after adding it to their consideration set. That is, an individual’s utilization of consistency is determined by the interplay of a required cognitive resource (their limited working memory) in the information search process, as well as their computational capabilities, which is a key assumption in behavioral decision theories (Bettman et al., 1998; Chewning & Harrell, 1990; Lang, 2000).

For example, in the seeking consistency strategy, the major motivation is to reduce numerous alternatives to a manageable number by using a heuristic to identify products with consistency. Participants initially used their limited cognitive capacity to identify products that were present in more than one source (i.e., consistency). Under the assumption that such products are of higher quality, using consistency can potentially yield superior recommendations with less cognitive effort.

In the anchoring consistency strategy, the major motivation is supporting one’s confidence in a product’s quality by finding and interpreting information in ways that confirm existing beliefs or expectations. Hence, participants used the anchoring strategy to test the hypothesis that the quality of a product already included in their consideration set is satisfactory. They sought consistency to build their confidence in the quality of the product(s) in their consideration set. They also used consistency as a heuristic instead of performing a full in-depth examination of all the data on the attributes of the alternatives in the consideration set, which is in line with the predictions of the theory of limited cognitive capacity (Bettman et al., 1998). Cognitive consistency can be used to confirm the quality of a product already in the consideration set through the interpretation of recommendations featuring that product.

In the deliberating consistency strategy, although participants were more focused on developing an overall understanding of recommended products in order to find attractive ones to add to their consideration sets, they were now made aware of potential misappraisals by identifying the consistency of products (i.e., identified as being present in more than one source) that had not been added to their consideration set previously. Hence, to reduce uncertainty about product quality and minimize misunderstanding in their cognitive belief system, participants reexamined such products after reactively identifying recommendation consistencies.

In the validating consistency strategy, participants were motivated to find and accept self-supporting evidence. To confirm their previous choice of adding an alternative to a consideration set, individuals tend to accept consistency as valid evidence that justifies the collection of in-depth information about the alternative. However, inconsistency in the validating consistency strategy may cause cognitive dissonance and make individuals avoid alternatives for which no confirmatory evidence is found.

## 4.3.2 Confirmation Bias

In addition to cognitive dissonance theory and the information search process model, there are alternative theories that might explain consistency strategies in using multiple sources. For example, studies in various fields have shown that cognitive biases can distort consumers’ decision-making processes. Confirmation bias—the tendency to search for, interpret, or recall information in a way that confirms one’s beliefs—is a cognitive bias that provides a theoretical lens through which to understand anchoring and validating consistency strategies (Nickerson, 1998). People use these strategies after adding a product to a consideration set as a way to test their hypothesis that the quality of the product already included in their consideration set is satisfactory and/or to build their confidence in the quality of the product. However, confirmation bias cannot explain other strategies, such as seeking and deliberating consistency strategies. People who use these strategies have not examined the product, built the hypothesis that the quality of a product is satisfactory, nor added the product to their consideration set. For example, people using the deliberating strategy change their previous belief that the quality of a product is inferior and decide to add it to their consideration set.

## 5 Supplementary Analysis: Impact of Consistency Strategies on Decision-Making Performance

As a supplementary analysis, this study investigates whether the application of consistency strategies leads to better perceived decision-making performance (i.e., perceived decision quality and perceived decision effort) in product selection. In accordance with the information search process model (Kuhlthau, 1991) and theory of limited cognitive capacity (Bettman et al., 1998; Chewning Jr. & Harrell, 1990; Lang, 2000), consumers can efficiently and effectively improve product selection performance by means of utilizing extra information (i.e., consistency among multiple advice sources) within the limits of their cognitive capacity. From this perspective, consistency strategies may increase decision quality and decrease decision effort by decreasing the number of alternatives and increasing the amount of information considered.

First, to explore the impact of consistency strategies on the performance of product selection decision-making, we performed a t-test that compares perceived decision quality and decision effort between participants who utilized consistency strategies and those who did not utilize them. Results show that the participants who utilized consistency strategies perceived higher decision quality than those who did not (M = 5.06 vs. 4.61, t(62) = 1.789, p = 0.039, Cohen’s d = 0.780). In addition, participants who utilized consistency strategies perceived lower decision effort than those who did not (M = 4.87 vs. 4.02, t(62) = 3.157, p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.840). Overall, this analysis shows that the use of consistency strategies leads to better perceived decision-making performance (see Figure 11).

Following the results, we compared the impacts of consistency strategies on perceived decision-making performance. Since participants utilized consistency proactively (i.e., seeking and validating) or reactively (anchoring and deliberating), we performed an analysis of covariance that compared perceived decision quality and decision efforts between proactive and reactive consistency strategies after controlling for the effects of perceived product knowledge and perceived task involvement. The results show that the participants who utilized proactive consistency strategies perceived higher decision quality than those who utilized reactive consistency strategies (M = 5.52 vs. 4.89, F(1,48) = 8.346, p = 0.006, <sup>2</sup> = 0.148) (see Table 16. However, there is no statistical difference in perceived decision efforts between proactive and reactive consistency strategies (M = 4.71 vs. 4.93, F(1,48) = 0.774, p = 0.383, <sup>2</sup> = 0.016). Overall, our findings show that proactive consistency strategies lead to better decision quality than reactive consistency strategies, while there is no difference in decision efforts between proactive and reactive consistency strategies (see Figure 12).

## 6 Discussion

This study has both theoretical and practical implications. From a theoretical perspective, we identify the decision-making strategies used in an environment of multiple recommendation and review sources through process models and propose a consistency-based decision-making model to open up traditional variance models. This is important because previous research has primarily focused on the impact of such information from a single source through the variance model; however, process models provide a complementary representation of the variance model for understanding diverse perspectives of decisionmaking. To date, online consumers’ utilization of diverse recommendations and reviews from multiple sources has been largely ignored. Although recent IS studies have recognized the need to examine the utilization and impact of multiple sources on product selection decision-making performance (Baum & Spann, 2014; Li et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2020), these studies have mainly focused on studying variance models. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that identifies the use of consistencies among multiple sources across the exploration and collection stages in the information search process through a process model and proposes a consistency-based decision-making model.

Through concurrent verbal protocol analysis, this study examines the thus-far unexplored intervening processes of decision-making and proposes process models representing online consumers’ use of multiple advice sources mainly relying on consistency among sources. Although research has emphasized the need to understand the decision-making process in extracting appropriate information for the design and evaluation of decision-aid systems (Todd & Benbasat, 1987), few studies have examined the decision-making process in terms of the use of multiple sources or types (Lee et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2020). Through a rigorous, multiround, multicoder coding procedure, this study provides guidelines for using concurrent verbalization as an exploratory approach for theory building. It also demonstrates the implications of a process-tracing approach by revealing the differential impact of recommendation and review consistency strategies across the exploration and collection stages in information search processes. Because the use of consistency is an intrinsic process occurring in an unobservable decision-making process, concurrent verbalization is a valid method to trace and examine the intrinsic process, which has been deductively assumed to be present.

Through the analysis, this study identifies four consistency strategies (seeking, anchoring, deliberating, and validating) that more than 81% of participants used during the product selection process. The remaining 19% of participants used classical decision-making strategies, mainly heuristic strategies (e.g., EBA, SAT). The seeking consistency strategy is used to identify a product that deserves further focused examination. People invest their limited cognitive capacity in identifying products that are present in more than one source (i.e., consistency) because these products are presumably of higher quality, and fewer cognitive resources are potentially needed to find superior recommendations. Consumers use the deliberating consistency strategy to correct their misappraisal of a product. When they initially fail to add a product to their consideration set only to find it is recommended by other sources, they carefully reexamine its attributes and reviews and then add it to their consideration set. Anchoring and validating consistency strategies are used to validate and support one’s beliefs about and understanding of a product. After adding a product to their consideration set, people tend to find evidence to test their hypothesis that the quality of a product already in their consideration set is satisfactory and/or become more confident in the quality of the product. When people encounter consistency, they are more likely to choose a frequently encountered product as their final decision. Thus, this study will help researchers better understand online consumers’ process of product selection and their utilization of recommendations and reviews from multiple sources.

From a practical perspective, this study improves our understanding of online consumers’ processes of product selection decision-making, including the use of information sources as part of this process, which in turn forms a basis for designing better decision aids. As online consumers are developing new decisionmaking strategies to cope with multiple advice sources, online stores need to provide decision aids that can support their strategies. Since the strategy restrictiveness of decision aids can decrease online consumers’ intention to use a decision aids system (Wang & Benbasat, 2009), decision aids need to support decision-making strategies by providing relevant information, such as consistency, and/or functionality supporting the use of the information across information search processes rather than restricting their strategies. Accordingly, online stores can help consumers (by offering appropriate decisional support functionalities under the users’ control) identify consistency among multiple advice sources. They can accomplish this by highlighting consistency among advice sources and/or providing the similarity/differences scores among reviews for the various products in the full set of alternatives at appropriate stages during the information search process. For example, to support the seeking consistency strategy, online stores could provide decision aids to provide a list of products recommended by two or more advice sources without the user necessarily choosing an anchor product to start from. To support the anchoring and/or deliberating consistency strategy, decision aids could present the consistency of an alternative chosen by the user with those in other sources after the product is examined but before consumers add it to their consideration set. Finally, to support the validating consistency strategy, decision aids could notify consumers about the consistency between other alternatives and those already included in their consideration set.

These consistency-identification tools supporting online consumers’ use of consistency are candidates for further empirical examination. However, this does not mean that consistency strategies would always lead to better objective decision performance (which is difficult to determine in preferential choice problems anyway). If there is an RA that captures the users preferences well and there is the possibility of fake reviews from experts or other consumers, users should not discard recommended options that fit with their preferences. For example, an RA-only situation can be good enough when users are experienced with the product and understand their needs with a high degree of accuracy and confidence. Given that experts are highly knowledgeable and other consumers reflect the wisdom of the crowd, one might presume that their advice is likely to be just as good. However, experts and other consumers do not know the true needs of the particular individual user, and experts’ and consumers’ reviews can potentially be faked or biased by the entity (e.g., online store) providing them. Therefore, relying on experts and consumers might lead to worse decision-making than the RA-only situation. However, if this is not the case, experts’ and other consumers’ recommendations could be helpful for users. In addition, an RA without other advice sources may reduce serendipity in the discovery of interesting products. In conclusion, we need to carefully think about cases where the RA-only situation is best and cases where experts’ and other consumers’ evaluations might be faked/biased.

At the same time, online stores should consider how well decision aids support consumers’ decisionmaking strategies with relevant information since the strategy restrictiveness of decision aids can decrease online consumers’ intention to use them (Wang & Benbasat, 2009). Interestingly, one outcome of the availability of such consistency-identification tools might be to trigger (1) incidences of proactive strategies that manage one’s limited cognitive capacity to deal with information overload and/or (2) incidences of reactive strategies that correct previous misconceptions and in turn may lead to a more efficient and effective product selection decision.

Moreover, as online consumers use consistency as a cue in their decision-making process, inconsistency among advice sources can trigger the attribution of “doubt” to the advice source, which is the main cause of the difference in opinion. In particular, if the RA, which represents one’s personal preferences, is the source of the inconsistency among advice sources (that is, the expert and consumers are in agreement but the RA is not), the user could reconsider their attribute preference values and get a new set of RA recommendations. If the difference still persists, the user might begin to have doubts about the RA’s trustworthiness. This is an interesting question worthy of further empirical examination concerning the increasing concerns about the potential deceptiveness of advice sources and the identification of “fake reviews.”

The results have several limitations. First, in this study, online consumers’ use of and reaction to consistency are examined without a public or social context. While this could be seen as a conservative test in the laboratory, future research should consider how the recommendations and review consistency strategies are influential in: (1) an online shopping context with social interactions, such as online collaborative shopping or an online social shopping network (where recommendations and reviews from friends, family members, and acquaintances are considered); and (2) nonsearch goods and experiential goods (e.g., apparel, hotels, restaurants).

Second, the participants in this study were undergraduate and graduate students and may not have precisely represented the overall population of online shoppers. However, because the participants have the potential to become heavy users (Kim et al., 2013) and 95% of the participants reported previous experience with online shopping, the use of students in this study is not a significant threat to external validity (McKnight et al., 2002). In addition, since millennials currently make up the largest proportion of online shoppers,<sup>14</sup> how they make a decision with multiple advice sources is important in itself. As the four consistency strategies might not be applicable to all consumers, future research should validate our findings to diverse groups.

Third, the design of our experimental website is relatively artificial and unrealistic, as no e-commerce website actually provides recommendations and reviews from multiple advice sources. The findings might thus not accurately represent the real ecommerce context. However, in our exploratory lab experiment, we tried to prevent the effects of different interface designs and to allow participants to compare recommendations from different sources. Moreover, as we used concurrent verbalization for tracing participants’ decision-making process, frequent transitions between webpages to access different advice sources could have interrupted their concurrent verbalization. This could have caused a distorted representation of online consumers’ decision-making process. Thus, to minimize such problems, this study used a relatively artificial experimental website design for the exploratory lab experiment. Future research should validate our findings in a more realistic ecommerce context.

Fourth, because previous research on the RA design has proposed that the strategy restrictiveness of the RA would decrease consumers’ intention to use decisionaid systems (Wang and Benbasat, 2009), future research examining the utilization and impact of consistency strategies across the exploration and collection stages of the information search process could explore the design and implementation of a decisional aid system supporting consistency strategies. Although our experimental website design allowed users to compare advice from multiple sources, there could be a better design of decisional aid tools for the support of consistency strategies. For example, to support the use of consistency strategies, online shopping stores could provide numeric values and/or graphical representations that show the extent of consistency between sources. Future research should consider which kind of interface design is the best or most appropriate way to present consistency within and among multiple advice sources and examine whether the use and impact of different consistency strategies are associated with information search processes.

Likewise, the coding schema we used focused completely on consistency strategies and did not reflect other aspects of decision-making strategies. Since the goal of this study was to identify consistencyoriented decision-making strategies, the coding schema does not reflect aspects of other decisionmaking strategies (e.g., EBA, SAT, etc.). However, decision-making is a constructive process and people can use multiple decision-making strategies simultaneously. Therefore, future research should investigate how and when people use traditional decision-making strategies with consistency-oriented decision-making strategies to clarify how decision makers make trade-offs, use heuristics, and combine internal attribute processing, and external evaluation perspectives with different information sources. Further, internal and external evaluation perspectives can be blended and external evaluation processes can help consumers develop an internal perspective about the product attributes and their importance. For example, expert reviews could help consumers better understand the product attributes and their importance while customer reviews could help people understand whether this is meaningful in practice.

Lastly, while our sample size of 64 may seem low for studies testing variance models, our sample size is considerably larger than that used in typical process tracing studies published in leading IS journals (Burton-Jones & Meso, 2006; Kim et al., 2000; Todd and Benbasat, 1987). Nevertheless, although this study manipulated consistency between diverse sources (i.e., RAs and experts, RAs and consumers, experts and consumers) and product knowledge (i.e., laptops versus digital cameras), we were unable to find any statistical evidence that reveals differences in recommendation consistency use across diverse sources. Future studies using larger sample sizes could identify how the combination of advice sources impacts the use of consistency strategies. This would provide insights into the baseline tendency of participants to seek consistency.

## Appendix A: Measurement Items

The measurement items are listed in Table A1, along with their sources. All measurement items have been validated by prior research work. To examine the effects of information consistency, we controlled for three constructs (i.e., task involvement, product knowledge, perceived deception) that could have affected the results. In particular, since we adjusted recommendation consistency by modifying recommendations from the RA, this treatment might be perceived as deceptive recommendations from the RA that could have influenced the utilization of recommendation consistency between the RA and others. Therefore, to control for confounding impact, we measured perceived deception.<sup>15</sup>

This study uses confirmatory factor analysis using the SmartPLS to validate the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of measurement items. Table A2 shows the descriptive statistics and composite reliability of the constructs. All composite reliabilities are greater than 0.7, the recommended cutoff (Barclay et al., 1995; Fornell & Bookstein, 1982). Thus, the reliability of the measurements is acceptable.

Convergent validity is the extent of the relatedness of items that theoretically should be related. Convergent validity was assessed by individual item reliability, the composite reliability of the construct, and average variance extracted (AVE) (Barclay et al., 1995; Hu et al., 2004). Individual item reliability was assessed by examining the loadings of the measurement items on their corresponding construct, and all the item loadings were significant and exceeded 0.7. All the composite reliability values exceeded 0.7, the recommended criterion (Barclay et al., 1995; Fornell & Bookstein, 1982) and AVE values exceeded 0.5, the generally accepted criterion (Hu et al., 2004) (see Table A3). Therefore, these results show good convergent validity for the measurement items.

Discriminant validity is the degree of difference between a given construct and other constructs. Thus, the measurement items should be distinct from other constructs and load on their construct. Discriminant validity was assessed by comparison of the square root of AVE and the correlations among constructs. To show good discriminant validity, all the square roots of the AVE should be greater than the off-diagonal elements in the corresponding rows and columns. This result indicates that the construct shares more variance with its measures than others (Fornell & Bookstein, 1982). The diagonal values of Table A3, the square roots of AVE, are greater than the correlations among constructs, demonstrating good discriminant validity for all of the constructs. Thus, all conditions for convergent and discriminant validity are satisfied.

Harman’s one-factor test was performed by principal component factor analysis to assess common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003), and each factor explained roughly equal variance. Thus, there is no evidence of common method bias.

## Appendix B: Identifying Consistency Strategies

This study used protocol analysis using concurrent verbalizations to identify online consumers’ decision-making strategies with multiple advice sources. The following are procedures to identify the consistency strategies using the coding schema. While most cases are very long and complicated, we choose a simpler case in the following example to clarify our analysis.

First, individuals’ verbalizations were recorded and transcribed (see Table B1). Second, to categorize verbalizations in the coding schema and array them in time order, we segmented them into sentences. When adjacent verbalizations were categorized in the same schema, they were grouped and numbered (see Table B2). Third, to validate transcribed verbalizations and clarify details such as which products and reviews were examined, we compared verbalizations with a video clip recording an individual’s decision-making process. Through this step, we added supplementary coding such as (1) recommendation source when selecting a source, (2) ranking and source of examined product when examining a product, (3) review source when examining product reviews (see Table B3). Fourth, to understand the role of recommendation and review consistency as a part of decision-making strategies, we examined consumers’ processes before and after examining consistencies. Specifically, we focused on the verbalizations that described perceptions of recommendation and review consistencies, behavioral and cognitive responses, and reasoning for their reactions. This procedure applied to all the participants’ verbalizations to represent their decision-making processes. Fifth, to identify general decision-making strategies, we grouped a list of participants who used similar utilization of recommendation and review consistencies (see Table B4). For example, Participant No. 37 perceived recommendation consistency between the fourth of Customers (CU4) and third of Experts (EX3) that had not been added to a consideration set when examined in Experts’ recommendations. As soon as she realized the consistency, she deliberated over the product with customer reviews and added it to her consideration set. That is, she changed her assessment of the product after perceiving the consistency. After further examination of other alternatives, she chose this product (CU4) as her final decision. Participant No. 28 showed a similar decision-making process. Through this procedure, we captured common decision-making processes across the utilization of multiple sources and could simplify individuals’ complicated decision-making processes by means of the diagram in Figure B1.

## About the Authors

Hongki Kim is an assistant professor in the College of Business at Clemson University. He holds a PhD in management information systems from the University of British Columbia. His research focuses on computer-aided decision support systems in general, and more specifically on recommendation systems, virtual worlds, social media, artificial intelligence, human-computer interaction, and e-commerce. In the areas of system implementation and empirical methods, he uses decision-aids tools based on behavioral theories and data analytic algorithms, such as text mining and machine learning; and investigates their impacts on individuals’ decision-making process through lab experiments. His work has been published in MIS Quarterly and Information and Management.

Izak Benbasat is a member of the Order of Canada, a fellow of the Royal Society of Canada, and professor emeritus at Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia. He holds a PhD from the University of Minnesota and a Doctorat Honoris Causa, Université de Montréal. He served as editor-in-chief of Information Systems Research, editor for Information Systems and the Decision Support Systems Department of Management Science, and senior editor for MIS Quarterly. Dr. Benbasat received the LEO Award for Lifetime Exceptional Achievements in Information Systems from the Association for Information Systems and is a Distinguished Fellow of the Institute for Operations Research and Management Sciences (INFORMS) Information Systems Society.
