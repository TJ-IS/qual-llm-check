## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Technostress Appraisal process Coping process ICT-enabled productivity

## A B S T R A C T

Transactional Theory of Stress (TTS) suggests that appraisal and coping processes determine the impacts of stressors. Prior research applies TTS at the technology level, where the presence of information and communication technologies (ICTs) triggers the above-mentioned two processes and technostress creators are appraisals of ICTs. We apply TTS at the application level, where the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work triggers the two processes and technostress creators are characteristics of the demand. Results suggest that technostress creators in<sup>fl</sup>uence appraisal outcomes inconsistently. Problem- and emotion-focused coping strategies, respec tively, mediate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of challenge and hindrance appraisal outcomes on ICT-enabled productivity.

## 1. Introduction

Information and communication technologies (ICTs) have been ubiquitously implemented in organizations. These technologies bring many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to industrial organizations, such as enhancing communication and liberating workers from fussy and ine<sup>f</sup>ective manual work [1,2], as well as “technostress” [3]. Technostress is the stress induced by the use of ICTs, which has been primarily investigated in the organizational context by Information Systems (IS) scholars [4]. Factors creating technostress in this context are termed technostress creators [5]. The experience of technostress creators makes the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work to be stressful.

Existing research on technostress in IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has predominantly posited that technostress is harmful to employees’ work performance by showing that the overall e<sup>f</sup>ect of technostress creators on work performance is negative (e.g., [4–7]). However, there are also <sup>fi</sup>ndings that the overall e<sup>f</sup>ect of technostress creators is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant or even signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive on productivity [8,9] and organizational commit ment [10]. An explanation to these <sup>fi</sup>ndings is that the e<sup>f</sup>ects of different technostress creators are masked. That is, some technostress creators have positive e<sup>f</sup>ects on work performance, while others exert negative e<sup>f</sup>ects. Studies examining separate e<sup>f</sup>ects of individual technostress creators support this explanation by demonstrating that impacts of di<sup>f</sup>erent technostress creators are inconsistent [8,10]. The inconsistency is noteworthy when managers seek to promote employees’ work performance via managing technostress. It may be a waste of resources if positive technostress creators (factors that lead to positive outcomes) are viewed as equal to negative technostress creators (factors that lead to negative outcomes).

In explaining the inconsistent e<sup>f</sup>ects of stressors, the Transactional Theory of Stress (TTS) [11] is considered "extremely in<sup>fl</sup>uential" [12]. TTS indicates that stressors in<sup>fl</sup>uence performance outcomes through appraisal and coping processes where appraisal outcomes of the stressful demand determine what coping strategies are adopted, leading to di<sup>f</sup>erent performance outcomes [11]. A stressful demand can be appraised as a challenge or a hindrance, depending on whether ful-<sup>fi</sup>lling the demand has a potential for personal gain or loss [11]. Although TTS has been widely applied to technostress research, only in recent years have a few studies begun to conceptualize the appraisal and coping processes (e.g., [13–16]). Therefore, a theory-based understanding of the appraisal and coping processes is limited.

Existing research mainly applies TTS at the technology level, where the presence of ICTs is an environmental demand and technostress creators are appraisals of the demand [14]. Thus, they theorize the appraisal process before technostress creators and explain how technostress creators are induced by characteristics of ICTs (e.g., [14,15]). However, the perceived e<sup>f</sup>ect of ICTs on work is de<sup>fi</sup>ned at the application level, where an application refers to “the use of information technology to accomplish work" ([17], p. 329). Thus, at the application level, individuals appraise and cope with the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work and technostress creators represent characteristics of the demand. Appraisal outcomes at both levels can be di<sup>f</sup>erent because potential gains and achievements vary with what demands are ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. At present, there is a lack of empirical investigations on the appraisal of coping processes of technostress at the application level.

Our research draws on TTS to theorize the appraisal and coping processes at the application level to examine the relationship between technostress creators and ICT-enabled productivity. ICT-enabled productivity indicates the extent to which ICT use enhances employee productivity [17]. We propose that the experience of technostress creators makes the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work to be stressful. When encountering the stressful demand, individuals may appraise it as a challenge or a hindrance for personal gain, growth, development, and well-being (i.e., the appraisal process). In response to appraisal outcomes, problem- or emotion-focused coping strategies are set into motion (i.e., the coping process), which lead to higher or lower ICT-enabled productivity. There are two research questions to be ad dressed.

RQ1: What are the associations of individual technostress creators with challenge and hindrance appraisal outcomes?

RQ2: Do problem- and emotion-focused coping strategies mediate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of challenge and hindrance appraisal outcomes on ICT-en abled productivity respectively?

The article proceeds as follows. In Section 2, the theory and litera ture basis of the present study are provided. Then, we build the conceptual model and research hypotheses. In Section 4, research method is presented. In Section 5, we validate the measurement tools and test the conceptual model. Comprehensive discussion of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings is presented in Section 7. Contributions and limitations are summarized in Sections 7 and 8.

## 2. Theoretical and literature background

## 2.1. Transactional theory of stress

Early view on stress de<sup>fi</sup>nes it as a "stimulus, response, or the in teraction between the two" ([18], p. 24). However, as this view does not consider the psychological processes through which stressful demands are appraised, many researchers agree with Lazarus and Launier’s view on stress as a process involving a transaction between the individual and the environment [19]. Based on this transaction-based view, Lazarus and Folkman (1984) developed TTS [11], which has been one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential theories of stress so far.

TTS theorizes that the transactional process of stress involves two subprocesses, i.e., appraisal process and coping process [11]. Appraisal and coping processes mediate how individuals react to the environmental demand. The appraisal process evaluates the extent to which the transaction between the person and the environmental demand is stressful, whereas the coping process determines the behavioral and emotional e<sup>f</sup>orts to manage the stressful demand ([11], p. 19). The appraisal process is set into motion when individuals encounter an environmental demand that exceeds their resources and capabilities. In this process, the stressful demand can be appraised as a challenge or/ and a hindrance, which concerns anticipated gains and losses to personal growth, achievements, and well-being. TTS highlights that chal lenge and hindrance appraisal outcomes are not mutually exclusive and usually occur simultaneously ([11], p. 33). In response to appraisal outcomes, individuals may adopt various coping strategies, resulting in di<sup>f</sup>erential performance outcomes. Problem-focused and emotion-fo cused coping strategies are typical coping types. Problem-focused coping strategies aim to master the stressful demand and solve the problem, while emotion-focused coping strategies target at altering how individuals interpret the situation and regulating negative emo tions [11]. Problem- and emotion-focused coping strategies were well established to be associated with opposite performance outcomes, such as work performance and expatriate adjustment [20,21].

Applying TTS to our research, we consider using ICTs to accomplish work as an environmental demand that exceeds employees’ resources and capabilities due to the experience of technostress creators [5]. Thus, the experience of technostress creators sets into motion the appraisal and the subsequent coping processes in response to the demand.

## 2.2. Literature review on appraisal and coping processes of technostress

Technostress is a type of work stress that is induced by characteristics of ICTs [22]. As TTS has been widely applied in general work stress research [23–25], many technostress studies apply TTS to theorize their research models. However, IS researchers have not reached a consensus to conceptualize the components of TTS.

At the earliest stage of applying TTS to technostress research, only <sup>fi</sup>ve components of TTS are conceptualized, i.e., environmental conditions, stressors, strain, other organizational outcomes, and situational factors [5,26]. Since then, many constructs which parallel to these components have been developed [6,27–30]. Although these components enable our preliminary understanding of technostress, they do not capture the appraisal and coping processes which are crucial as indicated by TTS. In recent years, increasing attention has been focused on this direction. A literature review summary of studies that explicitly conceptualize either the appraisal or the coping process in research models is shown in Appendix 1.

A common practice for IS researchers to study the appraisal process of technostress is to apply TTS at the technology level, where technostress creators are conceptualized as threat/hindrance appraisals of ICT characteristics without explicit conceptualization of appraisal constructs (e.g., [14,31]). Thus, an underlying hypothesis of these studies is that technostress creators lead to adverse work outcomes or performance. However, this hypothesis is refuted by the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings that the e<sup>f</sup>ects of technostress creators on work outcomes are not always negative (see a review, [32]). While ICT-enabled work outcomes are de<sup>fi</sup>ned at the application level [17], these empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that technostress creators may not only be associated with hindrance appraisals at this level. Cali<sup>f</sup> et al.’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm that not all the currently identi<sup>fi</sup>ed technostress creators are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with hindrance appraisals [15]. However, they did not test whether technostress creators are associated with challenge appraisals. We agree with Lei and Ngai’s proposal that technostress has a "double-edged nature" and technostress creators can be related to both challenge and hindrance appraisals of using ICTs to accomplish work [13]. When a person appraises using ICTs to accomplish work as a challenge to personal growth and development, which deserves the discomfort of experiencing technostress creators, his/her productivity can be enhanced by ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the demand.

In prior research, the coping process is conceptualized to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the relationship between technostress creators and work outcomes. The roles coping strategies play in the stressor-outcome relationship are suggested to be mediators and moderators based on di<sup>f</sup>erent theoretical perspectives [33]. Technostress research thus conceptualizes coping strategies inconsistently in the technostressor-outcome relationship. For example, D’Arcy et al. [31] and Gaudioso et al. [16] theorize coping strategies as mediators, whereas Pirkkalainen et al. [34], Galluch et al. [35] and Weinert et al. [36] theorize them as moderators. Following TTS, appraisal and coping processes are two mediators in the stressor-outcome relationship [11,37]. Our literature review reveals that prior technostress research provided no evidence on how the coping process translated the e<sup>f</sup>ects of appraisal outcomes to work outcomes.

The literature review demonstrates that existing technostress studies did not di<sup>f</sup>erentiate appraisal outcomes at technology and appli cation levels, appraisal and coping processes were conceptualized separately in their research models, and empirical evidence about appraisal and coping processes of technostress was insu<sup>fi</sup>cient. Research gaps exist that empirical evidence about the motivational aspects of technostress creators at the application level is still limited. The mediating roles coping strategies play in the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of appraisal outcomes on work outcomes have not been fully investigated.

## 3.1. Efects of technostress creators on appraisal outcomes

According to [26], techno-overload describes “situations where ICTs force users to work faster and longer”. It makes the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work to be stressful in ways of increasing the amount of workload and the level of time pressure. High workload and time pressure are well-established challenge stressors in the organizational context because these demands are viewed as rewarding for personal gain and growth (e.g. [12,23,24],). In ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the demand of using ICTs to work faster and longer, employees gain skills and personal development [39,40]. Overcoming this stressor can increase the feeling of achievement for better performance and increase the probability of being rewarded or praised [41]. Therefore, techno-overload can lead to a higher challenge appraisal of using ICTs to accomplish work.

It is noted that techno-overload is also expected to be associated with the hindrance appraisal outcome because of two reasons. First, techno-overload requires excessive time and e<sup>f</sup>ort to process more information than they can e<sup>f</sup>ectively handle in ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work [42]. In this regard, using ICTs to accomplish work can be appraised as a hindrance to personal gains and development because employees cannot process the information e<sup>f</sup>ectively. Second, processing unnecessary information takes up employees’ resources from other activities. For example, salesmen communicate information with company colleagues and external clients via the email system. Responding to excessive bureaucratic group sending emails can divert the time and energy needed to deal with customer inquiries. In this case, the use of email to accomplish work can be appraised as a hindrance to gain personal achievements. Thus, technooverload can lead to higher hindrance appraisal of using ICTs to accomplish work.

H1a. Techno-overload is positively associated with challenge appraisal outcome.

H1b. Techno-overload is positively associated with hindrance appraisal outcome.

The e<sup>f</sup>ect of techno-invasion on appraisal outcomes is twofold. On the one hand, techno-invasion may blur the work–home boundaries, which con<sup>fl</sup>icts with employees’ need of recovering from work e<sup>f</sup>ort during o<sup>f</sup>-hours, damaging their health and well-being [43]. The work–home con<sup>fl</sup>ict has been widely recognized to associate with psychological distress (e.g [44–46].), which is usually appraised as hindering an individual’s ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of personal goals and achievements [47]. Meanwhile, techno-invasion may create role con<sup>fl</sup>ict and ambiguity in the work and family lives [22]. Role con<sup>fl</sup>ict and role ambiguity, which reduce the e<sup>f</sup>ort that should be put to work, are ty pical hindrance stressors because they thwart personal gains [12]. Form these perspectives, employees tend to appraise the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work to be threatened.

On the other hand, intrusive features of ICTs exert more workload and higher responsibilities on employees [22], which are generally appraised as challenges [48]. Although the ICT use during o<sup>f</sup>-work time may invade employees’ personal lives, it also provides an alternative way for employees to stay current with and maintain control of their work even at home [49]. Ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work leads to higher achievement in career, and thus can be appraised as opportunities for obtaining personal achievement and gains, even if it accompanies a sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce of personal life and family time, which is stressful. From this perspective, the experience of technoinvasion is positively associated with challenge appraisal of using ICTs to accomplish work.

H2a. Techno-invasion is positively associated with challenge appraisal outcome.

H2b. Techno-invasion is positively associated with hindrance appraisal outcome.

Techno-uncertainty refers to “contexts where continuing changes and upgrades in an ICT unsettle users and create uncertainty for them, in that they have to constantly learn and educate themselves about the new ICTs” [26]. Based on this de<sup>fi</sup>nition, we argue that the e<sup>f</sup>ects of techno-uncertainty on appraisal outcomes depend on the characteristic of the change or the update. First, organizations usually upgrade ICTs for <sup>fi</sup>xing problems, which bene<sup>fi</sup>ts employees and organizations with an improvement in ICT functionalities. In this situation, employees are faced with more adaptational demands [50] and have to spend more time and energy learning a new tool [51]. Although using ICTs to accomplish work can be stressful in this situation, employees are more powerful in ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the demand with the constant improvement of ICTs. Keeping up with the improvement may bene<sup>fi</sup>t personal growth and increase the feeling of ful<sup>fi</sup>llment [40]. From this perspective, techno-uncertainty may increase challenge appraisal of the demand.

Second, when employees are required to unnecessarily switch from one ICT to another, they may experience role ambiguity and role con-<sup>fl</sup>ict in learning new technologies [52]. Ambiguity in roles and con-<sup>fl</sup>icting cognition restrain individuals’ abilities and development [24,48,53]. Given that employees should spend much time in adapting new ICTs, their gains and development can be damaged. Thus, techno uncertainty may lead to hindrance appraisal of using ICTs to accomplish work. From these perspectives, we posit that

H3a. Techno-uncertainty is positively associated with challenge appraisal outcome.

H3b. Techno-uncertainty is positively associated with hindrance appraisal outcome.

Techno-complexity describes “situations where the complexity associated with ICTs makes users feel inadequate as far as their skills are concerned and forces them to spend time and e<sup>f</sup>ort in learning and understanding various aspects of ICTs” [26]. The perception of complexity in using ICTs to accomplish work is believed to increase work load [22], which is typically categorized as a challenging situation [12]. Learning complex ICTs provides employees an opportunity to improve personal skills. Their competitiveness in organizations can also be strengthened. In this case, techno-complexity may enhance the challenge appraisal of using ICTs to accomplish work.

Nevertheless, employees are more likely to be evaluated by their performance on tasks than by their skills in using ICTs. When employees spend more time on learning complex ICTs, fewer e<sup>f</sup>orts should be spent on dealing with tasks. Therefore, techno-complexity may raise the di<sup>fi</sup>culty of ful<sup>fi</sup>lling tasks [54], increasing hindrance appraisal of using ICTs to accomplish work. Meanwhile, complexity in using ICTs is associated with the feeling of role ambiguity and con<sup>fl</sup>ict [52], which may also result in hindrance appraisal of the demand [53]. Hence, we hypothesize that

H4a. Techno-complexity is positively associated with challenge appraisal outcome.

H4b. Techno-complexity is positively associated with hindrance appraisal outcome.

As the rapid development of ICTs is driving the revolution of replacing human labor with machine labor [55], employees may “feel threatened about losing their jobs as a result of a new ICT replacing them, or to other people who have a better understanding of the ICT”, facing techno-insecurity [26]. The threatened feeling of losing a job is widely viewed as an impediment to development [56,57] and wellbeing [58], and is well-recognized as a hindrance stressor because it is uncontrollable to employees [12].

Meanwhile, other researchers suggest that job insecurity can si multaneously be appraised as a challenge because this situation can motivate employees to work harder and be more dedicated [59]. This argument also <sup>fi</sup>ts in ICT-induced job insecurity. Although it is threatened to be replaced by others who are more skilled in using ICTs, the experience of techno-insecurity can be a challenge if employees believe that high performance protects them from being laid o<sup>f</sup> [60,61]. In this case, employees are motivated to learn new ICTs and get improved in using ICTs. Thus, the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work can be appraised as a challenge because employees would get more experienced in using ICTs and be more competitive in the job market. Thus, techno-insecurity can increase the challenge appraisal of using ICTs to accomplish work. Based on these arguments, we hypothesize that

H5a. Techno-insecurity is positively associated with challenge appraisal outcome.

H5b. Techno-insecurity is positively associated with hindrance appraisal outcome.

## 3.2. Mediating role of coping strategies on the influences of appraisal outcomes on ICT-enabled productivity

According to TTS, individuals adopt di<sup>f</sup>erent coping strategies in response to di<sup>f</sup>erent appraisal outcomes, leading to disparate performance outcomes. TTS suggests that problem-focused coping strategies are more likely to occur when an individual appraises the demand as having potential gains and opportunities [11]. This e<sup>f</sup>ect has been widely proved in various contexts. In a team context, Pearsall et al. argued that when team members appraised the situation as a challenge, the team would exploit new solutions to cope with it [62]. In a training context, Lapine et al. found that trainees who experience challenge stress have a higher motivation to learn [23], where learning is a source of problem-focused coping [63]. In the ICT use context, researchers suggest that challenge appraisal outcome is associated with problemfocused coping strategies because this appraisal outcome may trigger pleasure emotional reactions, such as excitement and achievement [64], which inspire e<sup>f</sup>orts to solve problems [65–67]. In our research context, if an employee appraises the stressful demand of using ICTs to accomplish work as opportunities for personal growth and development, s/he is more likely to take e<sup>f</sup>orts to solve problems to acquire the potential gains and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. We posit that individuals who have higher challenge appraisals of using ICTs to accomplish work adopt more problem-focused coping strategies.

In addition, TTS suggests that problem-focused coping strategies are typically associated with favorable performance outcomes because they direct people to overcome the problem [11]. In ICT-related contexts, problem-focused coping strategies have been well-recognized as an important facilitator of positive work outcomes [16]. Majchrzak et al. claim that members of a virtual team, who keep modifying their tasks when they face di<sup>fi</sup>culties in accomplishing challenge tasks with new ICTs (a type of problem-focused coping strategies), achieve higher productivity [68]. Beaudry and Pinsonneault suggest that employees work e<sup>fi</sup>ciency can be enhanced via adapting the work (e.g., modifying work routine), the technology (e.g., updating ICTs), and the person (e.g., searching for technical training), which are all problem-focused coping strategies [69]. Therefore, we posit that problem-focused coping strategies have positive e<sup>f</sup>ects on ICT-enabled productivity. Overall, we hypothesize that problem-focused coping strategies mediate the in<sup>fl</sup>u ence of challenge appraisal outcome on ICT-enabled productivity.

H6. Problem-focused coping strategies mediate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of challenge appraisal outcome on ICT-enabled productivity.

H6a. Challenge appraisal outcome is positively associated with problem-focused coping strategies.

H6b: Problem-focused coping strategies are positively associated with ICT-enabled productivity.

According to TTS, emotion-focused coping is more probable when an individual appraises the demand to be harmful and helpless [11].This is because the hindrance appraisal outcome is more likely to trigger negative emotions such as anxiety and anger [25]. Emotionfocused coping strategies, including distancing or avoidance, blaming self or others, and thinking the positive sides, which direct at altering how individuals interpret the stressful demands, are adopted to regulate negative emotions [70]. Similarly, when individuals appraise ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the demand as unrewarding and helpless, they experience the feeling of anger [71] and may blame others who are supposed to be responsible for the negative outcomes [72]. Laux and Weber <sup>fi</sup>nd that in coping with angry emotions, people would express their negative feelings, blame themselves or others [73]. Beaudry and Pinsonneault also report that employees who get angry at using ICTs tend to complain about the demand [66]. Thus, we posit that hindrance appraisal outcome of using ICTs to accomplish work is positively associated with emotion-focused coping strategies.

In addition, emotion-focused coping strategies are associated with negative work outcomes because they distant people away from resolving problems [11]. Anderson indicates that emotion-oriented coping behaviors, such as withdrawal and aggression, negatively a<sup>f</sup>ect performance [21]. Other examples of emotion-focused coping strategies involve avoidance and blaming of the situation or relevant persons [66,74]. Although keeping away from the stressful situation releases the negative impacts of negative emotions to some extent [75], it leads to inaction in solving problems and thus, unfavorable outcomes [76]. In a similar vein, a defensive emotional response (e.g., blaming others) also distracts employees from focusing on solving problems, reducing their work performance [21]. In the context of ICT use, if an employee emphasizes on complaining about the di<sup>fi</sup>culty in using ICTs to accomplish work and tries to keep away from using ICTs, their productivity can be negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uenced because problems remain unsolved. Therefore, we posit that emotion-focused coping strategies have negative in<sup>fl</sup>uences on ICT-enabled productivity. Overall, we hypothesize that emotion-focused coping strategies mediate the relationship between hindrance appraisal outcome and ICT-enabled productivity.

H7. Emotion-focused coping strategies mediate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of hindrance appraisal outcome on ICT-enabled productivity.

H7a. Hindrance appraisal outcome is positively associated with emotion-focused coping strategies.

H7b. Emotion-focused coping strategies are negatively associated with

ICT-enabled productivity.

## 4. Method

We conducted a survey to test our research model. As a form of supplementary data, we interviewed eight Chinese employees who work full-time and frequently use ICTs at work. Descriptions about the interviews are presented in Appendix 3.

Survey data were collected through So-Jump (https://www.sojump. com/), a leading marketing research company in China. About 2.6 million members existed in its sample pool. To improve the quality of the collected data, some traps will be included in the collecting process. For example, some calculating tasks will randomly appear among questions. Respondents that failed the trapping test will be excluded from both the respondents and the company’s sample pool. This mechanism can make the data more reliable. This online data collection mechanism has been utilized by many academic studies [77–82].

The target population of the survey is full-time employees who use ICTs in workplaces. Two screening questions, presented at the begin ning of the questionnaire, were adapted from Ayyagari, Grover [22], i.e., “Do you work full time?” and “Do you use any of ICTs (a list of ICTs was provided)?” The questionnaire will proceed if and only if the an swers are “Yes”.

In accordance with previous technostress in the organization, our focus is on the ICT-related stressful situations where employees may experience technostress creators. According to Ayyagari et al., there are seven types of ICTs, i.e., mobile technologies, network technologies, communication technologies, enterprise and database technologies, generic application technologies, collaborative technologies, and other work speci<sup>fi</sup>c technologies [22]. The joint consideration of all kinds of ICTs in a survey may be confusing because individuals’ perceptions of technostress creators may vary with di<sup>f</sup>erent ICTs. As associations between stressors and appraisals tend to be stable in organizational environments [38], we ask the respondents to think about their experiences of working with the most frequently used ICT to reduce confusion. Therefore, we target “this technology” in the measurement items at one of the seven types of ICTs that the respondent most frequently uses at work.

A total of 513 respondents from 24 provinces all over China completed the online survey. Descriptive statistics are shown in Table 1. The demographics show that females and males were evenly distributed in our respondents. More than 80 % of the respondents were among 26–40 years old. About 70.8 % of the respondents got a bachelor’s degree. Moreover, about half of the respondents had used the ICT that they were most familiar with in workplaces for more than 5 years. Most of the respondents (about 97.1 %) reported that ICT-related tasks are not too di<sup>fi</sup>cult.

## 4.1. Measures

We adapted well-established measures from prior literature to assess all the constructs. All items were measured with 5-point Likert scales where 1 represents ‘strongly disagree’ and 5 represents ‘strongly agree’. To ensure content reliability, we leveraged the backward translation methodology [83] to translate the original English scales into Chinese. All of the questions were required to be answered to avoid missing data.

## 4.1.1. Technostress creators

The scale for technostress creators was developed by [26]. A list of measurement items exhibits <sup>fi</sup>ve factors through exploratory and con <sup>fi</sup>rmation factor analysis, each of which represents a particular di mension of technostress creators, including techno-overload, techno invasion, techno-complexity, techno-insecurity, and techno-uncertainty.

## 4.1.2. Appraisal outcomes

Items of appraisal outcomes were derived from [48]. Measurements for these two constructs were adapted to measure the extent to which an individual appraises the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work as a challenge and a hindrance.

## 4.1.3. Coping strategies

As coping strategies rely on speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts [11], previous studies customize items to measure them (e.g. [74,84],). Following this approach, we adapted 15 items from [66], which were designed for ICT usage contexts, to measure problem- and emotion-focused coping strategies. Then, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis using principal components extraction with varimax rotations to identify coping strategies constructs. The analysis yielded three factors: seeking instrumental support (looking for technical help from colleagues or manuals), psychological distancing (i.e., avoiding the tasks), and venting (i.e., openly express negative emotions). Seeking instrumental support is a problem-focused coping strategy, whereas psychological distancing and venting are emotion-focused coping strategies.

## 4.1.4. ICT-enabled productivity

Items of ICT-enabled productivity were adapted from [17]. These items have been utilized by [26] and [4] in the technostress research.

4.1.4.1. Control variables. First, demographic variables, including gender, age, and educational background, are controlled as predictors of the dependent variable ICT-enabled productivity in our research model. We also introduced task dificulty and experience in ICT use as control variables to ICT-enabled productivity, as individuals who perform easier tasks and are more experienced in using ICTs tend to achieve higher productivity with the use of ICTs. Task di<sup>fi</sup>culty is measured by "how di<sup>fi</sup>cult the ICT-related tasks are? (1=simple, 3=hard)” and experience in ICT use is measured by "to what extent do you think you are experienced in using this technology? (1=low, 3=high)".

## 5. Results

There are two structural equation modeling (SEM) estimation approaches, which are covariance-based (CB) and partial least squares (PLS)-based. We chose PLS to test the measurement and structural models of our research because our data were not normally distributed on many indicators. PLS demands less on data distribution than CB-SEM estimation [85,86]. Hence, PLS was more suitable for our analysis. The software that we utilized was SmartPLS 2.0.M3.

## 5.1. Assessment of measurement model

We began by evaluating the reliability and validity of the construct measures. At <sup>fi</sup>rst, we run the model with SmartPLS. Items with factor loadings on their subjective construct less than 0.5 are dropped out [87]. The remaining items are shown in Appendix 2. Then, we examined the reliability by calculating the composite reliability (CR) for constructs. As shown in Table 2, the CRs of all constructs in our research model fall within the range of 0.80–0.92, exceeding the threshold of 0.7 recommended by [88]. Guided by [89], we evaluated discriminant and convergent validities of the constructs. First, discriminant validity was ensured. As Table 2 shows, each construct’s average variance extracted (AVE) exceeds 0.50, and the square root of each construct’s AVE is greater than the construct’s correlation coe<sup>fi</sup>- cients with other constructs. Second, convergent validity was ensured. As Table A2 of Appendix 2 shows, factor loadings of each construct are almost over 0.70, except PRO2, PRO3 and TIS2 whose loadings are close to 0.70 at an acceptable level. The cross loading of each item on other constructs is less than the factor loading on its substantive construct [90].

## 5.2. Common method bias

To evaluate the common method bias in our research, we conducted a Harmon one-factor test through factor analysis [91]. The rationale for the test suggests that when there is a factor that can explain the most variation of the indicator variables, there is a common method bias issue [91]. Our results showed that the extracted factor only accounted for 21.9 % of the variation, indicating the common method bias was not salient in our research. Furthermore, we performed a more comprehensive full collinearity test for identifying common method variances using warpPLS 6.0, following [92]. Their approach creates a model by linking all of the constructs to a new dummy variable storing random values with any range. The PLS regression gives the results of variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors (VIFs) for all the constructs which are shown in Table 2. All the VIFs are less than 3.3, indicating that the common method bias is not conspicuous in our research.

## 6. Discussion

Our research model is tested with a survey design. In this section, we discuss the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and provide explanations with the evidence from theories and empirical studies.

Analysis of the survey indicates that techno-overload in<sup>fl</sup>uence challenge appraisal outcome of using ICTs to accomplish work instead of hindrance appraisal outcome. That is, although ICTs force employees to work faster and take more responsibilities in workplaces, making the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work to be stressful, employees tend to appraise the stressful demand as a challenge that bene<sup>fi</sup>ts personal gains, rather than a hindrance. We give an explanation that work overload makes employees feel that the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work can be met with extra e<sup>f</sup>ort, which may bring about personal gains and growth in the organization [12,24]. Our supplementary interviews (please refer to Appendix 3) also provide an alternative explanation that employees have got used to the fast work<sup>fl</sup>ow enabled by ICTs. They think (subconsciously perhaps) humans should catch up with the rapid development of technologies, rather than impede the technological progress. Therefore, they would not treat techno-overload as a hindrance to their personal gain and growth.

With the analysis of survey data, we surprisingly <sup>fi</sup>nd that technoinvasion is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with both challenge and hindrance appraisal outcomes. One explanation can be built on the cultural norm, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the supervisor-subordinate guanxi in China. This cultural norm refers to a "personal relationship between a supervisor and a subordinate developed largely from non-work related social interac tions both inside and outside working hours" [94]. Given the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence of supervisor-subordinate guanxi on the supervisor’s administrative decisions [95], subordinates tend not to turn down their su pervisors’ requests. Even though the presence of ICTs that enables employees to be constantly connected during the o<sup>f</sup>-job time can lead to hindrance appraisal of ICTs (at the technology level) [14], employees may not appraise the use of ICTs to accomplish work to be a hindrance to their personal growth and development in career (at the application level). Thus, techno-invasion is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to hindrance appraisal outcome. Furthermore, as it has become a cultural norm that is accepted by the society, most employees may not refute to work during the o<sup>f</sup>-job time. With the presence of techno-invasion, ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work may not make employees more competitive than their colleagues, but not ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the demand will make them less competitive. Thus, techno-invasion is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with challenge appraisal outcome.

Results that are contrary to our hypotheses are found in technocomplexity and techno-insecurity, which are negatively related to challenge appraisal outcome. It is suggested that the motivational effects of techno-complexity and techno-insecurity can be counteracted by the increase of the extent. For example, using a complex new ICT to accomplish work can be appraised as a challenge to gain constant improvement and keep competitive in careers. However, as techno-complexity increases, employees may feel more uncomfortable to use it to work. This is because a human’s cognitive capacity is limited [96]. Higher techno-complexity requires larger cognitive capacity to deal with technical problems, raising the cost to obtain the gains. Thus, challenge appraisal outcome may decrease. Similarly, when employees experience job insecurity in using ICTs to accomplish work, they may treat it as an opportunity to get improved and be more competitive. However, as the insecurity increases, employees may feel harder to obtain the gains, thus reduce the challenge appraisal outcome.

Finally, contrary to our hypothesis, psychological distancing does not mediate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of hindrance appraisal outcome on ICT-enabled productivity. One reason for the non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between psychological distancing and ICT-enabled productivity can be that psychological distancing does not necessarily lead to actual behavioral distancing. Psychological distancing acts as a psychological comfort but does not a<sup>f</sup>ect actual behavior. Spector also reported that cognitive avoidance might even improve productivity by o<sup>f</sup>ering compulsory rest [97]. Another reason is from interviews where the interviewees suggest that psychological distancing is usually adopted when they are busy in dealing with more important work tasks. In this case, their productivity was less a<sup>f</sup>ected. Hence, psychological distancing will not a<sup>f</sup>ect ICTenabled productivity.

## 7. Contributions and implications

Our primary theoretical contribution to technostress research is that we extend existing technostress research based on TTS from the technology level to the application level. This extension is enabled by conceptualizing the appraisal and coping processes as mediators linking technostress creators and ICT-enabled productivity. Although TTS has been widely applied in the technostress <sup>fi</sup>eld, attention is mainly focused on antecedents of technostress, technostress creating factors, mitigating factors, and adverse work outcomes [98]. Less is known about the associations between technostress creators and appraisal outcomes, as well as the way that individuals cope with these appraisals, and the di<sup>f</sup>erential e<sup>f</sup>ects of coping strategies on work outcomes. We note that the indirect e<sup>f</sup>ects of technostress creators on ICT-enabled productivity via challenge appraisal outcome and problem-focused coping strategies are positive, whereas those via hindrance appraisal outcome and emotion-focused coping strategies are negative.

Our second theoretical contribution is that we furthered the un derstanding of associations between currently identi<sup>fi</sup>ed technostress creators and appraisal outcomes. When conceptualizing the appraisal process of technostress, researchers usually consider technostress creators as threat appraisal outcomes of ICT characteristics [14,31]. Although it is well established that currently identi<sup>fi</sup>ed technostress creators may lead to adverse consequences, it receives little attention that such stressful situations have motivational aspects [11]. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that techno-overload and techno-uncertainty are positively related to challenge appraisal outcome at the application level. Our study furthers previous knowledge of technostress creators (e.g., [14,15]) by showing that currently identi<sup>fi</sup>ed technostress creators can not only be appraised negatively but also positively. We also provide a theoretical explanation to why technostress creators exert di<sup>f</sup>erential e<sup>f</sup>ects on work outcomes [8,9]. It is because they are associated with di<sup>f</sup>erent appraisal outcomes.

Our third theoretical contribution lies in opening the black box where technostress creators in<sup>fl</sup>uence ICT-enabled productivity. In response to researchers’ call for designing interventions to manage the negative impact of technostress [14,22], our research model provides a theoretical basis for designing interventions that alter the appraisal and coping processes. As challenge appraisal outcome and problem-focused coping strategies direct individuals at dealing with technostress in a positive way where ICT-enabled productivity can be enhanced, future research can be conducted to design interventions that motivate individuals to appraise and cope with technostress in such ways.

Our study has several managerial implications for inhibiting the adverse consequences of technostress. For employees, our research provides a theoretical understanding of how technostress creators in <sup>fl</sup>uence their work performance. Employees should be aware that technostress is not awful if they deal with it in a positive way. For example, focusing on the motivational aspects of using ICTs to accomplish work can increase challenge appraisal outcome, which helps solve problems and leads to favorable outcomes. Also, employees should avoid complaining or venting anger because these actions can make them less rational and result in detrimental outcomes.

Our study also contributes to organizations. First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in dicate that not all technostress creators should be eliminated. As a complement to previous studies which suggest that technostress creators are detrimental to work-related outcomes [5], we propose the idea that organizations should emphasize their limited resources on tech nostress creators that are appraised or coped negatively in order to achieve better e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. For instance, organizations do not need to eliminate techno-overload from their organizational environment because it is a motivational factor that may even enhance ICT-enabled productivity. For other technostress creators that are positively related to hindrance appraisal outcome and negatively related to challenge appraisal outcome, organizations should either simply eliminate these factors or alter the way how their employees appraise and cope with these factors. Attempts to mitigate techno-complexity include involving employees in the implementation phase of a new ICT, and increasing the attainability of technical supports. Those mitigating techno-insecurity can be starting sta<sup>f</sup> care programs or encouraging employees to participate in technical training.

Another alternative way to manage technostress is to alter the appraisal and coping processes. One approach is to give incentives to employees for ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the demand of using ICTs to accomplish work in order to make the demand more linked to employees’ personal gains and developments. In such a way, employees are more likely to appraise the demand as a challenge. Our results also demonstrate that problemfocused coping strategies have positive in<sup>fl</sup>uences on ICT-enabled pro ductivity, whereas emotion-focused coping strategies have non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant or negative e<sup>f</sup>ects. Thus, managers should motivate their employees to focus on solving problems, such as modifying their task procedures and seeking instrumental support from colleagues, rather than blaming and complaining about the environment or other people. Meanwhile, as a response to hindrance appraisal outcome, psychological distancing coping strategy won’t lower the productivity but venting will. Hence, organizations can give their employees psychological hints that problems will be addressed later, as well as release them from complaining or being angry.

## 8. Limitations and future work

This research has several limitations. First, as we examined the appraisal and coping processes with cross-sectional data, causal relationships in the research model cannot be tested. Although our hypotheses comply with a theoretical framework, we still suggest more longitudinal or experimental studies to obtain rigorous causal in ference. Second, as the e<sup>f</sup>ects of technostress creators on ICT-enabled productivity are determined by the appraisal and coping processes, future research can identify some moderators in both processes. For instance, our supplementary interviews reveal that cultural norms may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the e<sup>f</sup>ects of techno-invasion on appraisal outcomes. [8] also suggests that the Chinese culture of being tolerant can explain the positive e<sup>f</sup>ect of techno-overload on productivity among Chinese subjects. However, the avoidance of saying no to bosses in Chinese organiza tional culture may not be the case in Western countries. Cultural norms may even di<sup>f</sup>er across companies in China. Further investigation on the role cultural norms play in the appraisal process of technostress is thereby warranted. Third, we only examined three coping strategies (one problem-focused and two emotion-focused). As Lazarus and Folkman have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed other types of coping strategies (e.g., wishful thinking, seeking social support) [11], it will be important to adapt them to technostress research. Future studies can extend our research model with more coping strategies. Lastly, we only investigate the <sup>fi</sup>ve currently identi<sup>fi</sup>ed technostress creators. As suggested by [14], more technostress creators should be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. An examination of which technostress creators are associated with challenge appraisal and which are related to hindrance appraisal can shed new light on technostress research.
