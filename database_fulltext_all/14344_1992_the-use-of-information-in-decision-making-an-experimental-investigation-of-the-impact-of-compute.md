## Abstract

The traditional assumption in the decision support systems (DSS) literature is that if decision makers are provided with expanded processing capabilities they will use them to analyze problems in more depth and, as a result, make better decisions. Empirical studies investigating the relationship between DSS and decision quality have not borne this out. The explanation for such outcomes could be found in behavioral decision-making theories. The literature on behavioral decision making indicates that the conservation of effort may be more important than increased decision quality in some cases. If this is so, then the use of a decision aid may result in effort savings but not improved decision performance. The two experiments reported here use verbal protocol analysis to compare the extent of information use by unaided decision makers and users of a decision aid designed to support preferential choice problems. The results of the two studies indicate that subjects with a decision aid did not use more information than those without one. Overall, subjects behaved as if effort minimization was an important consideration. For

DSS researchers these studies indicate that to understand the DSS-decision quality relationship, it is necessary to consider the decision maker's tradeoff between improving decision quality and conserving effort. For DSS designers these results imply a need to focus on the moderating role that effort will play in determining DSS effectiveness.

Keywords: Decision support systems, information processing, cognitive cost-benefit theory

ACM Categories: H.1.2, H.4.2

## Introduction and Objectives

Decision support systems (DSS) research has focused primarily on measures of decision performance to determine the influence of a DSS. Reviews of the literature indicate that the influence of DSS on decision performance has been mixed; in some studies performance improved, in others there was no effect, and in still others decision quality worsened when a DSS was employed (Benbasat and Nault, 1990, and Sharda, et al., 1988, provide reviews). Researchers often claim these equivocal results are due to differences in task and experimental conditions and in some instances to poor research design (Sharda, et al., 1988). While these are plausible contributing factors, it is also likely that these studies have not assessed all of the potentially relevant variables that determine DSS impact. This paper investigates one such factor, the role of decision-making effort, or cognitive cost, and examines how it influences decision making with a DSS.

Recently, there has been a series of papers that examined the role of cognitive cost as a key factor influencing the use of decision aids (Jarvenpaa, 1989; Kleinmuntz and Schkade, 1989; Todd and Adams, 1989). In general, these papers argue that in order to understand the benefits of a DSS, it is important to understand the cognitive costs associated with its use, and more generally, the process whereby decision makers trade off effort expenditures against decision quality. However, with the exception of the work by Jarvenpaa (1989), these arguments have been made at the theoretical level only. The Jarvenpaa study provided an empirical analysis of the cost-benefit model in the context of varying information presentations (bar charts organized by attribute, by alternative, or in a combined grouped version), and it focused on strategy measures.

This paper reports on two experiments that examine the cognitive effort expended by decision makers when assisted with a DSS. Whereas Jarvenpaa (1989) focused on passive support based on information presentation, we look at active support tools that allow for the manipulation of information (Johnson, et al., 1988). In addition, while previous papers have discussed decision strategy changes (Jarvenpaa, 1989; Todd and Benbasat, 1991), this paper discusses information use and processing.

The focus of this investigation from the point of view of the DSS researcher is on whether (1) the availability of a decision aid that replaces or automates certain cognitive processes leads to an increase in effort expenditure and information processing by the decision maker, thus resulting in more effective decision making, or, alternatively, that (2) DSS use leads to more efficient decision making with no impact on quality because decision makers place a higher value on reducing effort than on attaining the best possible decision quality. Argument 1 is implicit in traditional DSS research and is rooted in the notion of the bounded rationality. It is based on the belief that decision makers would like to do a more complete analysis but cannot due to limitations of humans as information processors (Taylor, 1975). Argument 2 is based on the cognitive cost perspective developed in behavioral decision theory; this is discussed in detail in the next section. Briefly, this literature indicates that tradeoffs between effort and accuracy may place a higher value on effort considerations. This is thought to occur because feedback on effort is both more immediate and tangible than feedback on accuracy (Einhorn and Hogarth, 1978; 1981; Kleinmuntz and Schkade, 1989).

From the perspective of the DSS user, control over the problem-solving process is desirable; that is, the tradeoff between the amount of effort put into problem solving and the benefits expected from it should be left to the user's discretion and not imposed by the system. This is consistent with the notion that a DSS should flexibly support a variety of decision-making approaches (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978; Sprague and Carlson, 1982). Furthermore, Silver (1990)

states that the notion of a non-directive DSS is common to much of the DSS literature. Thus, from the user's viewpoint, a DSS provides a means to devote more effort to problem solving by automating some of the tasks previously handled by the user. The user must choose between investing this effort in the pursuit of a better quality outcome or performing the task more efficiently at the same (unaided) level of quality.

From the perspective of the DSS designer, the existence of a tradeoff implies that it is difficult to determine the benefits of a DSS precisely in terms of efficiency or effectiveness. Therefore, the lack of an improvement in effectiveness should not necessarily be considered a failure of the DSS if one believes that it should be non-directive (Silver, 1990; Sprague and Carlson, 1982). However, the designer has to understand the link between the type of features included in the DSS, DSS use, and the potential reduction in decision-making effort in order to understand how DSS use will influence decision behavior. In addition, the designer may face other pressures from organizational forces. For example, the organization may dictate certain desirable problem-solving strategies and direct users toward them. Though these can be built into a decision aid, they may not be used by the decision maker unless they are implemented with a thorough understanding of the effort factors associated with their use. Thus, understanding the effort-accuracy relationship is a key issue for DSS users—who need to be cognizant of the factors that influence their decision behavior—and for DSS designers—who need to understand how to direct decision makers effectively toward prescribed strategies.

The paper proceeds as follows: In the next section, the literature on cognitive effort in decision making is reviewed. This is followed by a review of the decision aid used in the experiments. The remaining sections present the hypothesis development, the research framework for the two experiments, and the results. The paper ends with a discussion of the results and concluding comments.

## Cognitive Effort and Choice Strategies

This section presents the empirical and conceptual work on the importance of cognitive effort in strategy selection and information processing and discusses the nature of the task used in these experiments.

## Cognitive effort perspective

Empirical studies of choice problems indicate that decision makers are highly adaptive in selecting strategies (Johnson and Payne, 1985). Researchers have proposed a variety of mechanisms that influence strategy selection (Einhorn and Hogarth, 1981). Among these, effort and accuracy have received considerable attention. Payne (1982) proposed a cost-benefit framework of cognition that incorporates the notion that decision makers focus on tradeoffs between quality and effort in decision making. A subsequent series of both empirical and simulation work undertaken, among others by Payne and his colleagues, is largely supportive of this notion (see for example, (Bettman, et al., 1990; Creyer, et al., 1990; Jarvenpaa, 1989; Johnson and Payne, 1985; Johnson, et al., 1988; Payne, et al., 1988; Stone and Schkade, 1991).

According to the cost-benefit framework, the joint objectives of a decision-maker are to maximize accuracy (or decision quality) $^{1}$ and minimize effort. As these objectives often conflict, tradeoffs are made between the two. An open question in the current literature is the emphasis or value placed on effort and accuracy in making the tradeoff. The traditional DSS literature emphasizes the importance of decision quality (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978). On the other hand, literature in behavioral decision theory indicates that effort may be an important overall consideration in many problem contexts (Kleinmuntz and Schkade, 1989; Russo and Dosher, 1983). While the tradeoff between effort and accuracy is not fully understood and is likely to be highly dependent on characteristics of the decision task (see Beach and Mitchell, 1978, for a detailed discussion), there are two predictions that can be made:

1. Given two strategies that are expected to require the same effort, the one that is expected to produce a more accurate outcome will be preferred, and

2. Given two strategies that produce equivalent outcomes, the one that is expected to require less effort will be preferred.

Conceptual models of decision making have suggested that effort may be the key to explaining much decision behavior. Conceptual models of contingent decision behavior argue that effort is a key determinant of strategy selection and that, other things being equal, decision makers will try to minimize effort (Beach and Mitchell, 1978; Jungerman, 1985; March, 1978; Shugan, 1979). Effort may be weighted more heavily than accuracy because feedback on effort expenditure is relatively immediate, while feedback on accuracy is subject to both delay and ambiguity (Einhorn and Hogarth, 1978, 1981; Kleinmuntz and Schkade, 1989).

Similarly, Monte Carlo simulations of decision strategies demonstrate that heuristics will save decision makers considerable effort relative to a normative approach while sacrificing little in terms of accuracy (Johnson and Payne, 1985; Payne, et al., 1988; Thorngate, 1980). Numerous empirical studies have concluded that decision makers attend more to effort reduction than to accuracy maximization (Bettman and Kakkar, 1977; Bettman, et al., 1990; Christensen-Szalanski, 1978; Russo and Dosher, 1983).

Overall, the empirical, simulation, and conceptual literature indicate that effort is an important factor influencing strategy selection. By paying careful attention to how a DSS influences effort, it may be possible to predict how it will be employed. Because the approach taken to date of focusing on how a DSS might improve decision quality has generated conflicting results (Benbasat and Nault, 1990), it may be fruitful to adopt an effort perspective in trying to understand the behavior of decision makers using DSS.

## Preferential choice problems

The focus in this research is on support for preferential choice problems, which are among the generic classes of decision-aiding technologies identified by Zachary (1986). These problems deal with tasks where a decision maker chooses one from among a set of alternatives, each of which is described by a common set of attributes. For example, alternative apartments may be described by a set of attributes, such as number of bedrooms, size, and rent. Svenson (1979) describes 12 strategies applicable to preferential choice problems. Of these, four prototypical decision strategies have been the focus of attention in empirical studies of preferential choice (see for example, Biggs, et al., 1985; Jarvenpaa, 1989; Olshavsky, 1979; Payne, 1976; Sundstrom, 1987). These are: additive-compensatory, additive-difference, conjunctive, and elimination by aspects.

The additive-compensatory model (AC) is based on evaluating one alternative at a time along all relevant attributes. A score for each alternative is determined by summing the product of each attribute's value and its weight. The alternative with the highest final score is chosen.

The additive-difference model (AD) is based on a comparison between attributes for two alternatives at a time (Tversky, 1969). For each attribute-pair a difference is taken. The differences are weighted and summed to produce an overall evaluation. The preferred alternative is retained while the other is eliminated. This process is repeated until only one alternative remains.

The conjunctive (CNJ) model is based on the evaluation of each attribute against a minimum threshold level. Information is examined by alternative; if an alternative violates a specified threshold for any of the attributes, it is dropped from further consideration. The search is repeated until only one alternative remains.

The elimination by aspects (EBA) model compares attribute values against some threshold level (Tversky, 1972). However, unlike CNJ, values for an attribute are examined across alternatives. Any alternative that does not meet the threshold level for any attribute is eliminated. All remaining alternatives are evaluated against the next attribute. This procedure is repeated until a single alternative remains.

A detailed analysis of the effort required to implement each of these strategies is provided in Todd and Benbasat (1991). In general, the additive strategies (AC and AD) require that all available information be processed; they thus require greater effort than the elimination strategies (CNJ and EBA), which ignore some information.

Though the additive strategies require greater information use and, thus, effort, they are also thought to lead to more accurate solutions because they integrate all available information into the choice process in a comprehensive fashion and take individual preferences into account in a more detailed and sophisticated way by weighting attributes. Keeney and Raiffa (1976) point out that elimination-based strategies such as EBA and CNJ are “naively simple” and will rarely reflect the true preferences of the decision maker. They therefore suggest additive strategies similar to AC and AD as a normative approach to the solution of such choice problems.

## The Decision Aid

Johnson and Payne (1985) have proposed that understanding the components of a decision strategy will assist in building systems to support particular decision tasks. Our approach to building the DSS for this study followed this philosophy. The general strategy descriptions provided above were developed into formal models of the strategies. These formal models describe the elementary information processes (EIPs) that are required to execute each strategy. EIPs are basic cognitive operations, such as reading and comparing two data values, adding two values together, or shifting attention from one value to another. Based on these EIPs, specific decision aids can be constructed. The aids automate one or more of the EIPs that the decision maker would use in following a particular strategy. The effect of these aids is to reduce the cognitive effort required to implement a specific strategy. That is, a decision maker without the DSS would need to expend more effort than one using the decision aid to achieve the same result.

Of course, use of the decision aid also results in the expenditure of effort. With this in mind, the interface to the decision aid was designed to minimize this effort expenditure. Command syntax was kept simple, with little effort needed to enter commands, and the number of commands was kept relatively small. In addition, extensive pilot tests were conducted to verify that the interface was understandable and easy to use. The command syntax is shown in Table 1. To enter a command the user simply typed the command name (or any unique truncation) and the appropriate attribute or alternative identifiers. Thus, to drop column C, the user could enter DROP C or DR C. To sort based on the level of rent the user would enter SORT 3, where 3 refers to row three, which contains the values for rent.

In using the decision aid, subjects are presented with an attribute by alternative matrix for an apartment selection problem on a computer display terminal (see Figure 1). Initially the matrix is blank. Subjects access information by opening rows (attributes), columns (alternatives) or individual cells. A subject with no decision aid has access only to the (unconditional) OPEN command used to uncover any cell, row or column of the matrix. A subject with the decision aid has a variety of commands available for accessing and manipulating information (see Table 1). The commands are designed to provide varying levels of support for four different preferential choice strategies, as indicated in Table 2.

The commands, are divided into two groups: those that support computation, thus reducing processing load, and those that support data display management, thus reducing the load on memory and making it easier to focus attention. Display management aids allow users to focus more easily on items of current interest. For example, the MOVE command allows users to juxtapose alternatives of interest in order to facilitate attribute-based comparisons (AD and CNJ). This is valuable because the time taken to search for an information item increases with the physical distance between target values. The OPEN/CLOSE commands are used to manage the presentation of information; they allow for the focusing of attention on specific information. For example, in comparing two alternatives, the display of extraneous information increases the possibility of error in locating and extracting the correct values. It also increases the time and effort required to isolate particular values because users search the matrix entries serially (Coren and Ward, 1989). Commands such as OPEN/CLOSE, by allowing decision makers to quickly identify certain salient information, support the focusing of attention, thus reducing the effort associated with information search.

As indicated in Table 2, the commands built into the decision aid could be used to support various aspects of the four preferential choice strategies. There are, for example, a variety of approaches to supporting EBA. First, because the strategy specifies that attributes are examined in order of importance, the MOVE/REORDER commands could be used to rearrange attributes (rows) in the matrix. Second, the CONDITIONAL DROP command automates the operations associated with EBA. Using the CONDITIONAL DROP the user would specify an attribute and a threshold value, for example DROP RENT > 500. The effect of this command would be to automatically eliminate any alternative for which rent was greater than \$500.

Though the CONDITIONAL DROP is the most powerful tool supporting EBA, other system commands, such as CONDITIONAL CLOSE, could also be used. In this case, violated alternatives would be masked, but not eliminated, from the matrix. SORT could also be used to support EBA. By sorting on a particular attribute, from best to worst, a serial search of alternatives can quickly be made to identify those alternatives that violate a specified threshold. Given a sorted series of values, once a single value violating the threshold has been discovered, the user would know that all subsequent values would also violate the threshold. A DROP could then be employed to eliminate the remaining alternatives. Even if the decision maker wanted to perform a serial search and mentally compare each attribute value against a threshold, the ability to OPEN/CLOSE or DROP attributes and alternatives would facilitate this process by helping to focus attention on specific values. Finally, the RESTORE command permits eliminated alternatives to be reconsidered at a later time if, for example, the use of a CONDITIONAL DROP resulted in the elimination of all alternatives.

As demonstrated above, there are multiple ways that a specific strategy can be supported, given the commands available. Thus, the user is not forced to proceed in a predetermined way once a strategy selection is made. In this sense the system is not restrictive or directive (Silver, 1990). Table 2 also indicates that a single command may support more than one strategy. Consider, for example, the SORT command. Its applicability to EBA has been described above. It can also be used to support AC. By sorting alternatives on the important attribute values, those alternatives that are likely to score highly on an overall additive evaluation will be grouped together and more easily identified. Indeed, if there were a dominant alternative it would be revealed by the use of SORT commands. SORT can also support CNJ. Sorting on important criteria will bring alternatives likely to meet minimum thresholds to the beginning of the matrix, and a satisfactory solution may be found more quickly. Thus, an individual command may support more than one strategy.

To reiterate, the overall effect of the system commands is to replace EIPs that a decision maker would use in following a choice strategy. This alleviates some of the cognitive demands placed on the decision maker. The question that must be addressed is what, if anything, does the decision maker do as a result of this saved effort? The assumption in the DSS field is that the effort saved will be reinvested in problem solving and that better decisions will result (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978; Sprague and Carlson, 1982).

It is, however, possible that the decision maker will merely conserve the saved effort so that it can be devoted to other activities.

## Hypothesis Development

Decision makers typically trade off the amount of effort to be spent in making a decision and the benefits they expect as a consequence of effort expenditure. The effect of a DSS is to replace or reduce the effort expended in information acquisition, management, and processing. Thus, from this perspective the DSS is viewed primarily as a vehicle for effort reduction; that is, a well-designed DSS should reduce the effort required to follow one or more decision strategies. The decision maker then has the option of (1) investing the effort saved by DSS use into finding a better quality solution, or (2) making the decision at a lower level of cognitive cost than they would have without the DSS.

The impact of a DSS on decision-making effectiveness is therefore indirect because the DSS provides the potential to improve decision quality by expanding the cognitive resources available to the individual decision maker. $^{2}$ However, for this potential improvement to be realized, several conditions must be fulfilled. First, in order for a DSS to be of value, the effort required to interact with it must be less than the effort reduction in problem solving it provides (Keen, 1979). In other words, the DSS must provide a net reduction in effort. Second, the decision maker must be dissatisfied with the current level of decision quality in order to be motivated to use the DSS. To the extent that the decision maker is an effort minimizer, such motivation to improve decision effectiveness may only occur if the effort associated with using more accurate decision processes is made equivalent to the cost of other simpler, low accuracy heuristics. Thus, a central question in understanding the role of a DSS in decision making is the extent to which the decision maker is motivated by effort or accuracy considerations.

The previous section presented a decision aid designed to reduce the amount of cognitive effort required to acquire and process information for choice problems. A decision maker who values decision quality more than decision effort would reinvest the effort saved back into the task in order to process more information and make a better-informed decision. However, a decision maker who places a higher value on effort would be inclined to save resources and conduct no extra information processing. This implies that the null hypotheses presented below can be interpreted in two different contexts, one that emphasizes accuracy maximization and the other that emphasizes effort minimization. The hypotheses are:

There will be no differences between aided and unaided decision makers in terms of:

H1. Unique units of information examined

H2. Total units of information examined

H3. Number of alternatives examined in detail

We will next discuss the expected outcomes of the hypothesis testing for a decision maker who is viewed as an effort minimizer and for a decision maker who is viewed as an accuracy maximizer.

## Emphasis on effort

If the decision maker places a higher value on effort than on accuracy, then no difference would be expected in the number of unique units of information examined, the total number of information units examined, and the number of alternatives examined in detail. Thus, if subjects behave as effort minimizers, H1, H2, and H3 will not be rejected. Rather than reinvesting any effort savings, the decision maker will simply look for a “low effort” or efficient way to find a satisfactory solution to the task when using the decision aid. Such a prediction would be consistent with the findings of Goslar, et al. (1986), who observed that there was no difference in the amount of data considered by DSS supported and non-supported groups. This interpretation would also be consistent with the behavioral decision theory literature cited earlier, which indicates that a decision maker is willing to sacrifice some degree of decision quality or accuracy in favor of conserving decision effort.

## Emphasis on accuracy

Assuming that decision makers are capacity-constrained, a decision maker who emphasizes accuracy would be performing a task using all available cognitive resources. The effect of introducing a decision aid is to expand the pool of processing resources available (Taylor, 1975). Given an expanded resource pool, the decision maker can invest more effort into problem solving leading to greater information use and more complete search of alternatives. Thus, if subjects behave as accuracy maximizers we would expect that H1, H2, and H3 would be rejected.

## Interaction between the decision aid and problem size

A second factor expected to influence information processing and, thus, the number of units of information used is the number of alternatives from which a choice is to be made. Moreover, we expect that the problem size and decision aid may interact, with the decision aid being more influential as problem size increases.

The amount of information processed is influenced by the size of the choice set provided to the decision maker (Biggs, et al., 1985; Olshavsky, 1979; Payne 1976). In general, the findings of these studies indicate that while more information is processed as problem size increases, the proportion of information processed falls. This implies that the decision maker changes processing strategies in response to information load. It is likely that the availability of a decision aid might moderate this relationship. Certain commands, such as CONDITIONAL DROP, SORT, and REORDER (see Table 1), apply across all alternatives in the choice set and, hence, replace multiple cognitive operations. The effort to invoke each of these commands does not change as a function of problem size, yet the benefits in terms of cognitive effort saved does. Based upon this reasoning, the value of a decision aid may increase with the size of the choice problem. Thus, we expect that the hypothesis

H4. There will be no interaction between the decision aid and problem size for

a. Unique units of information examined

b. Total units of information examined

c. Number of alternatives examined in detail

will be rejected. The difference in unique and total information use and number of alternatives examined, between aided and unaided decision makers, should increase as problem size increases.

## Framework for Experiments

The experimental design and procedures for both experiments 1 and 2 (E1 and E2) are described together in this section as they are very similar. The findings of the two experiments are then presented in the next major section.

## General overview of experiments

Experiment 1 (E1) investigates the influence of the decision aid for choice problems having five and 10 alternatives. Such problems have been studied previously in the choice literature; thus E1 serves as a point of comparison to earlier work that did not study decision aids. Experiment 2 (E2) examines the influence of the decision aid for problems having 10 and 20 alternatives.

The decision aid used in E2 was identical to that used in E1. The commands available to the aided group subjects are shown in Table 1. Subjects in the unaided group still used the computer but could only use the OPEN command to access information. Presentation of information differed between the two experiments. Since it was not possible to display 20 alternatives on a single screen, the display was provided via two 10-alternative screens. The 10-alternative problem, in E2, was presented on two five-alternative screens. Thus, subjects in the 10-alternative treatment could see any five consecutive alternatives on the screen at one time, and those in the 20-alternative treatment any 10 consecutive alternatives at a time. Subjects used cursor keys to scroll across the two screens. In summary, the screen sizes used were:

E1 (5 alternatives): one 8 x 5 display

(10 alternatives): one 8 x 10 display

E2 (10 alternatives): two 8 x 5 displays

(20 alternatives): two 8 x 10 displays

Other than these differences, E2 was identical to E1 in terms of the decision aid used, experimental procedures, task, and dependent variables measured.

## Independent variables

The experiments manipulated two factors, each at two levels. The primary factor was the presence or absence of a decision aid. The second factor was the number of choice alternatives.

## The Decision Aid

The decision aid contained a set of commands outlined in Table 1. Unaided decision makers could only use the system to access data (via the OPEN command); no processing or data manipulation capabilities were provided. Aided decision makers could use any of these commands to support the choice process. Interaction with the aid took place by entering commands at a prompt line. The commands consisted of a command name and parameters to indicate rows, columns, or threshold values against which to apply the command. For example, to sort all values of rent (located in row three of the matrix) in ascending order, the user would enter SORT 3 A. To drop alternative H, the user would enter DROP H. All commands could be abbreviated using truncation.

## Problem Size

Problem size was manipulated at 5 and 10 alternatives for E1, and at 10 and 20 for E2. This permitted the effects of decision aid use to be examined across a variety of information loads. The five and 10-alternative problems were employed to be consistent with previous research. The number of attributes describing each alternative was held constant at eight. Eight is the mean number of attributes used by Payne (1976). Subsequent research of this type has used seven attributes (Biggs, et al., 1985; Jarvenpaa, 1989). Overall, the number of alternatives has been more influential than has the number of attributes on information processing (Jacoby, 1984; Malhotra, 1984; Stone and Schkade, 1991).

## Dependent variables

In this study we are interested in the effect of the use of decision aids on information processing effort. Cognitive effort can be viewed as a process by which cognitive operators are applied to specific operands, or units of information. In these studies we measure the operand component of effort by tracking the information that was used as input to a choice process. Similar measures of effort have been employed by Payne (1976) and others (e.g., Bettman, et al., 1986; Shugan, 1980).

The three dependent variables were: (1) unique units of information referenced, (2) total units of information referenced, and (3) number of alternatives analyzed in detail. All information usage measures are taken from an analysis of verbal protocols that were recorded concurrently with the experimental session. The taped protocols were transcribed and parsed into individual phrases or statements. The individual statements then form the basic unit of analysis. The coders (the first author and a research assistant) examined the transcripts for implicit and explicit references to information cells. The cell references were then marked on a coding sheet, which was a reproduction of Figure 1. This coding provided information on the number of cells referenced, the number of references to information, and the number of alternatives for which all cells were referenced. For the purpose of the measures used, the three ways in which a cell may be referenced are defined as follows:

1. If the subject makes an explicit reference to the value in the cell.

2. If the subject implicitly references the cell as part of a more general comment. For example, “I see apartment D has the lowest rent” implies that rent values for all alternatives have been examined.

3. For the aided subjects, the use of certain commands imply information use. For example, if a subject sorts rent in ascending order then he or she would be considered to have examined all the values for rent. Other commands that lead to multiple information use counts are: CONDITIONAL DROP, CONDITIONAL OPEN, CONDITIONAL CLOSE, and CALCULATE. In each case these commands replace some set of operations that the subject would otherwise be required to perform; thus, they allow information to be factored into the choice process without ever being explicitly referenced by the subject.

## Unique Units of Information Referenced

The number of unique information references is measured by counting the number of distinct cells (of the choice matrix) used by the decision maker as input to the choice process. The maximum value for this measure is the size of the attribute by alternative matrix. This measure focuses on the breadth of search. In general, the more unique units of information extracted, the more complete the evaluation of alternative choices has been.

## Total Units of Information Referenced

Total information references are determined in the same way as the unique information reference measure. The difference is that it counts all references to a particular unit of information rather than counting the number of unique references. Thus, if a subject refers to the value of rent for apartment D on five different occasions, that would count as five units for this measure but only as one unique reference. This measure approximates the total amount of effort expended during the decision process by counting the total units or operands on which elementary operations were performed. Similar measures have been proposed previously by Shugan (1979) and Bettman, et al. (1986).

Both of the information reference measures are conservative estimates of actual information processing. Though verbal protocols provide a dense trace of the choice process, it is possible that a subject would use a piece of information without explicitly or implicitly referencing that item. However, reference to an item of information would imply that value has at least been read. In this way, the information reference measures can be considered as a lower bound on actual information acquisition and processing.

## Number of Alternatives Analyzed in Detail

The number of alternatives examined in detail is another measure of the completeness of information searched. More exhaustive, additive strategies require complete examination of alternatives, while elimination strategies, such as EBA and CNJ, result in the complete evaluation only of those alternatives that are acceptable on all attributes.

An alternative is considered to be analyzed in detail if it has been examined along all of its attributes except those for which the decision maker has explicitly specified (in the verbal protocols) not to be relevant to the choice.

## Task environment

Subjects performed an apartment selection task similar to that employed by Payne (1976). This task was chosen because: (1) it is a common choice problem for which a large proportion of the subject population (university students) have had previous experience, (2) the task requires no specialized knowledge, and (3) observations during pilot testing indicated that subjects could relate easily to this problem. Taken together we would argue that these factors increase the decision makers' level of involvement with the task. Involvement, or personal relevance, is thought to be important because it is hypothesized to lead to more attentional and information processing resources being devoted to the task (Houston and Rothschild, 1979). Thus, it is more appropriate to test the relationship between decision aids and effort spent for high involvement tasks.

For these experiments we argue that our subjects would tend to have relatively high levels of intrinsic involvement with housing decisions because of their age, background, and lifestyles. Many would have recently made such decisions or would be making them in the near future. Situation-specific involvement may be lower for the subjects because there were no specific incentives in this case to motivate the selection of a specific (best) apartment. However, Celsi and Olson (1988) argue that experimental settings in and of themselves tend to increase situational involvement because they invoke goals for subjects to “look good” and “be clever,” which would induce greater attention to the task and lead to more information processing. Thus, on balance, even in absence of performance-specific incentives, the apartment selection task would be relevant and involving for the subjects. In order to further reenforce this involvement, care was taken to use relevant and realistic attributes and attribute values for the experimental tasks.

The eight attributes used to describe each apartment were: rent, size, number of bedrooms, noise, brightness, availability of laundry, cleanliness, and distance to campus. The data concerning rent, number of bedrooms, size, laundry, and distance to the university were taken from advertisements in local newspapers. This helped to ensure that the choices approximated those available in the local market. Where possible, data were reported in the appropriate metric (for example, rent in dollars per month). Ratings for noise, brightness, and cleanliness were provided on a five-point ordinal numeric scale. These values were assigned at random. Laundry was represented by a binary (yes/no) value. Although there is evidence that data format (e.g., numeric versus text) can affect processing (Stone and Schkade, 1991) this was not tested in this study; all treatment groups received identical attribute presentations. Information for each attribute was presented as a row in the matrix (see Figure 1). Ordering of the rows and columns was determined randomly prior to the experiment.

Each alternative had both good and poorly valued attributes. There was no alternative that was either superior to or inferior to any other alternative along all attributes. In E1, all alternatives used in the five-alternative setting could be found in the 10-alternative problem set. In E2, all alternatives used in the 10-alternative problem set could be found in the 20-alternative set. In addition, no data were repeated between the practice session and the experimental task.

## Subjects

Subjects for E1 and E2 were undergraduate business students. Participation was strictly voluntary. Monetary incentives of \$50, \$40, \$30, \$20, and \$10 were awarded at random to five subjects in each experiment after the study was completed.

Twenty-eight subjects participated in E1, of whom 15 were female. There were also 28 subjects in E2, of whom 14 were females. None of the subjects for E1 was included in E2. For each experiment seven subjects were assigned at random to each of the four combinations of decision aid and problem size.

## Experimental procedures

Subjects were run through the study one at a time. Each subject went through three steps: (1) tutorial, (2) practice session, and (3) experimental session. The tutorial was provided to explain each of the commands and enable subjects to perform various tasks that required the use of all the commands. The tutorial did not make any direct linkages between the commands and various choice strategies. Further, the tutorial was based upon a “dummy” problem of selecting among alternative widgets, each described by a set of meaningless attribute names. Its purpose was to familiarize subjects with the way the commands functioned. Because the unaided group subjects received only the OPEN command, their tutorial focused only on how to open rows, columns, and cells. During the tutorial a lab assistant was present to answer questions about the decision aid.

Following the tutorial, subjects went through a practice session and then the experimental session. While making a choice the subjects were asked to think out loud. The practice problem was intended to give the subjects a chance to familiarize themselves with the task setting, gain further experience with command use, and become comfortable with verbalizing. Instructions were provided to the subjects on how to verbalize, following the recommendations of Russo, et al. (1986).

No time constraints were imposed. Subjects were free to use as much or as little information as they wished. The aided group subjects were told to use whatever commands they thought were appropriate. Subjects were to treat the problem as if they were making a selection for themselves and to choose the one apartment they would like to rent. A typical session required 60 to 90 minutes total elapsed time.

During both the experimental and practice sessions a lab assistant was present, though out of sight of the subjects. The lab assistant would prompt subjects to “please say what you are thinking” if they were silent for more than 10 seconds. An audio tape was made of each subject’s verbalization of the choice process. Subjects were aware that they were being recorded. A computer log captured command use. The data used in the following analyses were those collected during the experimental session.

Finally, the decision process of the aided decision maker was more streamlined and consistent. Each of these observations is elaborated upon below.

## Additional Processing with the Decision Aid

Overall, it is clear that the decision aid was used to replace information references by the decision maker. However, not all of the effort absorbed by the decision aid was saved by the decision maker. There was additional processing related to weighting attributes and determining tradeoffs. These were pre-decisional planning activities. Examples of planning statements would include such things as: "Rent is important so I'll look at that first" or "I'm trying to decide what is more important, laundry or distance." Planning such as this guides the direction of search, influences final choices, and requires effort.

A post hoc reexamination of the protocols from E1 indicates that aided decision makers did more planning; 7.5 planning statements per subject versus 3.4 for the unaided group (f=5.15; p=0.04). The use of the decision aid appears to shift the focus of subjects to preferences and planning. This is consistent with the findings reported by Dickmeyer (1983). Similar results were found for E2. The use of the decision aid again led to an increase in planning statements (f=4.92; p=0.04). The aided group subjects made, on average, 16.5 such statements, while those in the unaided group averaged 12. Significantly, this result shows some evidence that decision makers redirect their effort toward activities not supported by the decision aid.

## Reducing Dysfunctional Behavior

Another impact of the decision aid was to simplify the decision process and make it more linear. It was more common for unaided decision makers to iterate through the problem several times and to return to alternatives that had been previously eliminated. Seven of the 28 unaided decision makers (25 percent) showed evidence of this type of behavior. By contrast, such “forgetting” behavior appeared in only one aided decision maker’s protocol. From this observation it is apparent that one advantage of the decision aid is to assist with the “mental bookkeeping” associated with making a choice. By managing the information presentation, through dropping or closing, the system helps direct attention to appropriate alternatives. This would account for the large number of DROP and CLOSE commands used.

The “forgetting” in the unaided groups may have two dysfunctional consequences. First, it causes work to be needlessly repeated and alternatives to be reexamined after they have already been deemed unsatisfactory. This reduces the efficiency of problem solving. An examination of Tables 3 and 4 shows that as problem size increases, the gap between aided and unaided decision makers widens in terms of unique information references; but this gap narrows in terms of total information references. This is indicative of the reprocessing of information by the unaided subjects. Second, the decision maker may forget about possible choices being considered. For example, one unaided group subject had found one acceptable alternative, G, at the end of a 10-alternative set. He then returned to the beginning of the problem space to reexamine some initial alternatives that had satisfied the basic threshold values and chose one of these alternatives without further reference to alternative G. Apparently this subject simply forgot about alternative G and then made a choice based upon the remaining alternatives. Such behavior can lead to substandard or undesirable choices being made, reducing the quality of the solution. The ability of the decision aid to avoid such problems by managing and keeping track of information is an important benefit.

## Discussion and Concluding Comments

This section examines the findings of the two experiments in terms of the effort accuracy context and then reviews competing alternative interpretations of the results. After this, implications of the research for both DSS design and research are discussed.

## Effort versus accuracy

The results of E1 and E2 are consistent with the view that decision makers place a high value on effort minimization. Despite the fact that the decision aid decreases the effort required to examine information for any one of the preferential choice strategies, there is no indication that decision makers translated that saved effort into greater information usage. The number of unique units of information referenced (Tables 3a and 3b) fell far short of the complete information use that would be required of the AC and AD strategies. $^{3}$ Similarly, the decision makers examined only a portion of the full set of alternatives available in detail and looked at only a portion of the attributes within each alternative. In addition, for larger problem sizes (20 alternatives) the proportion of information units referenced dropped significantly, further indicating effort-minimizing behavior. All these behaviors are consistent with elimination strategies.

The data also indicated that the decision aid was responsible for many of the information references in the aided condition. Because total information references, which include both references by the decision maker and the decision aid, were not different between the aided and unaided decision-making groups, we conclude that the aided decision makers actually reduced their effort expenditure. They processed less information themselves and relied on the decision aid to reduce their information processing burden. Such behavior is consistent with effort minimization.

## Alternative interpretations of the results

Though the data are suggestive of effort-minimization behavior on the part of the subjects, it is important to evaluate possible alternative explanations for the observed behavior. The behavior of the subjects may have been an artifact of the task setting and the decision aid used. The task may not have provided adequate incentive and motivation to induce accuracy-maximizing behavior. In addition, the effort associated with using the decision aid may have overburdened the subjects and inhibited additional information processing. Each of these alternative explanations is explored below.

In this study it was difficult to provide decision makers with an incentive to focus on making “high quality” decisions and, thus, to invest additional effort in the task. Correct outcomes are difficult to determine for preferential choice problems because they depend on individual preferences, which may be different for each decision maker. This means that in an experimental setting it is difficult to tie incentives to performance (though see, for example, Goslar, et al., 1986, for one such approach). In settings where the decision maker must live with the consequences of the decision, it may be that decision quality becomes the primary concern and increased effort expenditure will result from decision aid use. However, it should be pointed out that choice studies within more realistic settings, but without decision aid availability, have yielded a similar pattern of results.

For example, in a study of bank loan officers, Biggs, et al. (1985) determined that information processing was limited and that significant filtering occurred when choices were made between competing loan applications. Complete information use was observed only in cases where three alternatives were made available. In addition, even in decision settings where subjects were offered significant monetary incentives to completely process information and use normative models (in this case “expected value”), limited use was made of available information (Johnson, et al., 1988).

Furthermore, there was evidence from both E1 and E2 that subjects were involved with the task and motivated to perform well. After completing the task, subjects were asked open-ended questions in terms of its inherent interest and realism. They were also asked to what extent the exercise captured their attention. In terms of realism, 41 of the 56 subjects indicated that they found the task to be realistic; only six rated it as not realistic. Furthermore, 43 of the 56 participants indicated that the task was interesting and attention-grabbing, while none specifically indicated that it was not. Taken together these responses seem to indicate that the task captured the attention of the subjects and that they were involved to a sufficient extent to be motivated to process the available information. To the extent that the decision aids provided effort savings, we might have expected additional processing to occur if accuracy maximization was the paramount objective of the decision maker. The fact that it did not is supportive of effort-minimization behavior.

Another plausible explanation for the lack of increase in information processing by the aided group is that the combination of the task demands and the effort required to use the decision aid overloaded the aided group subjects, thus suppressing additional processing. However, the problem size effects indicate that the total amount of information processed actually increased with problem size (f = 4.7; p = 0.01). This increase would have been unlikely if the decision makers were overloaded. In addition, those subjects who used the decision aid rated the commands as easy to use. The median ease-of-use rating for all commands was 2 on a seven-point scale where 1 was “very easy to use” and 7 “very hard to use”. Even commands such as CONDITIONAL DROP and SORT, which might be perceived as more complex, had median ratings of 2. Based on this data it does not seem reasonable to argue that the decision makers were overloaded by either the task or the decision aid.

Based on this analysis we conclude that the effort-minimizing behavior of the subjects was not simply due to the nature of the task and the decision aids provided. Nevertheless, it would be useful to test the robustness of these findings by examining the behavior of users for other tasks and with other decision aids. To the extent that the findings are generalizable across tasks and decision aids they have implications for the approaches taken to DSS design. These are discussed in the next section.

## Implications for DSS design

Recently, Silver (1990) pointed out that DSS design can be broken into two schools of thought; (1) directed change, where the objective of the DSS designer is to determine the appropriate normative decision model to solve a problem, observe the process that the decision maker actually follows, and then design a DSS that moves the decision maker from the current process toward the normative model, and (2) non-directed change, where the DSS designer provides information processing support via a basket of tools and leaves the decision maker to decide if and how the systems capabilities should be employed. According to Silver, these two design philosophies will cause differences in what the users can and will do with the DSS, both in terms of structuring the initial decision process and in terms of how that process is executed.

The effort-minimization argument has implications for how a DSS might be used, whether it is designed from a directed or non-directed change perspective. First, consider the notion of directed change. The goal is to move the decision maker toward a normative strategy, which presumably provides a more accurate outcome. The key question for a DSS designer in this case is how to design a system that effects this shift in the problem-solving process. Our results indicate that effort may be a key factor in facilitating or inhibiting this shift. The effort minimizer is likely to move toward the normative strategy only if it is as easy or easier than some competing alternative process that is available. Thus, DSS design should focus on this effort objective, otherwise the DSS may either be rejected or used counterproductively. The effort considerations of the designer must incorporate both the effort required of the decision maker to interact with the DSS and the effort required to process the information generated by the system. If these factors are properly taken into account, then it is possible for the use of a DSS to lead to higher quality decisions.

Now consider the case of non-directed change. To the extent that users are left to their own devices they are likely to use the DSS tool kit to minimize their overall effort expenditure in completing the task. The outcome of DSS use in this case is likely to be more efficient decision making rather than more effective decisions. While this may be a legitimate outcome of DSS use, it is probably not the one intended by designers. For example, Sprague and Carlson (1982), whom Silver (1990) cites as the prime advocates of non-directed change, also assert that DSSs are intended to improve the effectiveness of people in organizations. According to the effort perspective, such a goal may be unattainable because efficiency is a more likely outcome of DSS use. Given a choice of tools, an effort-minimizing decision maker is likely to follow a path of least resistance.

Thus, it is important for the DSS designer to understand (1) the link between the features included in a DSS and effort reduction for problem solving, and (2) the effort needed to use the DSS. It is likely that DSS features that provide the greatest benefits (in terms of pursuing a course of action that is most effective) are more difficult to use. This is a common argument for the low usage of many management science models. Therefore, the designer has to think about the user making a second tradeoff where cost is the effort to employ the DSS or its particular features and the benefit is the effort reduction on problem solving achieved by its use. If the tools included are difficult to use or their potential value in reducing effort is not evident to the user, then the designer has failed to provide an adequate support tool.

## Research implications and challenges

The key message of these studies is that the amount of information processed does not necessarily increase with the use of a decision aid. Instead, it appears in this study that decision makers use decision aids to reduce the amount of effort required to complete a task. $^{4}$ This outcome challenges the commonly held belief that a well-designed DSS will lead to improved decision making (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978; Sprague and Carlson, 1982). Keen (1979) argued that the DSS-decision quality relationship was largely advocated as an “article of faith.” He further asserted that it was important to understand the role that effort plays to determine the net benefit of DSS use:

...the argument in this paper is that the marginal economics of effort are such that a DSS or any similar decision aid may impose costs that outweigh the perceived gains because existing incremental or heuristic strategies are extremely cheap....A DSS will be accepted mainly because it provides adequate benefits for low incremental effort (p. 2, emphasis in original).

The studies reported here offer empirical evidence that effort is an important construct to understand in trying to predict the impact of a DSS. Furthermore, they show that decision makers appear to weigh effort considerations heavily. As a result, DSS use may not have the impact on decision quality usually assumed in the DSS literature; instead the effect is likely to be modified by the effort considerations of the decision maker. This could partially explain some of the contradictory findings in the literature. It is, therefore, important to direct the attention of DSS researchers to understanding precisely how effort considerations influence DSS use.

Future research should focus on refining measures of effort and on developing categories of possible effort expenditure. Three types of effort were identified in this study: (1) processing effort associated with information in the choice matrix, (2) planning effort related to determining tradeoffs between attributes, and (3) effort devoted to understanding and using the decision aid. A finer decomposition of these measures would assist in understanding the effort tradeoffs of decision makers in using various types of decision aids.

As noted above, future research should examine the findings reported here in a wide variety of task settings and conditions to determine the extent to which the findings are generalizable. Thus, incentive-based performance conditions, such as risky choice or realistic field conditions, might be examined. It will also be important to establish the degree to which task characteristics and DSS use interact to influence effort expenditures. To the extent that decision makers are sensitive to changes in task conditions it would be useful to study the extent to which various decision aids can moderate the influence of task contingencies.

In conclusion, the cognitive cost perspective is helpful in understanding the influence of DSS on decision making. Decision makers utilize the tools provided in such a way as to limit their overall expenditures of effort. This extends the findings of the behavioral decision-making literature to the DSS field. The extension of this work to larger problem sizes, different reward conditions, and task domains is necessary to determine the extent to which these findings can be generalized.
