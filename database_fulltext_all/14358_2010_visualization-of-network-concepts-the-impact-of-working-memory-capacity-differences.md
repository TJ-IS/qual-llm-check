## Introduction

Social networks are ubiquitous today, linking organizations, groups, and individuals around the world. Analysis of social networks has become an important tool in many organizations. For example, IBM began using social network analysis (SNA) internally in the mid-1990s to analyze knowledge flows, and one year later they credited SNA with enabling a tenfold increase in cross-functional communication and expansion of their cluster of decision makers (Stamps 1997). Many firms are using it to prepare for the potential loss of expertise and knowledge stemming from turnover (Beazley 2003). For addressing these and a growing number of other managerial challenges, SNA is fast becoming an important tool in organizations (Cross and Prusak 2002), and its practitioners are demanding effective support for this work.

Since the 1950s, researchers have been developing concepts to characterize social networks, such as network centrality (Shaw 1954, Freeman 1979), betweenness (Freeman 1977, Pitts 1979), and structural equivalence (Lorrain and White 1971). These key social network concepts have been very helpful and widely used for understanding networks (Wasserman and Faust 1999), but they are difficult to present in tabular format because in this format the structural patterns embedded in the quantitative results are not visible. Visual representations enable better comprehension of information than when it is represented using text (Jarvenpaa and Dickson 1988, Tan and Benbasat 1993). For this reason, computer-generated visualizations of SNA data have become the norm for presenting social network concepts (Brandes et al. 2001, Chen 2005). However, no theoretically based empirical research has been done to evaluate these visualizations. It is not enough to show that these data visualizations are superior to tabular formats, because tabular formats are no longer the standard way to present social network data. If we are to provide social network analysts with the most effective computer-based support possible, we need a systematic framework for understanding why one social network visualization approach serves comprehension of that data better than another. Here we use the widely accepted theory of cognitive fit to evaluate the performance implications of two different approaches to visualizing social networks. Although cognitive fit theory has traditionally been used to compare tabular with graphical computerized data presentation, we apply it here to two visualization approaches at varying levels of task complexity. We also extend cognitive fit theory by integrating it with the individual difference factor of working memory capacity (WMC). Recent research into data representation acknowledges that individual cognitive differences (i.e., WMC) play a role in information acquisition.

The contribution of this research is threefold. First, it applies cognitive fit theory to the important organizational phenomenon of SNA visualization, enabling comparison, and assessment of different graphical social network visualizations. Second, it amplifies cognitive fit theory by integrating the role of individual differences in WMC. Findings indicate that when there is high cognitive fit between the network comprehension task and the network visualization, individual differences in WMC do not affect performance, yet they do affect performance when such fit does not exist. Thus, visualizations with good cognitive fit can equalize individual differences in WMC. Third, it explicates how cognitive fit theory can be applied to the information acquisition phase of problem solving specifically, using current cognitive research. We begin by presenting background theory that is necessary for understanding the model: SNA and the importance of the betweenness centrality concept to organizations in particular, followed by a discussion of cognitive fit theory and the roles of information load and differences in individual working memory capacity within this theory. Next we present our theoretical model and the hypotheses derived from it predicting performance outcomes. We then discuss the current approaches to visualizing SNA data, present the two network visualizations approaches we used to evaluate our model, and explain why these two approaches are the most appropriate choice for model assessment. Next we present the two experiments we performed to assess our model, along with results of our data analyses and implications of these results. Consistent with cognitive fit theory, findings suggest that network visualizations can alter the cognitive processing of those using them, and so affect performance outcomes.

## Theoretical Background

## Social Network Concepts

In social networks, actors or groups of actors are represented as nodes that move dynamically amongst each other such that some are closer to others, and this creates emergent patterns that reflect underlying social dynamics (Granovetter 1973). SNA has become an important managerial tool in organizations, and intensive academic research has been conducted on the application of SNA, resulting in several research streams. For instance, the identification of subgroups in communication networks can help managers discover coalitions among employees (Brass 1984) and facilitate collaboration and knowledge sharing (Cross and Prusak 2002). The various network analysis concepts have different implications for management, but it is beyond the scope of this paper to discuss all of them. In this paper we focus on one network concept: centrality. Network centrality describes the most important actors in a network—important actors have high centrality. Two widely investigated types of network centrality are degree centrality and betweenness centrality. Degree centrality measures the popularity of an actor based on the number of actors that actor is linked to (Wasserman and Faust 1999). Betweenness centrality goes a step further, explaining how powerful a network actor is (Pitts 1979) based on how many other actors this actor is connected to, when these other actors are not connected to each other. This means that the actors linked to the focal one must communicate through the focal actor to reach one another. Removal of the focal actor leaves the other actors without a means of communicating with each other. Thus, removal of actors with high betweenness centrality disrupts knowledge and communication flows. In organizations, centrality has been used to identify key communication channels (Stamps 1997) and subject matter experts (Beazley 2003). Individuals with high centrality in their social networks tend to be highly influential in their organizations, with more access to resources, better performance, and faster promotions (Brass 1984, Ibarra and Andrews 1993, Baldwin et al. 1997). At the group level, organizational units with high centrality have greater innovative capability than groups that are less well positioned (Tsai 2001). Also, both the centrality of a group and the centrality of its leader are positively related to the performance of that group (Burt 2000, Mehra et al. 2006). Organizations that are considering staff movement need to know if doing so will considerably disrupt their knowledge and communication flows. Thus, betweenness centrality is a critical SNA concept for organizational knowledge management. For this reason, this research focuses on the SNA concept of betweenness centrality.

## Cognitive Fit Theory

According to the theory of cognitive fit (Vessey 1991), task performance is enhanced when there is a fit or match between the information that is emphasized in a data representation and the information that is necessary to solve the task at hand. When such a fit exists, the mental processes engaged in by the person performing the task, and therefore his mental representation of the problem, match the task, and this facilitates problem solution. The theory explains performance differences stemming from variations in information format and has been applied to such varied domains as conceptual modeling (Khatri et al. 2006b), expertise management (Huang et al. 2006), e-commerce (Hong et al. 2004), and software maintenance (Shaft and Vessey 2006). Cognitive fit theory has been extended to include individual differences in problem-solving skills (Vessey and Galletta 1991), and individual cognitive differences in field dependency (Mahoney et al. 2003). However, the role of differences in WMC has not been integrated into cognitive fit theory as we do here. WMC is a critical individual difference for organizations. WMC has been found to correlate with people’s general intelligence (Susz et al. 2002), their reasoning ability (Barrouillet 1996), and their ability to follow spatial orders (Engle et al. 1991). It thus is a critical individual difference for organizations. It is for this reason that we focus on WMC here as a key individual difference that should be integrated into cognitive fit theory. Also, much of the cognitive fit research has investigated decision tasks involving simple information acquisition tasks, although the paradigm is beginning to be applied to more complex acquisition tasks (Speier 2006). This study extends this investigation into cognitive fit for complex tasks, because comprehension of betweenness centrality is a complex semantic spatial task (Khatri et al. 2006a).

Cognitive researchers understand how cognitive fit works in the context of visualizations: Comprehension of visual representations occurs in working memory (Seagull and Walker 1992). Prior visualization research has identified two important cognitive processes that take place in working memory when individuals use visual displays to solve problems (Larkin and Simon 1987). Perceptual processing occurs when people make meaningful inferences from visualized patterns without undertaking conceptual processing (Bolles 1991). Conceptual processing integrates this new perceptually processed information with information held in longterm memory to accomplish the task (Kieras and Meyer 1997). Image comprehension results from the interaction of these two processes (Pinker 1991). Perceptual processes convert information represented by visual variables (i.e., color, shape) into a mental representation of the visualization (Pinker 1991). Conceptual processes then use this information to generate conceptual content. If the generated conceptual content does not provide an adequate mental representation of the solution, conceptual processing continues to generate new conceptual content (Pinker 1991), by applying mathematical and logical rules to the existing content, or by retrieving additional information from the environment or long-term memory. These additional retrieval and inferential processes require that more information be held and processed in working memory. When a task demands more information processing than the capacity of working memory supports, people tend to adopt simplification strategies to conserve cognitive resources, and these usually lead to decreased task performance (Streufert 1973). Compared with conceptual processing, perceptual processing demands less information processing, and, hence, less WMC (Pinker 1991). Thus, visualizations can make some kinds of information easy to comprehend by shifting the need for conceptual processing to perceptual processing (Card et al. 1999, Kim et al. 2000). Images that support perceptual processing demand fewer cognitive resources than images that demand more conceptual processing (Zhang 1997). Thus, data representations with high cognitive fit provide strong support for users’ perceptual processing.

Cognitive fit theory recognizes that this is a taskdependent process: A visualization that has high cognitive fit for one task may not have high cognitive fit for another task. A task that demands extensive conceptual processing using one visual representation may be accomplished primarily through perceptual processing using a different visual representation. Therefore, it is possible to align a particular visualization with a particular task such that it has high cognitive fit and so supports maximum perceptual processing, reducing cognitive load for that task. Thus, given two network comprehension tasks, the task that demands less conceptual processing supports higher cognitive fit for a particular visualization.

## Task Complexity

Task complexity can increase demand for conceptual processing by increasing the number of information cues that must be processed (Wood 1986). In the context of visual comprehension, a large number of data points leads to high information load, which increases task complexity (Myers et al. 1996). For example, to comprehend the betweenness centrality of a network actor, a user needs to investigate whether other actors connected to the actor of interest are connected to each other. This task would be much easier if the network actor of interest were connected to only 3 other actors rather than to 50 other actors. As the information load associated with an SNA task increases, the amount of information that must be stored and processed increases correspondingly, and this can lead to decreased task performance.

When a high cognitive fit visualization shifts conceptual processing to perceptual processing, it also reduces the impact of the task complexity caused by the information load. Human perceptual processing is very powerful because human eyes can process visual cues in parallel (Ware 2000). Thus, for the same increase in information load, visualizations that require conceptual processing may overload users’ working memory, whereas visualizations that only need perceptual processing for concept comprehension will not be affected.

## The Role of the Individual and Working Memory Capacity

The discussion thus far has not addressed the issue of individual differences in cognitive capacity. Cognitive capacity consists of two main components: working memory (WM) and long-term memory (Kieras and Meyer 1997). Working memory integrates incoming visual information with that loaded from longterm memory for the purpose of solving a particular problem. It then stores this information while it accomplishes the impending task. In this way, WMC encompasses both memory capacity and processing capacity. The capacity (or span) of working memory therefore significantly affects task outcomes because it determines the amount of information that can be stored and processed (Daneman and Carpenter 1980). Working memory capacity (WMC) refers to the capacity for cognitive integration—the process that individuals use to understand concepts—in this case from a visual representation. WMC serves as an upper limit to processing capacity (Miller 1956). One critical way that individuals differ cognitively is in their WMC.

Long-term memory stores information associated with a lifetime of experiences and thoughts. It is a network of linked concepts (Yufic and Sheridan 1996) and is the locus of domain knowledge. We focus on the impact of working memory as opposed to long-term memory for this research because the comprehension of network concepts from a visualization does not involve extensive domain knowledge that is stored in long-term memory. For instance, a centrally located network actor could be in a network of software developers, biochemists, or film production workers, and an analyst could identify the centrality of this actor from a visual representation without having expertise in any of these industries. He or she needs only to synthesize visual cues from the visualization and find the network actor with the highest centrality value, a process that does not require that domain knowledge be retrieved from long-term memory (although it may subsequently be applied during the decision-making process).

Working memory processes and stores verbal and visual information in different subsystems (Baddeley and Hitch 1974, Luck and Vogel 1997). The visual submemory system constructs, stores, and manipulates mental images. The verbal submemory system does mental arithmetic and logic operations. Both spatial ability and the capacity of verbal working memory can affect the effectiveness of visual interfaces (Speier and Morris 2003, Loshe 1997). Visual comprehension of network concepts thus involves the coordination of the two submemory systems. For this reason, we measure this coordination function of WM in this study.

## Theoretical Model

Figure 1 below presents the full theoretical model. It illustrates the effects of the constructs of cognitive fit, information load, and WMC on performance. When visualization designers create visualizations with high cognitive fit, their designs decrease demand for conceptual processing by increasing perceptual processing, and so reduce demand for WMC. They filter out excess demand for conceptual processing by presenting abstract concepts in concrete ways that visually reflect the nature of the underlying concepts. For each hypothesis, we define performance in terms of effectiveness (Hypothesis A) and time-totask (Hypothesis B). Thus, for identifying betweenness centrality, we use the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Users supported by a high cognitive fit visualization will have higher performance outcomes than users supported by a low cognitive fit visualization.

The impact of information load associated with a task can be moderated by how the data is presented (Kumar and Benbasat 2004, Speier and Morris 2003). Thus, information load can have more or less impact on visualization comprehension performance, depending on the visualization interface. For this reason, the information load of a task affects the cognitive fit of images differentially, such that the cognitive fit of some images is more adversely affected by high information load than others. High cognitive fit visualizations take advantage of human perceptual processing to support image comprehension (i.e., betweenness centrality here), whereas users have to use conceptual processing to accomplish the same task with a low cognitive fit visualization. Thus, for the task of identifying betweenness centrality from a visualization, see Hypothesis 2.

Hypothesis 2 (H2). For users of low cognitive fit visualization, differences in performance between those with high-load tasks and those with low-load tasks will be greater than for users of high cognitive fit visualization.

Relative to conceptual processing, perceptual processing is less likely to be overloaded by high information loads. Therefore, high-load comprehension tasks will benefit more from a high cognitive fit visualization than will low-load comprehension tasks. Thus, for the task of identifying betweenness centrality from a visualization, refer to Hypothesis 3.

Hypothesis 3 (H3). The difference in performance outcomes between high and low cognitive fit visualization users for a high-load task will be greater than the difference in performance outcomes between high and low cognitive fit visualization users for a low-load task.

Because image comprehension occurs in users working memory (Seagull and Walker 1992), individual differences in WMC should moderate performance outcomes; individuals with lower WMC using visualizations with high levels of cognitive fit should perform as well as individuals with higher WMC. Thus, WMC moderation effects will be apparent for those using lower cognitive fit visualizations, but not for those using higher cognitive fit visualizations. When one visualization has lower cognitive fit than another, we should see the effects of individual WMC differences on performance for users using the visualization with lower cognitive fit. In such cases, the visualization with high cognitive fit frees up WMC in users with low levels of it, essentially equalizing task performance across variations in WMC. Thus, for identifying betweenness centrality from a visualization, see Hypothesis 4.

Hypothesis 4 (H4). The difference in performance outcomes between high and low WMC users with low cognitive fit visualization will be larger than the difference in performance outcomes between high and low WMC users with high cognitive fit visualization.

Visualizations with high cognitive fit tend to enhance the information acquisition capacity of people with low WMC, essentially compensating for low WMC. Thus, we would expect to see a smaller differential in performance outcomes between low and high cognitive fit visualization users that have high WMC, relative to those with low WMC. Thus, for identifying betweenness centrality from a visualization, refer to Hypothesis 5.

Hypothesis 5 (H5). The difference in performance outcomes between high and low cognitive fit visualization with low working memory will be greater than the difference in performance outcomes between high and low cognitive fit visualization users with high working memory.

According to the theoretical model developed above, the outcome of comprehension of a network concept will depend on how well a particular visualization supports that task, in conjunction with the WMC of the individual performing the task. We empirically assessed this model in two experiments using two different sets of network data, described below.

## Method

In this section, we first explain the two visualization approaches we compared and the specific visualizations that we used as a result. Following this we discuss the particular SNA task we investigated and why, and describe the experimental procedure used for both experiments. We then report on results of our data analyses.

## Existing Network Visualization Approaches

The design of visualization systems consists of two stages. The first stage determines what aspects of the data to display, whereas the second stage determines how these selected data aspects are displayed. Decisions made in the first stage lead to the selection of one of two data-processing approaches: direct mapping and preanalysis (Card et al. 1999, Chi et al. 1998, Zhu and Chen 2005). The direct mapping approach in network visualization (Eades 1984, Fruchterman and Reingold 1991, Ham and Wijk 2004) presents direct relationships among network actors, supporting general network exploration. Most visualization tools provided in existing SNA packages such as Uncinet (www.analytictech.com/ucinet/ucinet.htm) apply this popular direct mapping approach. The second approach applies preanalyses to the social network data to extract network concept information before the network data is displayed. The more recent visualization systems described in Brandes et al. (2001), Chen (2005), Henry and Fekete (2006), Perer and Shneiderman (2006), and Zhu et al. (2009) use this approach.

After the first stage, during which the designer has decided which information to present and extracted it, he then decides how this information should be presented. This second stage often combines two types of techniques for information presentation: image construction and user-image interaction (Shneiderman 1996). Image construction techniques map the information extracted in the first stage into visual metaphors, whereas the user-image interaction technique enables the users themselves to modify and navigate the visual metaphors on the screen. For example, the SNA package Uncinet and the system described in Ham and Wijk (2004) directly map the network data into a “node and line” image, and also provide several user-image interaction features to allow users to closely examine or filter network actors.

In this second stage, there are also two ways to present the results of data extracted using preanalysis. Systems in Brandes et al. (2001), Chen (2005), and Zhu et al. (2009) focus on image construction, using visual cues to generate a “node and line” metaphor, such that network actors with the highest centrality values can be highlighted (Chen 2005, Brandes et al. 2001, Zhu et al. 2009). On the other hand, systems developed in Perer and Shneiderman (2006) and Henry and Fekete (2006) combine image construction with userimage interaction. Such systems provide (at least) two views of the same network. One view is the conventional direct mapping, whereas the other view is a statistic graph that displays the values of network concepts. Users can select and filter network actors in one view based on their values in this other view, but they usually need to combine the information from both views to comprehend the network concepts the system seeks to deliver.

According to cognitive fit theory, when the visualization of information emphasizes the same information as that needed to solve the task, there will be a fit between the task and the visualization. Therefore, visualizations that apply preanalysis to emphasize the delivery of particular network concepts should support the comprehension of these network concepts more effectively and efficiently than a visualization using the direct mapping approach, because this only supports general network exploration tasks. The paper compares these two dataprocessing approaches; direct mapping versus using preanalysis in the first stage of visualization development. To make a fair comparison between these two approaches, this study uses a single-image visualization from each of these approaches with minimum user-image interaction features. We constructed two visualizations, described below, each of which is typical of these two respective approaches.

## The Visualizations Used to Empirically Evaluate Our Model

The first image, which we call the force (Figure 2), uses the direct mapping approach. It explicitly indicates the connectedness of network actors. The second visualization, which we call the circle (Figure 3), uses the second approach, applying “preanalysis” before the visualization such that specific network concepts can be directly comprehended. Both the force and the circle visualizations have identical user-image interaction capabilities. On both the force and circle, as displayed in Figures 2 and 3, a user can click on a network actor to have all its direct connections highlighted.

In both the force and the circle, the size of an actor is proportional to the number of links this actor has. The bigger the size, the more links the actor has. One important difference between the two visualizations is the way that betweenness centrality is represented. On the circle, the betweenness centrality of actors is calculated by the underlying algorithm and is indicated by its location: Actors closer to the center have higher betweenness centrality than those farther away from the center. Removing an actor that is close to the center will disconnect more actors in the network than removing an actor that is farther away from the center. Betweenness centrality is less readily discernable on the force because it must be visually inferred by counting actors and their links.

We performed two experiments comparing the circle and the force, each based on a different SNA data domain. As discussed above in the context of long-term memory and domain knowledge, the comprehension of a network concept from a network visualization is independent of the particular domain of the social network visualized. We used two different data sets (i.e., one of criminal gangs, the other of employee e-mail communication) to emphasize that this research focuses only on the information acquisition phase of decision making and hence findings are generalizable across task domains.

Decision makers acquire related information before they make optimal decisions (Carroll and Olson 1987). An important role that data visualizations play in decision making is to facilitate this information acquisition process. In this study we investigate the visual comprehension of network concepts from network visualizations, leaving to future research the extension of this to domain-based decision making. However, if one were to attempt to generalize these results to a specific domain, that of technology-supported training of SNA analysts would be an appropriate one. Experimental participants were not familiar with SNA at the beginning of these experiments, but most were able to successfully comprehend betweenness centrality from the visualizations designed to support this. Thus, these users learned to do SNA concept comprehension online during the course of the experiment in a manner consistent with the e-learning literature (Santhanam et al. 2008).

## Mapping the Visualizations to Comprehension of Betweenness Centrality

We elected to investigate the comprehension of betweenness centrality (i.e., which actor would disrupt the most communications if removed relative to other network actors) in this study for two reasons. First, as discussed above, the comprehension of this concept from a network visualization is an extremely important SNA concept for organizations to understand expertise and knowledge flows among their members. The other reason we selected comprehension of betweenness centrality as the focal task for this research is that betweeness centrality is an extremely complex concept to comprehend: One needs to know not just the connectedness of each of the focal actors under consideration, but the interconnectedness of all the actors to which each of these focal actors are linked. Both of the visualizations investigated here display how the actors connect to each other, but they differ in how betweenness centrality is represented; the circle has high cognitive fit for this task, whereas the force does not. Differences in cognitive fit between the force and the circle are highest for betweenness by design.

There are two reasons that it is desirable for organizations to use visualizations rather than tables of numbers to identify network actors with high betweenness centrality. First, visualizations enable dynamic what-if scenario analyses, which are important for identifying betweenness centrality because the removal of a focal actor immediately affects the betweenness centrality of other network actors. This makes comparison of the betweenness centrality of two actors a very dynamic task that is not well supported by static tables of numbers. The second reason that organizations tend to prefer visualizations for identifying actors with high betweenness centrality is that multiple comparisons of actors can be accomplished much more quickly with a visualization than with tables that require comparing many numbers.

## Experimental Procedure

We conducted two separate experiments using two different data sets of actual social networks. The first uses the social network data of criminal gangs provided to us by a city police department. The second uses e-mail communications among Enron employees. The experiments were conducted 18 months apart, using the same experimental procedure. Participants were recruited from a subject pool at a large East Coast university, where research participation is a school requirement. Participants were told that the highest performer would win \$100. The experiment consisted of two parts, and was conducted as follows: In the first part, we measured working memory and demographics. In the second part, participants interpreted information presented in the visualizations described above. Both parts of the experiment were conducted in the same computer lab with 30 seats. Because of this room-size limitation, we conducted the experiment in multiple sessions. Each was administrated by the same experimenters, following a standard protocol. An online survey was developed specifically for the experiment.

The first part of the experiment was conducted as follows: Participants were told that all instructions were online and that they should read these instructions carefully. They then entered their demographic information online, followed by the operation span task developed by Turner and Engle (1989) to measure WMC. This validated WMC measure has been used to investigate individual differences in following directions (Engle et al. 1991) and judgment accuracy (Sprenger and Doughert 2006), among others. Our experiment adopted an online version of the operation span developed in Unsworth et al. (2005). This task presents a mathematical equation with an answer simultaneously with an alphabetic letter, asking participants to both verify the accuracy of the math answer and remember the letter. It then advances to a second equation and letter pair, then a third, and so on, in varying length sets. At the end of each set, participants are asked to recall the letters in the set in the order in which they were presented, and their answers to this is what their WMC score is based on. See Turner and Engle (1989) for a detailed description of this measure. When performing a complex task consisting of multiple subtasks, people tend to temporarily store information related to one subtask component in their working memory while they process information about other subtasks. The memory task we used measures the capacity of the working memory system to store and retrieve task-related information. The calculations demanded by the mathematics served to absorb working memory processing capacity, at the same time that participants were asked to remember a letter in their WM. In this way the task measures both the storage and processing capabilities of working memory. The mathematics questions served as a distraction from the storage task, so the mathematical accuracy was ultimately ignored even though the participants were told that it was important, preventing participants from focusing on the letters without paying attention to the mathematical calculations. We monitored the math accuracy and dropped the results of participants whose math accuracy was lower than 85%. Following this, participants did a paper-based test to measure their spatial and visualization ability using an instrument developed by Ekstrom et al. (1976). We included this measure as a control variable.

During the two weeks between the two parts of the experiment, we calculated the working memory scores of all participants and grouped them into highor low-WMC categories, divided by the median score. For the second part of the experiment, we randomly split the high-WMC participants into two groups, such that each would work on the same network comprehension tasks, but with different visualization support: the force or the circle. We also did this randomized visualization assignment for low-WMC participants. We did this to ensure that a similar number of high- and low-WMC participants were assigned to each of the two visualization types. Because other individual differences may also affect participants experience (e.g., personality, cognitive style, spatial ability, and personal computing experience), this randomization of treatment assignments served to control for these possible confounding effects.

The process of the second part of the experiment was as follows: The visualizations were made available on the desktops in the laboratory. Participants read the contextual information and instructions on how to use the visualization, and did a training task that was similar to the actual experimental task. Answers and explanations were provided for the training task. Participants then read the task questions online and entered their answers into an online survey form. The backend database of this online survey recorded both the answers entered and the time spent on each task. After answering each question, participants rated on a 7-point Likert scale how confident they felt about the answer they found.

Participants assessed betweenness centrality by identifying which of two actors should be removed to disconnect more people in that network. All participants conducted one low information load task and one high information task using their assigned visualization interface. This task order was randomly assigned. Three categories of independent variables were measured or manipulated: the visual representation type, WMC, and information load. Information load was manipulated by varying the number of network actors connected to the focal actor, rather than by increasing the number of pairwise comparisons. We did this because it would have been an extremely difficult task (virtually impossible) for force users to compare more than two actors. For the low-load task, participants were asked to assess the betweenness centrality of two actors, where each actor was connected to three other network actors. In the high-load scenario, each of the two focal actors was connected to 25 other network actors. Perceived confidence was measured as a manipulation check. The two dependent variables measured were both objective performance outcomes: first, whether the solution was correct, and second, the number of seconds it took to complete the task. For each hypothesis listed above, we report two sets of results: first, for the effectiveness score (Hypothesis A), and second, for the time-to-task score (Hypothesis B).

## Analysis and Results

To increase the generality of the study, the two experiments used network data sets from different domains. The first experiment used criminal analysis as the context of study. Criminal analysts depend heavily on network analysis, because most criminals do not operate in isolation (McAndrew 1999). Understanding the features of these criminal networks is crucial for discovering the crime-related information embedded in them (Harper and Harris 1975). This experiment used criminal network data constructed from 272 police department incident summaries (Chen et al. 2004), involving 164 crimes committed from 1985 through 2002, provided to us by a metropolitan police department in the U.S. Southwest.

Seventy-seven female and 61 male undergraduate students participated in the first experiment, with an average age of 19.5 and 3.09 years of work experience. A T -test indicated that the random assignment of participants to different interface types was successful. There were no significant differences in WMC (t <sub>=</sub> <sub>−</sub> 0035 $p { = } 0 . 9 7 2 )$ , spatial capability $\left( t = - 0 . 5 7 8 , p = 0 . 5 6 4 \right)$ , or visualization capability (t 1237 p 0219) between force and circle users.

The second experiment used e-mail archive data from Enron, made public by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission during its investigations into the Enron case. This data set contained 200,339 messages belonging to 158 users. Most of the users are senior managers in Enron, Participants in this second experiment were 49 female and 52 male undergraduate students, with an average age of 20.57 and 3.14 years of work experience. Again, a T -test indicated that the random assignment of participants to different interface types was successful. There were no significant differences in WMC $( t = - 0 . 8 2 0 , p = 0 . 4 1 4 )$ , spatial capability $( t = - 1 . 5 3 5 , p = 0 . 1 2 8 )$ , or visualization capability $( t = - 0 . 3 1 3 , p = 0 . 7 5 5 )$ between force and circle users. We used ANOVA and t-tests to analyze the data throughout. A number of recent authors have used ANOVA to effectively investigate visualization phenomena (Hong et al. 2004, Kumar and Benbasat 2004, Speier and Morris 2003). For all hypothesis tests we report the actual sample means to show the direction of results.

## Hypotheses Testing

Tables 1(a) and 1(b) present the results of ANOVA and T -test analyses for both experiments, indicating that the experiment results are consistent across the two domains. In both experiments, there is a significant main effect of interface type on both effectiveness and time-to-task, supporting H1A and H1B. The interaction of interface type and information load is significantly associated with effectiveness, supporting H2A and H3A. The interaction between interface type and WMC is significantly associated with time-to-task, supporting H4B and H5B. Users supported by the circle perform these tasks faster and more effectively. Results indicate that users supported by the circle have better performance outcomes than users supported by the force, regardless of information load. ## Discussion and Implications

This study investigates the broad phenomenon of network visualization evaluation. Findings support the basic premise that for identifying betweenness centrality, some visual representations are more effective than others: Results indicate that the circle is superior to the force for this task. Because the circle was designed to have high cognitive fit whereas the force was not, we expected circle users to have better performance than force users on this task, regardless of information load. We found this to be the case for both effectiveness and time-to-task (H1) across both experiments, with the exception of effectiveness under low load, which was marginally significant $( p < 0 . 1 0 )$ in both experiments. One possible explanation for this is that the identification of betweenness centrality under low load was a relatively easy task for both force and circle users. Mean perceived confidence ratings for the low-load task was above five for all participants in both experiments, supporting this explanation. It seems that most force users were able to arrive at the correct answer, although they took longer to do so than circle users did.

H3 investigates this, positing that the difference in performance outcomes between circle and force users for a high-load task will be greater than the difference in performance outcomes between circle and force users for a low-load task. Because the circle was designed to have high cognitive fit and the force was not, the associated difference in outcomes will be most apparent under high-load conditions. This hypothesis was supported for effectiveness outcomes across both experiments. In both cases, the effectiveness of the circle over the force was an order of magnitude greater under low load than under high load. However, for time-to-task outcomes, it was supported only for high WMC users. Once again, we believe that goal-setting theory (Locke 1968, 1990) can provide some insight here, as follows: We believe that low WMC force users found the high-load task very difficult, and so gave up on it quickly, as evidenced by the fact that low WMC force users took much less time on the high-load task than on the low-load task. Force users’ effectiveness on the high-load task was also much lower than their effectiveness on the low-load task. For those low WMC participants using the circle, the visualization appears to have made the high-load task less complex. Thus, where users felt that a correct answer was within their grasp, they were less inclined to guess and took the time they needed to perform the task effectively. In retrospect, we should have designed the experiment to measure for this, and we did not. However, we are convinced of the efficacy of this explanation, and have no alternative ones to suggest.

## Effects of WMC

Hypotheses 4 and 5 investigate the effects of WMC on the main effect of H1. For circle users, we anticipated no WMC moderation effects because the strong perceptual support it provides enables low WMC participants to perform as well as high WMC ones. On the other hand, we expected that, of the force users, those with high WMC would outperform those with low WMC (H4). For time-to-task outcomes, there were significant differences between high and low WMC force users, but this difference was not found between high and low WMC circle users. The force visualization does not offset the negative effects of low WMC by moving information acquisition from conceptual to perceptual processing, as the circle was designed to do. For effectiveness outcomes, results were not significant, but were in the hypothesized direction. Force users with high WMC were more effective than those with low WMC, whereas circle users with high WMC were slightly less effective than circle users with low WMC. WMC appears to moderate performance outcomes for visualizations with low cognitive fit as our model suggests.

Hypothesis 5 predicts no WMC moderation effects for circle users because its high cognitive fit enables low WMC participants to perform as well as high WMC ones. We expected that the differences in performance outcomes between circle and force users with high WMC would be smaller than the differences in performance outcomes between circle and force users with low WMC. This reflects the fact that the benefits of high cognitive fit visualizations such as the circle accrue to those with low WMC. By moving conceptual processing to perceptual processing, high cognitive fit visualizations release cognitive resources in low WMC users that would otherwise have been used in service of the necessary conceptual processing. H5A is supported only under low load. One possible explanation for this is that the high-load task is too complex even for high WMC force users, therefore we see significant differences in effectiveness between circle and force regardless of their WMC. Results for time-to-task from the two experiments are inconsistent. H5B is supported in the first experiment, because WMC had no moderating effect on efficiency for circle users, but it did for force users. In the second experiment, it seems that even the simple task seemed to be too complex to the low WMC users, possibly triggering the “giving up” effect again.

Overall then, results support the proposed model, which highlights the important role of information load and individual differences in WMC in cognitive fit. Cognitive fit minimizes demand for working memory, but because people vary in their WMC, cognitive fit has a greater impact on those with lower WMC than on those with higher WMC. This occurs because high cognitive fit visualizations reduce demand for conceptual processing by moving this processing into the perceptual processing realm. In this way, visualizations that are designed to have high cognitive fit reduce the associated cognitive load, making these cognitive resources available for information acquisition. Note that this effect considers fit between visualization and a task as a continuum and not a threshold. It is not the case that the cognitive fit becomes “WMC-releasing” at some threshold level.

## Conclusions

Within the domain of SNA concept comprehension, this study extends cognitive fit theory to explain how visualizations with high cognitive fit compensate for individual differences in WMC. Such differences are inevitable in organizations, and are often responsible for performance differences among employees, but they can be managed more or less effectively. The lesson for SNA visualization designers is obvious: Assess the extent to which low WMC users can perform the focal task using a particular visualization and, if necessary, modify the visualization to a lower level of complexity. Our findings suggest that higher cognitive fit network visualizations can equalize performance differences—in the task of comprehending betweenness centrality—caused by individual differences in WMC. We suggest that this equalization effect should be a criterion in the evaluation of design effectiveness. Just because a visualization has high cognitive fit for high WMC users does not mean that it will have high cognitive fit for low WMC users.

Consistent with cognitive fit theory, we found that our visualization with high cognitive fit was able to compensate for the negative effects of high task complexity. By shifting conceptual processing to perceptual processing, high cognitive fit visualizations can equalize the effects of task complexity caused by information load. Findings also suggest that there may be task-visualization combinations that are simply too complex to be solved by those with low WMC. If visualization support cannot be improved for such tasks, additional support for the task should be marshaled, or higher WMC users should be assigned to it. One challenge of this complexity-based approach is that high and low WMC users are likely to have different criteria for what constitutes a complex task. Therefore, one avenue for research to clarify this model is to investigate the operationalization of complexity in the context of variations in cognitive fit and working memory.

Results confirm that SNA visualizations with high cognitive fit can enhance acquisition of network concept information relative to those with low cognitive fit. This replicates prior cognitive fit research in this new domain: Network visualizations that are higher in cognitive fit for a particular task provide better task support than those with low cognitive fit for that task. However, the contribution of this work is not simply replication of cognitive fit theory in the domain of SNA visualization. Significantly, this research incorporates and empirically assesses the interacting effects of task complexity and individual differences in WMC into the cognitive fit model. Findings suggest that it is not enough to simply take into account the fit of the visualization to the task. Rather, by taking individual differences in WMC into account during design, we can understand the limits of cognitive fit for supporting the problem-solving needs of those with low WMC.

Results are limited in their generalizability to the problem of comprehending betweenness centrality. Also, we have focused here on only one aspect of the visualization system, image construction, leaving investigation of user-image interaction to future research. Another potential limitation of the work is the use of student participants. However, this is not a major concern for investigations of visualization phenomena because they apply to generalized human cognition (Besuijen and Spenkelink 1998). Undergraduates have been widely used in other IS research on visual interfaces (Hong et al. 2004, Kumar and Benbasat 2004, Speier and Morris 2003). We worked to control for differences in domain knowledge that might otherwise have invoked the effects of long-term memory differences. By conducting two experiments using social network data from two different domains and obtaining consistent results, we have illustrated that our model is domain independent, and therefore should generalize to other SNA comprehension tasks that involve comprehension of betweenness centrality. Also, the fact that these two experiments were conducted 18 months apart further supports the generalizability of our findings to other user groups.

For managers, these results have implications for decisions about adoption of computer-based visualization systems. For example, tasks performed by those in units with wide variations in WMC levels are strong candidates for this type of visualization support, because it can increase the performance of those with low WMC. From the perspective of the visualization designer, results suggest that there is not a one-size-fits-all network visualization, even if the visualization fits the task well. This is particularly important when we consider the important role that designers play in network visualization: Typically, networks reflect very large quantities of data that— when mapped directly in hub-and-spoke fashion— crowd the visual field and make concept identification very difficult. Therefore, designers often preprocess the data to reflect the various network concepts in it. This entails deciding which aspects of the data will be visualized. Also, to know what to visualize, designers need to know which network concepts are important for performing the network analysis task at hand. Hence, designers need to take cognitive fit into account when they do the preprocessing necessary to visually represent large databases. This preprocessing should use visual cues that generate visualizations with high cognitive fit for those with the lowest WMC, and that are robust with respect to information load. The framework developed here offers an approach for evaluating various visualizations in context, based on theory that enables comparison to determine which design elements are most suitable for specific network comprehension tasks. It provides a cohesive rationale for addressing the following types of questions: How well does a particular network concept support the task that users are trying to accomplish? How efficiently do users perceive the network concepts selected for presentation? To what extent does the visualization decrease the impact of individual differences in WMC?

Findings from this study support our theoretically derived model to explain how individual-level WMC interacts with the cognitive fit of the visualization, such that performance outcomes are affected by all three factors of WMC, task, and visualization design. The model suggests a fruitful and theoretically robust path for future visualization evaluation research. Results are limited by the focus on a single network analysis task and the use of undergraduate participants. Despite this, we believe that these results are of widespread interest to researchers and practitioners. The model provides a means for researchers and practitioners to evaluate the impact of visualization options on SNA tasks, and a framework for new design insights to emerge from such evaluations. Future research may include extending what we have found in this study to the presentation of other network concepts, investigating design factors that equalize the impact of high task load, and applying the model to other visualizations in domains such as content visualization, software visualization, and social visualization. The model is theoretically robust, and parsimonious enough to inspire other researchers to develop and refine it. We hope it motivates a stream of visual evaluation models that shed light on visualization design for a broad set of information tasks. As large complex data sets become increasingly ubiquitous, we need guidelines for understanding and assessing why some visualizations provide better support than others. This research takes up this thorny problem without oversimplifying it.
