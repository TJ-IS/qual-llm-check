# Revisiting the role of web assurance seals in business-to-consumer electronic commerce

Dan J. Kim <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Charles Steinfield <sup>b</sup>, Ying-Ju Lai <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Computer Information Systems, University of Houston Clear Lake, United States

<sup>b</sup> Department of Telecommunication, Information Studies and Media, Michigan State University, United States

Received 18 October 2005; received in revised form 20 November 2007; accepted 29 November 2007 Available online 8 December 2007

## Abstract

There is conflicting evidence as to the current level of awareness and impact of Web Assurance Seal Services (WASSs). This study examines the effects of an educational intervention designed to increase consumer's knowledge, of security and privacy aspect of business-to-consumer (B2C) e-commerce websites and assurance seal services. The study further explores the relationships among consumers' perceptions about online security, including WASSs awareness, importance of WASS, privacy concerns, security concerns, and information quality, before and after the educational intervention. The study finds that educating consumers about the security and privacy dangers of the web, as well as the role of web assurance seals, does increase their awareness and perceived importance of the seals. However, despite this increased awareness, there is little association between these assurance seals and the two indicators of trustworthiness, concerns about privacy and perceived information quality, of an ecommerce site, even after the intervention. Only security concerns have a statistically significant relationship with WASSs awareness before and after the educational intervention. Implications for theory and practice are discussed. © 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Web assurance seal service; Awareness of assurance seal service; Online privacy; Online security; Information quality

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade, a number of studies have found consistently high levels of concern regarding online privacy and security [1,2,11,14,26,35,45,62,65]. In the U.S., the National Fraud Information Center of the National Consumers League (NCL) showed that consumers reported losses of \$895 per victim in Internet fraud in 2004, up from \$527 in 2003 [46]. Moreover, despite growing concerns about the protection of consumer privacy in online commerce, there have been several highly publicized incidents where online vendors have sought to sell customer's private information despite having pledged against doing so in their privacy policies. Two very good examples are the cases of bankrupt Internet retailer (eretailer) Toysmart [16] and Toys ‘Я’ Us [47].

Without protection mechanisms and/or practical regulations for online privacy and security, electronic commerce (e-commerce) will be impeded from developing into a true electronic marketplace of the future; where both consumers and businesses participate in exchanges and transactions as spontaneously as in the traditional marketplace. Thus, as one of the efforts to alleviate consumer concerns, a variety of assurance seal services on websites (e.g., WebTrust, Verisign, TRUSTe, BBBOnLine) have entered the online commerce arena to help consumers judge the trustworthiness of an online vendor. These firms offer a form of institution-based trust which is formed through societal institutions such as certification by account or governmental regulations [41,54,70].

There is conflicting evidence as to the current level of awareness and impact of assurance seal services. Some studies [21,32,69] find that the presence of an assurance seal results in an increased willingness to purchase by consumers, while others [11,27,29] show that the effect is limited due to the consumer's lack of awareness and understanding of assurance services. The lack of understanding and awareness of assurance services suggest that there may be a need for greater effort to educate consumers about the role of assurance seals on the web.

Thus, the purpose of this study is to examine consumer's awareness of assurance services and the extent to which they perceive assurance seals to be important in business-to-consumer (B2C) e-commerce. We explore whether an intervention to educate consumers about the security and privacy dangers of the web, as well as the role of assurance services, influences their perceptions about the relative security and privacy of e-commerce sites they visit.

Research question: Does an intervention to educate consumers about website security features including privacy, security practices, information quality, and Web Assurance Seal Services affect consumer's perception of the website security features?

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present some background literature regarding assurance services in e-commerce and the effect of educational interventions. Section 3 proposes a research model with a discussion of the research constructs in the model with hypotheses. Section 4 discusses research methodology and data collection. The results of the data analysis of study follow in the Section 5. Section 6 concludes the paper with a discussion of the findings, as well as the limitations and implications of this study.

## 2. Web Assurance Seal Services (WASSs) and educational interventions: literature review

Given the uncertainty of the e-commerce environment, along with escrow services, third-party certification services such as WASSs have been considered a major feature of institutional trust [70]. The institutional view of trust has been widely studied by e-commerce researchers [27,39,53,55,63]. In this study, assurance seal services refer to WASSs for online retailer's (eretailer) web site offered by third-party certifying bodies (e.g. banks, accountants, consumer unions, and computer companies) which are designed to assure that the eretailer's behavior will be consistent with accepted standards in online commerce.

Recently, as an institutional trust building mechanism, a wide variety of third-party seal services were introduced to help engender trust in electronic commerce. Fig. 1 shows some of the assurance seals. The certifying bodies (i.e., the third-party seal program operators) design and advocate a set of standards that help online consumers to alleviate their fears related to e-commerce transactions with e-retailers. E-retailers who are interested in participating in the seal programs can apply and follow the standards promoted by the certifying bodies. After verification, the e-retailers place the seal on their websites, indicating that the site conforms to the certifying body's standards [10]. Under the assurance service programs, the site is periodically examined by the assurance service providers. The premise behind displaying such third-party seals is that when Internet customers see the seal on a given site, they should perceive less privacy and security risks associated with online transactions with that e-retailer. Essentially, WASSs provide assurance to consumers that a website discloses and follows its operating practices, handles payments in a secure and reliable way, has certain return policies, and/or complies with a privacy policy, that states what it can and cannot do with the collected personal data [8,31,60].

An example of an assurance seal service provider is WebTrust, which was launched in 1997 by AICPA (American Institute of Certified Public Accountants). WebTrust provides an assurance seal service related to a broad set of principles: online privacy, security, business practice integrity, transaction integrity, and availability. The WebTrust seal on websites informs consumers that the online venders have openly agreed to disclose their information gathering and dissemination practices. Their disclosure is backed by credible third-party assurance [5]. Kim, Sivasliam and Rao [27,61] categorize assurance seal services into three types: security (e.g., Verisign, WebTrust), privacy (e.g., WebTrust, TRUSTe, BBBOnline Privacy), and business integrity (e.g., BBBOnline Reliability, BizRate.com certified). They find that despite the importance of assurance services, many companies seem lukewarm about the need to use third-party seals. Kimery and McCord [29] also categorize all assurance seals as three types based on the underlying content of the seals: privacy assurance, process assurance, and technology assurance.

A number of researchers [21,32,36,48] argue that the presence of assurance seals has a significant impact on the consumer's trust and intention to purchase from a website justifying the resources that companies invest in meeting assurance standard requirements. Recently, Hu, Lin, and Zhang [23] empirically tested the effect of popular trustpromoting seals, including VeriSign Secure Site, BizRate, BBBOnLine Reliability Program, TRUST.e, and AOL Certified Merchant Guarantee. They find that all except BizRate are generally effective at increasing consumer's intention to purchase, thereby promoting web sales. Using experiments conducted on undergraduate students, Zhang [69] empirically examined the effect of trust-promoting seals on consumer's online shopping decisions. He categorizes the various seals into two types (informationrelated and reliability-related trust-promoting seals), and the products into two types (commodity products and look-and-feel products). The results show that trustpromoting seals generally increase purchases, though some are more effective than others. In this view, the presence of assurance seals acts as a visual indicator to consumers that the website is trustworthy.

However, several other studies [11,29,42] have argued that the third-party assurance seal has no effect of any significance on how a consumer views the trustworthiness of an online merchant. Kimery and McCord's study [29] found no relationship between the viewing of assurance seals and consumer trust of a specific e-retailer. In their study, among the respondents who reported seeing an assurance seal on the website, only 7.5% were interested or knowledgeable enough to click on the seal to reveal additional information about the seal and the merchant. McKnight and his colleagues [42] tested the effects of privacy assurance and industry endorsement seals, on level of trust and found that the assurance icons have little impact on perception of trust in the e-retailer. They argue that part of the reason may be that many customers who saw the seal did not know what it meant. This reason is consistent with Mauldin and Arunachalam's finding that people were not really recognizing the third-party assurance seals [36]. Thus, consumer's lack of understanding or awareness of assurance services implies a need to further educate consumers. If consumers are not aware about security and privacy threats, as well as the function of WASS efforts to protect online consumers, WASS may not achieve their intended goals [13].

In this study, we rely on an educational intervention, in which we mean, an act of involvement that is designed to affect perceptions among our subject population. There is an immense literature on learning theories [15,37,38,57,68], which form the theoretical foundation for the educational intervention used in this study. In general, there are three broad perspectives in learning theories: behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism [15]. From the behaviorism perspective, learning is achieved by changing an observable behavior. The primary concern of the behaviorists is how the association between the stimulus (S) and response (R) is made, strengthened or maintained. As basic instructional principles, positive reinforcement (e.g., rewards, feedback) is used to develop good behavior, while negative reinforcement (e.g., punishment) can be used to reduce bad behavior. By contrast, from the cognitivism perspective “Learning is an internal purposive process concerned with thinking, perception, organization and insight” (p25) [38]. The cognitivists believe that learning is a mental activity that needs internal coding and structuring by the learner and they are concerned with what learners know and how they come to acquire it. Reasoning, problemsolving, gathering, processing information, and drawing on past experiences are considered as learning methods [68]. Social psychologists [12,43] have made important contributions to the development of social learning theory which stems from social cognitive theory. Social learning theorists assert that in the human learning process, there is a cognition process (O) as a mediator between stimulus and response, placing individual control over behavioral responses to stimuli [12]. In constructivism,<sup>1</sup> learning is creating meaning from individual experiences and interactions with environmental factors. From the constructivist view point, the aim of education and development is to assist learners to achieve selfactualization, which leads to individual fulfillment and personal growth [15,37].

Focusing on counseling interventions, Gerber [19] developed two classes of learning models: one-stage and two-stage learning models. In a single-stage learning model, behaviorists assume that stimulus causes response (i.e., S→ R). It is a single-stage dynamic because the relationship between S and R is one step. In the two-stage model, the possibility of a particular response is enhanced or diminished by the cognition process (i.e., S → O → R). It is a two-stage process; the first stage is occurrence of a stimulus and its perceptual impact on the person (S→ O) and the second stage is the decision and action process (O→ R) based on his or her perceptions [19].

Drawing from these two classes of learning models and several theory components related to counselor education curricula, such as developmental theories, personality theories, learning theories, counseling theories, and assessment procedures, Gerber [18] also identified four families of learning models: association, reinforcement, cognition–perceptual, and cognition– rational/linguistic. Each family model has different view of learning and/or teaching. Association theories, such as those of Pavlov and of Guthrie [59], view teaching as a process of arranging stimulus and response events to occur contiguously to one another [18]. Reinforcement theories view learning as a process of the contiguous occurrence of three related or contingent events (i.e., the stimulus, the response, and the reinforcer) and teaching as a process of managing the contiguous relationship of the three contingent events [18]. Cognition–perceptual theorists, such as Wertheimer [66] and Tolman [64] describe learning as a process of varying perceptual frames until one is accomplished that corresponds to a universal structure. They consider teaching to be a process of managing experiences, real or vicarious [18]. Cognition–rational/linguistic learning theorists such as Festinger [17] and Rokeach [58] posit that “Teaching is a process of providing a) standardized labels and/or descriptions for experiences and referents; b) organizational structures of abstraction to enhance memory retrieval, reasoning, and classification of new experiences; and c) supplanting of inefficient or self-defeating structures with new ones” ([18] p.47). The latter two families of learning theories are two-stage models, while the former two families are one-stage models [18].

In this study, we use an educational intervention as a teaching technique focusing on the two stages found in cognition–perceptual and cognition/linguistic perspectives, since consumer education about website security features involves mental processes enhancing understanding as well as knowledge of security through experiences.

## 3. Research model: the effects of an educationa intervention to increase consumer knowledge of security and assurance seal services

Our research model investigates whether an intervention to educate consumers changes their perceptions of an online vendor's security and privacy practices, as well as increases the extent to which they are aware of assurance seal services and perceive them to be important. We select consumer's privacy concern, security concern and perception of the information quality of the website as research variables related to WASSs because they directly influence B2C consumer's online transaction decisions [4,9,28,42]. Fig. 2 shows the research model with hypotheses related to the model.

To provide insight on the conflicting findings about WASSs in B2C e-commerce, we test the effect of an educational intervention on consumer's awareness and the extent of importance of WASSs. Since it has been reported that many consumers do not recognize and understand the meaning of WASSs on a website [29,36], we argue that by educating the consumer about website security features this will result in their enhanced awareness, as well as the importance of WASSs. Thus, we can hypothesize that:

H1. After the educational intervention, consumer's awareness of the Web Assurance Seal Services (WASSs) on the website will increase.

H2. After the educational intervention, consumer's perceptions of the importance of the WASSs on the website will increase.

An educational intervention about security features may also increase consumer's privacy concerns. A consumer's privacy concern refers in this study to a consumers' perception of the e-retailer's likelihood or intention to release consumer's personal information collected during electronic transaction for unauthorized use. More and more online users are aware of the fact that most of the websites they have visited may collect their personal information, both directly and indirectly, and use it for marketing purposes or monitoring their online behaviors in a way that may be regarded as intrusive [65]. For many online consumers, loss of privacy is an important concern.

In a recent survey, 92% of survey respondents indicated that they do not trust companies to keep their information private, even when the companies promise to do so [34]. These increasing consumer concerns are forcing sellers to take privacy protection measures to increase their trustworthiness in order to encourage more online transactions. By providing consumers with information about how their personal data are used as well as offering consumers privacy preferences, websites are able to build trust and consumers will be more willing to purchase online. Since the loss of privacy is a major issue for online consumers, we expected that an educational intervention about e-commerce privacy threats (e.g., identity-theft and hacking of personal information) might increase consumer's privacy concerns. Thus, we posit that:

H3. After the educational intervention, consumer's privacy concerns about the website will be higher.

In addition to the privacy issue, security concerns are another set of reasons that consumers give for not shopping online. In this study, security concern refers to a consumers' worry that the e-retailer will not fulfill security requirements, such as authentication, integrity, encryption, and non-repudiation. Since, e-commerce transaction information packets must flow over the public Internet and a number of eavesdropping programs or hacking tools are available to monitor information traveling over the Internet, consumers believe the greatest security threats occur at the level of Internet communications. Furthermore, when consumers make an e-commerce transaction, they take potential risks such as credit/ debit card fraud, non-delivery of products, and sniffing of transaction information. For instance, sniffers<sup>2</sup> enable hackers to steal proprietary information from online transactions. Thus, the perceived risk of unauthorized third-party access to consumer's personal and financial information suggests that the users' distrust in online security systems may decrease purchase behaviors.

To achieve a more secure transaction environment, new technologies have been developed. However, these technologies by themselves do not solve the problem. Reducing risks in e-commerce is a complex process that involves technological solutions, practical policies, procedures, laws, and industry standards [33]. For practical perspective, Miyazaki and Fernandez [44] discussed three potential online practices designed to reduce consumer's security concerns: secure transactions, online credit card security guarantees, and alternative payment options. Providing consumers with these options may increase consumer's willingness to trust an online vendor and make online transactions.

A set of available tools were discussed during the educational intervention to achieve site security, the variety of e-commerce security threats, and the vulnerability of technologies in e-commerce transactions. Since this intervention may have enhanced consumer's awareness of threats, and promoted beliefs that any security system can be broken if enough resources are put against it, we expect that consumer's security concerns may increase after the intervention. Thus, it is hypothesized that:

H4. After the educational intervention, consumer's security concerns about the website will be higher.

The educational intervention affects consumers' perceptions of the website information quality. Information quality refers to a consumer's perception of the accuracy and completeness of website information as it relates to products and transactions. It is well recognized that information on the Internet varies a great deal in quality, ranging from highly accurate and reliable, to inaccurate and unreliable, and to intentionally misleading. Pack [52] pointed out that it is often very difficult to tell how frequently the information on websites is updated and whether the facts have been checked or not. Thus, online consumers are likely to be particularly attentive to the quality of information on a website because it should help them make good purchasing decisions. Although the intervention focuses mainly on website security, privacy, and assurance seal services, it might also induce online consumers to be more critical of the quality of the information that they find at an online retailer's website. Therefore, the fifth hypothesis is that:

H5. Consumer's perceptions of the information quality on the website will increase after the educational intervention.

In addition to investigating whether perceptions of the security and privacy aspects of the site, and the role of WASSs changed after the intervention, we also look for changes in the relationships among the various constructs that may have occurred as a result of the study treatment. Fig. 3 shows the additional research model.

One important relationship in the model is the link between WASSs awareness and importance of WASSs. Consumers who devote more attention to WASSs, reading the seal information and/or clicking the seal to follow the hyperlink, will be more influenced by

WASSs [29]. Thus, we expect that the more consumers are aware of Web Assurance Seal Services, the more they will perceive these services to be important both before and after the educational intervention. Drawing from the discussion above we posit that:

H6. Consumer's awareness of the WASSs will affect their perception of the importance of the WASSs on the website both before and after the educational intervention.

We would also expect that consumers who are more concerned about their privacy and security would try to find a way (e.g., third-party assurance seals) to reduce risk. Therefore, we predict that the more consumers perceive privacy and security concerns with a site, the more likely they are to be aware of WASSs as a mechanism for reducing their concerns, both before and after the educational intervention.

H7. Consumers who are more concerned about privacy are more likely to be aware of the WASSs both before and after the educational intervention.

H8. Consumers who are more concerned about security are more likely to be aware of the WASSs both before and after the educational intervention.

The quality of information on a website helps consumers make good purchasing decisions [56]. As consumers perceive that a website presents quality information they are more likely to have confidence that the vendor is reliable, which may then reduce their concerns related to privacy and security. They are also more likely to be aware of the WASSs because thirdparty assurance seals are part of the information that the website provides to customers. Thus, we argue that information quality is positively correlated with WASSs awareness.

H9. Consumers who perceive higher information quality are more likely to be aware of the WASSs both before and after the educational intervention.

As for the relationship between privacy and security concerns, consumers may perceive security and privacy to be somewhat equivalent concepts even if they are considered distinct factors in electronic commerce research [44]. It is generally acceptable that consumer's privacy and security concerns are highly correlated with each other [27]. Thus, we expect that there will be a positive relationship between security and privacy concerns before, and after the educational intervention.

Regarding the relationship between information quality, privacy, and security concerns, we expect that information quality is negatively correlated with privacy and security concerns because higher information quality alleviates consumer's concerns about privacy and security and makes customers confident.<sup>3</sup>

## 4. Research methodology and data collection

A series of survey questions were partially adapted from previous literature and revised to provide insight on online consumers' perceptions regarding website security features (i.e., privacy & security practices, information quality, and WASSs). Most of the items used a 7-point Likert scale system ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). The items are presented in Appendix A.

The data collection process consisted of two phases: “Before” the educational intervention (i.e., a three-hour class on security and privacy issues on commercial Websites) and “After” the intervention followed by a website analysis. As a part of a class assignment, two weeks prior to the lecture, respondents were randomly assigned to review a click-and-brick Business-to-Consumer (B2C) retail website. The website had been randomly selected from a national e-business database provide by Dun and Bradstreet, Inc. (http://www.dnb.com/US/index.asp). We had several reasons for focusing on “click-and-mortar” or “click-and-brick” companies. First, the click-and-brick strategy has become a much more common approach to ecommerce for traditional retailers [33]. In addition, it allowed us to control for variation that would be introduced if we compared purely online with mixed online and offline companies. Moreover, since many of the firms in the sample were outside the region of the study, respondents were not likely to recognize them, creating a situation where trust would not already have been established due to prior shopping experience.

To make certain all respondents had a similar experience on a B2C website in the “Before” phase, respondents were asked to take some time browsing the site and trying out its shopping cart as a customer. No specific information regarding the security and privacy practices of the site were mentioned at the “Before” phase. After browsing the site, respondents answered questions about the extent to which they felt that the site protected their privacy, used good security practices, and provided quality information. In addition, they provided baseline data about their awareness of assurance seal services and indicated the extent to which they felt such services were an important component of any vendor's site.

Respondents then received a lecture about e-commerce web security and privacy features, including both managerial aspects (e.g., assurance seal services, privacy and security policy, spam protection policy) and technical aspects (e.g., cookies, Secure Sockets Layer (SSL) protocol, Secure HTTP (S-HTTP) protocol, Secure Electronic Transaction (SET) protocol, Privacy Preferences Project (P3P), and Electronic Payment Systems). The lecture also covered threats related to privacy and security in e-commerce such as identify-theft, fraud and hacking of personal information. In order to make sure that respondents understood these web security features, they were asked to examine the same B2C website assigned in the “Before” phase. Respondents analyzed the content of the site, guided by questions regarding the presence of a range of security features, privacy statements, and assurance seals. After finishing the content analysis, respondents were directed to a posttest survey with the same questions as the pre-test.

Data was collected in an undergraduate class at a large mid-western university. The assignment was a part of a course curriculum and students received extra credit for completing the two-phase survey questionnaire and the content analysis assignment. Total 141 students took part in the study. After eliminating incomplete responses, there were 125 valid entries used for the analysis. Although student samples have long been criticized for low external validity of their representativeness of the general electronic commerce population [20], our choice of a student sample has the following two advantages. First, because our study involved three stages (i.e., “Before”, the educational intervention (a lecture) with website analysis, and “After”), it would be very difficult to collect data from actual electronic commerce consumers. Second, since the online consumer population is generally younger and more highly educated than the conventional consumer [32,51], university student samples can be a good proxy of the population [40]. The gender composition — 54.4% male and 45.6% female — is quite similar to the results of the Ernst and Young's “Global Online Retailing 2000 survey (59% male shoppers). ## 5. Data analyses and results

To ensure the appropriateness of the research instrument, the data was examined to assess the convergent validity, reliability, and discriminant validity of the scales. An acceptable level of convergent validity is when all item loadings are greater than 0.50 [67], and the items for each construct load onto only one factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1.0. Even though all survey questions were adapted from previous research and revised to fit the context of this study, we conducted two Exploratory Factor Analyses (EFAs) of all the datasets (before and after the educational intervention) to verify discriminant validity of the scales. The results of EFAs are summarized in Table 2, and reveal the same five distinct factors in both the before- and after-intervention datasets. All of the items loaded highly (above .60) onto only one factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1.65.

To verify the relationships among the constructs, we conducted two Structural Equation Modeling/Path Analyses (SEM/PA) using AMOS 6.0. As a powerful SEM/PA tool, AMOS has several strengths such as easeof-use, more confirmatory in nature, and various overall goodness-of-fit indices to assess model fit [7]. In addition, AMOS allows creating a model which has direct paths, indirect paths and covariance relationships among privacy concerns, security concerns, and information quality, which are fit into the additional research model of the study [3,7].

Based on the WASS awareness model provided in Fig. 3, we set up two models and conducted two SEM/ PA analyses (before and after the intervention). All measurement items were treated as reflective manifest variables. Fig. 4A and B illustrate the results of analyses including the convergent and discriminant validity of the latent constructs [40].

The results showed that the overall fit of the models is acceptable with RMSEA (.047 and .058), CFI (.972 and .970), GFI (.880 and .875), AGFI (.840 and .817), and NFI (.883 and .907) (before and after the intervention respectively) except the NFI index in the before intervention model. The models offer a good fit with the data using the criteria of RMSEA below 0.06, CFI/NFI above 0.9, TLI above 0.06, and GFI/AGFI above 0.8. Tables 5 and 6 summarize all model fit indices of the WASSs awareness model with suggested values in detail and the result of hypotheses before and after-intervention respectively.

The results show that privacy concerns, security concerns, and information quality are strongly correlated with each other in the before-intervention stage. The higher the perceived information quality on the site that a respondent reviewed, the less likely he or she perceives threats to security or privacy. However, in the after-intervention model, the relationship between information quality and privacy is no longer statistically significant at the .05 level, while there is still a strong link between information quality and security concerns. As for the relationship between the awareness of WASSs and the importance of WASSs (H6), it is significant in both models.

Regarding the relationship between awareness of WASSs and the other three constructs (privacy concerns, security concerns, and information quality), interestingly only the security concerns constructs show a statistically significant effect on WASS awareness (H8) across the before and after-intervention. It appears that the causal structure and relationships among constructs are similar across the before and after-intervention model.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

In order to reduce consumer's perceived privacy and security risks, e-retailers have adopted WASSs as an institutional trust signal to consumers, warranting that the licensee website has met some standard with respect to privacy and security practices. However, due to the limited awareness of WASSs, it appears that many consumers are not using them to make inferences about the trustworthiness of e-commerce sites. This study investigated whether an educational intervention would increase consumer's awareness of WASSs, as well as the extent to which they perceived such seals to be important. We further examined the relationships among the latent variables.

## 6.1. Findings and future research

As we expected, the results of the study provide strong support for Hypotheses 1 and 2; educational intervention can influence the awareness and perceived importance of assurance seal services. Even though the levels of security and privacy concerns were changed slightly after the intervention, the differences are statistically not significant so that Hypotheses 3 and 4 are not supported. There are a couple of possible interpretations for these results. First, since consumers' security and privacy concerns are relatively long-term perception, a one-time educational intervention may be too little to change long held views. Second, even though the education intervention improves consumers' security and privacy concerns, the effect of third-party seals may alleviate the concerns which have been enhanced by the educational intervention. Thus, future investigation is required to identify whether the moderating effect of third-party seals on consumers' security and privacy concerns.

The educational intervention does not change consumers' perception of the website information quality so that the Hypothesis 5 is not supported. A possible explanation for the lack of effect is that consumers are already attentive to the quality of information when they make online transaction decisions. Hence an educational intervention only focusing on security, privacy and assurance seal services would do little to change these perceptions.

The SEM/PA analysis sheds additional light on how perceptions of security and privacy may be shaped. As for the relationship between information quality and security concerns, the results indicate that there is a strong, but inverse association between a site's information quality and consumer's security concerns, both before and after the educational intervention. That is, higher information quality reduces consumer's concerns about security of the site. The study highlights an interesting effect of the educational intervention on the extent of privacy risks associated with online transactions. The results show that there is a strong relationship between a site's information quality and consumer's privacy concern before the educational intervention. However, this strong relationship disappears after the educational intervention. This result implies that once consumers received more information about the potential privacy abuses of otherwise legitimate-looking businesses, the connection between a site's information quality and privacy disappeared. That is, simply having high quality information no longer predicts the perception that the merchant would engage in good behavior vis-à-vis consumer data. Hence, one effect of better consumer awareness of online privacy abuses is that consumers may use other indicators besides vendor supplied information to determine whether a site will protect their privacy.

Surprisingly, contrary to our expectations, only security concern has a statistically significant relationship with WASS awareness both before and after the educational intervention. This finding implies that although third-party seal programs may focus on several areas: privacy, security, and business integrity [27], WASSs are mainly recognized by consumers as a security protection mechanism. As Belanger, Hiller and Smith pointed out in their study [4], security is often seen by consumers as a more important issue than privacy. Whether this is indeed true is an interesting future research question. In addition, the findings of the study suggest that further research should be necessary for several areas, such as discovering why information quality interacts with security concerns and privacy concerns differently, and exploring why security and privacy concerns play different roles on consumer's awareness of WASSs.

## 6.2. Limitations

There are several limitations that need to be addressed in future work. First, the student sample does not represent the general e-commerce population and therefore can be criticized for a lack of external validity. Second, a sample of 125 subjects is relatively small for the analysis and may be subject to unexpected results based on idiosyncrasies in the data sample. Third, since the data had been collected through two stages for two weeks, there was a possibility of confounding effects of other variables, such as a social effect through discussion with peers. Finally, we also note that the study did not control for individual propensity variables, such as self-efficacy, and learning or goal oriented attitude, which may have influenced outcomes.

## 6.3. Contributions

Despite these limitations, the results presented in this study highlight the ambiguous role of WASSs in influencing consumer perceptions of online store trustworthiness, and suggest that greater efforts to inform consumers about these services are needed. The contributions of this study are twofold. From a practical perspective the findings show that educating consumers about the role of WASSs increases awareness and perceived importance of the seals. E-retailers that invest in these services should thus make greater efforts to educate their customers about the value of the seal. This is consistent with Dekleva's argument [13] regarding the importance of consumer education to achieve meaningful consumer protection from online security and privacy threats. Once online consumers are equipped with better knowledge of security and privacy issues, they will be better able to assess the trustworthiness of e-commerce websites and recognize the possibility of security threats. In addition, it appears that information quality has a major influence on customer's confidence in the security of online transactions. It will be important for e-retailers to improve information quality in order to build customer's confidence.

From a theoretical perspective, this study provides empirical evidence of the effects of an educational intervention on the level of awareness and consumer knowledge of security and privacy aspects of B2C ecommerce. While several fields of study (e.g., Dekleva [13]) has emphasized the importance of consumer education, i.e. banking, software implementation, and so on, to our knowledge there is no study concentrating on the influence of consumer education on security and privacy features in an electronic commerce context. Therefore, we hope that this study has provided some initial insights into the effects of consumer education on web security and privacy features especially Web Assurance Seal Services.
