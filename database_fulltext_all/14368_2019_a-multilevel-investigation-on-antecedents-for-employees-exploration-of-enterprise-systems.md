# A multilevel investigation on antecedents for employees’ exploration of enterprise systems

Zeyu Peng<sup>a</sup> and Xitong Guo<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Business, East China University of Science and Technology, Shanghai, China; <sup>b</sup>School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, P.R. China

## ABSTRACT

Employees’ system exploration behaviour is critical for contemporary <sup>fi</sup>rms to fully derive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from investments in an enterprise system (ES). Based on the adaptive structuration theory (AST), it is proposed that employees’ exploration of an ES is mainly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by three fundamental components at di<sup>f</sup>erent theoretical levels: task, technology, and the organisational environment. Accordingly, we develop a multilevel research model to interpret how task variety, system modularity, and local management commitment jointly a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration. Our model is tested with a survey of ES users in 35 business units of six <sup>fi</sup>rms that have already implemented enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, and several meaningful <sup>fi</sup>ndings were discovered. At the individual level, both system modularity and task variety can directly a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration, and the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of system modularity is positively moderated by task variety. Further, unit-level local management commitment can either directly a<sup>f</sup>ect, or positively moderate, the relationship between task variety and system exploration. Limitations and implications for research and practice are discussed.

## KEYWORDS

Having expended billions of dollars on enterprise systems (ESs), contemporary <sup>fi</sup>rms are eager to maximise the returns on their investments. In practice, to fully realise the potential of an ES, employees are expected to be fully involved in system exploration; that is, experimenting with new features and exploring new approaches to using the introduced system (Ke, Tan, Sia, & Wei, 2013; Li, Hsieh, & Rai, 2013). The signi<sup>fi</sup>- cance of system exploration has long been underlined by previous research. For instance, Zubo<sup>f</sup> (1988) suggested that the disappointing performances of IT users to a great extent can be attributed to their low level of system exploration. Moreover, Saga and Zmud (1994) proposed that system exploration can enable users to accommodate more comprehensive work tasks. It is argued that system exploration is an important indicator of the success of an ES (such as the enterprise resource planning (ERP) system and customer relationship management (CRM) system), because exploration can ultimately exert a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on an individual user’s job performance, which in turn ensures full returns on the introduced system (e.g., Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Ke et al., 2013; Li et al., 2013; Liang, Peng, Xue, Guo, & Wang, 2015; Magni, Angst, & Agarwal, 2012).

Magni, 2012); however, critical gaps still exist in the literature. First, an ES is often implemented in largescale <sup>fi</sup>rms consisting of many business units, and the role of each unit’s local management needs to be further scrutinised. In practice, each unit’s local management, rather than the top management of a <sup>fi</sup>rm, takes responsibility for the implementation and interacts directly with employees on a daily basis (Lewis, Agarwal, & Sambamurthy, 2003; Peng, Fang, & Lim, 2011); it is, thus, believed that the behaviour of employees is more likely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by local management. As a kind of complex information system (Liang et al., 2015), ES requires coordinated use by users from multiple business units (Markus, Tanis, & Van Fenema, 2000; Sharma & Yetton, 2007), and its usage demands support from each unit’s local management. It is believed that employees perceptions of the support tend to be consistent within a certain unit, but vary among di<sup>f</sup>erent units (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000). This paradoxical feature of support (i.e., consistency vs. variation) indicates that it is not su<sup>fi</sup>cient to investigate the role of local management support based on a single level of analysis (e.g., Lewis et al., 2003), while investigation with both individual and unit levels of analysis would provide a more comprehensive understanding. Therefore, in our context, this more delicate support perceived by employees calls for a crosslevel analysis of the role of local management; that is, how unit-level support from local management a<sup>f</sup>ects individual-level system exploration.

Second, while system functions and job tasks are interlinked (Davenport, 1998), and system exploration is often utilised by employees to adapt the system to various job tasks, there is limited knowledge on how task and system a<sup>f</sup>ect system exploration. On the one hand, because of the dynamic work environment in current workplaces (Schyns, 2004), employees’ tasks are often characterised as frequently changing and full of uncertainty (Raub & Liao, 2012). While system exploration is often undertaken by employees to adapt to changing work tasks, we have little knowledge on how the dynamic nature of work tasks a<sup>f</sup>ects employees’ exploration behaviour. On the other hand, an ES consists of many functional modules. At the functionality level, system exploration involves applying multiple modules embedded in the system to accomplish various tasks (Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Saga & Zmud, 1994). Moreover, “best practices”, which have been proved to be e<sup>f</sup>ective methods for accomplishing relevant tasks, are embedded in system functions (Davenport, 1998). In other words, system functions indicate the potentials for accomplishing tasks, and these potentials can be realised by exploring the system functions. To this end, it is thus necessary to investigate how task and technology interact with each other to a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration.

Based on the adaptive structuration theory (AST) (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994), it is proposed that system exploration mainly derives from the interactions among three multilevel components: task, technology, and organisational environment. Drawing on prior relevant studies, and scrutinising their fundamental linkages to system exploration, we identify task variety as a task characteristic (Morgeson & Humphrey, 2006), modularity as a system characteristic (Chung, Tang, & Ahmad, 2011; Davenport, 1998), and local management commitment as a unitlevel environmental factor (Leonard-Barton, 1987; Lewis et al., 2003). A research model is developed and empirically validated to explain how these variables a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration.

Several signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions can be drawn from this paper. First, while AST is often employed to investigate technology acceptance and usage, we extend this theory to studying users’ exploration of ES, which implies multiple levels of analysis. By incorporating the relevant features of ESs, AST is proven to be a meaningful multilevel theoretical framework for studying ES exploration. Second, at the individual level, the exploration of the positive impacts of system modularity provides fresh insights that have yet to be reported. Our study also reveals the mechanisms through which task variety a<sup>f</sup>ects system exploration; moreover, despite its direct e<sup>f</sup>ect, task variety can also positively moderate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of system modularity on system exploration. Third, this paper integrates a unitlevel environmental factor, local management commitment, into the research frame, explaining how it interacts with individual-level antecedents to better predict system exploration. In fact, local management commitment can positively moderate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of task variety on employees’ system exploration. Finally, our cross-level research design extends the existing literature by proposing a research frame for employees’ system exploration, into which both individual-level and unit-level antecedents are integrated (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000). This opens a new window for future research to investigate system exploration from a multilevel perspective. Moreover, scholars are urged to explore theories of a multilevel nature, rather than traditional technology acceptance theories, which often involve only single-level analysis, to investigate the focal phenomenon.

The remaining sections of this paper are organised as follows. In the next section, we present the theoretical framework and explain the selected constructs, following which the research model and hypotheses are proposed. We next describe the research method and report the results of the data analysis. After comparing major <sup>fi</sup>ndings with prior literature and discussing the results, we note the limitations and theoretical and practical implications.

## 2. Theoretical development

## 2.1. System exploration

Contemporary ESs have often been characterised by feature-richness, which provides great latitude in the types of tasks involved in organisations and enables users to move beyond routine usage to value-adding, creative use (Jasperson, Carter, & Zmud, 2005; Karahanna and Agarwal (2006); Maruping & Magni, 2012). Orlikowski (1992) suggested many potential ways of applying appropriate technology in organisations, and users can choose to explore the features of the introduced system. Following prior research, we conceptualise system exploration in our study as the extent to which a user experiments with new features and explores new ways of using the introduced system (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Karahanna & Agarwal 2006). It is suggested that the various speci<sup>fi</sup>c features in the systems have critical implications for employees’ e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and productivity (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006; Hsieh, Rai, & Xu, 2011). By exploring the introduced systems, employees in an organisation can identify meaningful applications that may enhance their productivity (Magni et al., 2011) or optimise organisational processes (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005).

System exploration is particularly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for ES. In practice, the routine use required by a <sup>fi</sup>rm can only cover the basic functions of the introduced system and routine tasks (Li et al., 2013). As they try to assimilate the system into their tasks, employees often <sup>fi</sup>nd that the de<sup>fi</sup>ned routine use is insu<sup>fi</sup>cient for them to e<sup>f</sup>ectively accomplish job tasks (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Li et al., 2013). There are a number of potential explanations for this. On the one hand, while employees’ tasks and system functions are interlinked (Davenport, 1998), the task environment is dynamic (Schyns, 2004). To adapt to changing tasks, employees are expected to explore the system. On the other hand, to further improve the quality of the software package, the ES upgrades accordingly (Nah, Faja, & Cata, 2001). In practice, every upgrade involves changing some functions, implying that employees need to explore the newly introduced functions to <sup>fi</sup>nd more e<sup>f</sup>ective ways of accomplishing tasks.

In the organisational context, technology use can be conceptualised as using the introduced technology to accomplish tasks in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c organisational environment (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994), and prior research has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed some technological, task, and environmental antecedents for system exploration. For instance, Ahuja and Thatcher (2005) proposed that job autonomy is positively related to system exploration, while work overload can prevent users from partaking in system exploration. Perceived usefulness, attitudes, and intrinsic motivation can also assist employees in exploring the introduced system (Karahanna and Agarwal (2006); Li & Hsieh, 2007).

Magni, Susan Taylor, and Venkatesh (2010) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the hedonic and instrumental factors that can motivate users to explore the new features of a system. More recently, Magni et al. (2011) and Maruping and Magni (2012) found that a good teamlevel climate can drive users to explore the introduced system. Although these studies have greatly improved our knowledge of system exploration, limited research e<sup>f</sup>ort has been exerted on integrating the impacts of task, technology, and organisational environment. In particular, considering the multilevel nature of ES implementation (i.e., an ES is often implemented in multiple speci<sup>fi</sup>c business units; (Markus et al., 2000), it is imperative to undertake a multilevel investigation (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000).

## 2.2. Theoretical frame

In this study, we employ AST to develop our theoretical frame. AST posits that social structures, including rules (e.g., working procedures and the de<sup>fi</sup>ned ways of system use) and resources (e.g., stored data, functional features, and technical support), can either enable or constrain user behaviours, and it is meaningful to investigate the interactions among advanced information technologies, social structures, and human actions (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 2000). AST underlines the roles of social structures and proposes that social structures exist within both technology and human actions. As shown below, social structures in our context can be categorised into di<sup>f</sup>erent theoretical levels, and AST is accordingly proposed as appropriate for incorporating the multilevel antecedents for ES exploration.

There are two types of social structures within technology: the structural features of the technology and the spirit of the features. Structural features refer to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of rules and resources, or capabilities embedded within a system, while the spirit depicts the way in which a user acts when using a system, interpret its features, and appropriate it for job tasks (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Poole & DeSanctis, 1990). These two types of social structures are of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance for interpreting the impacts of ES technical factors on system exploration, because they eventually form the structural potential for human– technology interactions (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). On the one hand, the structural features of an ES indicate that the introduced system is materially fertile for users to engage in exploration. On the other hand, the spirit of the features implies that the introduced systems are ready to be explored. Since users are likely to have di<sup>f</sup>erent perceptions towards a focal technology (Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Karahanna & Agarwal 2003), the impact of technology should be investigated at the individual level.

Social structures within human actions, which are of a multilevel nature (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000), are typically represented by two aspects in the workplace: task and organisational environment (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). The content and constraints of a work task de<sup>fi</sup>ne the way in which a technology is appropriated (McGrath, 1984; Poole, Seibold, & McPhee, 1986). This is particularly the case for ES exploration, because employees’ daily tasks are embedded in the introduced system (Davenport, 1998), and thus, the method of system appropriation is greatly determined by the demands of the work tasks. In our context, employees are assigned di<sup>f</sup>erent tasks, and perceptions towards these tend to vary among employees. Consistent with prior research on the impact of task on system exploration (e.g., Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Liang et al., 2015), we conceptualise task as an individual-level factor.

Organisational environment, on its part, depicts the circumstances within which system appropriation is undertaken. A user’s appropriation of a certain technology is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the surrounding social environment (Burton-Jones & Granger, 2013). In our context, it is believed that organisational environment is signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant for ES exploration. First, an ES is technically complex (Liang et al., 2015), and exploring it would demand relevant support and resources from an organisation. Second, an ES always embeds a social nature in its features (Orlikowski, 1992), and its exploration often involves interactions among di<sup>f</sup>erent social actors (e.g., users, managers and departments). To balance the di<sup>f</sup>erent interests of social actors for system exploration, a supporting organisational environment is a prerequisite. In the workplace, regarding the organisational environment, while they are likely to have di<sup>f</sup>erent perceptions, employees in the same business unit tend to share a similar understanding (e.g., Klein & Kozlowski, 2000; Liao & Rupp, 2005). To this end, an organisational environment should be conceptualised at the unit level (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000) – this conceptualisation would enable us to investigate whether the impacts of organisational environment on system exploration vary across business units.

The two previously mentioned forms of social structure (i.e., structures within technology vs. structures within actions) regularly intertwine in practice, and consequently, there is a recursive relationship between technology and human actions (Orlikowski, 2000). This recursive relationship is particularly meaningful for ES exploration, because such exploration demands continuous interactions between technology and human actions (e.g., Hsieh et al., 2011; Li et al., 2013; Liang et al., 2015; Magni et al., 2012; Maruping & Magni, 2012, 2015). On the one hand, technical functions can facilitate and constrain human actions because of structural features and the spirits embedded in the system’s own features and functions (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). To this end, a system itself can a<sup>f</sup>ect ES exploration. On the other hand, a system can also be the outcome of human actions (Orlikowski, 1992, 2000). In other words, the structures within technology are more likely to be invoked by the structures within human actions (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Thus, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of technical functions on system exploration is contingent on task and environment. Moreover, interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects also exist between the two forms of social structures within human actions (task and organisational environment). In our context, users’ interactions with technology aim to accomplish job tasks, and the features of tasks can inform the manner of system exploration. While users interactions with technology can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the organisational environment (Orlikowski, 1992), it is believed that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of task on system exploration is contingent on the organisational environment.

Based on the preceding arguments, it is proposed that employees’ system exploration mainly stems from the interactions among three types of antecedents: task characteristics, system characteristics, and organisational environment. Although this proposed research frame can be tested by involving di<sup>f</sup>erent types of factors, to <sup>fi</sup>ll the research gaps noted above, we selected the most critical and relevant factor for each type of antecedent. In this study, task variety is selected as the most critical and relevant task characteristic, modularity as the most critical and relevant system characteristic, and local management commitment as the most critical and relevant environmental factor. This selection is further identi<sup>fi</sup>ed below.

## 2.3. Task variety

Task variety refers to the extent to which an individual engages in a wide range of tasks at work (Barling, Kelloway, & Iverson, 2003). It represents the dynamic nature of an employee’s job, because high-level variety indicates that the employee needs to adapt to changing tasks (Morgeson & Humphrey, 2006). While prior literature has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed various task characteristics (Hackman & Oldham, 1976; Morgeson & Humphrey, 2006; Oldham & Cummings, 1996) related to system exploration, such as task autonomy and overload (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Ke et al., 2013), in this study, we focus on task variety because of its fundamental connections with system features and system exploration.

First, when employees’ tasks are characterised as “high variety”, they are more likely to be exposed to new system features that they have to learn before they can use them. Second, high-level task variety requires employees to possess business knowledge in various task domains (Hackman, 1987), and the integration of the di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of knowledge enables employees to adapt the system to several interdependent tasks (Burton-Jones & Granger, 2013). Therefore, task variety has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for employees’ system exploration.

## 2.4. System modularity

Among the various system characteristics, we focus on modularity because it has been recognised as the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant characteristic of ESs, and also because of its signi<sup>fi</sup>cant theoretical implications for system exploration (e.g., Chung et al., 2011; Uwizeyemungu & Raymond, 2005). In general, current ES designs often employ the module-based approach (Duncan, 1995), where each module is correlated with certain types of business tasks (Davenport, 1998). Following prior research (Chung et al., 2011; Schilling, 2000), we define system modularity as the extent to which the modules of an implemented ES can be separated and recombined. In practice, modules in an ES are tightly linked to business tasks, and high system modularity means that the introduced system can provide users with reusable modules that can be <sup>fl</sup>exibly applied to changing task domains (Davenport, 1998; Markus & Cornelis, 1999; Saga & Zmud, 1994; Turner & Chung, 2005). That is, modules in an ES indicate “best practices” that have been proved to be e<sup>f</sup>ective methods of accomplishing relevant tasks, and these “best practices” imply many potentials for current users to accomplish their work tasks (Davenport, 1998).

Theoretically, system modularity is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for employees’ exploration of an ES. Prior research has suggested that modularity represents system <sup>fl</sup>exibility at the functional level (Duncan, 1995); this relates to ease of use (Byrd & Turner, 2000), which is a critical predictor for system exploration (Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Karahanna & Agarwal 2003). However, high-level modularity provides employees with more possible ways to use a system to e<sup>f</sup>ectively accomplish various job tasks. For instance, a task can be accomplished through the application of di<sup>f</sup>erent modules (e.g., modules A, B, and C) (Saga & Zmud, 1994), where it is possible that Module A is more e<sup>f</sup>ective than modules B and C for accomplishing the task. Further, some tasks demand employees to integrate di<sup>f</sup>erent system modules, and integration is more e<sup>f</sup>ective for a certain task. For example, say Tasks 1 and 2 can be accomplished by adopting two of the three modules A, B, and C. By exploring the system, an employee may <sup>fi</sup>nd that the portfolio of A and B is more e<sup>f</sup>ective for Task 1, while the portfolio of A and C is more e<sup>f</sup>ective for Task 2. As the employee strives to assimilate the system into various tasks, she/he often <sup>fi</sup>nds that the existing modules are insu<sup>fi</sup>cient to e<sup>f</sup>ectively accomplish job tasks (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Li et al., 2013). Regarding Task 1 in the above example, the employee later may discover that by integrating a new module – D, say – Task 1 can be more e<sup>fi</sup>ciently accomplished than with the portfolio of modules A and B. It is thus believed that only by continuously exploring system modules can employees <sup>fi</sup>nd the most e<sup>f</sup>ective ways of accomplishing various tasks.

## 2.5. Local management commitment

In general, management support is critical for technology acceptance and use (Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003). This is particularly true with regard to large-scale ESs, which are often implemented in multiple business units. This is because while top management makes the decisions for implementation, business units’ local management directly interacts with employees and takes responsibility for reaping the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the introduced system (Markus et al., 2000). An ES may be either underutilised or inappropriately utilised because of factors within the particular local context (Orlikowski, Yates, Okamura, & Fujimoto, 1995), for which the local management takes responsibility and with which it is familiar. To this end, local management plays the role of a human agency (Liang, Saraf, Hu, & Xue, 2007) and can translate the top management’s messages into managerial actions, which, in turn, can a<sup>f</sup>ect employees perceptions and behaviour with regard to system usage (Lewis et al., 2003). Following Lewis et al. (2003), we employ local management commitment to depict facilitation from local management. Accordingly, local management commitment is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which local management encourages and supports employees to engage in, and recognises their e<sup>f</sup>orts in adapting to, system usage. A high level of local management commitment re<sup>fl</sup>ects an employee’s belief that local management is committed to system use within the unit, and will provide relevant support such as training opportunities and encouragement (Lewis et al., 2003).

Considering its social nature, the environment represents opportunities and constraints that can potentially promote or thwart an individual’s goal attainment (Previn, 1987). When an employee’s goal is to explore the introduced system, which may potentially improve the performance of the business unit (e.g., Liang et al., 2015), local management commitment is likely to promote this behaviour. Indeed, local management commitment implies opportunities for employees’ goal attainment of system exploration. Towards this end, with regard to system exploration, local management commitment can be considered a form of organisational environment. With a high level of local management commitment, employees are likely to believe that their system exploration behaviour, which can potentially enhance productivity, would be encouraged and recognised by local management. Prior research has suggested that local management commitment in<sup>fl</sup>uences employees’ selective use of the features of the introduced system (Purvis, Sambamurthy, & Zmud, 2001). Further, when applying an ES to conduct their work tasks, employees often experience ambiguity with regard to the value of the introduced system (Weick, 1990). In other words, employees may be confronted with di<sup>fi</sup>culties when appropriating technology for their work tasks. However, with high-level local management commitment, these di<sup>fi</sup>culties will be greatly weakened because of the support and encouragement from the local management.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Figure 1 depicts the research model of this study. At the individual level, we propose that both task variety and system modularity directly a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration (H1 and H2), while the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of system modularity is positively moderated by task variety (H3). Further, it is proposed that unit-level local management can either directly a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration (H4) or indirectly generate in<sup>fl</sup>uence by positively moderating the individuallevel e<sup>f</sup>ects of task variety and system modularity on employees’ system exploration (H5 and H6).

## 3.1. Individual-level analysis: impacts of task and technology

It is proposed that employees whose job tasks have a high level of variety are more likely to explore the introduced ES. First, according to AST, the way in which the introduced system is appropriated is dependent on the content and constraints of work tasks (McGrath, 1984; Poole et al., 1986). In other words, how employees use the introduced system is dependent on the demands of work tasks, and tasks impose constraints on how the introduced system is appropriated. Second, contemporary businesses are deeply embedded in the introduced ES, and employees’ daily work tasks are tightly interlinked with system functions (Davenport, 1998; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). From this perspective, high-level task variety often creates a “mis<sup>fi</sup>t” between task requirements and the features with which employees are familiar, which in turn, requires employees to engage in using more features and modules of the system to better perform their tasks. For instance, say an employee has made a substantial e<sup>f</sup>ort to adapt the system to Task 1, and found that Module A is most e<sup>fi</sup>cient for accomplishing the task. Then the employee is required to deal with Task 2, which is quite di<sup>f</sup>erent from Task 1, implying that she/he has to employ new modules to accomplish the task. That is, Module A cannot help her/him to <sup>fi</sup>nish Task 2; rather, she/ he needs to explore the system to <sup>fi</sup>nd a new module that is more suited. To this end, task variety can create opportunities that stimulate employees to perform system exploration. In a prior empirical study, Liang et al. (2015) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the impact of task variety on system exploration. Accordingly, based on the arguments above, and to further con<sup>fi</sup>rm Liang et al.’s (2015) <sup>fi</sup>nding, we hypothesise that:

H1: Task variety is positively related to employees’ system exploration.

It is proposed that system modularity can potentially promote employees’ system exploration. Structural features and the spirit of the features can be employed to understand the e<sup>f</sup>ect of system modularity (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Poole & DeSanctis, 1990), and it is proposed that system modularity can positively a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration. First, the structural features of system modularity indicate that the introduced systems are materially fertile for users to engage in exploration. Having system modules spread throughout the whole business structure (Chung et al., 2011) enables employees to adapt de<sup>fi</sup>ned functionalities to new task domains. In addition, system modularity can also render the introduced system loosely coupled with separated modules (Duncan, 1995), hence potentially allowing employees to apply some functions that they were unfamiliar with previously to accomplish existing work tasks more e<sup>f</sup>ectively. Second, the spirit of the introduced system describes the way in which users act when using the system, interpreting its features, and appropriating it for job tasks (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Poole & DeSanctis, 1990). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the spirit represented in system modularity refers to the fact that system modularity provides employees with increased autonomy and room for system exploration. That is, when the introduced system is featured as high-level modularity, employees are endowed with the autonomy to decide how to appropriate the system to accomplish a certain task, either with Module A or with Modules B and C. And the crossfunctional modules embedded in an ERP system can enable an employee to cooperate with other members in the workplace (El Amrani, Rowe, & Ge<sup>f</sup>roy-Maronnat, 2006; Marciniak, Amrani, Rowe, Adam, & F, 2014), which may potentially help her/him <sup>fi</sup>nd more e<sup>f</sup>ective ways of applying the system to her/his tasks. To this end, a high level of modularity provides employees with more options to re-integrate the modules from di<sup>f</sup>erent functional areas to <sup>fi</sup>nd an e<sup>f</sup>ective way of accomplishing tasks.

Based on the preceding arguments, we propose that:

H2: System modularity is positively related to employees’ system exploration.

We further propose that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of system modularity on system exploration is contingent on task variety. Prior research suggests that the introduced system is more likely to be appropriated in a workplace when technology can satisfy the requirements of the tasks (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995; Orlikowski, 1992). When employees perform various tasks, they are urged to adapt the de<sup>fi</sup>ned system modules to more task domains. In other words, because of higher task variety, regarding certain numbers of modules, employees need to <sup>fi</sup>gure out more ways of accomplishing various job tasks by fully exploring the potential of these modules. Moreover, while high system modularity provides employees with more room and options for system exploration, high-level task variety encourages employees to act on these.

Thus, we hypothesise that:

H3: Task variety positively moderates the relationship between system modularity and system exploration, such that when task variety is perceived as high, the positive efect of system modularity on system exploration is strengthened.

## 3.2. Cross-level analysis: impacts of local management commitment

It is proposed that local management commitment can directly a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration. AST posits that users’ technology appropriation is greatly a<sup>f</sup>ected by the organisational context (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). In general, local management directly interacts with employees in daily tasks, often even assuming responsibility for reaping the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the introduced system; it is thus believed that local management can a<sup>f</sup>ect employees system usage (Lewis et al., 2003; Liang et al., 2007). While employees’ system exploration is often orientated towards more e<sup>f</sup>ective task accomplishment (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Karahanna and Agarwal (2006)) (and potentially contributing to the returns on investment in an ES), local management is likely to expect and desire employees to engage in system exploration which, in turn, may stimulate employees to behave as expected. When local management commitment is perceived as high, employees are likely to believe that they should engage in exploratory behaviour, as this behaviour is encouraged and rewarded (Orlikowski, 1992; Scott, 1995).

Based on the preceding arguments, we hypothesise that:

H4: Local management commitment is positively related to employees’ system exploration.

Despite task variety directly a<sup>f</sup>ecting employees system exploration, we propose that this direct e<sup>f</sup>ect is positively moderated by local management commitment. On the one hand, while task variety can motivate system exploration, it demands more e<sup>f</sup>ort from employees because of its various content (Narayanan, Balasubramanian, & Swaminathan, 2009). In the work context, employees are likely to evaluate the trade-o<sup>f</sup> between e<sup>f</sup>ort and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts derived from system exploration. When the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts fail to justify the e<sup>f</sup>ort, employees’ exploratory behaviour will be inhibited. However, with highlevel local management commitment, employees e<sup>f</sup>orts in system use will be encouraged and recognised (Lewis et al., 2003). In other words, in the presence of local management facilitation, employees are likely to perceive more bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and thus choose to expend more e<sup>f</sup>ort on exploration, which in turn, can result in more system exploration. On the other hand, task variety also has some constraints, such as ambiguity and risks from system use (Weick, 1990). With regard to unfamiliar tasks, attempts to apply speci<sup>fi</sup>c functions to these tasks may fail. However, with a high level of local management commitment, employees are likely to believe that the potential risks of applying the system to these tasks will not prove troublesome, and be more actively involved in system exploration. Therefore, we hypothesise that:

H5: Local management commitment positively moderates the relationship between task variety and system exploration, such that when local management commitment is perceived as high, the positive efect of task variety on system exploration is strengthened.

In the presence of a high level of local management commitment, employees are likely to believe that their work context is safe and supportive of risktaking with regard to system usage (Lewis et al., 2003). In this situation, employees are likely to be endowed with su<sup>fi</sup>cient resources (e.g., training and technical support) to further explore the materially fertile system modules. Additionally, while system modularity provides employees with increased autonomy and room for system exploration, local management commitment guarantees that the promised autonomy and freedom are legitimate and available in the work context (Lewis et al., 2003).

Based on the preceding arguments, we propose that:

H6: Local management commitment positively moderates the relationship between system modularity and system exploration, such that when local management commitment is perceived as high, the positive efect of system modularity on system exploration is strengthened.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Data collection

To test the hypotheses, a <sup>fi</sup>eld survey with a tworound selection of targeted <sup>fi</sup>rms was conducted. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst round, 33 top managers from 33 di<sup>f</sup>erent <sup>fi</sup>rms in an EMBA programme were invited to an interview. Among these 33 top managers, 15 were interested in the study and promised to facilitate the survey should their <sup>fi</sup>rms be involved. In the second round, further interviews were conducted to identify whether the volunteering <sup>fi</sup>rms would be suitable for this study. It turned out that a further nine <sup>fi</sup>rms were removed from the target list because of either their brief experiences in ERP implementation (less than one year) or the small potential sample sizes (less than <sup>fi</sup>ve). Accordingly, the remaining six <sup>fi</sup>rms were involved in this study. Since the core businesses of these <sup>fi</sup>rms varied, they adopted di<sup>f</sup>erent ERP modules (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nance, production, accounting, and logistics) to support their businesses; thus, the ERP systems involved are quite representative. Further, these <sup>fi</sup>rms operate in six industries, belong to three ownership types and employ four mainstream ERP providers, indicating that they are quite representative of the sampling.

With the support from the six top managers, a <sup>fi</sup>eld survey was conducted in 35 business units of the six Chinese <sup>fi</sup>rms (see Appendix A for detailed information) that had already implemented ERP systems. Totally, 35 normal units (operating in six different functional areas: accounting, marketing, manufacturing, purchasing, R&D, and marketing) in these <sup>fi</sup>rms that rely heavily on the ERP system to operate their businesses were required to participate in the survey. In total, 240 employees from these normal units were selected as survey respondents, based on the following criteria: (1) respondents have experience in using an ERP system for at least one year, and (2) they depend on the ERP system to accomplish their daily work tasks.

With the facilitation of the top managers in these <sup>fi</sup>rms, 240 paper-based questionnaires were distributed to the respondents by either the department heads or the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s human resource o<sup>fi</sup>ces. Respondents were informed that they would remain anonymous and that they needed to submit the completed questionnaire within a few days. One week later, three researchers (including one of the authors and two PhD students) collected the questionnaire personally, from each site.

A total of 221 respondents returned the survey questionnaire; a response rate of 92.1%. Owing to an excessive amount of missing data, eight questionnaires were dropped, leaving 213 valid samples for the <sup>fi</sup>nal data analysis. The number of respondents from each unit ranged from 3 to 16, which is acceptable in multilevel research (Chen, 2005; Erdogan & Bauer, 2009), with an average unit size of 6.09. Among the 213 subjects, 94 (44.1%) were males and 119 (55.9%) were females. A majority of the respondents (93%) ranged in age from 20 to 39 years, and 95.3% had educational levels of an associate bachelor’s degree or above.

## 4.2. Measurement development

Because the respondents were native Chinese speakers, a committee was employed to translate the questionnaire from the English language into Chinese (Van de Vijver & Leung, 1997). First, three native Chinese-speaking PhD students, majoring in management and also <sup>fl</sup>uent in English, independently translated the English language questionnaire into Chinese. The group then discussed the individual items to achieve consensus, after which another two PhD students were asked to translate the Chinese items into English. It was found that the English version did not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>er from the original.

To ensure the face validity of the measures, a three-step revision procedure was employed. First, two expert scholars in IT di<sup>f</sup>usion and organisational behaviour were invited to check and revise the questionnaire. Second, one of these two scholars interviewed several end users in one of the participating companies to validate the questionnaire. Finally, to further ensure that it was appropriate for surveying the respondents, the questionnaire was then distributed to 10 professionals (including <sup>fi</sup>ve end users and <sup>fi</sup>ve IT sta<sup>f</sup>) in other companies for further suggestions and comments. These procedures resulted in minor revisions of some constructs, after which it appeared that the respondents found the questionnaire understandable.

All multi-item measures were adapted or adopted from existing validated scales, using a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 “strongly disagree” to 7 “strongly agree.” The speci<sup>fi</sup>c measurement items are shown in Appendix B.

We adopted the instruments of Ahuja and Thatcher (2005) and Karahanna & Agarwal (2003) for system exploration. Task variety was measured in line with Morgeson and Humphrey (2006). The measures for system modularity were adapted from prior relevant research (Byrd & Turner, 2000; Rivard, Poirier, Raymond, & Bergeron, 1997), and the items for local management commitment were adapted from Lewis et al. (2003).

In addition to the above core constructs, we also included control variables; speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and system self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, considered antecedents of system exploration in prior research, were adopted from Venkatesh and Bala (2008). Personal innovativeness with IT was adopted from Agarwal and Prasad (1997). Also, since the quantity of modules can a<sup>f</sup>ect users’ cross-functional awareness (El Amrani et al., 2006; Marciniak et al., 2014), which might be relevant to applying a system to <sup>fi</sup>nishing tasks, we controlled for the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the number of functional modules. In addition, length of ERP in the <sup>fi</sup>rm, employees’ gender and tenure were included in the model to further validate our research hypotheses.

## 5. Data analysis

We employed partial least square (PLS), a “secondgeneration” technique with the ability to model relationships among multiple predictors and criterion variables (Chin, 1998a) that can assess the validity of constructs (by estimating loadings). Further, the multilevel research model was tested using the hierarchical linear modelling (HLM) technique. This HLM technique is an ideal analysis technique for testing multilevel models, with the dependent variables being individual-level constructs and the predictors consisting of both individual- and unit-level variables (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002), and is thus suitable for our study. When employees are nested within units, the HLM technique can be employed to conduct random coe<sup>fi</sup>cient modelling, which deals with a cross-level data structure (Liao & Rupp, 2005). In our study, employees were nested within di<sup>f</sup>erent units, and we estimated the e<sup>f</sup>ects of local management commitment by aggregating the responses from employees in the same unit, so that the HLM technique could process data based on the shared unit codes between individual- and unit-level variables (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002).

## 5.1.2. Unit-level validation

Because of the multilevel nature of our research model, it is necessary to verify the appropriateness of aggregating the data measured for the unit-level variable; that is, local management commitment at the unit level. A two-step approach was employed to validate the aggregation (e.g., Liao & Rupp, 2005). First, $\bf { r } _ { w g }$ captured the absolute level of agreement across raters, indicating the extent to which raters make similar mean-level ratings. The mean ${ \bf r } _ { \mathrm { w g } }$ across units of 0.90 for local management commitment, exceeding the conventional threshold of 0.70 (James, Demaree, & Wolf, 1993), suggested adequate withinunit agreement (James, Demaree, & Wolf, 1984).

Second, ICC(1), an index of inter-rater reliability, was found to be 0.19 for local management commitment, meeting the acceptable threshold value of 0.10 reported in prior research (Liao & Rupp, 2005). ICC (2), commonly used to estimate the reliability of group means, was 0.58 for local management commitment, which, based on prior literature, is deemed satisfactory (e.g., Liao & Rupp, 2005; Schneider, White, & Paul, 1998). Thus, we conclude that local management commitment can be aggregated at the unit level by considering its mean unit values.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Discussion of <sup>fi</sup>ndings

Based on AST, this study attempts to explore how task variety, system modularity, and local management commitment a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration in work settings. The results support most of the hypotheses, thereby yielding insightful <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, task variety can directly and positively a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration. This <sup>fi</sup>nding extends our knowledge of the impacts of job properties on system exploration (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005). Second, as expected, system modularity is positively related to system exploration. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that the functionality of the introduced system, which has been largely ignored in prior relevant literature, is also signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for employees’ system exploration. Moreover, the results indicate that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of system modularity on system exploration is dependent on task variety. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that system exploration is a process in which employees leverage technology for tasks, in turn, rejecting technology determinism in the workplace (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994).

While local management commitment has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as an antecedent for users’ beliefs and behaviour (Lewis et al., 2003), this study further suggests that local management commitment at the unit level can either directly facilitate, or indirectly interact with, individual-level task variety to a<sup>f</sup>ect employees’ system exploration. As expected, local management commitment can directly a<sup>f</sup>ect system exploration. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that the facilitation and support of local management is a prerequisite not only for routine use, but for exploratory use. Further, we found that local management commitment can strengthen the relationship between task variety and system exploration. This <sup>fi</sup>nding further extends our knowledge on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of task characteristics on system exploration by additionally underlining that task characteristics can interact with organisational context (local management commitment in this study) to better facilitate the exploration of the introduced system.

Contrary to our postulations, the moderation e<sup>f</sup>ect of local management commitment on the relationship between system modularity and system exploration is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. Possible explanations are as follows. The ultimate goal of using an ES is to accomplish tasks (Davenport, 1998; McAfee, 2006); in other words, it is only when employees accomplish tasks with the introduced system that they can practically claim bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the usage. Although system modularity implies potential value for employees, realising the “potential” may involve implications that are beyond the e<sup>f</sup>ect of local management commitment. Instrumentally, before employees successfully <sup>fi</sup>nd e<sup>f</sup>ective ways of applying relevant modules to work tasks, they are less likely to expect bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from local management; that is, the e<sup>f</sup>ort involved before the success of exploration cannot be well compensated by local management. In practice, this type of e<sup>f</sup>ort is demanded to a great extent by system exploration; much greater than the e<sup>f</sup>orts in engaging in successful ways of usage, from which employees can claim bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Spiritually, system modularity provides employees with autonomy and room for exploration, and this potentially implies that employees may be intrinsically underlining the autonomy and room themselves while neglecting the existence of local management commitment.

## 6.2. Limitations and suggestions for future research

This study has several limitations, and addressing these would potentially provide future research with promising directions. First, despite prior research suggesting that many organisational factors can shape innovative behaviour (Amabile 1988; Oldham & Cummings, 1996) that features system exploration, we underscore the role of local management commitment. It is obvious that future research can provide further insights by integrating more relevant organisational factors in the research frame, such that a more comprehensive understanding of system exploration is achieved. Second, while task is an important component in the proposed research frame, we only involved task variety. Future research may integrate more task characteristics (e.g., task autonomy and work overload) into the frame to further extend our understanding of task in the research frame. Third, there is only one type of ES involved in our research, the ERP, and this may limit the generalisability of this research. Future research may extend the generalisability of this study by replicating it, and also by using more ESs. By scrutinising the di<sup>f</sup>erences among ESs, future studies could draw a more comprehensive picture of system exploration and usage.

In addition, this study was conducted in China, which may also limit its generalisability. It would be interesting to conduct relevant research in other countries by considering culture-speci<sup>fi</sup>c organisational factors. This would potentially enable us to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the focal phenomenon. Further, although this study has focused on the exploratory use of ES at the individual level, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the level of analysis of system exploration can also be extended to the <sup>fi</sup>rm level. For instance, while system modularity is positively related to employees’ exploration, it is possible that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s system design can predict its exploratory use, and we may further investigate the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of an entire organisation’s task design and external environment. To this end, future studies can employ <sup>fi</sup>rm-level theories (e.g., the technology–organisation–environment (TOE) framework, which is usefu in explaining <sup>fi</sup>rm-level IT di<sup>f</sup>usion) to investigate the exploratory usage of ES.

## 6.3. Theoretical implications

This study provides several signi<sup>fi</sup>cant theoretical implications. First, while AST is often employed to investigate technology acceptance and usage, our study extends this theory to an investigation of users’ exploration of complex information systems. In an interpretive study, Orlikowski (2000) suggested that AST is potentially useful for explaining critical users’ behaviours, which she termed as application (i.e., users choose to use the introduced system to augment or re<sup>fi</sup>ne their existing methods of doing things) and change (i.e., users choose to employ the introduced system to change their existing ways of doing things). Both application and change in her study are highl relevant to system exploration; our study, however, takes a step forward by theoretically and empirically justifying the appropriateness of AST for investigating system exploration. Going beyond that, we have further identi<sup>fi</sup>ed AST as a meaningful multilevel theoretical framework for studying ES exploration by analysing social structures at di<sup>f</sup>erent theoretical levels. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we propose that system exploration is mainly derived from the interactions among three multilevel components: task, technology, and organisational environment. While prior relevant research has either ignored some antecedents or focused on a single level of analysis (e.g., Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Ke et al., 2013; Magni et al., 2012; Maruping & Magni, 2012, 2015), our proposed integrative multilevel theoretical frame is able to provide a more comprehensive understanding of ES exploration, considering that ES implementation often involves the interactions among task, technology, and environment (Liang et al., 2015).

Second, going beyond prior research investigating the roles of task properties and technology features in a<sup>f</sup>ecting system exploration (e.g., Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Karahanna & Agarwal 2003), we have integrated two critical yet understudied individual-level factors (system modularity and task variety) into a single research frame. Indeed, the positive impact of system modularity is an insightful <sup>fi</sup>nding that reveals the fact that technology can a<sup>f</sup>ect system exploration at the functionality level. Consistent with

AST, this <sup>fi</sup>nding empirically proves that structural features and spirits embedded in system features and functions can a<sup>f</sup>ect human actions (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). In addition, the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of system modularity on system exploration is moderated by task variety, implying that we need to avoid technology determinism regarding system exploration. In other words, although technology itself is important, its e<sup>f</sup>ect on system exploration is dependent on whether it can satisfy the demands of employees’ work tasks.

Third, this study integrates an environmental factor, local management commitment, into the research frame as a unit-level antecedent, explaining how it directly a<sup>f</sup>ects, and indirectly interacts with, individual-level antecedents to further predict system exploration. Our <sup>fi</sup>nding of the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of local management commitment on system exploration, together with the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of task variety, empirically justi<sup>fi</sup>es that social structures within human actions can a<sup>f</sup>ect user behaviour (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Moreover, local management commitment can positively moderate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of task variety on employees’ system exploration. This <sup>fi</sup>nding con<sup>fi</sup>rms that social structures within human actions can generate an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on technology acceptance and use (e.g., Joshi, 1991; Orlikowski, 1992). This attempt stimulates researchers to investigate other factors that represent the social structures within human actions, which are at the business unit level rather than the individual level, as well as their impacts on system exploration.

Finally, the cross-level research design, involving both individual-level (task variety and system modularity) and unit-level (local management commitment) factors, can provide us with a more comprehensive understanding of employees’ system exploration (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000). While system implementation in an organisation is often indicative of a hierarchical nature, and system exploration can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by factors from di<sup>f</sup>erent theoretical levels (Peng et al., 2011), prior relevant research has mainly focused on a single level of analysis (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Karahanna & Agarwal 2003; Ke et al., 2013). It is argued that the reason for this is that most of the well-established technology acceptance theories (e.g., TAM, TPB, and motivation theory) are only appropriate for individual-level analysis. However, in contemporary <sup>fi</sup>rms, ES implementation often occurs in multiple business units, implying that employees usage behaviour can be predicted by antecedents at di<sup>f</sup>erent theoretical levels (Markus et al., 2000; Peng et al., 2011). To this end, our study provides fresh insights for future research by suggesting that, to re<sup>fl</sup>ect hierarchical nature and investigate user behaviour based on multilevel analysis, scholars need to integrate a theory of a multilevel nature (AST in our case) with the speci<sup>fi</sup>c multilevel context. This study can encourage scholars to employ more theories characterised as multilevel to further extend relevant literature.

## 6.4. Managerial implications

This study also has several important practical implications for practitioners concerning returns from ES investment. Our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that it is necessary for <sup>fi</sup>rms to take managerial actions to increase system modularity, task variety, and local management commitment. First, several managerial measures can be employed to enhance system modularity. At the system design stage, both internal and external (e.g., system providers) IT professionals need to scrutinise the “best practices” of <sup>fi</sup>rms, and embed these best practices in modules. Moreover, considering that system modules are interlinked with employees’ tasks (Davenport, 1998), top management is urged to initiate task redesign to better appropriate technological modules for tasks, which, in turn, would help employees in perceiving that the system modules are <sup>fl</sup>exible for work tasks.

A second practical implication is that the management of a <sup>fi</sup>rm needs to carefully design employees’ tasks to <sup>fi</sup>t the introduced ES. For employees who need to use a high-level modularity system, management could employ certain measures to design their tasks, for instance, to reconstruct jobs by either adding more task activities to employees’ current jobs (Morgeson & Campion, 2003; Morgeson, Delaney-Klinger, & Hemingway, 2005) or requiring coordination among employees and work groups to broaden employees’ duties and activities in the workplace (e.g., Gist & Mitchell, 1992). Another practical implication is that measures need to be taken to increase employees’ perceptions of local management commitment; for instance, local managers need to frequently communicate with employees, so that employees are likely to believe that local management emphasises and supports system implementation and use in the unit.
