## a b s t r a c t

Studies of Internet market have found that consumers' purchasing behaviors including information search, channel selection, and brand evaluation processes are impacted by the product/service types. In this research, we perform a comprehensive review of existing online product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation methods. An in-depth analysis of the rationale behind each method was studied, and important product characteristics were determined for integrating different approaches. Grounded on the theory of the components of perceived risk in product purchase and the recently emerged service science, the integrated classi<sup>fi</sup>cation will help companies to better understand the online consumer purchasing behavior and to design their e-commerce strategies accordingly. Survey results indicate that the integrated classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is an effective way to classify products and services marketed on the Internet.

## 1. Introduction

There is a broad range of products and services marketed on the Internet. Products include both physical and digital products which range from consumer goods to industrial goods. Services marketed on the Internet also range from professional consultation to social networking services. Researchers in service science such as Vargo and Lusch [47] consider all businesses are service businesses and service provision rather than goods is fundamental to economic exchange. Online retail industry is part of service industry that counts for close to 80% of GDP in the United States. Services are often packaged and referred to as products (e.g., Web hosting packages). Therefore, in this research we chose to use “product” as a generic term to represent both products and services marketed on the Internet to be consistent with the terminology used in most of the literature reviewed. The consumer purchase decision process varies with the type of product being considered. Complex and expensive products are likely to involve greater buyer deliberation. A recent study by Choudhury and Karahanna [11] showed that consumers seem to divide the purchase process into two distinct stages: gathering information and executing transactions. They asserted that “the relative advantage of electronic channels, as well as the influence of each dimension of relative advantage on the adoption of electronic channels, would vary across the different stages of the purchase process.” The <sup>fi</sup>ndings concur with Pavlou's [39] conceptualization of two dependent variables in B2C adoption: information gathering and purchasing.

Therefore, in this research we focus on the understanding of relative advantages of electronic channels and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the adoption of online channels for various product/service across the two stages of the purchasing process. Our review of literature in consumer channel selection also reveals the same two schools of thoughts in online product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation: information-oriented and transactionoriented approaches. The information-oriented classi<sup>fi</sup>cation method can be exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by the search-experience-credence framework proposed by Nelson [35,36] and Darby and Karni [13] while the transaction-oriented classi<sup>fi</sup>cation method is represented by the three-dimension product feature category proposed by Peterson, Balasubramanian, and Bronnenbert [41].

The search–experience–credence scheme clusters products into search goods and experiences goods according to how consumers acquire product information. Search goods are goods that can be evaluated using external information. This category includes products such as digital cameras and computer hardware, whereas experience goods have to be evaluated in person such as apparel, shoes, and furniture. Study showed that if a product is a search good, it is more suitable to be sold online. On the other hand, selling experience good on the Internet is more challenging. New advancements in web technologies such as virtual reality allow the experience goods to be more effectively communicated (e.g., be seen or heard if not touched) online, especially for routinely purchased experience goods. We believe that the differences between search and experience goods have started to blur.

Peterson et al. [41] categorized the products/services along three dimensions: cost and frequency of purchase, value proposition, and degree of differentiation. Their study suggested that it is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to sell products online when they are relative cheap and have to be purchased frequently, tangible in their value proposition, and less differentiated. Although Peterson's framework has been widely adopted, it failed to consider product features related to the information acquisition process and focuses mainly on the features related to the transaction process.

Extant literature showed that a large proportion of consumers use the web to gather information but make <sup>fi</sup>nal purchase from traditional channels. This can be evidenced by the high abandon rate of shopping carts. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that a consumer's intended usage of the online channel varies between the two separate, yet highly interrelated stages, of the purchase process: gathering information and executing transaction. Therefore, a product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme that is designed for a particular stage of the process will not be effective when used in analyzing consumer behavior at the other stage of the process. Our research extends the literature in e-marketing and product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the goal of identifying and understanding product characteristics that impact consumers' online purchasing behavior. Grounded on the theory of the components of perceived risks in product purchase [24,27], we analyze and integrate the two schools of thoughts using serviceoriented logic viewpoint. Quality of online services is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to compare [12]; hence the purchase decision is often determined by the word of mouth reputation of the products/services as well the online web sites [6,16]. The rest of this paper is organized into the following section. Section 2 reviews and summarizes the literature of online product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Section 3 proposes the integrated online product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and provides a theoretical foundation for the integration. A survey that attempts to understand the online purchase intention and perceived risks in relation to online product categories was conducted, and the results were analyzed and discussed in Section 4. The survey results were used to validate the effectiveness of the integrated classi<sup>fi</sup>cation method. The integrated online product/ service classi<sup>fi</sup>cation can contribute in various e-marketing strategies and decision making related studies. The paper is concluded in Section 5 with summary of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and limitations of this research.

## 2.1. Information-oriented product classification schemes

Drawing from the theory of information economies, Nelson [35,36] <sup>fi</sup>rst classi<sup>fi</sup>ed products into search and experience goods. The purpose of the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation was for analyzing whether the availability of product quality can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the monopoly power of consumer goods, store location, advertising, and inventory policy. Search goods are products whose quality can be objectively evaluated before purchase based on information that is readily available, while experience goods need to be personally tried and examined. Since most products are neither pure search goods nor pure experience goods, Nelson later generalized his classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and de<sup>fi</sup>ned search goods as products dominated by product attributes for which product information can be acquired prior to purchase. While experience goods are dominated by product attributes that cannot be known until consume of the product or for which acquiring product information is more costly and/or dif<sup>fi</sup>cult than direct product experience.

Norton and Norton [37] extended Nelson's model based on the frequency of purchase by further dividing experience goods into durable and non-durable experience goods. It is believed that wordof-mouth is more important for durable experience goods than nondurable ones. They also added “credence goods” as an additional category to represent goods whose quality is hard to determine even after the product is consumed. A vitamin supplement is an example of credence goods. Opinions of experienced users or experts are valued by potential purchasers in judging the quality of such product. Another extension of Nelson's model by Wright and Lynch [49] includes “after using” rather than “after purchasing” when sampling is available. One shortcoming of adopting Nelson's classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is that the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation was proposed before the popularity of e-commerce, therefore it does not take into consideration product characteristics pertinent to online market that allows consumers to evaluate experience goods before purchase. For example, the Internet makes downloading movie and music clips as well as demo software and games for evaluation before purchase possible. Recommendation systems deployed at many e-commerce web sites may also help to increase the level of con<sup>fi</sup>dence of a consumer about the quality of experience goods based on other people's experiences. Realizing the need for a new classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme, several studies have attempted to propose new classi<sup>fi</sup>cation schemes appropriated for online market.

De Figueiredo [14] classi<sup>fi</sup>ed products into a spectrum of four categories. These are: (1) commodity: whose quality can be easily determined by its description (e.g., oil), (2) quasi-commodity (e.g., book), (3) look and feel (e.g., perfume), and (4) look and feel with variable quality (e.g., used car). This classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is based on information asymmetry between sellers and buyers in cyberspace. The commodity product corresponds well with search goods in Nelson's model while the look and feel with variable quality product has similar characteristics as experience goods. Jahng et al. [25] proposed an online product typology based on two interactive functions of e-commerce for effective design of online stores: Product Presence (PP) and Social Presence (SP) needs. Product presence needs represent a sense of presence (e.g., seeing or hearing) of the target product to help buyers to make a product choice decision. Social presence needs represent the required interactions between buyers and sellers. We believe that the interactions among buyers are even more critical in the Web 2.0 environment and for service-oriented offerings. As the need for product and social presence increases, richer media and greater interaction support should be provided. Rosen and Howard [44] differentiated products as homogenous and heterogeneous products according to product features such as size, texture, and <sup>fi</sup>tness.

For the purpose of providing website implementation guidelines for online retailers from both technological and consumer stand points, Choi et al. [10] proposed a product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation framework based on degree of information quality <sup>fi</sup>tness and consumer involvement. Information quality <sup>fi</sup>tness refers to the extent to which a product presented online can match with shopper's requirements for accessing online information. Similar to the concept of search and experience goods, low information <sup>fi</sup>tness products resemble experience goods that require touch and feel, while high information <sup>fi</sup>tness products resemble search goods. Involvement refers to the degree of psychological identi<sup>fi</sup>cation or emotional ties consumers have with a particular product. The amount of involvement in<sup>fl</sup>uences online shoppers' perception of the product's presence and the amount of effort on searching for product information. High involvement products are usually more complex and expensive, and consumers tend to spend more time to understand the various aspects of the product before making a purchase.

## 2.2. Transaction-oriented product classification schemes

One of the earliest product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme that distinguishes online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel impacts is the three-dimension scheme proposed by Peterson et al. [41]. The three product dimensions are: cost and frequency of purchase, value proposition, and degree of differentiation. The cost and frequency of purchase dimension ranges from inexpensive and frequently purchased goods to expensive and infrequently purchased goods. His study suggests that individuals tend to avoid purchasing inexpensive and frequently purchased goods online. The product value proposition classi<sup>fi</sup>es products into tangible (physical) and intangible products. The degree of differentiation classi<sup>fi</sup>cation refers to the degree of product customization that enables businesses to create competitive advantage. Eight types of products are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed by dichotomizing these three dimensions.

After analyzing consumer complaints for different types of products sold online, Scott and Gregg [45] classi<sup>fi</sup>ed products using four product features: asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city (description complexity), sensory requirements (demand on feeling, touching, and smelling), variable functionality (complexity in product functions), and fragility. Teboul [46] considered any activity as a composite of front-stage (service aspect) and back-stage (product aspect) elements. Products and services are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into four categories based on the relative importance of service component: 1. Commodities or pure goods. 2. Consumer durables that are service-intensive goods. 3. Good- and information-intensive services. 4. Relative pure services. Serviceoriented goods and services are considered experience goods or even credent goods in the literature review.

## 2.4. Review of studies that adopted different product classification schemes

Although not originally designed for e-marketing, the search/ experience goods classi<sup>fi</sup>cation has gained popularity in e-market research after its adoption by Klein [28] in studying the impact of the

Internet as an interactive media. Subsequent researches in understanding consumer online search behaviors, information presences, and web design have attempted to validate the search/experience goods classi<sup>fi</sup>cation empirically and important <sup>fi</sup>ndings have been drawn and published in [3,7,9,17,20,21,25]. While we recognize the contribution of the search/experience goods classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme, it fails to consider other important product features such as price, purchase frequency, and tangibility, hence it has limited applicability. On the other hand, the transaction-oriented product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation mainly focused on studying the <sup>fi</sup>tness of goods for ecommerce, especially at the transaction processing stage [31,42,48]. Likewise, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation fails to consider the relative advantage that could be gained at the information acquisition stage. Appendix A summarizes extant research that adopted different online product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation schemes and categorizes them by research question, problem domain, classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme implemented, and key <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Review of empirical studies showed that information-oriented product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation has been applied to the study of consumer online information search behaviors, consumer purchase preference/ intention, and the impact of <sup>fi</sup>nancial, social, structural bonds, demographics, shopping orientation, and online retailer attributes on purchase decision. The transaction-oriented product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme has also been widely adopted in channel selection, consumer shopping intention, acceptance of online shopping, website qualityloyalty, and purchase intention. The problem with separating the analysis of the two distinct but closely related stages of the purchase process may result in conclusion that is biased or de<sup>fi</sup>cient. For example, transaction-oriented classi<sup>fi</sup>cation targets at the understanding of goods–Internet match and does not distinguish the difference in channel selection at different stages of the purchasing process. A product may be considered unsuitable for selling online based on the product characteristics related to transaction-oriented classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, but these disadvantages can be mitigated through the proper use of the online media at the information gathering stage; hence improving the overall presence of the product. A recent study by Pavlou [39] also pointed out the importance of integrating the two stages of the purchase process in order to describe and predict consumer online behavior effectively. They argued that adoption of ecommerce shouldn't be viewed as a monolithic behavior, but should include the processes of both information gathering and purchasing. Pavlou's [39] e-commerce adoption model attempts to establish associations between information gathering and product purchase stages by combining important factors from both processes in one model. Therefore, a new product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation that integrates the important product features considered in both stages of purchase process such as the one we propose will provide the basis for conducting related research.

## 3. The relationship between the components of perceived risk and online product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation

The distant and impersonal nature of e-commerce leads to greater information asymmetry and higher risks than the traditional shopping environment [50]. Perceived risk has been shown to negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' intention to shop online, both for products [5,18,26,29,32,38] and services [15]. Several researchers have argued that risk related factors are the key to the success/failure in the online market. Consumers have shown reluctance to complete simple online purchase transactions, primarily due to risk concerns [26,38]. Perceived risk is posited as a prominent barrier to consumer acceptance of e-commerce [18,19]. Mitchell [34] also argued that perceived risk is very important in explaining consumers' behavior since consumers are more often motivated to avoid mistakes than to maximize utility in purchasing. Kotler [30] further suggested that the behaviors of consumers to change, delay, or cancel purchase decisions are greatly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by consumers' perceived risk. Firms can adopt favorable return policy to reduce the perceived risk of the consumers.

When compared with other factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing e-commerce adoption such as trust and convenience, perceived risk is found to have a stronger negative correlation with willingness to shop online [29]. Pavlou's [38] research also suggested that although trust and perceived risk have both shown to be direct antecedents of intention to transact; hence risk reduction is the key factor in increasing consumer acceptance of e-commerce. The risk involved in product purchase is a multi-dimensional construct that mainly includes product performance risk, <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk, and time/convenience loss risk [24]. Product performance risk refers to the probability a product fails to meet the expected performance/quality requirements. Financial risk is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the likelihood of suffering a <sup>fi</sup>nancial loss due to any hidden costs, maintenance costs, or replacement cost due to lack of warranty and a faulty product. Time/convenience loss risk represents the probability of the purchase resulting in lost time due to delivery, <sup>fi</sup>tting/customization, or repair [24,40].

As suggested by Zhou [50], the effect of perceived risk may be subject to product characteristics. We believe the two schools of thoughts on product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation discussed in Section 2 each capture certain aspects of consumer-perceived risks in the product purchase process as shown in Table 2. Information-oriented classi<sup>fi</sup>- cation focuses on whether the consumer perceived online product information presence can re<sup>fl</sup>ect the true product features and/or quality (like function, quality, color, size, feel, and style.) Therefore the performance risk component is determined by whether a consumer can properly evaluate a product without experiencing it. Bhatnagar et al. [4,5] indicated that consumer perceived product risk is likely to be greater when there is little information about the product and the consumer has low self-con<sup>fi</sup>dence in evaluating brands. Forsythe and Shi [19] showed that product performance risk may result from a poor product choice due to the shoppers' inability to accurately judge the quality of the product online. For search goods, since the product attribute information can be acquired prior to purchase, the consumers' perceived product performance risk is relatively low. The performance risk for experience goods is higher.

Several studies [1,4,22,41] showed that “convenience” is the main advantage of the online market vs. the of<sup>fl</sup>ine/traditional market for both physical and digital goods. The value proposition dimension of Peterson's framework captures the importance of perceived time/ convenience risk of product marketed online. Digital products require no delivery time and receive no damage during the delivery process, hence have lower convenience/time loss risk. Online search capability provides lower time/convenience loss risk to all product categories. Another dimension of Peterson's framework, outlay/purchase frequency, re<sup>fl</sup>ects the <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk factor in product purchase. The higher the product price is, so is the perceived product <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk [2,5]. Mitchell [34] has reviewed several studies and concluded that high value, complexity, and involvement products are perceived to have higher risk than low value, complexity, and involvement products. Expensive and infrequently purchased goods usually have higher perceived <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk when purchased online. The degree of differentiation/customization dimension mainly affects the time/ convenience loss risk and focuses on differentiating the product features from the seller's stand point for enhancing competitive advantages.

In summary, information-oriented and transaction-oriented product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation schemes can only partially re<sup>fl</sup>ect consumers' perceived risks in online product purchase, thus a more comprehensive product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation that integrates both approaches is needed to better understand consumer online purchase behavior. Drawing from the theory of the components of perceived risk [24], we adapt and integrate the two popular schools of thoughts and propose a new online product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation consist of three dimensions and eight product categories as shown in Table 3.

## 3.1. The integrated classification scheme

The proposed method classi<sup>fi</sup>es products along three dimensions: degree of standardization/brand recognition, outlay/purchase frequency, and digitizability (value proposition). Outlay/purchase frequency and digitizability dimensions are directly adopted from Peterson's model [41]. Unlike outlay/purchase frequency and value proposition dimensions, often times it is hard to determine if a product is a search or experience goods. As stated earlier, research has argued the differences between search and experience goods may be blurred as technology advancement in web application allows the touch features of experience goods to be more effectively communicated through the Internet. Several extensions have been proposed; all attempted to provide a clearer de<sup>fi</sup>nition for the category [i.e. 14,35,37]. However, there is still limited success in offering an objective measure for product performance/information <sup>fi</sup>tness. Therefore, a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation dimension that provides clear and objective criterion for evaluating product performance/information <sup>fi</sup>tness will substantially improve the applicability and performance of the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme.

In the proposed classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, we combined and broadened the classi<sup>fi</sup>cations of search/experience goods [35], search/experience credence goods [37], commodity/quasi-commodity/look and feel goods/look and feel with variable quality goods [17], product presence/social presence needs [25], homogeneity/heterogeneity [44], and information quality <sup>fi</sup>tness [10], and formed the degree of standardization/brand recognition dimension. The new dimension refers to the extent and accuracy of product performance/quality information can be acquired from the Internet. The feature/quality of high standardization product can easily be articulated through accepted calibration such as kilogram for weight and inch for height while low standardization products more likely to contain feature/ quality that conventional measure does not exist such as smell and texture. The complexity in describing products of low standardization increases the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty for consumers to evaluate the performance quality of such products, thus, standardization can re<sup>fl</sup>ect the degree of required experience as well.

We believe that the degree of product standardization and brand recognition are two features that complement one other. The degree of product standardization has greater impact on the consumer trust level of the online channel during the information acquisition stage. Brand name recognition of the products and the Web sites improves the level of quality assurance and also has a positive effect in the consumer trust level of the online channel. But, when most of the product features can be objectively described and evaluated using standardized measures, the effect of brand recognition is less signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. On the other hand, when the degree of product standardization is low, hence more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to judge the performance/quality of the product, the importance of brand recognition increases and can serve as the surrogate for quality. The study by De Figueiredo [14] classi<sup>fi</sup>ed products into four categories based on degree of product heterogeneity from low heterogeneity includes commodities such as oil and paper, to high heterogeneity, such as used cars and works of art. Although the study also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the importance of degree of heterogeneity in product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, it fails to provide an objective way of measure and did not consider the complementing effect of brand recognition. We believe the degree of product standardization brand recognition can be an effective indicator for product performance-information <sup>fi</sup>tness.

The degree of product standardization/brand recognition dimension of the proposed classi<sup>fi</sup>cation also encompasses the product differentiation/customization dimension suggested by Peterson et al., [41]. Differentiation can include both brand differentiation and product differentiation (customization). Brand differentiation can be represented by brand recognition feature of the new classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, while product customization is made possible by the standardization of important product features or components. There are many successful cases where online stores have bene<sup>fi</sup>ted from standardized product features and allow product customization/differentiation to be more easily speci<sup>fi</sup>ed and objectively evaluated. For example, the exceptional performance of Dell Inc. in recent years through its strategies of direct sales and build-to-order production have proven successful in reducing inventory and shortening product-tomarket time. The build-to-order (customization) process is made possible by allowing customers to con<sup>fi</sup>gure a computer online by choosing standardized product features such as processor model and speed, memory, hard drive, video card, display device and other peripherals, the dimensions and weight of the product, and warranty and service. Similarly, with the help of standardized wine features such as region and country, year, type, and brand, it makes the searching and evaluation of wine quality possible without the need for smell and taste as can be evidenced by the success of www.wine.com. The successful story of the online jewelry store, www.BlueNile.com, can also be attributed to the extensive use of standardized product features that allows unbiased evaluation of the once considered experience goods by Nelson [35] and Girard et al. [21]. For example, a diamond can be described by features such as shape, carat, cut, color, clarity, polish, and symmetry. The availability of grading reports and documentation, such as GIA diamond grading report and Blue Nile appraisal, also enhance the objectivity and accuracy of the product evaluation, and thus can reduce the performance risk of the product.

## 4. Survey results to validate the proposed new classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme

The empirical studies by Girard et, al. [21] and Bei et al. [3] focused on the understanding of consumer online search behavior differences across different product categories have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed consumer purchase intention as an effective proxy of <sup>fi</sup>nal purchase decision for measuring the goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t between product category and e-market. The study of product–Internet marketing channel <sup>fi</sup>tness by Phau and Poon [42], consumer purchase preferences/intentions/behaviors differences across different product categories studies by [6,20,48], and the studies of effects of different product categories on the relationships between purchase decision and other factors by [20,23,33] have all fully/partially addressed the importance of consumer perceived risk and purchase intention. There is a negative correlation between the two indicators: the higher perceived risk, the lower purchase intention and vice versa. Since the proposed classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is grounded on the theory of the components of perceived risk in product purchase, we decided to use consumer perceived risk and purchase intention as two indicators for measuring the effectiveness of the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

A survey questionnaire was designed to evaluate the differences in consumer online purchase intention and perceived risk among the eight product groups. We assume that if there is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in consumer purchase intention and perceived risk among the products along the three dimensions, the new classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is an effective way to classify products marketed on the Internet. According to the 2008 China Internet Purchasing Situation Survey Report from CNNIC [8], the age group of majority online consumers in China ranges from 18 to 35 years old. Thus, the typical university student's pro<sup>fi</sup>le <sup>fi</sup>ts well and can be a good representative of online consumers. In this study, we surveyed junior and senior undergraduate students, graduate students, and part time MBA students with prior online shopping experience from a major university in China. The online shopping experience includes search for product information online and actually make purchase online. Convenience sampling was employed and the majority of the survey was conducted in a classroom setting. A total of 200 valid responses were collected, and 114 were <sup>fi</sup>lled out by undergraduate students, 68 by graduate students, and 18 by part-time MBA students. Appendix B shows the Internet experience and demographic information of the subjects.

## 5. Conclusion and discussion

A comprehensive review of literature in the areas of e-market and product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the objective of identifying and understanding product key characteristics that impact consumers' online purchasing behavior was performed. A new online product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is then proposed that integrates both information-oriented and transactionoriented approaches. Grounded on the theory of the components of perceived risk in product purchase, the new classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is expected to offer a holistic view of product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation framework that can be applied in both information acquisition and transaction processing stages. It can help in understanding the possible interaction and complementing effect between the two stages of the processes.

To validate the new classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, a survey was conducted to understand the differences in consumer purchase intention and perceived risk among the proposed eight product groups along the three classi<sup>fi</sup>cation dimensions. The proposed online product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation can contribute in various e-marketing related studies including: (1) determining product <sup>fi</sup>tness for Internet marketing, (2) understanding of consumer online search and purchase behavior, (3) understanding the impact of different product characteristics on online purchase decision, and (4) understanding the factors that would convert information seekers to purchasers. Different marketing strategies should be devised for different product categories. Based on the new classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, organizations can develop marketing strategies to convert an Internet unfriendly product to an Internet friendly one by enhancing on the three dimensions.

Numerous successful cases can be found in converting a traditionally product or service into a digital one such as from paper ticket vs. e-ticket for airlines and movies, mail-in of tax returns vs. e-<sup>fi</sup>ling, and CD and DVD rental and purchase vs. music downloads. The conversion will substantially reduce a consumer's perceived time/convenience loss risk as a result of instantaneous delivery. It might also lower the consumer's perceived <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk and cost because of no or lowered delivery expense. For physical products that cannot be converted to digital products, the seller can improve the Internet friendliness of the product through either brand name recognition or standardizing product features to allow objective evaluation of the product quality and as a result will reduce consumer perceived performance risk.

Due to limitation of the sample set collected, including sampling method, sample demographics, and sample size, a more detailed analysis of differences among the eight product categories would be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to validate. In order to empirically prove the validity of the new classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and further test the model, more data should be collected from other countries as well as from consumers with different demographic backgrounds. With additional sampling a cross-culture comparison can be performed to understand the consumer purchasing behavior for different products using the integrated product classi<sup>fi</sup>cation method.
