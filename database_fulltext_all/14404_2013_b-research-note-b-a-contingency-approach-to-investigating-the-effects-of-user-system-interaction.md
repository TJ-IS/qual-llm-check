# Research Note

# A Contingency Approach to Investigating the Effects of User-System Interaction Modes of Online Decision Aids

Weiquan Wang Department of Information Systems, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon, Hong Kong SAR, China, weiquan@cityu.edu.hk

Izak Benbasat

Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, BC V6T 1Z2, Canada, benbasat@sauder.ubc.ca

nteractive online decision aids often employ user-decision aid dialogues as forms of user-system interaction to Ihelp construct and elicit users’ attribute preferences about a product type. This study extends prior research on online decision aids by investigating the effects of a decision aid’s user-system interaction mode (USIM), which can be either user-guided or system-controlled, on users’ effort-related (number of iterations of using the aid and perceived cognitive effort expended in using it) and quality-related (perceived quality of the aid and acceptance of the product advice it provides) assessments. A contingency approach with two moderating factors is employed. One factor is the decision strategy (additive-compensatory or elimination) employed by the aid, and the other is the users’ product knowledge (high or low).

A laboratory experiment was conducted to compare online decision aids with different USIMs. Although the results largely confirm that users assess the user-guided USIM more positively than the system-controlled USIM, the effects of USIM are stronger in two settings: for the elimination-based aid than for the additivecompensatory-based aid and for users with low product knowledge than for those with high product knowledge, especially in terms of effort assessments. This research advances the theoretical understanding of the effects of interaction between two critical components of online decision aids (USIMs and decision strategies) and the moderating role of user characteristics (product knowledge) in affecting users’ evaluations. It also pro vides practitioners with design advice for developing these aids.

Key words: online decision aid; decision strategy; user-system interaction mode; product knowledge; cognitive effort; system quality; system restrictiveness

## 1. Introduction

Interactive online decision aids are Web-based decision support systems (DSSs) that help construct and elicit users’ attribute preferences about a product type and then follow a decision strategy in processing the elicited preferences to generate product advice for users (Kohler et al. 2011, Leavitt 2006, Wang and Benbasat 2009).<sup>1</sup> Extant literature confirms that a decision aid can benefit users by saving them cognitive effort and improving their decision quality (Häubl and Murray 2006, Häubl and Trifts 2000). These benefits in turn increase users’ intentions to shop online and revisit the website that provides the aid. However, effective design guidelines for online decision aids are still lacking, and many users have negative experiences with current decision aid applications (Wang and Benbasat 2009).

User characteristics play an important role in users’ evaluations of online decision aids. Online aids differ from traditional offline DSSs, which primarily support the decision making of a smaller number of, and presumably more homogeneous, professional and managerial users in a specific application domain (Straub and Watson 2001, Su et al. 2008). Compared with offline environments, the Web environment has a more diverse community of users, and decision support for such an environment is applied to a broader range of problem domains. Users of online decision aids include, for example, customers over the Internet, managers over the intranet, and suppliers over the extranet for various decision domains (Shim et al. 2002). These users come from diverse backgrounds and represent a highly heterogeneous population. Some use an aid to learn more about products and construct their preferences, whereas others use aids to integrate their preferences and limit their consideration set of product alternatives (Häubl and Trifts 2000). In particular, these users have different levels of product knowledge that affect their abilities to assess the product advice provided by a decision aid and the amount of assistance they need in the construction, elicitation, and processing of their preferences (Su et al. 2008). Thus, the first objective of this study is to investigate the impact of users’ product knowledge on their evaluation and use of online decision aids.

The restrictiveness of an online decision aid, which refers to the extent that the aid limits users’ choices and decision-making processes, is an understudied area that may be a cause of negative user experiences with decision aids (Silver 1988, Wang and Benbasat 2009). Extant research on the restrictiveness of online decision aids primarily focuses on decision strategies that the aids utilize in making product advice (e.g., Wang and Benbasat 2009); meanwhile, the interrelationships among different components of the aids are ignored. Online decision aids are mainly intended to (1) facilitate users’ construction and expression of attribute preferences via user-system interactions and (2) then integrate these preferences through the application of certain decision strategies to generate product advice (Häubl and Trifts 2000, Wang and Benbasat 2009). Accordingly, these aids can restrict users via two main components: (i) user-system interaction mode (USIM) and (ii) decision strategy. These two components are interrelated. On the one hand, a decision aid cannot generate high-quality product advice, regardless of the decision strategy used, if the desired and accurate preferences of the user (i.e., input to the decision strategy) are not faithfully elicited through user-system interactions. On the other hand, if users’ preferences are not fully or appropriately processed with the decision strategy utilized by a decision aid, users may have to use the aid iteratively to adjust their preferences through user-system interactions until they receive satisfactory product advice. Thus, the USIM and the decision strategy of a decision aid work together to influence consumers’ use and evaluation of the aid.

In light of the interrelations between the two components of online decision aids, the second research objective is to extend prior research on online decision aids by investigating the effects of USIMs with consideration for the decision strategies employed by the aids. Research on online decision aids has moved considerably toward a user-system interaction perspective (Reisen and Hoffrage 2010, Schiaffino and Amandi 2006). This advancement is partly due to the emphasis on the constructive nature of user preferences (Bettman et al. 1998, Payne et al. 1992, Slovic 1995), which indicates that most users do not have well-established preferences before their decision making but gradually form their preferences through interaction with decision aids. As such, decision aids often need to elicit and update users’ preferences through iterative user-decision aid interactions (Fasolo et al. 2005, Wang and Benbasat 2009).

An examination of extant applications of online decision aids in commercial websites shows that practitioners do not have clear guidelines on designing a suitable USIM for these aids. There are two USIM options for user-decision aid dialogues that a decision aid generally employs to elicit users’ attribute preferences (Armentano et al. 2006, Nakatsu and Benbasat 2006). One is user-guided, in which users decide on the product attributes relevant to them and the order in which they would like to key in, or adjust, their preferences for the attributes. The other is systemcontrolled, whereby the aid drives the interaction with its users by determining the specific preferences and the order in which they are elicited.

Practitioners may want to elicit fully users’ preference information by controlling the user-system interaction to understand users’ needs better. Under a system-controlled USIM, users are exposed to all preference elicitation questions one attribute at a time in a preset order. They are asked to disclose all their preference information by answering the questions posed by an aid. As more preference information is elicited from consumers, online vendors can understand consumers’ product needs better and subsequently, provide better services and more effective promotions by marketing products and services to suitable consumers (Hui et al. 2006).

In contrast, from the users’ point of view, a userguided USIM is more flexible; users may thus prefer this mode over the system-controlled one. Under a user-guided USIM, users dominate and control usersystem interaction. They only need to answer questions for product attributes they are interested in and can do so in any attribute order as they wish. However, is this preference for a particular USIM the same for all types of users (e.g., for users with different levels of product knowledge)? In addition, does this preference hold true for all types of decision aids using different decision strategies? This research intends to theorize and empirically test the effects of a USIM by using a contingency approach that considers users’ characteristics (product knowledge) and decision strategies used by the aids. In the absence of a proper fit between a USIM and a decision strategy, a decision aid may inadvertently restrict users’ decision making, thereby leading to an unsatisfactory user decision experience and to the failure of the online store in achieving returns on its investment in the aid (Wang and Benbasat 2009).

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the theoretical background and develops the hypotheses. Section 3 presents the research methodology. Section 4 reports the findings. Section 5 provides a discussion of the results, limitations, implications of the study, and the conclusion.

## 2. Background and Hypothesis Development

## 2.1. Background: System Restrictiveness, Decision Strategy, and USIM

System restrictiveness, first proposed as a key design consideration in the offline DSS context (Silver 1988), was later extended beyond the domain of DSS (Silver 2006). Researchers have recommended that contingent effects of restrictiveness across individual and task differences should be investigated (Davern and Kamis 2010, Silver 2006). Silver (2006) suggested that greater restrictiveness may be desirable when organizations require standard decision processes to facilitate communication and consistency; however, when different decision processes are desirable across decision makers and tasks, a less restrictive DSS is more appropriate.

In the context of an online decision aid, system restrictiveness plays a central role in designing the aid because it represents the potential cost to users who rely on the aid for their online decision making (Wang and Benbasat 2009). Online decision aids are for users with heterogeneous backgrounds who delegate some decision processes to the aids. However, some decision aids may provide decision-making support capabilities not necessarily desired by users with preferences for different decision processes. Thus, such reliance may lead to dysfunctional outcomes by overly restricting users to a limited set of capabilities, as seen in the case of using an elimination strategy to process users’ attribute preferences while ignoring the comparative importance of these preferences (Wang and Benbasat 2009). Developers may inadvertently provide designs that cause such restrictions, leading to dissatisfied users (Edwards et al. 2002).

Wang and Benbasat (2009) conducted the first empirical study that investigated the strategy restrictiveness of online decision aids as a specific form of system restrictiveness. They tested the effects of perceived strategy restrictiveness on users’ perceptions and acceptance of a decision aid and investigated strategy alternatives that affect users’ perceived strategy restrictiveness. They found that in line with the reactance theory (Brehm and Brehm 1981), when a user is restricted to the decision processes supported by a decision aid and the desired support functionalities are not provided (i.e., greater strategy restrictiveness), the user’s psychological reactance will be triggered. Such reactance leads to behavioral avoidance and reduces the user’s intentions to use that aid.

A decision aid needs to elicit users’ preferences during user-system interactions before a decision strategy can be used to process these preferences. Thus, a decision aid can also restrict users’ construction and expression of attribute preferences (Nakatsu and Benbasat 2006). This is referred to as interaction restrictiveness, which is affected by the USIM of a decision aid. A decision aid that allows users to express and adjust their preferences with flexibility and ease imposes less interaction restrictiveness.

This study examines two key design components of an online decision aid, the first of which is related to interaction restrictiveness and the second to strategy restrictiveness. The first component is the USIM utilized by the aid through which user preferences are elicited. The second is the decision strategy that the aid employs to process elicited preferences and to generate product advice for its users. The two decision strategies and the two USIMs investigated in this study are briefly described below.

2.1.1. Decision Strategies: Additive-Compensatory (AC) and Elimination. This study focuses on two most commonly studied decision strategies for online decision aids, namely, AC and elimination (Wang and Benbasat 2009). An AC strategy evaluates each product alternative based on all its relevant attributes. Each attribute is assigned an importance weight by the user. The AC-based aid generates a score for each alternative by adding up the products of each transformed (e.g., normalized) value and attribute weight. Users are provided with a list of recommended alternatives according to their weighted total scores upon completion of computations for these alternatives. The AC strategy considers the importance of each user attribute preference and makes tradeoffs among these preferences to generate product advice.

With an elimination strategy, each alternative is evaluated along with its various attributes, and any alternative that violates the value threshold specified by the user for an attribute is eliminated. Unlike the AC, the elimination strategy does not fully process users’ preferences; that is, lower-valued attributes are not compensated by higher-valued ones (Wang and

Benbasat 2009). Many product alternatives with satisfactory quality risk being prematurely eliminated by an elimination-based aid.

In summary, the key difference between the ACbased aid and the elimination-based aid is whether the aid makes tradeoffs among user attribute preferences by considering their importance levels, or it simply eliminates product alternatives when the specified threshold values of attributes are violated (Figures A and B in online Appendix A, available at http://dx.doi.org/10.1287/isre.1120.0445, provide screenshots of the interfaces of the AC- and elimination-based aids used in this study). Users generally evaluate an AC-based aid more positively than an elimination-based one because of this difference (Fasolo et al. 2005, Song et al. 2007, Wang and Benbasat 2009). A more detailed comparison between these two types of aids can be found in Wang and Benbasat (2009).<sup>2</sup>

2.1.2. USIMs: System Controlled and User Guided. Consumers’ attribute preferences are dynamic and are constructed during their interaction with a decision aid through feedback and product learning (Amir and Levav 2008, Bettman et al. 1998). A widely applied approach to facilitating construction and elicitation of user preferences is the use of user-decision aid dialogues in which users answer questions posed by a decision aid to indicate their attribute preferences about a product type (Reisen and Hoffrage 2010).

The two commonly employed USIMs are system controlled and user guided (Armentano et al. 2006, Nakatsu and Benbasat 2006). The difference between these two is in how they facilitate user preference construction and elicitation. With a user-guided USIM, users decide the types and the order of the attribute preferences they wish to indicate. They can choose any attribute to express their preferences and adjust their previously specified attribute preference values at any time while interacting with the aid.

In contrast, a system-controlled USIM dominates the interaction with its users by determining the types of attribute preferences and the order in which they are elicited. When a user-decision aid dialogue is initiated, users need to answer all preference questions presented by the aid in a predetermined order.

If users want to alter a previous preference, the dialogue needs to be completed and product advice generated. They need to go through the entire dialogue again, one attribute at a time in the prespecified order, and they can either indicate their new preference value for each attribute or leave it unaltered.

## 2.2. Dependent Outcomes

In line with the behavioral decision literature on individuals’ choice behavior with DSSs use, recent studies on online decision aids focus on users’ evaluations related to system quality and cognitive effort (Xiao and Benbasat 2007). Users’ acceptance and use of a decision aid are mainly driven by these evaluations. The current study accordingly focuses on these two criteria. Moreover, this study employs both self-reported perceptual measures and objective indicators based on actual use of an aid. Discrepancies between these two types of measures have been widely reported in literature on system usage and evaluations (Straub et al. 1995) as well as in other disciplines (Oswald and Wu 2010). The use of both perceptual and objective measures can provide a robust assessment of user evaluations of decision aids. The quality factor is represented by two variables: perceived quality of a decision aid (subjective) and acceptance of product advice provided (objective). The effort factor is similarly represented by two variables: perceived cognitive effort (subjective) and number of iterations users go through when using a decision aid (objective). Figure 1 shows the research model with these dependent variables.

Perceived quality of an online decision aid is defined as the extent to which the user perceives that the aid performs well the tasks delegated to it (McKnight et al. 2002). An online decision aid is provided by an Internet vendor to facilitate users’ decision making. Thus, its quality and technical performance are demonstrated by its capabilities to elicit and integrate user preferences effectively when generating product advice for users (Wang and Benbasat 2005, 2007, 2008).

Users’ acceptance of product advice is an objective indicator of the advice quality (Bonaccio and Dalal 2006). In online environments, the use of a decision aid is voluntary; users do not have to follow the product advice provided. If users consider the quality of the product advice unsatisfactory, they can find products using other search tools and make their choices independently. In contrast, when users perceive the product advice from an aid to be of high quality, they are more likely to follow it. Moreover, because online decision aids deal with preferential choice problems, no universally correct choice exists; the most appropriate choice depends on one’s particular preferences. Thus, users’ acceptance of a decision aid’s product advice is an appropriate, objective indicator of the advice quality.

Perceived cognitive effort influences users’ choice behavior and decision aid use because a major goal of using an aid is to minimize their effort (e.g., Todd and Benbasat 1992). This study uses an objective indicator: the number of iterations that users go through when using a decision aid. Although an aid automates the processing of user preferences to generate product advice, users need to construct and indicate their preferences to the aid. During the preference construction and expression process, users perform several iterations to refine their input to the aid. The number of iterations is a proper objective indicator of users’ effort in using an aid, given that a single iteration involves user effort in many activities such as providing input to the aid and examining recommended products (Fasolo et al. 2005).

## 2.3. Effects of the USIM

The key difference between user-guided and systemcontrolled USIMs lies in users’ flexibility in expressing and adjusting their attribute preferences. A less restrictive user-guided USIM empowers users to control their interactions with the aid. In contrast, a more restrictive system-controlled USIM does not allow user freedom to choose which attribute preferences to indicate and in what order. Unless a systemcontrolled USIM can perfectly predict what attributes users need to express and adjust and when to do so, it remains more restrictive than a user-guided USIM.

The difficulties of perfectly predicting users’ needs for preference expression and adjustment are due to the constructive formation of users’ attribute preferences (Bettman et al. 1998). Many users do not have well-established prior preferences. Moreover, for a preferential choice problem, different attributes matter to different users. For example, some users are more concerned with price and less with product features, whereas others are willing to pay a higher premium for certain product features. For a multi-attribute, multi-alternative decision problem, it is challenging for a decision aid to determine a priori what attributes are relevant for a certain user’s current needs and then use a prespecified sequence of procedures to elicit the user’s preferences (Nakatsu and Benbasat 2006). Therefore, users will generally prefer a user-guided USIM over a system-controlled one.

In terms of users’ effort in using a decision aid, a user-guided USIM will significantly reduce the number of iterations and reduce their effort because this mode allows users to express and adjust their preferences in a flexible manner. Users can choose any product attribute to indicate and can adjust their preferences at any time. They can go back and forth between different preference questions in the user- decision aid dialogues without additional iterations. For example, they can leave an attribute preference unspecified or indicate an initial choice, choose the preference for another attribute, and then return to the previous attribute to indicate their choice or adjust their initial choice. In contrast, the system-controlled USIM presents questions in a predetermined order for users to indicate their preferences. If they want to alter any of their preferences indicated previously, they have to go through additional iterations to locate their previous answer(s) and then make the changes, leading to higher cognitive effort. Thus, we posit the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1A).</sup> Users of a decision aid with a user-guided USIM will go through fewer iterations than users of a decision aid with a system-controlled USIM.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1B).</sup> The perceived cognitive effort of users of a decision aid with a user-guided USIM will be lower than that for a decision aid with a system-controlled USIM.

The quality of the product advice provided by a decision aid is influenced by users’ preferences elicited as input to the aid. When these preferences are reliably constructed and fully elicited, the product advice can better match user needs; thus, the aid will be perceived to be of higher quality. With the aim of receiving higher quality product advice, users need to go iteratively through user-decision aid dialogues to express and adjust their preferences (Song et al. 2007, Wang and Benbasat 2009). A user-guided USIM makes it easier for users to achieve this aim. In contrast, under a system-controlled USIM, users may receive product advice of unsatisfactory quality because of the aid’s inflexibility in indicating and adjusting their preferences, leading to lower perceived system quality and lower likelihood of advice acceptance. Thus, we contend that users will perceive a decision aid with a user-guided USIM to be of higher quality, and will more likely accept its product advice, compared with an aid with a system-controlled USIM.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> Users will be more likely to accept product advice provided by a decision aid with a user-guided USIM than that provided by a decision aid with a system-controlled USIM.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> The perceived system quality of a decision aid with a user-guided USIM will be higher than that of a decision aid with a system-controlled USIM.

## 2.4. USIMs for Decision Aids with Different Decision Strategies

Although we posit that user-guided USIMs have advantages over system-controlled USIMs, such advantages will be salient only when users need to go iteratively through user-decision aid dialogues several times to express and adjust their preferences. Without the need for such iterations, the specific USIM may not be so important. Herein, how a decision aid’s decision strategy influences the effects of the USIM of the aid is considered.

Previous studies have confirmed that users of an elimination-based aid generally undergo many iterations to adjust and express their attribute preferences because of the aid’s inability to consider the importance levels of users’ preferences and make tradeoffs among these preferences (Song et al. 2007, Wang and Benbasat 2009). In contrast, an AC-based aid elicits users’ preferences and importance levels of these preferences and processes such preference information in a comprehensive manner. The users’ intended preference requirements are conveyed to the AC-based aid with relative ease, and users do not need to go through the user-decision aid dialogues many times. Therefore, the effects of the USIM will differ significantly between an AC-based aid and an eliminationbased aid.

Elimination-based aid users need to express and adjust their preferences many times. The user-guided USIM addresses these needs by allowing users to express and adjust their preferences flexibly. Thus, this mode will significantly reduce the number of iterations users go through and accordingly their perceived cognitive effort. In contrast, for users of an AC-based aid, the USIM will not be so impactful because of users’ limited need for adjusting preferences many times. Therefore, we posit the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3A).</sup> The effects of the USIM of a decision aid on the number of iterations users go through will be greater for an elimination-based aid than for an AC-based aid.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3B).</sup> The effects of the USIM of a decision aid on users’ perceived cognitive effort will be greater for an elimination-based aid than for an AC-based aid.

Likewise, the user-guided USIM and the systemcontrolled USIM are compared in terms of perceived system quality and advice acceptance for the elimination- and AC-based aids. To receive more recommended products of improved quality, users of the elimination-based aid (compared with those of the AC-based aid) have a higher tendency to go iteratively through user-decision aid dialogues to express and adjust their preferences (Song et al. 2007, Wang and Benbasat 2009). A user-guided USIM will be particularly helpful for them to do so flexibly. As such, users will perceive the elimination-based aid with a user-guided USIM to be of higher quality and will be more likely to accept its product advice, compared with the one with a system-controlled USIM.

In contrast, an AC-based aid generally produces high-quality product advice by processing users’ preferences in a comprehensive manner. To save effort, users normally do not want to improve advice quality further by iteratively going through user-decision aid dialogues many times (Wang and Benbasat 2009). Therefore, the effects of the USIM for the AC-based aid will be limited in terms of users’ perceived quality of the aid and product advice acceptance.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4A).</sup> The effects of the USIM of a decision aid on the acceptance of product advice will be greater for an elimination-based aid than for an AC-based aid.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4B).</sup> The effects of the USIM of a decision aid on the perceived system quality will be greater for an elimination-based aid than for an AC-based aid.

## 2.5. USIMs for Users with Different Levels of Product Knowledge

The prevailing perspective on preference expression and elicitation in the field of behavioral decision research is that consumers’ preferences are gradually constructed during the decision process and preference construction is affected by many contextual factors (Bettman et al. 1998, Slovic 1995). In particular, when faced with a choice problem, consumers’ product knowledge influences their ability to construct their preferences (Payne et al. 1999, Su et al. 2008). Online decision aids are provided for users with different levels of product knowledge (Häubl and Murray 2006); hence, users’ product knowledge must be considered when designing effective online decision aids.

Consumers with high product knowledge have a better understanding of “facts” about a product (e.g., product attributes) and have firm attribute preferences for the focal decision task (Alba and Hutchinson 1987). Thus, they have an easier time constructing preferences than do low knowledge users (Su et al. 2008). Moreover, they have a more detailed and specific knowledge base regarding a product type. Using this knowledge base, they find it easier to convey their specific preferences to a decision aid; in contrast, low knowledge consumers often have “ill-defined” preferences and spend more time establishing their preferences by learning about the product type (Amir and Levav 2008, Mackay and Elam 1992).

We first examine how the effects of USIM on user effort differ across users with different levels of product knowledge. Users assess and learn subjective attribute values, adjust their preferences, and then eventually arrive at relatively stable preferences when interacting with a decision aid (Huber et al. 2002). Low product knowledge users have relatively fewer firm preferences compared with high knowledge users. Thus, they have a stronger need to adjust their preferences and learn from the product advice provided. They can gain insights about the product type by trying a decision aid (Mackay and Elam 1992) and then gradually form their preferences. A user-guided USIM will be particularly useful for low knowledge users because it allows them to try the aid by setting and adjusting their preferences flexibly, without going through the user-decision aid dialogues many times. Therefore, a user-guided USIM, compared with a system-controlled USIM, will significantly save the effort of low product knowledge users and reduce the number of iterations they need to go through.

For high product knowledge users, a USIM will not be so influential in terms of user effort for two reasons. First, high knowledge users have relatively firm preferences and can indicate their preferences to a decision aid without the need to adjust these preferences many times. As a result, a USIM will not significantly affect the number of iterations of these users. A dominating factor determining perceived cognitive effort of users is the number of iterations users need to go through (Fasolo et al. 2005); thus, the effects of a USIM on the perceived cognitive effort of high knowledge users will be limited.

Second, high product knowledge users have more sophisticated preferences compared with low knowledge users. They generally recognize and care about many product attributes, even though some attributes are more or less important than others (Alba and Hutchinson 1987). For less important attributes, a high product knowledge user can indicate a low importance level to the aid to make a better decision, assuming that for a reasonably designed aid, the majority of its preference questions would not be irrelevant to the user. High knowledge users tend to reply to most of the preference questions raised by an aid, regardless of whether the aid employs a user-guided or system-controlled USIM. As such, the particular USIM will not be so impactful for users with high product knowledge in terms of saving effort.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5A).</sup> The effects of the USIM of a decision aid on the number of iterations that users go through will be greater for low product knowledge users than for high product knowledge users.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5B).</sup> The effects of the USIM of a decision aid on perceived cognitive effort will be greater for low product knowledge users than for high product knowledge users.

In terms of quality-related evaluations, we do not posit that the effects of the USIM will differ across users with different levels of product knowledge. The USIM will affect the quality evaluations of both low and high product knowledge users. This argument is backed by two observations. First, low knowledge users have to go iteratively through user-decision aid dialogues many times. In this process, they can learn from the product advice provided by a decision aid and gradually adjust their preferences. The userguided USIM can address this need and help these users establish and express their preferences flexibly through product learning. Thus, low knowledge users will perceive the quality of a user-guided aid to be higher than that of a system-controlled aid and will be more likely to accept its product advice.

Second, high product knowledge users possess more expertise to scrutinize a decision aid’s product advice. If they receive product advice generated from their preferences that are not fully or accurately elicited, instead of assessing the product advice for product learning purposes, they will perceive the advice quality to be low and will be less likely to accept the product advice. Chances of receiving low-quality product advice from a system-controlled aid are high because of the inflexibility in indicating and adjusting user preferences in user-decision aid dialogues. Thus, for high knowledge users, a user-guided USIM will also lead to higher perceived quality of the decision aid and higher product advice acceptance.

## 3. Research Methodology

The present study used a 2 × 2 between-subjects experimental design. Both USIM (user-guided versus system-controlled) and decision strategy (AC versus elimination) were manipulated, whereas participants’ product knowledge was measured but not manipulated.

3.1. Experimental Decision Aids and Treatments The experimental decision aid employed by Wang and Benbasat (2009) was upgraded by further manipulating design features of interest to the present study (e.g., different USIMs). The aid was validated with the help of several digital camera experts, whose comments were incorporated in upgrading the aid. The decision aid was used to help participants choose digital cameras.

3.1.1. Decision Strategy. This study examined two decision strategies, namely, AC and elimination. Interface screenshots of AC-based and eliminationbased aids used in the experiment are provided in online Appendix A. The key difference between the interfaces of these two aids from the user’s point of view is that as the user answers each question from an aid, the AC-based aid asks the user to indicate the importance of the criterion, whereas the eliminationbased aid does not.

To assist users in understanding the inner workings of a decision aid, prior studies suggested that explanations about the aid should be provided (Wang and Benbasat 2007). The effect mechanisms of explanations have been examined in previous studies, such as those by Wang and Benbasat (2007, 2009). The explanations are helpful for users to use the features of an aid and evaluate it properly. In our experiment, the aid provided a set of explanations to clarify its line of reasoning, justify the purposes of the questions it posed to the users, and help the users choose between and use different aid features.

3.1.2. USIM. This research examined two commonly employed USIMs: system controlled and user guided. These two modes differ in users’ flexibility to express and adjust their preferences in userdecision aid dialogues. In the experimental aid, the user-decision aid dialogue includes 11 needs-based questions corresponding to 11 product attributes (e.g., price, brand, and resolution), and these questions are categorized into four groups (i.e., the four tabs shown in the figures in Appendix A: “Budget/Brand,” “Main Features,” “Preferences,” and “Extras”). For most of the attributes, users were provided with three options (levels) to indicate their preference. After the aid elicited users’ preferences in the dialogues, most users received several pages of recommended products, with each page containing five products.

Under a user-guided USIM, the user can click any tab and then choose any of the preference questions under that tab to start the dialogue. To express his or her preferences or adjust them, the user can go back and forth between different tabs and between different preference questions under any tab in the userdecision aid dialogue without undergoing additional iterations.

Under a system-controlled USIM, the four tabs in the user-decision aid dialogue are presented to the user from left to right, one by one. Likewise, under each tab, preference questions are presented to the user from left to right, one by one. The user has to answer the current question before the next one is presented. When the user finishes answering all of the questions located under one tab, preference questions under the next tab will be shown. When the last question (i.e., the rightmost question under the rightmost tab) is answered, the product advice is then provided. Upon receiving the product advice, if the user wants to change his or her preferences, he or she will have to go through the entire dialogue again to locate which answer(s) to modify.

## 3.2. Participants

The participants were 104 undergraduate and graduate students, composed of 35 males and 69 females,<sup>3</sup> from a large North American university. Twenty-six participants were assigned to each experimental condition. The average participant age was 22.1 years, which is consistent with the average Internet user demographic (Johnson 2005). The participants were randomly assigned to one of the treatment groups. Prior to the experimental tasks, they were surveyed regarding their comfort level with the Internet and online shopping. No significant differences were found among the participants assigned to different experimental groups. Furthermore, no significant differences were found among different groups of participants with respect to age, gender, Internet usage, or Internet experience.

## 3.3. Experimental Tasks and Procedures

For consistency, we used the same two experimental tasks employed by Wang and Benbasat (2009). One task was to choose a digital camera as a wedding gift for a good friend who enjoys photography, with the cost of the gift to be shared with four other friends.

The other task was to select a digital camera for a close family member who is an amateur photographer. The participants were informed that they could make as many assumptions as they wished.

Participation was voluntary. Each participant was given \$15 for taking part in the experiment. To motivate the participants into viewing the experiment as a serious online shopping session and to increase their involvement, as in Wang and Benbasat (2009), the participants were informed before the experimental tasks that 25% of them would receive an additional reward ranging from \$5 to \$100 based on their performance. Prior to the experiment, the participants were also informed that they would be asked to provide justifications for their product choices and that their performance would be judged based on how convincing their justifications were in supporting their choices.<sup>4</sup>

The experimental procedures began upon the participants’ arrival when they were asked to complete a questionnaire on their demographic background. Next, a research assistant trained the participants on using and navigating the assigned Web interface via recorded videos that showed a tutorial decision aid, which had the same features as the experimental aid. Each participant was then asked to finish the two tasks, one by one. The order of the tasks was systematically reversed from one participant to another. After each task, the participants were directed to an online form where they entered their product choice and stated their justification for the choice made. The tasks had no time limits. The participants were required to use the decision aid first but were allowed to use a simple search query (search by brand name) available on the home page of the experimental website if they were not satisfied with the aid. Finally, after completing the two experimental tasks, the participants were asked to answer a questionnaire that included measures of the dependent variables.

## 3.4. Measurement

Four dependent variables were measured in this study. Two of them, perceived cognitive effort and perceived quality of a decision aid, are perceptual constructs. Measures for perceived cognitive effort were taken from Wang and Benbasat (2009). Measures for perceived quality of a decision aid were adapted from the measurement scale of perceived website quality by McKnight et al. (2002) as well as that of perceived competence of a decision aid by Wang and Benbasat (2007). Measurement items for these two variables were rated on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The measurement items are provided in online Appendix B.

The other two dependent variables, advice acceptance and number of iterations, are objective indicators. Advice acceptance was measured by checking whether or not the participants followed the product advice provided by the decision aids (Wang and Benbasat 2009), and their choices were recorded in the experimental system. Advice acceptance was coded using a dummy variable. After answering the preference questions posed in the user-decision aid dialogues, the participants received product advice. They could freely adjust their preferences to receive updated product advice. Finally, the participants had two choices: they could follow the aid’s advice and choose one of the recommended products (then the advice acceptance was coded as 1), or they could select a product on their own using the search query available on the homepage of the experimental website (then the advice acceptance was coded as 0).

The number of iterations was measured by coding the screen videos taken unobtrusively during the experiment through the use of screen capture software (Wang and Benbasat 2009). The iteration score was increased by one when a participant used the decision aid to go through the user-decision aid dialogue page, answered the questions, obtained product advice, and then made a decision. The iteration score was also increased by one whenever a participant returned to the user-decision aid dialogue page after viewing the listed product advice, changed one or more of his/her attribute preferences, and re-obtained product advice before making a final decision.

For the two objective measures, each participant had two observations for each variable because all participants conducted two experimental tasks. To minimize potential concerns on the lack of independence of observations from the two experimental tasks, we used the average scores from the two tasks in our data analyses. We conducted t-tests to compare the two observations from the two tasks in terms of these two objective measures, but no significant differences were found. Thus, learning effects due to the use of the two tasks were not detected.

In addition to the four dependent variables, the participants’ domain knowledge was measured. The scale developed by Flynn and Goldsmith (1999) was used to measure the participants’ subjective knowledge about digital cameras. We created two levels of product knowledge (high and low) using a median split of the self-reported knowledge scores of the participants. For high knowledge participants, the product knowledge scores on a 7-point scale ranged from 4.0 to

7.0, whereas for the low knowledge participants, the scores ranged from 1.0 to 3.8. Mean scores of the high and low knowledge participants were 4.54 and 2.70, respectively. A t-test showed that these two groups significantly differed in terms of self-reported product knowledge level $( t = 1 4 . 7 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ .

We developed measurement scales for perceived strategy restrictiveness and perceived interaction restrictiveness by following the approach developed by Moore and Benbasat (1991). These two constructs were applied to check the manipulations of decision strategies and USIMs in the experiment.

## 4.1. Measurement Characteristics

The descriptive data of the dependent variables are presented in online Appendix C. For the two perceptual variables, perceived cognitive effort and perceived system quality, both reliability indicators measured by Cronbach’s  were above the cited minimum value of 0.7 (Nunnally and Bernstein 1994). The discriminant validity of these two constructs was assessed through an exploratory principal components factor analysis with direct oblimin rotation. All items had loadings above the commonly specified minimum value of 0.7 on the intended construct and did not have crossloadings above 0.4 on the unintended constructs (see results in Table C3 in online Appendix $C ;$ Hair et al. 1998). Therefore, the validity of the measures for the two perceptual constructs was satisfactory.

## 4.2. Manipulation Checks

The design alternatives investigated in this study were expected to differ in users’ perceived strategy restrictiveness and perceived interaction restrictiveness. As expected, in terms of strategy restrictiveness, the AC-based aid was perceived to be significantly less restrictive than the elimination-based aid: AC-based aid $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 4 . 4 4 , \mathrm { S D } = 1 . 0 1 )$ , elimination-based aid $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 5 . 2 8 , \mathrm { S D } = 0 . 9 3 ) , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ . In terms of interaction restrictiveness, the user-guided aid was perceived to be significantly less restrictive than the systemcontrolled aid: user-guided aid (mean = 2039, SD = 1025), system-controlled aid (mean = 4060, SD = 1028), $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ . These results provided strong evidence that the manipulations were successful.

## 4.3. Hypothesis Testing Results

A multiple analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted as the starting point of hypothesis testing to check whether the experimental treatments had an overall impact on the four dependent variables (Hair et al. 1998). The main effects of the USIM in the MANOVA were significant $( F = 4 . 6 2 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . The interaction effects between the USIM and the decision strategy were also significant $( F = 2 . 4 7 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$

whereas those between the USIM and users’ product knowledge were marginally significant $( F = 1 . 9 9 ,$ $p < 0 . 1 )$ . The marginally significant interaction effects are acceptable, given that we only posited the interaction effects between the USIM and users’ product knowledge on the two effort variables but not on the two quality variables. Next, a set of follow-up analysis of variance (ANOVA) performed on each individual dependent variable was run to test the hypotheses (see results reported in Table 1).

In terms of the main effects of the USIM, the participants using the user-guided aid underwent fewer iterations than those using the system-controlled aid $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and their perceived cognitive effort in using the user-guided aid was lower than that in using the system-controlled aid $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Thus, both H1A and H1B were supported. The participants were more likely to accept product advice provided by the user-guided aid than that provided by the systemcontrolled aid $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , although the difference between these two modes was not statistically different in terms of perceived system quality $( p = 0 . 1 6 )$ Thus, H2A was supported, whereas H2B was not.

To test the hypotheses regarding the differential effects of the USIM for the AC-based aid versus the elimination-based aid, we split the sample into two subgroups for the two aids. Then we ran ANOVAs for each of the two subgroups. Before splitting the sample, we ensured that the interaction term (USIM× decision strategy) in the ANOVA was significant at $p < 0 . 0 5$ . A similar analysis approach was adopted when analyzing the moderation effects of product knowledge.

With respect to the moderation effects of decision strategies, the interaction terms in ANOVAs (USIM × decision strategy) were significant for both number of iterations $( F = 4 . 1 2 , p = 0 . 0 4 )$ and perceived cognitive effort $( F = 5 . 4 3 , \ p = 0 . 0 2 )$ . The sample was then split into two subgroups, one for the AC-based aid and the other for the elimination-based aid. We ran ANOVAs for each of these two subgroups. For the AC-based aid, neither the effects of the USIM on the number of iterations nor the effects on the perceived cognitive effort were significant $( p > 0 . 1 )$ . In contrast, for the elimination-based aid, the effects of the USIM on these two variables were both significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ Therefore, both H3A and H3B were supported. Figure 2 illustrates the differential effects of the USIM for the AC- and elimination-based aids, respectively.

For the two quality variables, the interaction terms in ANOVAs (USIM × decision strategy) were not significant for either advice acceptance $( F = 0 . 3 6 ,$ $p = 0 . 5 6 )$ or perceived system quality $( F = 5 . 2 4 ,$ $p { = } 0 . 0 7 )$ . Thus, neither H4A nor H4B was supported. With respect to the moderation effects of users’ product knowledge, the interaction terms in ANOVAs (USIM × product knowledge) were significant for both number of iterations $( F ^ { ^ { \prime } } = 4 . 4 1 , ~ p = 0 . 0 4 )$ and perceived cognitive effort $( F = 3 . 9 8 , p = 0 . 0 5 )$ . For high knowledge participants, neither the effects of the USIM on the number of iterations nor on the perceived cognitive effort were significant (p > 001). ## 5. Discussion

Interactive online decision aids are increasingly being made available to assist consumers with their online shopping. User-decision aid dialogues are often employed as forms of user-system interaction to help construct and elicit users’ attribute preferences. This research investigates the effects of the USIM on users’ quality and effort evaluations of a decision aid by considering the moderating effects of the decision strategy employed by the aid and the users’ product knowledge. Although the experimental results largely confirm that users evaluate the user-guided USIM more positively than the system-controlled USIM, we find that such effects are stronger for the elimination-based aid than for the AC-based aid and for the low product knowledge users than for the high knowledge users (especially in terms of effortrelated assessments). ## 5.1. Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations. First, because the experimental participants were university students, caution should be exercised in generalizing the results of this study to other demographic user groups. Nonetheless, the experiment was conducted in the context of online shopping, and our sample generally matched Internet user demographic data (Johnson 2005). Moreover, an informal survey we conducted revealed that many undergraduates were considerably interested in digital cameras, which meant that the participants were motivated toward the tasks and actively involved in the experiment. Thus, the realism of the experiment was assured. Therefore, we believe that the sample used in the experiment was valid.

Second, this research focused on content filteringbased decision aids, which provide product advice based on preferences explicitly expressed by users. However, there are other forms of aids that are not based on content filtering. For example, a collaborative filtering-based aid utilizes user characteristics and is mainly based on user profiles to classify users into groups (Ansari et al. 2000). Shopping advice is then generated based on the products most frequently chosen by others in the same group. Design alternatives for such a different type of decision aid, as well as the interactions among its different design components, may differ and require further research.

In addition, because this research was conducted in the context of online decision aids, the generalizability of the findings may be limited and not applicable to other information systems (IS) contexts (e.g., group support systems—GSS). Nonetheless, insufficient attention has been paid to system restrictivenessrelated designs. Restrictiveness perceptions influence user evaluations of IS because most IS, if not all, can only provide limited support to users (Silver 1991, Wang and Benbasat 2009). Further research is needed to identify designs related to different types of restrictiveness perceptions in different IS contexts, such as process and content facilitation restrictiveness of GSS as examined by Khalifa et al. (2002).

Third, the operationalization of the USIM in this research (i.e., the user-guided USIM vis-a-vis the \` system-controlled USIM) reflected a very low level and a very high level of interaction restrictiveness, respectively. In the experiment, we did so in order to test the impact of the interaction restrictiveness. However, in practice, USIMs are probably not as restrictive as the system-controlled USIM and are often more restrictive than the user-guided one as operationalized in this study. This limitation should be noted.

Fourth, this study also had some limitations regarding the measurement scale used, especially for product knowledge. This study only measured the selfreported subjective product knowledge of experimental participants, but not their objective knowledge. Although numerous studies confirmed that subjective and objective knowledge are strongly correlated with each other in a product domain, these two constructs do not always exert the same effects on the behavior, decision processes, or perceived decision outcomes of consumers (Carlson et al. 2009). Therefore, when examining the role of product knowledge in consumers’ use and evaluation of decision aids, future research could examine both the subjective and objective product knowledge of users.

## 5.2. Contributions and Implications

Notwithstanding its limitations, this study makes several significant contributions to both research and practice. Its main contributions to research are twofold. First, building upon the contingency perspective of users’ evaluations and use of online decision aids, this study investigates how the effects of the USIM of a decision aid are influenced by the decision strategy of the aid. The USIM and the decision strategy are two critical components of a decision aid that relate to system restrictiveness. Investigations into different designs that may restrict the users are promising for research in various contexts of IS because extant literature primarily focuses on the benefits of decision support technologies in facilitating the decision making of users. Recent studies found that the restrictiveness of an IS can invoke users’ psychological reactance, which leads to behavioral avoidance that impedes their intentions to interact with and use the system (Wang and Benbasat 2009).

Extant research on online decision aids mainly investigated the effects of certain design components independently and ignored the interrelationships among these components. This research is an extension of Wang and Benbasat (2009), which focused on a single design component, namely, the decision strategy employed by decision aids. Different decision strategies employed by decision aids require different user input (attribute preferences); thus, the effectiveness of the way users’ preferences are elicited depends on the aid’s decision strategies. This study investigates the effects of the USIM and, more importantly, their differential effects across decision aids using different decision strategies. Thus, this paper provides a precise and in-depth understanding of the effects of various technological designs on users’ evaluations of the decision aids; such understanding is considered more difficult to theorize than the relationships among users’ perceptual evaluations of the aids (Benbasat and Barki 2007).

Second, adopting another contingency perspective and building upon the role of product knowledge in consumer decision making, this study provides theoretical insights into the differential evaluations of design alternatives for online decision aids across users with different levels of product knowledge. Web-based and consumer-oriented applications signal “a move from designing systems for a relatively homogeneous to a highly heterogeneous population” (Straub and Watson 2001, p. 338). Online decision aids no longer primarily deal with professional or managerial users; hence, designers have to consider the heterogeneous nature of the users.

Prior studies widely noted that users with different levels of product knowledge behave differently with respect to system use and evaluations in the context of decision-making with aids (Arnold et al. 2006). However, to our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate how users’ product knowledge influences the effects of USIMs of online decision aids. The user-guided USIM is more flexible; thus, it can better facilitate construction and elicitation of user preferences, especially for low product knowledge users. This finding is interesting because it seems to be counterintuitive. People might assume that low knowledge users would be better supported by a system-controlled USIM, yet this assumption does not work in the context of online decision making with aids. Low knowledge users lack prestored details about their preferences and are more likely to make a detour to learn subjective attribute values, adjust their preferences, and eventually arrive at relatively stable preferences (Huber et al. 2002). For a decision aid, the challenge is in identifying a priori what attributes are relevant for a low product knowledge user at a certain time during this learning process, and then using a prespecified sequence of procedures to facilitate the construction and elicit his or her preferences via a system-controlled USIM (Nakatsu and Benbasat 2006). In contrast, under a user-guided USIM, low knowledge users can explore and learn attribute values with ease and can flexibly adjust their preferences.

This study also has significant implications for practitioners. Its findings provide guidelines on the type of USIM to be adopted for an online decision aid using a particular decision strategy. Not considering the relation between a decision aid’s USIM and its decision strategy, users may prefer the userguided USIM because it is less restrictive and they can have better control over their interaction with the aid. On the other hand, practitioners may opt for the system-controlled USIM with the aim to elicit more preference information from consumers and to understand them better.

With respect to decision strategies employed by decision aids, the AC-based aid is less restrictive than is the elimination-based one, thus leading to users’ better perceptions of the AC-based aid (Wang and Benbasat 2009). In spite of such advantages of the AC-based aid, many existing online decision aids employ an elimination strategy (e.g., iGoDigital.com), probably because implementing the elimination strategy is easier for designers than implementing the AC strategy. For the AC-based aid, designers have to construct a utility function that integrates various user attribute preferences and considers the importance levels of these preferences. For each product type, designers of the AC-based aid have to understand how different people value the fulfillment of their needs through various product features and functionalities. However, many designers may not possess such knowledge; thus, they may choose to provide elimination-based aids to save design effort.

In light of the findings of this study, we offer two suggestions: (1) if designers possess the knowledge to provide an AC-based aid, they should consider a system-controlled USIM to elicit more user preference information and (2) if practitioners decide to provide the elimination-based aid, they should consider a user-guided USIM. These two suggestions are based on the results that the USIM exerts limited effects for the AC-based aid. In contrast, for the eliminationbased aid, the user-guided USIM is much better than the system-controlled one.

In addition, designers should pay attention to the heterogeneous backgrounds of online users, especially because information systems are increasingly migrating to Web-based environments and services are being provided for general Internet users rather than only for professional and managerial users. These users have different levels of product knowledge. Low product knowledge users need more supporting facilities to construct and elicit their preferences; hence they should be offered a user-guided USIM. For the same purpose, designers may want to offer additional guidance to low knowledge users on choosing different attribute preferences for their focal tasks to facilitate their preference construction (Wang and Benbasat 2007). In contrast, for high product knowledge users, practitioners may want to offer decision aids with a system-controlled USIM that is capable of eliciting more user preference information. Moreover, because such users have more sophisticated preferences, additional questions may be designed into user-decision aid dialogues to elicit their preferences on advanced product features.

## 5.3. Conclusion

This research was motivated by the need to investigate the relationships among different design components of online decision aids as well as those between these components and user characteristics. Different USIMs can be deployed by decision aids to help construct and elicit users’ attribute preferences. Choosing an optimal USIM for decision aids with different decision strategies and for users with different levels of product knowledge can improve users’ evaluation of the aids. By adopting a contingency approach to extending prior research on online decision aids, we not only provided a theoretical understanding of the effects of USIMs on user evaluations of a decision aid by considering other related technological components of the aid (decision strategy) and users’ characteristics (product knowledge) but also offered concrete advice for aid designers. With better designs, the effectiveness of online decision support services can be further enhanced. Consequently, users will have more positive online experiences.
