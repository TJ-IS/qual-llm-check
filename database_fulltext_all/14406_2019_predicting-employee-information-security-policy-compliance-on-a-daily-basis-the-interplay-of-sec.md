# Predicting employee information security policy compliance on a daily basis: The interplay of security-related stress, emotions, and neutralization

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Information security policy (ISP) Security compliance Afective events theory Coping theory Afect Emotions Security-related stress Experience sampling methodology

## A B S T R A C T

We conceptualized security-related stress (SRS) and proposed a theoretical model linking SRS, discrete emotions, coping response, and information security policy (ISP) compliance. We used an experience sampling design, wherein 138 professionals completed surveys. We observed that SRS had a positive association with frustratior and fatigue, and these negative emotions were associated with neutralization of ISP violations. Additionally, frustration and fatigue make employees more likely to follow through on their rationalizations of ISP violations by decreased ISP compliance. Our findings provide evidence that neutralization is not a completely stable phenomenon but can vary within individuals from one time point to another.

## 1. Introduction

Efective information security (InfoSec) management is largely dependent on an organization’s ability to cultivate a culture in which its employees adhere to the guidelines, procedures, and technical controls that are specified in its InfoSec policies (ISPs) [1]. To this end, academics and practitioners alike have devoted substantial eforts toward understanding and predicting employees’ ISP compliance. Amid the multitude of individual and contextual variables that have been linked to this behavior (e.g., see [2–5]), the stressful demands imposed by security requirements have emerged as particularly salient. For example, in one survey of employee users, “too busy to think about policies” and “policies are inconvenient to follow” were reported as chief reasons for ISP violations [6]. In another survey, one in five users recalled an instance where they were unable to complete a work assignment on time because of a security requirement [7]. Academic studies tell a similar story in that many users perceive the abundance of rapidly expanding security requirements to be a restrictive, inconvenient, and dificult to understand (e.g. [8–10]). There is also evidence that security requirements can backfire and provoke noncompliance due to the stressful demands they impose upon employees [11–14].

D’Arcy et al. [12] adapted the concept of security-related stress (SRS) from the technostress literature and found support for their theoretical model in which SRS engenders rationalizations of ISP violating behavior as a coping response, which, in turn, increase ISP violation intention. Building upon this extant work, in this study, we use afective events theory (AET) as an overarching framework and pair it with coping theory to build a theoretical model that considers employees’ discrete emotional reactions to SRS, and the influences of these emotions on rationalizations of ISP violations, and ultimately on ISP compliance behavior. Specifically, we ask (1) whether SRS engenders frustration with and fatigue from security requirements, and (2) whether these emotional reactions invoke neutralization of ISP violations. We also investigate whether high and low levels of frustration and fatigue moderate the linkages between SRS, neutralization, and ISP compliance in our model.

Motivation for this study comes from coping theory perspectives that consider stress, emotions, and coping as conceptually intertwined [15,16]. Prior SRS studies that have drawn on coping theory have not considered the role of emotions, which are central to dealing with stress [15] and known to result from stressful workplace encounters [17,18]. As we later describe in our theorizing, accounting for emotions afords predictions of specific coping responses to SRS, and thereby provides further insight into the SRS-to-ISP compliance relationship. More broadly, InfoSec scholars have called for a greater understanding of how emotional factors and neutralization influence ISP compliance decisions [19,20], and we aim to address these issues in our study.

As a departure from prior studies that utilize one-time, betweenindividual designs that implicitly assume the appraisal of SRS and its coping response as stable phenomena, we take a within-individual approach in building and testing our model that considers variation in these constructs. We do so for the following reasons. First, this approach is consistent with our overarching theoretical framework, AET, which is concerned with afective states<sup>1</sup> at work and their causes and consequences [18]. Afect refers to one’s feeling state or how one feels at a given moment, which, in the organizational context, is toward the job or some aspect of the job. This is in contrast to cognition, which refers to well-considered and stable evaluations of these elements [18]. In terms of the current study, a within-individual design allows us to more accurately capture emotions, which are transitory in nature, and which we predict fluctuate based on SRS events. We also seek to investigate whether neutralization has a state-like, afective dimension, similar to other job attitudes and beliefs (e.g., job satisfaction and organizational commitment [21]). Indeed, it may be that neutralization is not entirely stable (with some people being generally more apt to neutralize their ISP violations than others) but instead can vary within individuals from one point in time to the next based on one’s emotional states and/or the experience of certain stressful events in the workplace, such as SRS.

The paper proceeds as follows. The next section reviews relevant literature and highlights the focus of our work, while the third section lays out the SRS concept. This is followed by the theoretical foundation of our research and then the research model and its associated hypotheses. The methodology, comprising an experience sampling methodology (ESM) design in which computer-using professionals completed surveys on a Monday–Wednesday–Friday (MWF) schedule over a three-week period, and data analysis follow. The paper concludes with a discussion of the study’s results, implications, and limitations and areas for future research.

## 2. Relevant literature and Research Opportunities

Although InfoSec research has uncovered a host of factors that are associated with ISP compliance (e.g., see [2–5]), most pertinent to our study is research that examined the stressful demands imposed by security requirements (i.e., SRS). This literature includes investigations of (1) factors that create SRS and (2) its attitudinal and behavioral con sequences. Within the former category, Beautement et al. [22] found a prevailing attitude among employees that security requirements (e.g., password policies, file encryption) are mentally taxing and a barrier to productivity. Additional studies uncovered employee perceptions of security requirements as intimidating, dificult to understand, and incompatible with core job duties [9,10,23]. Lee et al. [8] developed and tested a model of antecedents of SRS and found that both perceptions of invasion of privacy and work overload due to security requirements were positively associated with SRS.

Regarding SRS’s consequences, studies by Bulgurcu et al. [24] D’Arcy and Lowry [11], and Hwang et al. [25] each suggest that ISP compliance decreases when security requirements are perceived as a work impediment (a stressful condition) because of the perceived costs (e.g., time and efort) they impose. In these studies, the work impedi ment construct showed either a direct negative association with ISP compliance [25], or an indirect negative association through a weak ening of attitudes toward ISP compliance [11,24]. In another study, Lowry and Moody [13] found that employees’ exposure to a new compulsory security policy that contained overly controlling and formalized language produced a negative attitudinal reaction, which, in turn, was associated with decreased ISP compliance intention. Posey et al. [14] likewise found that a combination of increasing and dynamically changing security requirements was associated with increased employee computer abuse. And finally, D’Arcy et al.’s [12] results suggest that SRS increases the likelihood of ISP violations by enabling the rationalization of this behavior. Notably, the D’Arcy et al. [12] results were later replicated in a separate study [26].

The foregoing studies provide a foundation for the stress-inducing aspects of security requirements and their influence on employee behavior. However, as evidenced by the relatively small number of studies reviewed, this literature is in a nascent state, and thus, there are opportunities for additional research. For one, the conceptualization and measurement of SRS has been that of stable phenomenon, which is not always consistent with the episodic nature of workplace stress [27], particularly in information systems (IS) contexts [28]. Bulgurcu et al. [24] ostensibly acknowledged this point in terms of SRS, describing perceived work impediment from security requirements as “an event with negative valence” (p. 532; emphasis added).

Similarly, based on their construct descriptions and between-in dividual designs, extant studies have assumed that the consequences of SRS – that is, the security-related attitudes and behaviors that are modeled as coping responses – are relatively stable over time, being the result of a cognitive-evaluative process. The organizational literature recognizes that job attitudes also have a more transient (state-like) affective dimension [29]. Support for this afective dimension comes from ESM studies, which found that a substantial portion of the variance in job attitudes is within-individual (i.e., fluctuates over time); and further, this within-individual variation has been predicted by time-var iant factors such as daily feelings related to the job and episodic workplace events, and itself has predicted within-individual variations in job behaviors such as task compliance and organizational citizenship (see [30] for review). In the InfoSec context, there is evidence that ISP compliance is not entirely stable and that fluctuations in ISP compliance can be partially explained by concurrent fluctuations in employees’ mood states and perceptions of the work environment [11], thereby suggesting that ISP compliance has an afective dimension. However, research has yet to consider SRS in episodic terms and whether this translates into fluctuations in coping responses (and neutralization in particular) and related behavioral outcomes.

A second issue is that prior SRS studies have not considered the role of emotions, which are central to dealing with stress [15] and known to result from stressful workplace encounters [17,18]. As previously noted, accounting for emotions afords an understanding of the underlying mechanisms that link stress to its coping response, and thereby would provide further theoretical insight into the SRS-to-ISP compliance relationship.

An aim of the current study is to address these research opportunities. To do so, we build and test a dynamic model of SRS events, discrete emotions, coping response, and ISP compliance that is based on reasoning from AET and coping theory. Before detailing the model’s theoretical underpinnings, we next describe our conceptualization of SRS.

## 3. Security-related stress (SRS)

Drawn from the concept of psychological stress [31], SRS is defined as stress caused by internal and/or external security-related demands appraised as taxing one’s resources or abilities [12]. The previously reviewed literature provides a few examples of SRS, with the most comprehensive construct being D’Arcy et al.’s [12] adaptation of the technostress overload, complexity, and uncertainty dimensions to the InfoSec context. We depart from this prior work by focusing on SRS events that can vary from day-to-day and drawing specifically from the organizational stress literature’s hindrance stress concept [32]. Focusing on SRS events is consistent with our theorizing, which posits state-like, afective reactions to SRS, and thereby requires changing circumstances as causal explanations [33]; and, as described below, hindrance stress is conceptually similar to SRS, and prior organizational research has found that perceptions of hindrance stress can vary from day-to-day [17].

Hindrance stressors are job demands that are appraised as impeding personal growth, learning, or goal attainment. Examples include role ambiguity, role conflict, situational restraints, and administrative hassles. Employees tend to view these stressful demands as thwarting their efective performance and any ensuing rewards [34]. Consequently, hindrance stressors deplete resources and thus have certain negative consequences (e.g., psychological strains, negative emotions, and mental fatigue) [32].

The security requirements specified in ISPs pose several demands that fit the notion of hindrance stressors. For example, employees are often required to compose new passwords and recall complex passwords for multiple resources, and on occasion require technical assistance for access to blocked websites or programs. In line with these examples, academic and practitioner studies indicate that employees view many security requirements as laborious and unnecessary overhead that impedes their productivity [9,10,14,22,35,36]. The imposition of security requirements can also create conditions of role ambi guity and role conflict. In terms of role ambiguity, employees may not know what to do or who to ask for help, particularly for more technical security requirements (e.g., encrypting email [10]), and, further, may perceive security as the sole responsibility of the information technology (IT) department [9,23]. Role conflict is salient because employees may perceive certain security restrictions (e.g., no USB devices, no password sharing, blocked access to cloud-based services such as Dropbox) as incompatible with their job duties. Although research indicates that employees experience such instances of SRS with relative frequency [9,12], they may not occur every day or to the same degree every day, which is why we conceptualize SRS in episodic terms.

## 4. Afective events theory (AET) and coping theory

The combination of AET and coping theory provides a basis for understanding how employees respond to instances of SRS. AET is a general framework that is concerned with afective states at work and their relationship with employee behavior [18]. The theory recognizes the multidimensional nature of job attitudes and behaviors, in which each have separate afective and cognitive dimensions. AET focuses on the afective dimension and posits that certain workplace events are afect-laden experiences, thus eliciting emotional reactions, which, in turn, influence both afective attitudes and afect-driven behaviors.

A fundamental element of AET is its temporal basis and specifically that afect levels fluctuate over time. Expounding this, AET holds that exogenous components, such as afectively relevant workplace events, and endogenous components, such as changes in emotions, can partially explain the variability in afective job attitudes and behaviors that employees experience on a day-to-day basis [33]. In the current study, we adopt the perspective that neutralization is similar to other job at titudes and beliefs, such as job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and organizational justice, which have been shown to fluctuate over time and translate into fluctuations in work behaviors ([21,30,37] note that we later provide evidence that neutralization has appreciable within-individual variation, thereby supporting its state-like, afective dimension). Likewise, because we focus on afective factors as drivers of fluctuations in ISP compliance, we consider the afective dimension of ISP compliance and accordingly conceptualize it as an afect-driven behavior. Implicit in our theorizing is that ISP compliance is, at least in part, afect-driven, just like several other work behaviors.

Although AET provides an overarching basis for temporal relations among workplace events, afective reactions, and behaviors [33], it does not ofer explicit, testable hypotheses with respect to SRS. Indeed, beyond the general notion that negative events elicit negative emotions, AET provides little guidance as to discrete emotional reactions to SRS and the ensuing coping responses that are tied to these emotions. To fill this void, we draw upon coping theory and in particular D’Arcy et al.’s [12] contextualized coping model in which SRS engenders rationalizations of ISP violations.

Coping theory originally focused exclusively on appraisals of stress and its resultant choice of coping mechanisms [31]. Adaptations to the theory added emotions, based on the recognition that stress, emotions, and coping are conceptually related [15,16]. This extended coping theory links specific sets of emotional reactions to diferent types of stressors [15]. Relevant to SRS is the delineation of certain emotional reactions to goal-relevant events. Stressful events that impede goal attainment, for example, engender negative emotions (e.g., anger, frustration), which are further distinguished based on appraisal factors such as the source’s and recipient’s controllability of stress. Coping theory, in conjunction with characteristics of security requirements, provide a rationale for two discrete, context-specific emotional reactions to SRS – frustration and fatigue – which we more fully explain in the hypotheses development section. Although fatigue may be more accurately labeled as a state rather than an emotion, it fits the definition of emotion as being an afective reaction to a specific event [18,38]. Hence, following Scott and Judge [38], we consider fatigue as an emotion in our AETbased model.

Another facet of coping theory is that emotional reactions to stres give rise to certain coping responses. This position fits with the broader AET propositions that emotional reactions to workplace events influence afective attitudes and behaviors. Coping theory delineates two major categories of coping: problem-focused and emotion-focused [31]. Problem-focused coping involves direct eforts to manage or alter the stressful situation and is used when individuals believe they can effectively address the stressor (i.e., high controllability). Emotion-focused coping involves dealing with the stressful situation internally and is used when the stressor is viewed as less controllable. Because employees often do not possess the power to change SRS conditions [12], at least in the short term, we focus on employees’ emotion-focused coping.

Emotion-focused coping encompasses a variety of internal processes aimed at restoring emotional stability (e.g., psychological avoidance, reinterpreting the situation, and rationalizations) [39,40]. Coping theory and its related literature provide some precision in tying these general forms of emotion-focused coping back to emotional antecedents. Pertinent to our study, negative emotions are said to motivate reinterpretations and rationalizations related to the stressful situation that aim to reduce the emotional distress [15]. Research in organizational contexts also suggests that negative emotions accruing from stressful work events induce mental disengagement from one’s job responsibilities [34].

These descriptions of coping responses align with the concept of neutralization, which involves techniques of justification and rationalization of an immoral or illegal act [41]. InfoSec studies have adapted the neutralization concept to describe how employees rationalize their ISP violations (e.g. [4,42–44]) and, as noted, D’Arcy et al. [12] drew upon similar themes in describing coping responses to SRS. Our theoretical model posits neutralization as an emotion-focused coping response to the negative emotions that emanate from instances of SRS. In this manner, neutralization can provide a degree of psychological control in dealing with the emotional distress from SRS and may also serve a cathartic purpose [12]. Neutralization, therefore, serves the emotion-focused coping function of alleviating emotional distress by means of cognitive maneuvers.

## 5.1. SRS and emotional reactions

A central thesis of AET is that afective work events trigger emotional reactions. Following Rodell and Judge’s [17] classification of instances of hindrance stressors as afective work events, we do the same with our security-related variants of these stressors. This position fits well with coping theory’s specification of goal-relevant events that produce particular emotional reactions [15,16].

SRS should produce negative emotions because it thwarts employees’ progress toward valued work goals, which is typically the ability to carry out one’s tasks efectively. The negative emotional efect should stem from security requirements’ negative impact on productivity, conflict between security requirements and primary job du ties, and uncertainty with regard to security responsibilities, or some combination of these factors. Since the conditions of SRS are imposed by the organization, and thus beyond employees’ control, two negative emotions that would seem relevant are anger and frustration [15,45]. Anger is foremost when the organization has full control over the stressful conditions; otherwise, frustration will predominate [16]. In the case of SRS, even though security requirements are enacted by the or ganization, the organization is not in complete control of their existence. For example, a variety of government regulations and industry standards (e.g., Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI-DSS), and Sarbanes-Oxley Act Section 404 (SOX 404)) mandate organizations internal security measures. Insofar as employees understand the genesis of these measures, it is likely that they will somewhat absolve the organization of full responsibility for security requirements, and thus be more likely to experience frustration as opposed to anger. In this vein, prior studies have documented employees’ frustration with security requirements, which have characteristics of SRS (e.g., ambiguous, ob structive, burdensome) [9,10]. Hence, we hypothesize:

## H1. Within individuals, SRS will be positively related to frustration.

Consistent with the notion that stressors can spark multiple emotions simultaneously [29], we also predict that instances of SRS will trigger fatigue. Studies conducted in numerous contexts provide evidence that the experience of stressful events diminishes one’s available physical, cognitive, and emotional resources [46,47]. In organizational contexts, stressful work events have been found to deplete employees mental and physical energy and elicit feelings of mental exhaustion [34,48,49]. Such outcomes are characteristic of employee fatigue.

Interestingly, similar to the seemingly innocuous events that typify SRS, it is the mildly negative events that are fairly commonplace (e.g., rude coworker comments and organizational memos) rather than major and infrequent events (e.g., changes in leadership and layofs) that appear to be the strongest culprits of employee fatigue [18].

Conditions of SRS are ripe for feelings of fatigue. As stated, employees often perceive security requirements as mentally taxing and a barrier to productivity, both of which increase the likelihood of fatigue. Additionally, the influence of role ambiguity, which is encompassed in the SRS construct, represents uncertainty with respect to security responsibilities (e.g., which tasks to perform) and should be somewhat trying on employees. Further, in recent years, employees have been inundated with new and complex security requirements, with little chance to develop a base of experience or assimilate security into their work routines [9]. Experiencing SRS events, such as unclear security responsibilities or security requirements that conflict with other job expectations, should be particularly draining for employees in such contexts and perhaps make them expectant of future instances of SRS that will further thwart their pursuit of valued work goals, thus contributing to feelings of fatigue. Hence, we predict:

H2. Within individuals, SRS will be positively related to fatigue.

## 5.2. Neutralization as a coping response

Coping theory predicts that emotional reactions to stressful events produce certain coping responses, and we posit neutralization as a coping response to the frustration and fatigue that stems from SRS. Again, our selection of neutralization is based on the coping theory prediction that negative emotions elicit reinterpretations and rationalizations related to the stressful situation [15], which aligns with the concept of neutralization. Neutralization involves mental techniques that individuals invoke in order to justify or rationalize their illicit behavior. Although there are many neutralization techniques in the literature, the original neutralization theory defined five common types [41] denial of responsibility, denial of injury, denial of the victim, condemnation of the condemner, and appeal to higher loyalties. Through such mental techniques, individuals can “neutralize” their values that would normally prohibit them from engaging in illicit behavior. For example, neutralization allows individuals to downplay the repercussions of their illicit behavior. Certain neutralization techniques are better suited to particular acts, and for this reason, researchers often utilize only a selection of the available neutralization techniques from the literature depending on the context of their studies.

Research on the influence of negative emotions provides a general basis for predicting that both frustration and fatigue from security requirements will increase the likelihood of neutralization of ISP violations. The rationale is that, from an evolutionary perspective, negative emotions narrow an individual’s thought and action repositories in preparation for quick action to address imminent threats [50]. Hence, feelings of frustration and fatigue may make employees less creative when it comes to dealing with SRS and instead turn to neutralization as a mental shortcut to cope with their emotional distress.

More specific to the discrete emotions in our model, when experi encing increased frustration from SRS, it is likely that employees will look to assert and regain a level of psychological control (perhaps even subconsciously) in an efort to alleviate their emotional distress [12], and neutralization provides a mechanism toward this objective. Feelings of frustration may also prompt employees to let down their guards in terms of their internal norms that prohibit violations of ISPs, thus facilitating rationalizations of this behavior. Research that has linked employee frustration at work to an increased tolerance toward certain unethical workplace behaviors supports this position [51]. The preceding logic is also applicable to feelings of fatigue. Psychological and physiological fatigue make it dificult to regulate the internal norms that prohibit illicit behavior [52,53], thus increasing the chances for justifications and rationalizations. In this vein, studies in both laboratory and work settings provide evidence that ethical values can be compromised when individuals are experiencing mental and physical fatigue [54,51,55,56]. These findings would seem to extend to our study context. Hence, we predict the following:

H3. Within individuals, frustration will be positively related to neutralization.

H4. Within individuals, fatigue will be positively related to neutralization.

## 5.3. Behavioral consequences of SRS

Prior InfoSec research supports a negative relationship between neutralization and ISP compliance [42–44,57,58]. Our study difers from the extant work in that we model both neutralization and ISP compliance at the within-individual level, consistent with their placement as afective attitudes and behaviors in our AET-based framework. Specifically, AET predicts that afective attitudes directly influence affect-driven behavior [18] and, in terms of the coping theory aspect of our model, engagement in the behavior of interest is posited as an outcome of the coping process [59]. Although a within-individual relationship between neutralization and ISP compliance has yet to explored, we expect it to hold just as it has in the extant between-individual studies, based on the logic that the rationalization of ISP violations makes ISP compliance less likely. Hence, we predict the following:

H5. Within individuals, neutralization will be negatively related to ISP compliance.

As a final addition to our model, we include general (i.e., not InfoSec-specific) positive and negative emotional states as control variables for our hypothesized relationships (H1-H5), based on the assumption that one’s general emotional states can influence his/her emotional reactions and coping responses to SRS, and also ISP compliance behavior.

## 6. Methodology

## 6.1. Sample and procedure

Because our model incorporates constructs that are expected to vary over time, we needed a research design that accounts for such variation. To this end, organizational researchers have employed ESM designs in which the constructs of interest are measured on multiple days over the course of one or more weeks with the objective of measuring fluctua tions in these constructs over time [60]. Such ESM designs account for the state-like nature of afective constructs by allowing the researcher to statistically separate the within-individual (i.e., variance within an individual’s responses over the study timeframe) from the between-individual variance in these constructs [21].

Participating in ESM studies requires considerable time and commitment, which makes it dificult to find organizations that are willing to have their employees partake in such a study [60]. For this reason, we used a market research firm to invite participants to our study, which was described as a study of daily feelings about the job and InfoSec. We instructed the firm to recruit employed, computerusing professionals from the United States, who were compensated up to \$50 for their participation, depending on how many surveys they completed. Upon viewing an email invitation, participants were presented with detailed instructions on the study. These instructions contained strong language that emphasized the time commitment involved and the requirements for compensation. Following acceptance of these parameters via a consent form, participants were directed to a one-time, online survey that contained the measures for demographic variables and other nontime-variant variables (this study was part of a larger research project).

Next, we used an ESM design in which participants completed online surveys on a MWF schedule for a three-week period (maximum of 9 MWF surveys). This time frame exceeds the recommended two-week threshold for ESM studies, which is considered a representative snapshot of one’s work life [61]. We chose the every-other-day pattern instead of consecutive days to create temporal separation that would help ameliorate carryover efects from the previous survey responses (i.e., consistency motif [62]). As we later describe, we also statistically controlled for such carryover efects in our analyses through the use of lagged variables. Additional impetus for the every-other-day pattern of surveys was to guard against participant fatigue that might occur from taking surveys on consecutive workdays over a three-week period.

Each MWF survey contained measures for the five constructs in our research model as well as the general positive and negative emotional states control variables. Participants received an email from the market research firm that contained a link to that day’s survey. The email was sent after 3:00 pm Pacific Standard Time (as stated in the study description), and participants were instructed to complete the survey at or after the completion of their workday. We checked timestamps to ensure that participants completed only one survey per workday.

Given the demanding nature of our ESM design, we took steps to help ensure quality responses. Specifically, we designed the MWF survey so that all items were randomly presented for each survey instance (using the randomization feature of the online survey tool) to mitigate any pattern of responses or ordering efects [63]. We also used diferent scale anchors and diferent numbers of anchor points (i.e., slider scale) for certain construct measures, which reduces common method variance (CMV)-related biases [62] we later detail additional methods to address CMV concerns in this study). And, following prior ESM studies [64,65], to the extent possible, we used shortened measurement scales to control the length of the MWF survey.

According to available statistics, 305 panel members accepted the invitation to participate in the study by completing the one-time survey. Of these, 104 were eliminated based on a response of either “no” or “not sure” to a question that asked whether they were aware of an ISP in their organization. We wanted to ensure that participants could reasonably respond to the ISP-related survey items (e.g., [24]). Of the remaining 201 participants that went forward with the MWF surveys, 63 were eliminated either because of response set biases $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ answers exhibiting certain unlikely patterns, such as all 5 or alternating 4 and 5, or survey completed in an unreasonably short time) or because they did not meet our inclusion criterion, which consisted of completing at least four MWF surveys (e.g. [61]). This resulted in a final sample of 138 participants and a total of 1058 MWF surveys (M = 7.7 MWF surveys per employee). Given that each participant could complete a maximum of nine MWF surveys (for a total of 1242), this corresponds to an MWF survey response rate of 85 percent. Results of t-tests revealed no significant diferences on any study variables between participants included in the final sample and those excluded because of missing MWF surveys. The demographic profile of the final sample is presented in Appendix A. Of note is that participants reported dealing with InfoSec issues, on average, about 30 percent of their workday, and their mean self-reported ISP awareness was 4.4 on a scale of 1–5. Hence, security requirements were salient to these individuals, and they appeared to have an understanding of the guidelines, procedures, and controls as prescribed by their organizations’ ISPs.

## 6.2. Measures

The MWF surveys consisted of previously validated scales that were adapted to our study context. All items were issued with instructions to capture strictly daily responses (e.g., “Today at work, I have adhered to the ISP adopted by my organization”). Appendix B contains a complete listing of the survey items and their accompanying instructions.

ISP compliance (COMP) was measured with a four-item scale that consisted of three items adapted from Bulgurcu et al.’s [24] ISP compliance intention scale and one item adapted from Posey et al.’s [66] listing of protective security behaviors. For SRS, we followed an approach similar to Rodell and Judge’s [17] daily measurement of hindrance stress and adapted items from prior validated job demand scales that fit the descriptions of instances of SRS. We did not use the multi dimensional SRS scale from D’Arcy et al. [12] because that scale was developed to capture stable, cognitive beliefs related to SRS as opposed to the episodic, afect-laden SRS events that are the focus of the current study. Indeed, in reviewing the D’Arcy et al. [12] items, many could not be reasonably adapted to a daily response format. Hence, readers should exercise caution in directly comparing our conceptualization of SRS to that of D’Arcy et al. [12]. Our SRS scale consisted of one item each from Avvagari et al.'s [28] IS-related role conflict and role ambiguity scales and two items from Bulgurcu et al.’s [24] work impedi ment scale. The specific items were chosen based on their (1) unique content related to the role conflict, role ambiguity, and situational restraint/administrative hassle aspects of SRS, (2) high factor loadings in prior studies, (3) adaptability to the InfoSec context, and (4) likelihood of varying over the workweek.

Neutralization (NEUT) was measured with four items taken from Siponen and Vance’s (2010) neutralization scale. The particular items correspond to the denial of injury, condemnation of the condemners, appeal to higher loyalties, and defense of necessity neutralization techniques. As with the SRS scale, we adapted only those items that were expected to vary over the workweek. Although other neutralization techniques would certainly be relevant to the ISP compliance context, our focus on the state-like, afective dimension of neutralization limited our measurement of this construct to a subset of techniques that would logically exhibit within-individual variation. In this manner, our approach is similar to that used in prior InfoSec studies wherein a subset of neutralization techniques was selected based on the unique context of the study [42,57,67].

To measure the discrete emotions, similar to Beaudry and Pinsonneault [45], we used Lazarus and Folkman’s [31] emotion in tensity scale and asked respondents to report the degree to which they felt each emotion (one item each for frustration (FRUS) and fatigue (FATIGUE)) in dealing with their organization’s ISP requirements. Distinct from the other measures, responses to these two items were on a slider scale (0 = not at all to 100 = extremely). Single items are common in ESM studies to reduce response burden and are fitting when straightforward recent feelings are requested [60] as research indicates that individuals can recall emotions reliably at the end of the same day, our emotion measures should be untainted by retrospective biases [27].

For the control variables, we measured the general positive (PE) and negative emotional (NE) states using the modified Diferential Emotions Scale [50]. This scale, which has been validated in numerous studie (e.g. [50,68]), consists of 20 items that assess the extent of positive and negative emotions experienced (10 items each), respectively. And finally, as reported in the demographic characteristics (Appendix A), we measured ISP awareness (ISPA) and percent of workday spent dealing with InfoSec issues. These items were included on the one-time survey that participants completed at the outset of the study. ISPA was measured with three items from Bulgurcu et al.’s [24] same scale, and percent of workday was an original item developed for this study.

## 6.3. Analyses

Because we used an ESM design in which days were nested within employees, we performed hierarchical linear modeling (HLM; see [69]), which allows researchers to simultaneously analyze variables at mul tiple levels of analysis in a series of regression equations that account for data nonindependence. For the current study, the hypotheses consisted of only one level (i.e., within-individual level, or Level 1 in HLM terminology), but given that the same individuals completed multiple surveys, the error terms were likely correlated. Hence, a statistical procedure such as HLM was needed to address this violation of independence of observations. We used HLM 7.03 for our analysis.

Before testing the hypotheses, we investigated whether a withinindividual assessment of our research model was appropriate by partitioning each variable into its within- and between-individual components. We did this by running null models in HLM (regressions with no predictors) for each of the variables. As shown in Table 1, a considerable proportion of the total variance in the variables (ranging from 19% to 56%) was within-individual; hence, each of the variables showed variability over the course of the study timeframe and there was appreciable within-individual variance to be explained in our de pendent variables. It is noteworthy that these percentages of withinindividual variance compare favorably with other ESM studies in the organizational literature (e.g., [70,71]).

Regarding the testing of our model, HLM requires a piecemeal ap proach in which separate regressions are run for each dependent variable. Hence, we ran four regressions with FRUS, FATIGUE, NEUT, and COMP each serving as a dependent variable. In each regression, we included a lagged score on the dependent variable (score on the pre vious MWF survey) to account for serial dependence (residual autocorrelation) that may be present in the data [60]. Our analysis was, therefore, based on the variance in the dependent variable that was unique to a particular day. Also, following the convention in the HLM literature (e.g. [17,72]), for the multi-item scales, we used the average of items belonging to the scale to operationalize the construct. More specifically, for purposes of our within-individual level hypotheses testing, the construct measures consisted of their average scores (or, single item scores for FRUS and FATIGUE) per MWF survey instance, resulting in 1058 total data points. This number was reduced to 921 data points when the lagged variables were added to the analyses $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ no lagged scores for the first MWF survey).

To appropriately test and interpret the within-individual relationships, we centered the predictor variables at each individual’s mean (i.e., group mean centering) [73]. Centering the predictor variables in this way removes any between-individual variance among the vari ables, meaning that the estimated within-individual relationships among the study variables are not confounded by individual diferences such as response tendencies or personality traits. As highlighted later, this form of centering helps alleviate some concerns about CMV.

We felt that in a similar manner, a relevant question is whether emotional highs and lows moderate employees responses to SRS. Again, InfoSec scholars have called for further inquiry into the role of emotional factors in ISP compliance decisions [19,20]. A theoretical basis for our additional analysis is that emotions are central to the stress process and influence how individuals cope with stress [15]. ## 7. Discussion and contributions

The objective of this study was to advance our understanding of SRS and its relationship with ISP compliance. To this end, we posed the research questions of (1) whether SRS engenders frustration and fatigue from security requirements, and (2) whether these emotional reactions invoke neutralization of ISP violations. We took a more nuanced and dynamic approach compared to prior work, and drawing from AET and coping theory and using an ESM design, examined within-individual relationships among SRS events, discrete emotions, coping response (in the form of neutralization), and ISP compliance.

Our results showed that an appreciable portion of the variance in each of our study constructs was within-individual (i.e., fluctuated over the study timeframe). This within-individual variance supports the position that SRS has an episodic dimension with afective consequences, consistent with the tenets of AET. More important than documenting within-individual variance, we were able to find meaningful relationships within it that support our hypotheses. For instance, in response to our first research question and in support of H1 and H2, we found that SRS events were associated with feelings of frustration and fatigue. Evidence that employees respond to instances of SRS with frustration and fatigue supports the AET and coping theory-based prediction that events that thwart progress toward one’s valued goals evoke negative emotions. SRS appears to induce frustration more so than fatigue as an emotional response, yet, the influence of fatigue that emanates from SRS should not be discounted, given the results of our subsample analysis. Our findings on frustration are consistent with qualitative studies in InfoSec [9,10], which documented frustration in response to stressful security requirements. However, to our knowledge, we are the first to explicitly measure and provide quantitative evidence of frustration, along with fatigue, in response to SRS.

Taking this further, in response to our second research question and in support of H3 and H4, our results suggest that feelings of frustration and fatigue from security requirements manifest themselves in terms of increased neutralization of ISP violations, which leads to decreased ISP compliance. By establishing frustration and fatigue as underlying mechanisms that link SRS and neutralization, we provide fresh insight into the previously established relationship between SRS and rationa lizations of ISP violations. Namely, frustration and fatigue are intervening variables in this relationship, something that was not captured in D’Arcy et al.’s [12] model. That these intervening variables are emotions is important, because as we demonstrated (Table 1), emotions are highly variable. This variability underscores the value of our withinindividual approach and ESM design, which allowed us to model and capture the fluctuations in frustration and fatigue, as well as that of their causes and consequences.

In terms of consequences, frustration and fatigue were shown to predict variability in neutralization, which itself predicted variability in ISP compliance. The variation in neutralization is a key finding because prior InfoSec studies have implicitly assumed that neutralization is a stable concept with, for example, some employees being more apt to neutralize their ISP violations than others. While recognizing this stable aspect of neutralization, our results suggest that there can also be dis crete episodes of neutralization that do not necessarily coincide with prior trends. In this sense, neutralization can be said to have a statelike, afective dimension, similar to other job attitudes (e.g., job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and organization justice), that is likewise driven by afective causes. In our case, these afective causes are changes in emotional states, which themselves are driven by instances of SRS; however, given the modest amount of variance explained in neutralization in our HLM results (see Table 2), there are clearly other drivers of its fluctuations. We leave this as an area for future research and suggest that researchers consider other time-variant factors, such as workplace disruptions due to IT failures or changes in workplace morale, as drivers of the variability in neutralization. In light of our findings, it also seems reasonable that other security-related attitudes (e.g., attitude toward ISP compliance and perceived benefits of ISP compliance) have an afective dimension, which would translate into fluctuations in security-related behaviors. This is another avenue for future research.

Beyond afirming the role of emotions in linking SRS to neutralization, our results point to the moderating influence of emotions in both the SRS-to-neutralization relationship and the relationship between neutralization and ISP compliance. The results regarding this latter relationship are of particular importance because they extend prior studies of neutralization in InfoSec contexts. Namely, these results suggest that when employees experience higher levels of frustration and fatigue from security requirements, they are more likely to have their neutralization thoughts translate into decreased ISP compliance. On the other hand, when experiencing lower levels of frustration, employees do not appear to allow their neutralization thoughts to translate into actual behavior. Our results here speak to the critical role of frustration as an emotional reaction to SRS and in the associated coping process. We speculate that a lower level of frustration (and presumably a more generally positive view toward security requirements) provides a bufer against the negative efects of SRS such that it allows other, more positive elements (e.g., strong ethical values and a conscientious personality) to override the neutralization thoughts that would otherwise manifest themselves into decreased ISP compliance. Obviously, additional research is needed to verify this conjecture and to delineate the specific elements that overtake neutralization in instances of low frustration.

Regarding the intriguing finding that the positive relationship between SRS and neutralization was stronger in the low fatigue subsample as compared to the high fatigue subsample, one speculative thought is that the rationalization process that is inherent in neutralization involves a degree of mental efort that is less likely to occur as fatigue increases. An assumption here would be that feelings of fatigue that emanate from SRS are more mentally taxing than those of frustration. However, once neutralization has taken place, it appears that fatigue increases the likelihood that neutralization afects ISP compliance decisions. On this point, it could be that fatigue hampers eforts to consider factors that could ofset the influence of neutralization on ISP compliance, such as perceived organizational sanctions for noncompliance or moral beliefs regarding ISP compliance. Collectively, our results suggest that frustration and fatigue are distinct emotions that have diferential influences on employees’ likelihood of neutralizing ISP violations and engaging in ISP compliance behavior, and we encourage additional research that explores these and other emotions in InfoSec contexts.

## 8. Practical implications

Given that even a single instance of ISP noncompliance can have major negative repercussions for organizations, it is incumbent upon organizations to predict these discrete episodes. Our results point to variations in SRS, certain negative emotions, and neutralization of ISP violations as somewhat influential in this regard.

In terms of SRS, organizations would be well advised to focus on strategies to mitigate its hindering efects as a means to promote ISP compliance. For example, organizations could manage the role ambiguity inherent in SRS events by providing employees with clear and precise ISPs, which delineate security responsibilities and expectations. Security education, training, and awareness (SETA) programs, which address these points, are also essential. In terms of the role conflict inherent in SRS, insofar as regulations, and standards allow for such flexibility, organizations should consider tailoring security requirements to certain positions to address the negative productivity efects. Based on an employee’s network access credentials, certain positions may not necessitate a highly restrictive blanket ISP. Organizations should consider, and perhaps make exceptions for, extreme situations in which business objectives conflict with security requirements (e.g., using a colleague’s password, if he/she may be unavailable, to meet a critical deadline). While allowing employees any latitude within ISPs is a risky endeavor, scholars have theorized that such an approach can be successfully integrated into the design of ISPs [75].

Our results also suggest that organizations need to make a concerted efort to ward of rationalizations of ISP violations and recognize that these rationalizations are malleable. SETA programs can again be influential in this regard, by articulating that ISP violations can never be justified regardless of day-to-day circumstances. ISPs and SETA programs can also emphasize the positive aspects of security require ments such that these administrative and technical controls contribute to overall societal welfare by protecting citizens’ sensitive information.

Further, organizations need to give strong attention to ways to counter the frustration that can emanate from SRS. One potential mechanism for reducing such frustration is involving employees in the planning, design, and implementation of security requirements. Based on the buy-in theory of user participation in systems development [76], participating in the development of security requirements should make employees more accepting of their hindering efects and less blameful of management, thus reducing frustration. Examples of user participation in this context include serving on committees, helping to identify vulnerabilities and areas where segregation of duties is necessary, testing new security requirements, providing feedback to management, and communication of security changes to coworkers [12,76].

Eforts to alleviate SRS and its negative emotional reactions should also tap into the knowledge base of human resource leaders. To this end, organizations could train employees on emotion management strategies for dealing with the frustration and fatigue that they ex perience from SRS. As our results suggest that neutralization is one avenue in which employees cope with these negative emotions, organizations need to make a concerted efort to promote more positive coping strategies (e.g., mental relaxation techniques, positive thoughts). Indeed, prior research shows that positive emotion regulation strategies can be efective in organizational settings and are fairly easy to instill [77].

## 9. Limitations and future research

It is important to consider the following limitations of this study, some of which point to avenues for future research. The first limitation is that all measures were self-reported, which raises concerns about CMV and social desirability bias. The latter issue is acute with respect to our self-reported ISP compliance measure. We considered using otherreports $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ , supervisor and/or coworker ratings) of ISP compliance but decided against this for the following reasons. First, ISP compliance is unique in that supervisors and coworkers may not have adequate opportunity to observe employees engaging in this behavior; indeed, most security-related behaviors are not readily observable and go undetected. Second, the demanding nature of an ESM design makes obtaining concurrent other-reports extremely dificult. We should also note that participants in our study were ensured anonymity (only the market research firm had their personal information), which helps mitigate socially desirable responding.

With respect to the CMV issue, statistical tests (see Appendix C) revealed that it was not prevalent in our study. Also, we centered the variables relative to individuals’ mean scores (i.e., within-individual relations were net of any between-individual diferences), and therefore, many of the usual CMV explanations, such as personality con founds and individual response tendencies, are ruled out [78].

A related issue is the inability to afirm causality in our model because the variables were collected each day at the same time. Although it would have been possible to have respondents complete multiple surveys per day (at diferent times and with diferent constructs being measured), and thereby achieve temporal separation for some or all of our variables, we decided against this option for both practical and theoretical reasons. First, such a design is extremely demanding on participants, and thus not recommended for ESM studies that exceed one or two weeks [60]. Second, specific to the SRS-to-emotions re lationships, there was no sound theoretical alternative because emo tional responses to stress are thought to occur nearly instantaneously with stress appraisals [15]. These points aside, future research with alternative research designs is needed to verify the full causal chain in our model.

It is also important to reiterate that while the amount of variance explained by the constructs in our model was somewhat modest $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ ranged from .05 to .16 with the focal variables only), these were daily measures that fluctuated over the course of the study. Hence, the $\mathrm { R } ^ { \dot { 2 } }$ values in our model should not be interpreted in the same manner as $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ values for stable constructs $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { i . e . } } ,$ , average values in between-individual studies). The emotional reactions to SRS and their consequences become increasingly relevant as employees experience additional SRS events over extended periods. This phenomenon is akin to Abelson’s paradox [79], in which he describes situations $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } ,$ , in baseball, the percentage of variance in any single batting performance explained by batting skill is about one-third of 1 percent) for which tiny influences cumulate to produce meaningful outcomes over time. To give an InfoSec example, forensic investigations of several large-scale data breaches have traced their roots to a progression of minor and seemingly benign (at the time) employee actions that were left unchecked [20]. Similarly, we view our results as contributing to a longer term understanding of what drives employees’ ISP noncompliance.

The compositions of our high and low subsamples for frustration and fatigue is also a potential limitation. On this point, the median values for frustration and fatigue were relatively low (6.71 and 5.33, respectively) based on their 100-point response scales. It could be that respondents had dificulty attaching meaning to the 100-point scales, which altered how they responded to these items. For example, a score of 30 may have been construed as “moderate” frustration or fatigue by some respondents, whereas others considered this same score as “low” levels of these emotions. Future research could address this issue by measuring frustration and fatigue with fixed response scales (e.g., 1 = not frustrated at all; 2 = somewhat frustrated; 3 = moderately fru strated; 4 = very frustrated; 5 = extremely frustrated). The scaling issue aside, the median values in our data suggest that many of the participants in our high frustration and high fatigue subgroups did not experience these emotional reactions to an extreme degree. Yet, organizational research indicates that even minor annoyances at work can be stressful to employees and necessitate a coping response [17,18,63]. Our results align with this view and suggest that even relatively low levels of frustration and fatigue that emanate from SRS can increase the likelihood of neutralization of ISP violations and violating behavior. That said, an improvement over our study would be an alternative research design and/or measures of SRS that elicit stronger emotional reactions to SRS, to better test the afective consequences of SRS.

Another limitation is the inconsistency in the timing of the survey invitations for participants in diferent time zones. As described, the market research firm sent the MWF survey invitations to participants after 3:00 Pacific Standard Time, which is during the course of a typical workday for participants in the Pacific and Mountain Time Zones of the United States, and closer to or at the end of the typical workday for participants in the Eastern and Central Time Zones. The MWF survey instructions stated that participants should complete each survey at or near the completion of their workday. Yet, it is possible that this was not the case, particularly for those participants from the Pacific and Mountain Time Zones. The survey responses were all time stamped based on Pacific Standard Time, so we could not include time zone dummy variables in our analyses. We did, however, take random samples of the data (which presumably contained survey responses from diferent time zones) and compared the mean values of the focal variables across the diferent samples, and the results were not significant. Hence, we do not believe the time zone issue to be a serious concern.

In terms of future research, our results suggest additional interesting avenues. First, as opposed to the general ISP compliance that is the subject of this research, future studies could test whether our model translates to more specific forms of ISP compliance such as compliance with password policies or Internet usage policies. Second, there is evidence that negative emotional reactions to frequently occurring daily hassles contribute to long-term distress [80] hence, future research could explore the longer term mental and physical health consequences of SRS. It would also be interesting to determine whether employee

continue to employ neutralization as an emotion-focused coping response to SRS over longer periods or whether their coping eventually morphs into problem-focused strategies that attempt to address SRS in a more direct fashion. Also, our model was limited to two discrete emotions – frustration and fatigue – that we chose based on the characteristics of SRS, as they relate to coping theory, and based on findings from the InfoSec and organizational literatures. Other emotional reactions to SRS could reasonably be considered, including some from Beaudry and Pinsonneault’s [45] classification of emotional responses to IT events. We leave it to future research to develop and test variations of our model that incorporate additional emotional reactions to SRS and their role in the coping process.

## Appendix C. Measurement Model and Common Method Variance (CMV) Analyses

We assessed the psychometric properties our multi-item scales through conventional tests of convergent validity, discriminant validity, and reliability [82]. We used the WarpPLS 5.0 software package for these tests. For convergent validity, all factor loadings should exceed 0.70, and average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct should exceed 0.50. For discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE for each construct should be greater than the interconstruct correlations, and items should load more strongly on their corresponding construct than on other constructs (i.e., at least .10 higher). For reliability, the recommended threshold is .70 for both Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability. As shown in Tables C1–C3, these conditions were met for all constructs.

To statistically test for the efects of CMV in our results, we used the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) marker variable technique [83]. We used the EQS 6.2 software package for this analysis (with maximum likelihood estimation selected). The a priori marker variable we used was a three-item measure (α = .82) of outside activity (OA; see the table in Appendix B) that is theoretically unrelated to the study constructs. The OA scale wa included on each MWF survey and its average correlation with the other variables was .03.

Following the steps described in [83], we first ran a CFA model that consisted of the indicators for the five focal study variables (SRS, FRUS, FATIGUE, NEUT, and COMP), and the OA variable in order to generate factor loadings and measurement error variance estimates for the three marker variable indicators. A baseline model, which used these factor loadings and error variances as fixed values for the marker variable indicators, was then compared with an alternative model (called the Method-C model) with additional factor loadings from the latent marker variable to each ot the remaining nonmarker variable indicators in the model. These additional factor loadings in the Method-C model were forced to be equal to each other based on the assumption of equal method efects across the study variables. The chi-square diference between the baseline and Method-C model was not significant $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } = 2 . 8 9 , \mathrm { d f } = 1 , \mathrm { n . } s . )$ , which suggests that CMV was not problematic. We also point to the combination of positive and negative correlations in our data (Table C3), such as the positive correlation between FRUS and NEUT and negative correlation between NEUT and COMP, as evidence that CMV was not prevalent, at least in terms of consistency motif and acquiescence biases [62].

His research interests include information assurance and security, IT risk management, and computer ethics. His research appears in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, and European Journal of Information Systems, among others. He serves as Associate Editor at MIS Quarterly.

Pei-Lee Teh is Associate Professor at the School of Business, Monash University, Malaysia.
