# More than a feeling: Investigating the contagious efect of facial emotional expressions on investment decisions in reward-based crowdfunding

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Reward-based crowdfunding Funding behavior Emotional contagion Facial emotional expressions Data-driven analysis

## A B S T R A C T

Reward-based crowdfunding provides a unique instrument to acquire capital from small investors for a specific project. To successfully campaign for the desired funds, however, entrepreneurs also need to understand the subconscious factors, which influence the funding behavior of potential investors. In pursuit of that goal, this study examines how facial emotional expressions, which are shown in the pictures of project presentations, afect the funding decision. Building upon the emotional contagion theory and evaluating the emotions displayed on the pictures of 18,696 project webpages with a machine-learning algorithm, we show that facial expressions of happiness and sadness positively affect (i.e., encourage) the funding decision. Other than in venture capital markets, an emotional approach of potential investors might hence be a viable strategy in reward-based crowdfunding. As persuasion tool, emotions should nevertheless be used judiciously since we found high intensities of facial emotional expressions to negatively afect the funding decision

## 1. Introduction

With a volume already exceeding that of the venture capital market and supposedly reaching over 93 billion USD in 2025 [1], the crowdfunding market particularly provides an opportunity for entrepreneurs to source capital from the crowd to realize innovative business ideas, which may not yet appeal to venture capitalists or angel investors. While the concepts of equity- and lending-based crowdfunding have similarities to acquiring venture capital, reward-based crowdfunding introduces a new funding mechanism. Here, investors do not contribute money to help starting or growing a business, but to realize a specific project. In return, they receive a reward, whose value typically reflects the amount of contributed money. Reward-based crowdfunding hence allows entrepreneurs to attract investors that are specifically interested in an advertised project [2]. Thereby, the funding decision usually is not encouraged by a business plan, but by the appeals of the project, which are highlighted in the text, pictures, and videos of the project presentation [3].

Together with its increasing popularity, however, the reward-based crowdfunding market also has become more competitive. This led to a notable decrease of the success probability of individual campaigns, which for example dropped from 43% to 37% on Kickstarter in only three years [4,5]. In a competitive environment, knowledge of how the project presentation should be framed to encourage the funding decision becomes particularly important. Prior research has found that several characteristics of a project presentation can influence the funding decision. Most studies thereby focus on factors such as the scarcity of the rewards [6], the text length [7], or the number of pictures [8], which are consciously processed by decision-makers. Few studies have analyzed the impact of subconscious factors like emotional expressions, which might trigger an afective reaction [9].

Generally, decision research has established that the afective state of decision-makers can have a considerable impact on their decisions [10]. While studies from the venture capital domain suggest that emotional expressions (like the entrepreneur's passion) do not influence investment decisions [11], they might be more efective in rewardbased crowdfunding for two reasons: Other than professional investors, backers of reward-based crowdfunding projects are not due-diligence experts, who have been trained not to let emotions interfere with their judgements [11]. Moreover, they seem not only to be driven by rational economic concerns, but also by the desire to put an interesting idea to life [12]. They might therefore be more susceptible to an emotional project presentation.

So far, however, it is not well understood how emotional expressions can influence the investment decision in reward-based crowdfunding. While literature provides some indications that projects whose presentation contains emotional expressions could have a higher chance of success [8,13,14], the phenomenon has not been investigated systematically yet. The efects of diferent types and intensities of emotional expressions are therefore largely unclear. This is especially true for the impact of visual emotional expressions contained in the pictures of project presentations, which has not been studied at all. Although pictures form an important part of the project presentations and can communicate emotional expressions particularly well [15], it remains unclear how they can be strategically used to influence the funding decision.

To help closing this research gap, we present the results of a study, in which we systematically examined the impact that facial emotional expressions in the pictures of project presentations have on the funding decision of potential investors. We focus on facial expressions (as a specific kind of visual emotional expression), since they are a primary biological means to display emotions and are recognized eficiently on pictures by human recipients [16]. Building upon the emotional contagion theory [17], which describes how and under what circumstances facial emotional expressions can influence the afective state of recipients, we particularly examine the following research questions: How do facial emotional expressions in the pictures of reward-based crowdfunding project presentations influence the funding decision? Which impact do diferent kinds of emotions have?

To answer the questions, we crawled the presentations of 18,696 reward-based crowdfunding projects published on Kickstarter. On average, they contained 8.7 pictures. We systematically evaluated the facial emotional expressions shown in these pictures using a machinelearning algorithm that recognizes faces and expressed emotions. The study results indicate that the depicted facial emotional expressions can influence the investment decision of potential backers. They also provide insights how the efect changes depending on the type and in tensity of the displayed emotion. The findings complement prior deci sion research, which has identified several decision-relevant factors of project presentations, and can help entrepreneurs to optimize their presentations accordingly.

We proceed as follows: in section 2, we describe the research gap and the theoretical background of the study in detail. In section 3, we derive the hypotheses guiding our analysis and specify the research model. The research approach and examined data are characterized in section 4. In section 5, we describe the study results. The findings as well as the implications for academia and practice are discussed in section 6. Section 7 contains conclusions and future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Related work

Prior studies in the field of decision research and related areas have identified several characteristics of the presentation, which can influ ence the investment decision in reward-based crowdfunding. They particularly found a detailed narrative to be important as it better transports the idea and reduces uncertainty. This is reflected by factors such as the length of the description [7], the number of pictures [8], or the availability of an introductory video [3]. Similarly, presenting a number of FAQ items [18] and posting timely updates [3] can have a positive impact on the funding decision since they signal high project quality and involvement. In contrast to such factors, which are typically evaluated on a factual level by potential investors, less is known about the impact of subconscious factors like emotional expressions, which might trigger an afective reaction.

So far, mainly the efect of textual emotional expressions on the investment decision has been studied. Mitra and Gilbert [13] found that using emotional words in the project description is positively related to funding success. Koch and Siering [8] emphasize that using more positive than negative emotional words can increase funding success. Zhou et al. [19] confirmed this efect, but also found indications that an excessive usage of emotional words might have a negative impact. In contrast, the efects of visual and vocal emotional expressions have rarely been studied. Li et al. [14] and Davis et al. [20] found that the passion displayed by the founders in the introductory video can impact the amount of intended funding [14,20]. However, the external validity of the findings is limited as only the intention to fund the project was studied and the data stems from controlled experiments, which underrepresent the financial risks involved in real investment decisions [11].

While they indicate an efectiveness of emotional expressions, it remains largely unclear how diferent types of emotions, intensities, and media impact the funding decision. The most detailed knowledge available deals with the efects of textual emotional expressions. But even these effects have only been studied based on a rough distinction of positive vs. negative emotional words and, if at all, moderate vs. excessive intensities. The efects of visual emotional expressions are even less clear. Especially the efects of emotional expressions shown in pictures of project presentations have not been examined at all, although they communicate emotions very efectively [15] and make up a major part of the presentations. Against this background, our study aims to contribute to a more systematic understanding of how emotional expressions influence the funding decision. By analyzing facial emotional expressions in the pictures of project presentations, we moreover focus on an unexplored but potentially very efective study subject.

## 2.2. Emotional contagion theory

To understand how and why facial emotional expressions shown in the pictures of project presentations can trigger afective reactions that influence the funding decision of potential investors, we refer to the emotional contagion theory [17] from social psychology. It suggests that a person's facial emotional expressions can have a contagious efect on others since emotional expressions flow from the sender to the recipients, who ‘catch’ the expressed emotions and develop feelings similar to those of the sender [21]. Emotional contagion typically is described as an autonomous, mostly subconscious process that happens within the recipient and consists of the stages mimicry, feedback, and contagion [17]. It is triggered when a recipient observes and processes another person's facial emotional expression (see Fig. 1). Basically, it can occur both in face-to-face conversations and, as in our case, when watching videos or pictures that show other people [16,22].

Emotional contagion starts with the mimicry stage. Generally, individuals tend to imitate and synchronize their non-verbal behavior, including their emotional expressions, with that of the observed person [21]. For instance, they start yawning, smiling, or looking sad when seeing the observed person yawn, smile, or look sad. Research in social neuroscience suggests that the observation of another person's facial emotional expression automatically activates autonomic and somatic responses, which lead to its mimicry by the observer [23]. During the feedback stage, the mimicry begins to afect the emotional experience of the observer. The neural feedback of the muscular, visceral, glandular, and other responses triggers the observer's emotional brain systems, which partially activate the associated emotional state in himself/herself to infer the expressed emotion [23]. During the contagion stage, the emotional sensation resulting from the mental processing evokes an afective state in the observer that matches the mimicked emotional expression [17,21]. Regarding the example, this means that the observer not only looks happy or sad but indeed feels that way.

While the mimicry of facial emotional expressions appears to be an innate, ubiquitous phenomenon, it was found that the outcome of the emotional contagion process varies depending upon the social context [21]. The contagiousness of facial emotional expressions hence seems to be determined by context-specific factors [24]. Extant research especially found three factors to influence the outcome of the emotional contagion process: the nature of the relationship between observer and expresser, the nature of the event eliciting the emotions, and the expressed emotion itself [10,25,26]. Prior studies in social psychology found that individuals are more likely to catch the emotions of others if they love, like, or identify with them or share their goals [26,27]. Emotions related to personal and socially close relationships accordingly seem to be more contagious than those related to relationships between professionals or strangers, which are characterized by higher social distance. The emotional expressions of others also seem to unfold a more contagious efect if the event eliciting the emotions is ambiguous in nature and can be interpreted in diferent ways [25]. In such a context, emotional contagion can help to disambiguate and synchronize an interaction by delivering additional information about the ex presser's intentions and appraisal of the situation [28]. A third determinant of emotional contagion appears to be the kind and intensity of the expressed emotion [10,29]. Literature suggests that some kinds of emotions are more contagious in nature than others. Prior research also found that the contagiousness of emotions might change depending on how intensively (i.e., strongly and frequently) they are expressed. As sumptions about the outcome of the emotional contagion process can therefore only be made considering its social context.

## 2.3. The impact of emotions on decision-making behavior

While the main purpose of emotional contagion seems to be to facilitate social interactions by delivering additional information about the expresser's appraisal of a situation [16,28], the aroused emotional state can also influence the observer's attitude and decision-making behavior [21,25]. Prior decision research found that immediate emo tions, which are experienced at the time of decision-making, can alter the decision-maker's expectations of the probability or desirability of consequences and change the way in which the consequences are processed [10]. For example, a state of happiness is associated with more optimistic judgements and heuristic processing [10,30].

According to the literature, immediate emotions seem to be parti cularly efective in scenarios, in which the expected consequences of decisions are afectively charged. Accordingly, they have been found to influence social, personal, and economic decisions [30,31]. Prior studies have shown that immediate emotions can, for instance, influence the evaluation of unfamiliar consumer products [10,32]. If deliberately triggered, emotional contagion might hence likewise serve a means of persuasive communication [25] in reward-based crowdfunding.

## 3. Hypotheses development and research model

Against this background, we propose a research model to systematically investigate the contagious efect of displayed facial emotional expressions in reward-based crowdfunding. We assume that displayed facial emotional expressions unfold a contagious efect, which influences the funding decision of potential investors. To obtain more de tailed insights, we also make an assumption of how the efect develops depending on the intensity of emotional expressions. Likewise, we argue how the facial expression of two diferent basic emotions will afect the funding decision.

## 3.1. Efects of facial emotional expressions on the funding decision

While browsing the project presentation to make a funding decision, potential investors notice the facial emotional expressions shown in the pictures. According to the emotional contagion theory, this will automatically trigger the first stage of the emotional contagion process (Fig. 1), during which the investors subconsciously begin to imitate the observed facial expressions. In stage two, the neural feedback of the muscular movement triggers the emotional brain systems of the investors, which will partially activate the associated afective state to infer the emotion. This activity eventually lets the investors feel an emotional state that is consistent with the observed emotional expression in the last stage. Resonating as an immediate emotion while the potential investor continues to browse the project webpage, it can influence his/her decision-making.

However, the impact of this process on the afective state of po tential investors depends on additional factors that can promote or inhibit emotional contagion (see section 2). Research has shown that recipients are more susceptible to the emotional expressions of others if they are related to more personally motivated, empathic relationship [26,27]. Regarding this criterion, we assume that a potential investor who inspects a project presentation has a basic interest in the described idea and/or reward. While his/her relationship to the entrepreneur is mostly professional in nature, it also seems to be characterized by empathy, since backers of reward-based crowdfunding projects are not strictly driven by rational concerns, but also by the wish to put an interesting idea to life [4,33].

Research has also shown that the contagiousness of emotional ex pressions depends on the nature of the event, which elicits the emotion. The contemplation of the webpage to make a funding decision is characterized by a considerable level of uncertainty. In particular, potential investors have to cope with information asymmetry and ambiguity when contemplating their funding decision, as the project idea is typically not described in full technical detail and the promised reward cannot be experienced yet [2,12]. Prior studies in social psychology suggest that emotional expressions might be particularly contagious in such a scenario as they help to clarify the meaning of utterances in ambiguous events and thus facilitate the recipient's appraisal of the situation [28,34].

Based on these arguments, we assume that the facial emotional expressions displayed in the pictures of the project presentation will impact the afective state of potential investors and, consequently, influence their funding decisions. However, research in social psychology found that the processing and appraisal of emotional expressions can vary considerably depending on their intensity (i.e., their strength and frequency). While emotional contagion occurs as a result of subconscious, mimicry-based processing, humans can also process emotional expressions in a conscious, inferential manner. Such a process particularly seems to be activated if the intensity of emotional expressions does not correspond to situational norms or the expectations of the recipient [34].

With respect to professional interaction contexts, extant findings suggest that high intensities of emotional expressions will be felt as inappropriate and interpreted as less authentic and trustworthy, since the rationality of the sender is found to be reduced [34,35]. The results of the inferential process hence seem to counteract and ultimately even dominate the efects of the emotional contagion process. Since the relationship between entrepreneurs and investors is mostly professional in nature, we assume that increasing intensities of emotional expressions will eventually trigger such a counteracting efect. Taken together, we therefore expect a concave curvilinear impact:

H0. : Facial expressions in project pictures have a U-shaped efect on the funding decision.

## 3.2. Efects of diferent kinds of emotions on funding behavior

Prior studies in social psychology suggest that the efect of an emotional expression also depends on the type of the expressed emotion [16,34]. To adequately consider this factor, we separately examine the efects of diferent types of emotions. Basic emotion theory suggests that humans experience a small number of psychologically basic emotions that are combined in various proportions to produce higher-orde emotions such as passion [36]. Basic emotions are often used to sys tematically study the efects of diferent types of emotions. We decided to examine the efects of happiness and sadness as they account for both positive and negative basic emotions regarding the valence [25]. Both moreover have a high potential to influence economic decisions [32,37].

Individuals typically express happiness if they are satisfied with the way things are going, after reaching a goal, or if they appraise a situation as favorable [35]. The emotional contagion process will likely evoke similar feelings in potential investors. Research in decisionmaking has found that happy feelings can lead to a more positive appraisal of the current situation and motivate the decision-maker to maintain the positive emotional state, for instance by interacting in more generous, friendly ways [29]. Happy feelings can also lead to a more positive appraisal of a presented object of choice (and characteristics like its perceived value) [32] and a more optimistic, heuristic decision-making [10]. We therefore assume that facial expressions of happiness in the project pictures trigger a more positive appraisal of the project idea and/or reward(s) as well as a more generous, optimistic action of potential investors, thus positively afecting their funding decision. Considering H0, we assume that this efect will occur until a turning point is exceeded and hypothesize:

H1. : Facial expressions of happiness have an inverted U-shaped efect on the funding decision.

Facial emotional expressions of sadness, in contrast, are typically expressed when individuals realize that something of value is lost or an undesirable condition is not subject to change [29,35]. The emotional contagion process will likely arouse similar feelings in the potential investors. Research in decision-making has found that sad feelings can lead to a more pessimistic appraisal of the current situation and moti vate individuals to escape this emotional state by taking compensatory “emotion-relieving actions” [10]. Several studies have, for instance, found that individuals tend to spend more money on consumption goods in such scenarios, a phenomenon called the misery-is-not-miserly efect [37]. Literature also indicates that recipients who catch sad emotional expressions often decide to acquire rewarding outcomes to reduce negative feelings, which can result in higher choice prices [22,37]. Sad facial emotional expressions moreover signal that the sender is in need of support, motivating recipients to sympathize with the sender and help changing his/her adverse circumstances [16,35,37]. We therefore assume that facial expressions of sadness in the project pictures trigger a more supportive behavior of potential backers. The perspective of receiving a reward might furthermore present itself a rewarding, emotion-relieving action, thus positively afecting their funding decision. Considering H0, we accordingly hypothesize:

H2. : Facial expressions of sadness have an inverted U-shaped efect on the funding decision.

## 4. Methods and data

To evaluate our research model, we followed a data-driven research approach [3,4]. The required data was collected from Kickstarter, one of the leading reward-based crowdfunding platforms [5]. The platform follows the “all-or-nothing” business model, in which a minimum pledge goal must be reached within a limited timeframe. This means that Kickstarter only transfers the accumulated funds if the founder manages to reach the targeted minimum pledge goal. Otherwise, the money is returned to the backers [3]. We used a self-developed web crawler to search the project webpages and gather the publicly avail able founder- and project-related information from all (successful and unsuccessful) projects in-between September 2016 and May 2017. The procedure led to a data set containing information about 19,452 projects. To limit the efects of extreme values, we followed the approach of Mollick [3] and excluded projects with a targeted pledge goal of over \$200,000. Projects that described the objective in less than 100 words and that had a project duration of less than seven days were also removed, since such projects usually do not show a serious efort to raise funds. All variables were furthermore winsorized at their top and bottom 1% [38]. The modified data set contained 18,696 projects with an overall success rate of 43%. This rate is slightly higher than the rate of 37%, which was oficially reported at the time we gathered the data [5], because our procedure mostly excluded unsuccessful projects. However, the data set still represented the typical distribution of pro jects based on their categories on Kickstarter [5].

## 4.1. Dependent variabl

To measure the impact of facial emotional expressions on the funding decision of potential investors, we analyzed the total amount of funding raised at the end of the campaign as dependent variable. Regardless of whether the campaign reached its targeted pledge goal or not, this variable reflects the persuasive power of the project presentation and the emotional expressions contained therein. In parti cular, the variable funding raised represents the investors' willingness to fund the project and, therefore, their decision to financially support the entrepreneurs' idea. The chosen measurement furthermore enables us to interpret our results in line with previous studies in the reward-based crowdfunding domain [e.g., 14, 39].

## 4.2. Independent variables

To analyze our independent variables, we measured the amount of happy and sad facial expressions that are displayed in the pictures of each crowdfunding project. In a first step, the facial emotional expressions were identified from the pictures using a state-of-the-art machine learning algorithm, which is part of Microsoft's Cognitive Services and was accessed using the Emotion API [40]. The algorithm recognizes human faces that are shown in the pictures and characterize their emotional expression using a vector of confidence scores for seven basic emotions: anger, contempt, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, and surprise. The scores are calculated and normalized to values between zero and one separately for each face. A value closer to “one” thereby represents a more dominantly expressed emotion. For instance, if the API returns the score “one” for happiness, the depicted face likely shows a bright smile. With an error rate of 3.6% during the ImageNet test [41], the API can be considered as an efective tool to identify facial emotional expressions in pictures.

Overall, we analyzed 165,776 pictures. The scores for happiness and sadness accounted for 88% of the identified facial emotional expressions. In case the algorithm detected more than one face in a picture, we calculated aggregated emotional scores for the picture by adding up the individual scores. In so doing, we considered that more faces can display higher intensities of emotional expressions. Likewise, we added up the aggregated happiness and sadness scores of all pictures to obtain the total scores for each project. To check for the expected curvilinear efect of the intensity of facial emotional expressions on the funding decisions, we calculated the quadratic terms of the total happiness and sadness scores which represent high intensities [19]. Fig. 3 shows a sample project webpage with two pictures as an example. The first picture depicts a face that expresses happiness with a bright smile. The second picture contains three faces. The left shows a slight smile, the middle one is neutral, and the right shows a bright smile. The algorithm quantifies the bright smile as a happiness score of 1.0, does not assign a score on the neutral face, and rates the slight smile with 0.65. The aggregated happiness score of this example is the sum of the values (i.e., 2.65).

## 4.3. Control variables

We included several founder- and project-related control variables into our research model. To control for variables characterizing the engagement of the founders, we considered the number of team mem bers, since a larger team signals productivity and efectiveness [33]. In addition, we controlled for the number of provided FAQ items [18], a quick update of the project status [3], and the publishing of a quick comment [42], since providing a detailed and informative webpage with frequent updates and timely responses sends a positive signal to potential backers. Such actions demonstrate that the founders are passionate to participate with the community. Next to the engagement of the founders, we controlled for variables that characterize the information contained in the project presentation. We considered the number of words [19], the number of pictures [8], the duration of the introductory video [13], the project duration and the pledge goal [3]. We also included variables to account for the efect of emotional expressions that are contained in the introductory video and project de scription. First, we measured how long faces were visible in the introductory video using the Video Indexer API [40]. We used these measurements as an indicator for potential facial emotional expressions. Second, we controlled for the number of positive and negative emotional words contained in the project description [19]. These words were measured using the text-analysis software LIWC 2015 [43]. As the kind of relationship between the entrepreneur and potential backers might differ depending on the type of the project. we also controlled for the project category. Following Gafni et al. [44], we diferentiated between more utilitarian, technology related campaigns and more hedonistic, artistically related campaigns. Projects belonging to the Kick starter categories technology, design, or games were assigned to the first category. The other projects were grouped into the second category. ## 4.4. Data analysis approach

We tested our hypotheses using ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions with funding raised as dependent variable. An analysis of the data set revealed that some variables followed a right skewed distribution and additionally contained values of zero, including our dependent variable. As they varied widely in range, we conducted an inverse hyperbolic sine transformation on variables with a skewness greater than two to normalize them: sinh $^ { - 1 } ( \mathrm { y } ) = \log ( \mathrm { y _ { i } } + ( \mathrm { y _ { i } } ^ { 2 } + 1 ) ^ { 1 / 2 } )$ [39]. We decided to apply this kind of transformation to preserve the interpretation of the zero values. These values are meaningful data points as they represent the absence of facial emotional expressions. Based on the transformed terms, we calculated the squared terms of happiness and sadness.

We tested our hypotheses using a hierarchical approach to analyze to what extent the independent variables explain the dependent variable. Model 1 only includes the control variables. ## 5.1. Hypotheses testing

Table 3 summarizes the results of our hierarchical regression analyses. The results for Model 1, which contains only the control variables, are in line with previous studies that analyzed comparable founder- and project-related variables regarding their impact on the funding amount [e.g., 14, 39]. Model 2 includes the efects of happiness and sadness on total funding raised. Our results show a positive and significant impact of the happiness term $( \beta = 0 . 2 4 5 , p < . 0 0 1 )$ and a non-significant impact of the sadness term $( \beta \ = \ 0 . 0 2 1 , p \ > \ . 0 5 )$ . It seems the expected negative efect for high intensities of sadness compensates the expected positive efect in Model 2. To test if the model fit improved after including the independent variables, we compared the models using an F-test and the Bavesian information criteria (BIC). The results show that the addition of the independent variables happiness and sadness improved the mode fitness $( \mathrm { F - T e s t } \chi ^ { 2 } ( 2 , $ $N = 1 8 , 6 8 0 ) = 6 7 . 0 1 , p \ < \ . 0 0 1 )$ . As suggested by Lind and Mehlum [46], we followed a three-step procedure to formally establish the existence of an inverted U-shape relationship: First, the squared term has to be negative and significant. Second, the slopes at the end of each data range must be suficiently steep. Third, the turning point must be situated within the data range. ## 5.2. Robustness checks

We conducted various robustness checks to verify the stability of our results and rule out alternative reasons for the observed efects. To test the robustness of our results regarding variations in the operationalization of our independent variables, we analyzed an alternative approach. This approach does not summarize the emotional expression scores when more than one face is recognized in a picture. Instead, it calculates the mean score of all emotional expressions per picture (i.e., it divides the aggregated emotional scores by the number of faces per picture). In so doing, it normalizes the emotional scores per picture. To determine the project scores, we then summed up the mean scores of all pictures. Referring to the example in Fig. 3, this would lead to a calculated happiness score of 1.55 (first picture 1.0; second picture 0.55). As Model 4 (Table A.2, Appendix) shows, the estimation results remain consistent with the reported values.

## 5.3. Post-hoc analysis

Our study demonstrates that both facial expressions of happiness and sadness have an inverted U-shaped relationship with the total funding amount. To compare the efects of expressing happiness and sadness, we conducted a paired-t-test. The results show that displaying facial emotional expressions of happiness has a significant higher efect on the predicted amount raised than displaying facial emotional expressions of sadness (p < .000, test details see Appendix). We furthermore analyzed our results with respect to the project category as prior studies have shown that the project presentation and the resulting kind of relationship between the entrepreneur and potential backers might difer depending on the type of the project [44]. Therefore, we distinguished between more utilitarian, technology related and more hedonistic, artistically related projects. To do so, we constructed a binary variable to split our data set into two diferent subsamples. For artistically related projects, the results indicate that both happiness and sadness scores have an inverted U-shaped relationship with the total funding raised (Model 7 in Table A.2, Appendix). While happiness also has an inverted U-shaped relationship for technology related projects, we could not confirm such an efect for sadness (Model 8 in Table A.2, Appendix). We speculate that facial emotional expressions of sadness might be more contagious in artistically related projects, since their presentations tend to be more personal and focus more on the entrepreneur [44]. In such a context, emotions that motivate recipients to sympathize with the sender and act in a supportive way might hence be more efective than in presentations, which tend to center around the utility of the project idea.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Key findings

The study results suggest that the facial emotional expressions shown in the pictures of project presentations can influence the funding decision in reward-based crowdfunding. Apparently, backers do not only base their funding decisions on rational arguments, but are also susceptible to visual emotional expressions and their contagious influence. A reason for the contagiousness of the expressed emotions could be the relationship between backer and entrepreneur, which is mostly professional in nature, but also characterized by empathy due to common goals and the desire to bring interesting ideas to life. As the funding decision must be made with uncertainty, emotional expressions may moreover help to disambiguate the situation and understand the entrepreneur's intentions.

However, we found that the contagious influence of facial emotional expressions cannot be increased indefinitely. It rather seems to be counteracted and eventually dominated by a negative appraisal of highly emotional presentations, which has also been observed in other professional relationships. Overall, the display of facial emotional expressions in the pictures of project presentations thus seems to have a U-shaped influence on backers' funding decisions. Presentations with medium intensities of facial emotional expressions should accordingly unfold the greatest impact.

The study results moreover suggest that the contagious efect difer depending on the valence and arousal of the expressed emotions. Admittedly, we found that both facial emotional expressions of happi ness (as a positive emotion) and sadness (as a negative emotion) have a positive, encouraging efect on the funding decision. Yet, there are two indications that sad facial expressions might be less contagious than their counterparts: First, we found the display of sad facial expressions to have a smaller impact on the total funding amount. Second, the efect caused by the display of sad expressions reached its turning point earlier, which means that they should be used less intensively. The results also suggest that expressions of sadness might be less versatile as an instrument of persuasion since they mainly seem to influence the funding decision in artistically related projects. The contagious efect of emotions might therefore also difer depending on the type of project.

## 6.2. Implications for academia

The results of our study contribute to a better understanding of the factors that influence the investment decision in reward-based crowd funding. In particular, we provide novel insights into the efects of visual emotional expressions, which so far have not been studied in detail. With the emotional contagion theory, we propose a rich theoretical lens that can explain the influence of the visual emotional expressions contained in the project presentation in relation to the context of the interaction. It allowed us to explain the efect of facial emotional expressions in terms of the relationship between entrepreneur and in vestors, the uncertainty of the decision-making process and the nature and intensity of the expressed emotions. These dimensions could provide a basis to study the efects of other kinds of visual emotional expressions such as postures or gestures in detail.

Our study moreover is the first to explore the efects of visual emotional expressions that are shown in the pictures of project presentations. So far, extant decision research only investigated what efect the number of the pictures in the presentation has on the success of reward-based crowdfunding projects. The findings of the according studies remained inconclusive, however. While some studies found that the number of pictures was positively related with crowdfunding success [19,47], others identified no or even a negative efect [8,33,48]. The results of our study complement these findings with a content-oriented perspective and provide a first explanation how pictures can be used as an efective means of communication in reward-based crowd funding.

Our study also contributes to the stream of research that examines the efects of emotional expressions in the text and video of the project presentation. In particular, our results suggest that the efect of emotional expressions cannot be increased indefinitely, but is U-shaped in nature. The findings of our study also suggest that the efect of emo tional expressions depends on the expressed emotion itself. While prior studies in reward-based crowdfunding only broadly diferentiated between positive and negative emotions and ignored the latter com pletely, our findings indicate that negative emotions can also unfold a positive influence on the funding behavior. Our findings hence call for a more specific investigation of the efects of diferent basic emotions and intensities.

## 6.3. Implications for practice

In practice, the study results inform founders of reward-based crowdfunding projects of the influence that an emotional presentation has on the funding decision. As the reward-based crowdfunding market gains in competitiveness, it becomes important for founders to better understand how the project presentation can be efectively framed to convince potential backers to invest into their project. The results of our study show how the pictures of project presentations can be actively used as a medium to communicate emotional expressions. Other than in the venture capital domain, such an approach obviously increases the emotional appeal of the project idea and/or the reward.

As the efects of diferent types of emotional expressions can vary across the project categories (e.g., artistically vs. technology related projects), founders should integrate pictures with situationally appropriate visual emotional expressions into their project presentation. To acquire funding for the realization of a new product, entrepreneurs could for instance display happy people experiencing and/or showing the promised reward (see Fig. 3). The entrepreneurs could also depict themselves with a happy smile. As such emotions afects the recipients' emotional state, it is likely that potential investors may then also per ceive the proposed product presentation more positive.

Yet, the results of our study also signal that emotional expressions should not be overused as the positive efect reverses with rising intensity levels. As a means of persuasive communication, they should hence complement the project presentation rather than dominate it. If used appropriately, however, facial emotional expressions can serve as an efective means to encourage potential backers to invest into the project. As facial emotional expressions currently seem to be displayed rather seldom (presentations in our sample only contained 0.92 pictures with facial expressions on average), the results of our study also afect the operators of crowdfunding platforms, who could advise founders how to use the project presentation to emotionally approach backers.

## 6.4. Limitations

Several limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the presented results. Most importantly, we could not provide empirical evidence that the examined facial emotional expressions indeed triggered an emotional contagion process. As the studied scenario falls under the scope and the observed results correspond well to the predictions of the emotional contagion theory, we assume that it provides an adequate lens to explain the observed phenomenon. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that alternative explanations for the phenomenon might exist as well.

Our data gathering and analysis procedure leads to further limitations. So far, we only analyzed data from one reward-based crowdfunding platform, which employs the “all-or-nothing” business model (cf. section 4). There also exist reward-based crowdfunding platforms with difering business models. Indiegogo, for instance, implements the “keep-it-all” business model, in which founders receive the already pledged funds even if the campaign fails to reach the pledge goal. Since backers face a substantially higher financial risk on such platforms, the presented results may not be straightforwardly generalizable. Due to the complexity of the data and the required run-time of our machine learning approach, we furthermore decided to investigate projects that were published within a period of less than a year. While the summary statistics of our data sample revealed no critical anomalies, future research is recommended to verify the validity of findings over an extended period.

Although we have taken care to minimize the risk of omitted-variable bias by including several control variables, it is possible that the efects of facial emotional expressions partially have to be attributed to other factors. In particular, we could not measure the perceived innovativeness or trustworthiness of the projects, which have been found to be relevant determinants of the project success. While our analysis included the duration, in which people are shown in the introductory video, as a proxy for the influence of the emotional expressions it contains, we were furthermore not able to analyze the displayed emotional content directly. Future research should therefore re-evaluate our findings and take additional factors into account that we could not analyze in this study.

## 7. Conclusion

The market for reward-based crowdfunding is quickly gaining in size and competitiveness. To support the setup of successful rewardbased crowdfunding campaigns in such an environment, knowledge of how the project presentation should be framed to maximize the impact on the funding decision of potential investors becomes particularly important [9]. Prior decision research has already identified several relevant factors such as the structure and scarcity of the reward(s) [6], the length of the text [7], the number of included pictures [8], or the social engagement of the founder(s) [49]. The presented study contributes to this research stream and provides complex insights into the efect of (facial) emotional expressions, a factor that so far hardly has been examined in detail.

The results of our study show that displaying facial emotional expressions of happiness and sadness in the pictures of project presentations can positively afect the funding decision of potential backers. The integration of pictures with facial emotional expressions hence provides an additional instrument to encourage the investment decision of backers in the reward-based crowdfunding domain. We also describe how the efect changes with diferent intensity levels of emotional expressions and provide details about the efectiveness of specific emotional expressions for the presentation of diferent types of projects.

The results of our study ofer several opportunities for future research. Apart from re-evaluating our results, future studies could also examine the efect of other basic emotions such as fear, disgust, anger, or surprise. While such emotional expressions seem to be much less frequently used in the presentations of reward-based crowdfunding projects according to our data set, they might nevertheless be efective in promoting specific kinds of projects. In this context, future research could also investigate if some emotions are more efective in supporting a specific kind of project than others. As project presentations can contain facial emotional expressions of several types, forthcoming studies could also examine the efects resulting from their combination more closely. Next to facial emotional expressions, future research could also examine the impact of other emotional expressions such as gestures and body language. With the presented results, we also want to provide a starting point for such endeavors.
