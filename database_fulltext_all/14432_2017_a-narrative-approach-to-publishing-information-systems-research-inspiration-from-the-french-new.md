# A narrative approach to publishing information systems research: inspiration from the French New Novel tradition

David Avison<sup>1</sup>, Julien Malaurent<sup>1</sup> and Philippe Eynaud<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>ESSEC Business School, Ave Bernard Hirsch, 95021 Cergy Pontoise, France; <sup>2</sup>IAE, Paris 1 Panthe´on-Sorbonne (Sorbonne Business School), Paris, France

Correspondence: David Avison, ESSEC Business School, Ave Bernard Hirsch, 95021 Cergy Pontoise, France E-mail: avison@essec.edu

## Abstract

In this paper, we suggest an alternative way of producing Information Systems (IS) case studies in which researchers act as narrators of stories following the spirit and techniques of the French New Novel (FNN). As such, authors attempt to represent the richness of a problem situation. The readers’ role is more demanding compared to traditional case studies, as they have to play detectives to discover meaning from the narrative. Hence, readers need to expose different interpretations from the text rather than accept a complete story by the author or an author-provided set of alternative interpretations. Following a discussion of the FNN, we present an extract of a narrative that follows this approach. It concerns use of technology in team meetings in a research project on the potential impact of climate change on organic farming in France. Based on this example, we discuss the techniques used in its construction, ways in which the text might be interpreted, and how it may become part of publishing qualitative case study research within IS. European Journal of Information Systems (2016). doi:10.1057/s41303-016-0022-1

Keywords: alternative genre; research case study; qualitative research; narrative; role of the reader: French New Novel

## Introduction

Text is the main medium whereby scientific findings are reported and even if writing is seen by some as ‘‘an unfortunate necessity’’ (Rorty, 1982, p. 94) it is the researchers’ most important activity in today’s academic world (Czarniawska, 1999b). Write-ups of Information Systems (IS) research typically follow a paradigm that has not been questioned as it has been in the field of Organizational Studies (OS) by, for example, Linstead (1999) and Phillips (1995). The dominating paradigm for publishing IS qualitative research case studies aims to present a logical and consistent story based on empirical observation, with interview citations supporting the story, without ambiguity or unresolved aspects, and with causality apparently established (Bruner, 1986, 1990). The researchers collect and analyze the data and as authors of these scientific articles they attempt to make an original contribution based on stories that are articulated in as few words and as transparently as possible (Rorty, 1982). As such, the authors own the dataset and share their findings in a selective way to fulfill the dominating paradigm, hoping that reviewers will be convinced by their authoritative presentations and that readers will absorb and learn from the case study through a somewhat passive posture. In this paper, we question this dominating paradigm and suggest an alternative narrative approach to writing and reading IS qualitative case studies inspired by the French New Novel (FNN).

Rowe (2012) calls for ‘‘a richer diversity of genres in IS research’’ (p. 469) including ethnographies and narratives, arguing: ‘‘We need powerful and smart techniques to describe situations rarely observed and for which a better understanding may have important consequences’ (p. 472). In terms of alternative genres, there have been some suggestions in both IS and OS research. The work of Lanzara (1983, 1991) demonstrates the richness of presenting multiple viewpoints for interpreting a case. Avital & Vandenbosch (2000) stage a play in representing their empirical work and the confessional account of Schultze (2000) demonstrates how self-reflexive material can contribute to IS research. Pentland (2003) shows how narratives (from both researchers’ and participants’ stories) can provide the basis for building process theory and Van Der Blonk (2003) discusses how case study research can be reported in different ways through chronology, play, biography and voices. More recently, Sherry (2012) represents his empirical work as a series of poems and Olsen (2012) develops stories based on her journals and fieldwork notebooks and proposes a self-narrative of an evolutionary relationship she has with some people living nearby. Development of narratives to discuss realworld settings in academic research has also been proposed earlier by, for example, Czarniawska (1997) and Van Maanen (2011). Czarniawska (1998, 2004, 2008) uses narratives as a way of exploring and explaining organizational phenomena, finding ‘‘the unexpected intelligible [whereby] instead of reproaching the practice and telling the practitioners which way to go, [the] task [is] to tell them a good story’’ (Czarniawska,1999b, p. 15). Even if this is more characteristic of novelists and poets, it does not mean that it is not scientific (Teske, 2010). Thus, the unitary and authentic narrative is seen by these writers as illusory. Narratives are competing and assume polyvocality (Bakhtin, 2010; Bakhtin & Emerson, 1993; Buchanan & Dawson, 2007).

Against this backdrop, we propose an alternative way of producing qualitative case studies in which researchers act as narrators in the style of the FNN literary genre. This particular genre has not been pursued elsewhere to support the publication of IS or OS qualitative case studies, raising the following questions: What are the insights from the French New Novel that might help write IS qualitative case studies? How might this be implemented? Why has this merit as an alternative genre for IS research publication? Although only the exemplar extract of a narrative in this paper is in the style of the FNN, we invite readers to reflect and indeed act with respect to how qualitative case studies might be presented and interpreted. To help the reader in these efforts, we have organized the paper as follows. In the next section, we discuss the characteristics of the FNN genre and how these might impact on qualitative case study writing. In particular, we draw attention to the role of the narrator and that of the reader. This section is followed by our attempt to transfer some of the FNN principles to the IS qualitative research case study with a case study extract written in this way. In conclusion, we discuss this exemplar extract of a narrative, the techniques used and the proposed alternative genre as a whole.

## The French new novel genre

We start by discussing the reasoning and features of the FNN literature genre and later we suggest how this same spirit might be incorporated into IS qualitative case studies. Just as the FNN authors reacted against the conventional novel, with its unchanging character descriptions, easy-to-follow and well-defined plot, with linear chronological development in time and space, we react against the conventional case study research description which does not give much freedom to the readers for interpretation and thinking. Just as the new novelists may be inconsistent, contradictory, deceptive, vague, fragmental and subversive in their descriptions (Schaeffer, 1999), so might case study authors include material from the case that might have similar attributes and be omitted by conventional case study authors to express their particular interpretation or interpretations. Similarly, just as the FNN writers reacted against novels reflecting some acceptable social, political, economic or moral message yet leading to a ‘comfortable’ reading experience (Holmes, 2010) and an antidote to the instability in the world (Migozzi, 2005), these ‘new’ case studies might reflect the complexity of IS situations rather than simplifying them.

The FNN (nouveau roman) has been the name given to the output of a group of authors writing in French mainly from the late 1950s to the late 1970s. The most notable were Nobel-prize winner Claude Simon, along with Alain Robbe-Grillet, Nathalie Sarraute, Marguerite Duras, Robert Pinget, Claude Ollier and Michel Butor. Robbe-Grillet and Duras also contributed to the French ‘new wave’ cinema in the same period. There were precursors – Marcel Proust, Samuel Beckett, Franz Kafka, James Joyce, Jorges Louis Borges and James Faulkner all have some elements in their writings which have transferred to the new novel – but it seemed at the time that the new novel was something of a literary ‘movement’, although none of the writers would agree with that as they valued their independence and the works themselves are very different.

Although, as we will see, these novels do have some common characteristics – but even then not all were shared by all of these writers – they are more linked by what their authors rejected than what they have in common. This was that none of them conformed to the common novelistic conventions. They can be seen as part of the modernist rebellion (Pavel, 2003). Balzac is attributed by the new novelists as exemplar of a writer following these conventions, which Robbe-Grillet (1989, p. 15) labeled the ‘bourgeois novel’. More constructively, they were all looking for new forms for the novel. Whereas conventional writers might give a particular interpretation of the plot, the new novelists avoided providing such messages to their readers with their novels being more literary and poetic.

At the time, some members of the literary press saw the works of the new novelists as ‘scandalous’, ‘error-prone’ as well as ‘difficult’ and ‘unreadable’ (Robbe-Grillet & Otten, 1987) and we would add ‘deliberately provocative’. Robbe-Grillet’s first book The Erasers (Les Gommes) (Robbe-Grillet, 1953, 1966) was received with a ‘‘half silence’’ and his second book The Voyeur (Le Voyeur) (Robbe-Grillet, 1955, 1958) received a ‘‘massive and violent reaction’’ from the French press (Robbe-Grillet, 1989, p. 7). Years later, John Updike starts his introduction to the English translations of three of Pinget’s novels with: ‘‘It is with some embarrassment that a critic recommends to readers a writer whom he scarcely understands, whose works are a little more than exasperating’’ (Pinget, 2005, p. 7), yet ‘‘convey an impression of integrity, intelligence and power’’ (Pinget, 2005, blurb). This would suggest that over the years, the audience became more appreciative even if readers still had significant work to do to gain value from reading the texts.

Robbe-Grillet attempts to exclude from his works the possibility of a single meaning, a unique and therefore ‘correct’ meaning (Ball, 1984, p. 7). Simon refuses to ‘‘move forward in a straight line. His novels are constructed as sequences of scenes of varying length’ (Sturrock, 1969, p. 94). The title of Pinget’s (1980, 1986) Apocrypha (L’Apocryphe) suggests that any conventional meaning is lost or hidden (Pugh, 1984) (Apocrypha are statements or claims that are of dubious authenticity). Indeed, Pinget seems to be following ‘‘a carefully thought-out plan for upsetting the expected’’ (Henkels, 1979, p. 12).

Before presenting a case study extract written in the style influenced by the FNN, we look at its features, in particular those from the perspectives of the narrator and the reader that we try to reflect in our case study description that follows.

## The role of the narrator

The role of the narrator in the FNN is very different from that in a conventional novel. In the latter, the narrator guides the reader – helping her to discover ‘what happened’. She provides a sense of order and consistency to help the reader. The narrator tends to know all (or at least as much as it is necessary to understand the development of the story at that time), irons out any incoherency that might otherwise be apparent and seems to be omnipresent in the action. The reader tends to be unsatisfied if there are any ‘loose ends’ after reading a conventional novel.

In the new novel, the narrator informs the reader but does not impose an interpretation on her. There may be unexplained breaks in the flow of the text so that the reader is not provided with a coherent and flowing whole, characters might be given multiple and inconsistent attributes, the narrator often digresses to describe stories within stories, and the ‘real’ and ‘imagined’ are confused.

In some novels, there are a number of narratees (narrators in the text) whose stories may well be inconsistent. In Sarraute’s (1980, 2010) The Use of Speech (L’Usage de la Parole), for example, the narratees are given various pronouns (Je-I) and (Nous-We). In Butor (1957b), the narrator is referred to as ‘vous’ (you) throughout, thus distancing the narrator from the author. Again, in Pinget’s (1980, 1986) Apocrypha (L’Apocryphe), there is a ‘dialogue’ between the author (master) and the reader (slave) (Pugh, 1984, pp. 161–162). Sometimes the job of the narratees might be as much to confuse as to enlighten the reader (Bell, 1984; Suleiman & Wimmers, 1980). Which narratee, in these novels, if any, can we consider reliable?

One of Robbe-Grillet’s narratees in Djinn (Djinn: un trou rouge entre les pave´s disjoints) is a blind man and so only a partial view of the ‘plot’ can be given (Robbe-Grillet, 1982). Simon’s (1986) Discours de Stockholm, a version of his Nobel prizewinning speech, follows this new novel trait even in its ‘autobiographical’ material where Simon claims to have experienced everything (revolution, war, capture, hunger, physical labor, grave illness, and so on) so that even his apparent autobiography cannot be trusted: ‘‘it is necessarily a mask, stylization, the supreme camouflage of the writer Simon ‘the man’’’ (Vinken, 1988, p. 45).

In Sartre’s preface to Portrait of an Unknown Person (Portrait d’un inconnu) (Sarraute, 1956a), he characterizes the era as one of reflection, where the new novelists are self-conscious and expose the process of composition. Bolton (2010) argues that reflection – spirited enquiry – challenges assumptions, ideological illusions, biases, inequalities and questions personal behaviors. Butor argues that his writings, see for example, Passing Time (l’Emploi du temps) (Butor, 1957a, 1960) are more like research as he describes the creative process in his novels. Many of these writers reflect on their own writing and it is clear that their work is as difficult to write as that of conventional writers, but different. A particular difference concerns their views about the role of the reader.

## The role of the reader

Some of these stories are detective novels such as Pinget’s Monsieur Levert (Le Fiston) (Pinget, 1961, 1964), where elements of the detective novel are used as a tactic to mock the traditional detective novels, such as the Miss Marple and Poirot stories of Agatha Christie (see also Czarniawska, 1999a). However, all novels are to some extent ‘detective novels’ metaphorically as the reader is expected to try to find out ‘what happened’ (the theme of ‘the quest’ in some form or another is very common in these novels). Commentators writing about the FNN often refer to the reader as detective. Rather than the writer giving the story ‘on a plate’ to the reader, in the FNN the reader has to play detective herself to try to make sense of the text (Hu¨hn, 1987). The novels demand of the reader ‘‘a high level of participation rather than passive receptivity’’ (Minogue, 1981, p. 2).

In an interview, Robbe-Grillet suggests that what ‘‘carrie(s) the reader from page to page in a text, which prevent(s) the reader from falling asleep’’ is an unresolved tension (Robbe-Grillet & Mistacco, 1976, p. 35). In another interview (Hayman & Robbe-Grillet, 1975), Robbe-Grillet makes clear that the reader is part of the creative process – part of the action and not merely a witness. The reader needs to be suspicious as the writer may attempt to mislead or manipulate. Alternatively, the narratee may be an unreliable source, such as that of a child or an adult acting as a child in Portrait of an Unknown Person (Portrait d’un inconnu) (Sarraute, 1956a), discussed by Minogue (1973).

The detective story element entices the reader to take part, but at the end there may be no explanation to the mystery or explanations may be very questionable. In Someone (Quelqu’un) (Pinget, 1965, 1984) and Passacaglia (Passacaille) (Pinget, 1969, 1978) alternative plots are described, with ‘‘none being more plausible than the other’’ (Reid, 1988, p. 98). The reader has to work hard, be suspicious of the narrative [see The Age of Suspicion (L’E<sup>\`</sup>re du soupc¸on) (Sarraute, 1956b, 1963)], and her role is much more active as she tries to search for the ‘truth’. The reader may be understood as someone who ‘invents’ her interpretation rather than simply follow what is given. Even after this search, however, ‘the truth’ may elude the reader. In Plough (Charrue) (Pinget, 1985, p. 82), the writer only provides ‘a semblance of reality’, and his works as a whole is seen as ‘‘part of an unsolvable crime, an impossible inquest, an unprovable guilt’’ (Reid, 1988, p. 100). However, it does give the reader an opportunity to at least attempt to interpret the ‘hermeneutic code (Barthes, 1970), that is, the mystery that moves the narrative forward.

## Transferring new novel principles to case study narratives

The authors of the new novel and social scientists share some ambitions, including providing improved knowledge that might lead to change. We suggest that the conventional case study write-up – with its consistent story, the characters described and the interview citations used to support this story, without ambiguity and developed in a consistent time sequence – may present a simplified, perhaps even simplistic, representation of the case. As Avison & Malaurent (2014a, b) have argued, there is a temptation of case study writers to revert to ‘ideal types’ in qualitative research that hide complexity. It is done to help readers make sense of the story and writers neglect (or even distort) aspects of the research setting which do not conform to the story that the authors want to tell. The published story might be interesting, easily assimilated and pass through the reviewing process, but some information about what actually happened might be hidden or distorted to achieve this aim. Thus, although we can never give a complete representation of the world, case studies may present a superficial representation of the phenomenon studied, and therefore actually mislead the reader. Moreover, the reviewing process may make the case description even ‘worse’ from this point of view by ironing out every ambiguity and inconsistency and also by insisting on the tenets of a traditional case study description. Consistent with the French New Novelists’ view of the traditional novel, we argue that this way of writing case study descriptions might represent values of the past.

If we reconsider the IS qualitative research case study following the tenets of the new novelists, readers will need to interpret the data themselves rather than the author imposing her interpretation on the reader. Data does not speak for itself, but much of the job of interpretation is passed on, to a greater or lesser extent, from the author to the reader. The framing of a case study (Adams & Avison, 2003) through a given interpretation or interpretations by the author might limit the potential of the paper. Thus, we open the ‘doors of perception (Huxley, 1971), and through different interpretations encourage further discovery and the potential to stimulate ‘blue ocean’ ideas (Straub, 2009) and further research developing these ideas.

So what would happen if the narrative contains ambiguity, inconsistency, partial knowledge and unresolved aspects? At the time the new novels came out, Robbe-Grillet complained that ‘no-one read us’ (Robbe-Grillet & Otten, 1987, p. 363) (though they were more widely read later). Would such case study papers receive the equivalent response in IS journals, that is rejection by reviewers, or if by chance they were accepted, would anyone read them and gain knowledge to develop IS theory or support practice? While they would be more difficult to read because they would go against the present habit of reading a ‘cleansed’ version of the true situation, they would be more representative of the case and potentially provide to the reader a deeper understanding of the real world. Moreover, the discussions that might ensue between readers (including the original reviewers and the author) – something like a literary circle – could potentially add greatly to the contribution of the original piece, and hence lead to greater understanding of IS empirical research. We discuss this further in our conclusion.

In his Nobel prizewinning speech 7 December 2014 in Stockholm, Stephane Modiano argued that ‘‘The reader knows more about a book than the author’’ (as reported in Le Monde, 8 December 2014). The reader adds something to the text by putting it in the perspective of her own experience and knowledge. With potential contributions from reviewers, responses from authors, practitioners and other researchers adding to the body of knowledge provided by the case account itself, this opens for a much more holistic contribution than the writtenup case study alone.

In narrating an IS case study following this style, deliberate ambiguity and errors are of course unacceptable. However, the text will include existing ambiguities, unresolved differences of view, citations where the interviewee may not be truthful or an unreliable source, and passages capable of multiple interpretations. Such narrative should enable the reader to make multiple interpretations of the ‘plot’ and decide for herself what interpretation she thinks is the most plausible, interesting, meritorious in some way, more likely to lead to theory building, more relevant to practitioners or most relevant to her in some way. That process may help the reader gain a deeper understanding with regard to the complexities of the issues raised in the research and make a bigger contribution to theory and practice.

Following the style of the new novelists, the reader’s role is difficult and the conclusions drawn by her are unpredictable. While ‘‘the temptation to attribute causal and logical relationships is powerful … such reflections on the mind’s capacity for finding links, or creating them, and imposing continuity and coherence on disparate elements’’ (Minogue, 1981, p. 19) may lead to inappropriate interpretations in an IS research case study. However, the reader’s involvement in trying to understand the case study may be more beneficial to her than the comparatively passive intake of a conventional research case, even if she is left feeling, like John Updike when reading Pinget, that she does not understand the full picture.

To illustrate and inspire, we present a short subset of a case study. It might seem like a draft, similar to how the works of the new novelists seem unfinished to the conventional reader. We have built the extract of the narrative on the basis of data collected during 2011–2015 as part of research into the use of technology amongst a group of scientists looking at the potential impact of climate change on organic farming in France. The presented text is only a small part of what could become a full case study written in this style. It is based on diaries and notes of the three authors, transcripts of recorded interviews, transcripts of discussions between researchers and also fictional texts that reflect the data.

M gets in the room immediately followed by S. They draw the curtains together while speaking at the same time: ‘‘No other choice if we want to use the whiteboard’’ says M. X, B, and L are approaching. They laugh in the corridor. M gets out of the classroom and waits for them with a large smile. Apparently they all know each other. P and J are late, the two outliers.

The meeting starts. The agenda of the day contains 5 items: (1) funding, (2) results from initial seeding, (3) survey from Mediterranean countries, (4) working together, and (5) any other business. The discussion about item 1 lasts two hours. From 9 am to 11 am. The situation is critical. The project will stop if there is no funding for years two and three. V leads the discussion about item 2. An engineering student from Chile will start a 6-month survey to analyze how Mediterranean countries are tackling climate change. C, A and M lead the conversation to define the job mission. The discussion about item 4 is led by M. ‘How can we work together on this project as most of us are coming from different institutions geographically dispersed and with different interests?’ Some are reluctant to meet regularly because it is too time-consuming and meetings are not seen to be efficient. An alternative approach is preferred: alternate physical and virtual meetings to minimize transportation costs. It implies that the meeting rooms should offer sufficient facilities for web conferencing.

‘‘If it works well, fine’’ concludes S.

Item 5 is skipped. No other things to be discussed. 6.45 pm. Most of the participants leave quickly. Some get to the train station, others carpool towards the South.

P, V and C scrutinize the room before choosing their seats. All three do so to sit next to a power outlet. Consequently, they are seating at three different sides of the square. No Wi-Fi network available nor 3G/4G coverage for connection sharing. It is problematic as C is planning to show some photographs of the crops to the group through the video-projector. Not feasible as the photographs are only stored on a cloud-based platform.

M cross-legged on a table near the right corner, close to the paperboard. All others sit on regular seats.

Mid-afternoon. The whiteboard is now full of drawings, and colors. Different handwriting. What does all this mean? M stands up at regular intervals and goes to the center of the room to take pictures of the board with a smartphone. M does that to get a trace of those symbols that aim to summarize their discussions and ideas.

Suddenly N moves a table towards the whiteboard and then jumps on it. N is short and cannot find any other spare space on the whiteboard than up there. Others laugh but it does not stop N from writing a few things.

## Reflecting on the narrative

The brief extract of a narrative inspired by the FNN serves as illustration for our argument. To highlight how such narrative may be developed and used in a comprehensive study, we discuss the structure of the text, the writing techniques we used to develop it, and possible directions for interpretation. Although the researcher’s deconstruction would not normally follow case study narratives inspired by the FNN – a new novelist would not do this for the reader – we find it appropriate that we discuss this exemplar text in the context of this paper on alternative genres for IS research publication for illustrative purposes.

The journey transforming the data collected (notes from on-site observations, meetings, phone interviews, system logs, diaries, etc.) to the short extract of a narrative above consisted of an iterative process involving the three authors. We were guided by our perception of the new novel genre discussed earlier, our own trial-and-error experiences and discussions, and advice from our reviewers. Because of questions of space, we needed to present a partial account of the Melibio project that was representative of the project as a whole. Our short extract needed to demonstrate our chosen genre, be relevant to IS and make a contribution to IS knowledge. We had many attempts. We finally chose the use of technology during meetings as our focus in forming the narrative. In the extract, we looked at three specific meetings that highlighted the challenges participants faced due to the different technologies employed at each of the meetings. Before submission, the project director was given a copy of the text and agreed it was a fair reflection of those meetings.

The extract is composed of three sub-stories symbolized respectively with \*, \*\*, \*\*\* and they each consist of four sections. All three stories follow the same structure. The first section provides a description of the room where the meeting is taking place. The second section focuses on the plot of the meeting with factual data regarding the agenda of the day and its goals. The third section focuses on the relationships between people and technology and how they cope with it during meetings. The final section provides extracts from discussions that occurred online, through a collaborative platform. As such, there are connections between the three stories as they discuss interactions of the same group about one collective project. However, there is much that remains ambiguous.

There are also some indications in the text that may help readers define a chronological order between these episodes, but again ambiguity prevails.

## Writing techniques

We built our exemplar new-novel narrative extract focused on techniques that would provide readers with the essence and aesthetic of the new novel genre. However, there is no consensus on the techniques required to develop a narrative of this genre. There are similarities among new novelists that have been identified and discussed in essays (e.g., Ricardou, 1973; Robbe-Grillet, 1963), but there is no generic set of guidelines to create a FNN per se, and we are obviously not providing one with this contribution. Nevertheless, in what follows, we discuss the techniques we used in our narrative and we provide the references, when specific, that inspired us.

• Narrator Our narrative is built from an external observer’s eye scrutinizing the room and people’s behavior without providing a complete, perfect, or universal view of the scene. The observer’s view on the scene is restricted. This technique is inspired from The Lover (L’Amant) by Marguerite Duras (Duras, 1984, 1986).

• Characters M, S, C, A, N, E, X, B, L, P, J, V, G. Thirteen characters are labeled just with initials in the manner of Robbe-Grillet in Jealousy (Jalousie) (1957, 1965b). Our intention is to concentrate on the actions and objects but not the characteristics of the participants. Essayists refer to it as ‘anti-humanism’ (Childers and Hentzi, 1995, p. 72). We do not want readers to get into the personal features of our characters. Nothing should distract them from what is taking place in the room. Giving first names to our characters would be the first step toward the creation of actors with personal traits, position, status, and so on. This technique also helps to prevent any judgment that could be derived from the first names of our characters such as gender and cultural background. One example of that technique in our narrative is ‘‘P and J are working on their presentations while C is presenting the initial findings from the survey. Remotely, V is busy doing other things.’’ (Readers might note that outside our exemplar extract of a narrative we have used the feminine personal pronoun in our text to describe the ‘author,’ ‘narrator,’ or ‘reader.’ Whatever we choose (he, she, he/she, a mixture of both or pluralizing the text) this does influence readers’ interpretations of text and the authors).

• Lack of subjective judgments Our narrative extract is composed mostly of short sentences, sometimes unstructured, seemingly describing only apparent factual data. It aims to provide a ‘realist’ narrative (Van Maanen, 2011). Essayists often refer to the FNN as kind of ‘new realism.’ In our narrative, we describe what is happening in the scene, not what people think about it. Our writing sticks to the description of apparent ‘facts’ to let readers interpret it in their own way, with their own social constructions, without being influenced by an omnipresent character or narrator that shares her emotions while describing the scene. One example of that technique in our narrative is: ‘‘There are posters and stickers on the walls plus graffiti here and there. It smells. Some crisps are scattered on the tables. Two bins located near the whiteboard are full of plastic bottles: soda mostly. The furniture is completely disorganized. Some tables are turned toward the windows, others towards the north, and few face the entrance door.’’

• No main personage Throughout our narrative, M seems to be more present and active than the others. However, we cannot qualify M as an omnipresent personage in the manner of a narrator in a classic novel. All our characters are meant to be equal. They are anonymous and transparent, simple subjects of the action expressed by the verb. One example of that technique in our narrative is represented in the following: ‘‘M asks a few questions about the platform’s functionalities. P gives an online demonstration to answer M. C, V and A do not react at all.’

• Only optical adjectives, no metaphors This is a technique taken from Robbe-Grillet in Jealousy (Jalousie) (Robbe-Grillet, 1957, 1965b). The narrator describes the scene only with optical adjectives to present the size of the rooms, the furniture, the lighting, etc. It comes in reaction to the over-use of metaphors by traditional novelists that lead readers toward specific interpretations. For example, in our story \*\*\* instead of writing: ‘‘Some crisps are scattered on the tables’’ traditional novelists might have written something like: ‘‘A few crisps are lying on tables like putrefied moth corpses.’ But this would have led readers toward a certain mood or a comprehension of the scene that is not neutral.

• Some sentences are without verbs to express a stream of consciousness It may look odd or incorrect at first, but the use of sentences without verbs is one of the techniques used by Nathalie Sarraute to express under-conversations (sous-conversations) or the stream of consciousness. These are particular moments disconnected from anything else. In The Planetarium (1961), Nathalie Sarraute wrote: ‘‘we have not discovered yet a language that could express in one shot what a person perceives from a glance’’ (p. 33) and then she suggests in The Age of Suspicion (l’Ere du soupc¸on) (1963, 1956b) that in doing so, we may: ‘‘break these movements into small bits and deploy them in the consciousness of the reader like a slow-motion movie would do’’ (ppII-III). One technique to achieve this is to concatenate very short sentences, sometimes without verbs. An example of that technique in our narrative is: ‘‘A109, in the west aisle. Digit code to get in: B189\*. A beep then the door unlocks. Inside, a perfectly square room. 15 square meters. Eight seats and two tables are turned facing two 50 inch LED screens. Stand-by mode.’’

• Fascination for objects French new novelists are also labeled as ‘the school of glance’ (L’e´cole du regard). In a number of new novels such as The Erasers (Les Gommes) (Robbe-Grillet, 1953) or Things (Les choses) (Perec, 1969, 1990) there are long and meticulous descriptions of objects. In our narrative extract, we were inspired by that technique to provide precise characteristics of the artifacts present in the meeting rooms that will even tually play a major role in these episodes. For example, we wrote the following in story \* and part repeated in story \*\*\*: ‘‘The classroom is a perfect square. Three large windows oriented towards the south provide light. They are situated opposite of the entrance door. It smells of fresh paint. Walls are pure white. Everything is clean and tidy. Wooden seats are shiny and well arranged. Some look brand-new. Tables are pure white. Some look brand-new as well.’’ Although brief, it demonstrates the importance given to objects (compared to people). Of course in the context of IS, this emphasis on artifacts is particularly interesting (see Orlikowski and Iacono, 2001).

• Uncreative writing and hypertextuality The fourth sections of each story are exact extracts from an online discussion between group members that occurred through a collaborative platform. The discussion has been translated from French to English but apart from that, no transformation has been made (excepting the choice of extracts used). This process of reusing, recopying, reframing, repeating, and regurgitating data available online is referred as ‘uncreative writing’ by Goldsmith (Chesley, 2012). It also relates to the concept of hypertextuality coined by Roland Barthes who defines it as a ‘‘text that branches, links, and connects, allowing information to be understood in random sequence. The nature or order of meaning is not pre-determined by the author, but is rather an interactive activity in which the reader is free to take any chosen direction’’ (Barthes, Living). In our narrative, we purposefully use this technique as we have split the online discussion into three chunks that reveal different elements of the project and participants’ interactions. It also aims to show readers some raw data about the material we collected without interpreting it. In the manner of Pinget (1963, 1966) in the Inquisitor (L’Inquisitoire), the reader has to glean what she can from the discussion. The fragmented style not only allows the reader to fill gaps but to some extent force the reader to fill gaps in her attempt to make sense of the narrative given.

• Enigmatic plot While reading the three stories, altogether, or separately, readers might be confused by the lack of intrigue. The narrative reads like a description of scenes and facts without a beginning or end. These episodes do not present any particular denouement. They stop as they start without any specific signification or takeaway obvious to the reader. This is also true of the whole case study. It is the reader who has to ‘play detective’ and discover the meaning(s) that work(s) for her. Hopefully, she is interested enough to think about what might lay behind this enigma and she may even discuss possible interpretations with other readers.

• The narrator remains subjective The expression ‘new realism’ does not imply that the narrator (observer) is offering a ‘pure objective’ account. Narratives of that genre focus on ‘real things’ but it does not mean that they aim to reach universal objectivity. That would be too naı¨ve (Robbe-Grillet, 1963, 1965a). For example, in Jealousy (Jalousie) (Robbe-Grillet, 1957, 1965b) the observer is a voyeur sick with jealousy. His perception of the scene is very meticulous but nevertheless highly subjective and limited as she presents the settings from her own perspective, and with a particular emphasis on the person she is observing. In our narrative, the narrator is transparent and anonymous, but she orientates the descriptions with a certain emphasis on some elements of the scene rather than others. Although it might ‘seem’ dispassionate, the choice of text in the narrative reflects the subjectivity of the narrator.

## Possible interpretations

This narrative extract covers a three-month period of the Melibio project and presents different episodes taking place during different meetings, as well as discussions that occurred online. However, there is neither a beginning nor end, nor a clear focus. Therefore, in terms of interpretation, the main question academic readers may have in mind while reading this short extract of a narrative is what might it tell us? Since there is no introduction to the case, it might be difficult for readers used to a conventional IS case study to get into it without any prior discussion of the research theme, a relevant literature review, a framework for analysis and so on. But as new novelists would argue, we do not wish to impose one single viewpoint or viewpoints on the case a priori. However, since our paper is exploratory in nature we propose an illustration of the whole process. In what follows, we therefore discuss four facets of the narrative that might be interesting to develop if we were playing the role of the reader. In that sense, we pursue the idea sometimes expressed by new novelists that the author is the first reader of the text.

A first possible angle would be to look at the narrative by asking ‘Is technology creating a culture of distraction? Stories \*\* and \*\*\* (where technology is more present than in story \*) suggest that people are easily being distracted during meetings as they are ‘multi-tasking’ (Willingham, 2010; Spink et al, 2002). They are doing things other than just participating in the meeting discussions thanks (or due) to technology. Some are preparing their upcoming talks or presentations, others are sending emails, or web browsing. Additionally, virtual participants attending meetings told in story \*\* and \*\*\* seem to be doing other things in parallel.

A second way of reading our exemplar extract of a narrative could be the following: ‘technology is ubiquitous, but unstable’.’ All three stories report episodes where technology is available, at different levels, but there always seems to be something wrong that prevents optimal use of it. In story\*, there is a video-projector in the classroom, but there is no Wi-Fi network available to access a cloud-based platform on which one of the presenters stored some documents. In story \*\*, there is something wrong with the internet connection of P, or the web conferencing system, which prevents P to be there virtually. As a consequence, P needs to revert to a phone line to participate. In story \*\*\*, while the connection was good enough for an online demonstration of P and J in the morning, V suffers in the afternoon from Wi-Fi signal deterioration which eventually ends up by M taking the lead in the discussion and so replacing V.

A third possible direction would be to ask ‘how do spatial arrangements make an impact during meetings and how do they influence the interactions and behaviors of participants? All three stories provide precise descriptions of the meeting room arrangements. The arrangement of the furniture, the lighting, the extension cords, the Internet access, the whiteboard, the flipchart, the web conferencing system, all play a role in the meeting scenarios. This could contribute to research related to spatial practices (De Vaujany & Vaast, 2013) and more broadly socio-materiality (Leonardi & Kallinikos, 2012; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008).

A fourth development could be built from the online discussions. For one, they present one actor (G) using the collaborative platform as a way of communicating with the group about a survey that took place in Mediterranean countries. Although the extracts do not provide details about ‘who is responding to whom’ it is clear that this discussion is centered on G’s mission. Given the platform adoption difficulties reported elsewhere in the narrative, we could analyze ‘how a technological entrepreneur may lead a group towards the adoption of an ICT platform.’ This relates to the role that human capital characteristics of individuals and teams play in the process of technological entrepreneurship (Wright et al., 2007).

Our story does not suggest analyzing these four directions separately, nor are these four interpretive approaches exclusive. However, these explorations suggest the narrative could (perhaps should) be regarded as an invitation for readers to develop a multi-layer analysis of the case. As such, our exemplar extract of a narrative is consistent with the quality criteria suggested by narrative researchers (Czarniawska, 2004; Fisher, 1984) to provide consistent, coherent, and plausible accounts of what is taking place in a real-world setting without reducing its complexity, richness, messiness, and discontinuity.

More generally, and following the FNN tradition, none of the above interpretations are in any sense ‘proved’ by the data in the case. However, reflections and discussion of the data by readers may lead to other cases (conventional or otherwise) to research further into one or more of the possible tentative interpretations that we have suggested (and others). In other words, the reader’s role as detective requires her to investigate further the ‘leads that she has made apparent on analyzing the original data and this may present a first step toward theory building.

## Envisioning a complete case study

We invite scholars interested in this alternative approach inspired by the FNN to engage in the writing of a complete narrative, in the manner of a short novel. Although it would need to respect the standards of academic journals in terms of length, a long and meticulous narrative should give enough space for authors (narrators) to develop coherent, consistent, and plausible accounts of what is taking place in the field (Fisher, 1984; Czarniawska, 2004). The classic structure of an academic paper would be shattered. We would expect that the only element apart from the narrative would be a short description of the data collected, the time spent on site, in the manner of a short methodology section. This description could be located somewhere at the beginning or end of the text or in an appendix.

Based on the framework of elements in empirical research papers suggested by Mathiassen et al (2012), the narrative itself should cover problem setting in the real world (P), area of concern in the literature (A), and methods of investigation (M). (P) and (A) would be the backbone of the whole story. The narrative can be messy, complex, uncertain, and chaotic, but the consistency should come from (P) and (A) acting as anchors. Writing techniques and storytelling strategies inspired by the new novel genre would be the foundational method of investigation (M), which can be split into two elements (Ma) and (Mb). First, (Ma) would provide details about the investigation process, the researchers position, the duration, the type of data from which the narrative is built, and so on. This would have to be discussed in a section apart from the narrative (in an appendix or elsewhere). Second, (Mb) would be the narrator’s position and role in describing scenes and actions. As we have seen in our literature review, new novelists have developed several techniques. It could be a transparent, anonymous narrator as we opted in our exemplar extract of a narrative or alternative one using multiple narrators. These could consist of quotes from interviewees. Regarding theoretical framing (F) this would not be explicated in the paper, and when it comes to contributions (C), this would be developed by the readers, possibly using explicit framing devices (F). Following the new novelists, we would invite readers to engage themselves actively. For that, we suggest that they should take the interpretation process in any direction they wish.

We suggest tentatively that an initial panel of reviewers and experts could be given the opportunity to publish their views and this would form part of the emerging contributions (C). These works would offer different interpretations developed by scholars (and possibly practitioners) and perhaps also include the interpretations of a reader’s circle. Of course, the authors themselves may wish to join the discussion. These contributions might all be included in the journal website as part of a published ‘bundle’ along with the narrative itself. At the time of writing (August 2015), the Australasian Journal of Information Systems has just published its first ‘post publication review,’ an approach that facilitates such a discussion for suitable papers (AJIS, 2015).

In terms of assessment and evaluation, each narrative should be ‘‘evaluated with respect to the main genre(s) and to the epistemology it adopts’’ (Rowe, 2012, p. 474). Hence, in the context of narratives inspired by the FNN tradition, accounts should be evaluated according to the techniques and reasoning discussed in our paper and similar work following this tradition.

## Conclusion

Our research questions were: What are the insights from the French New Novel that might help write IS qualitative case studies? How might this be implemented? Why has this merit as an alternative genre for IS research publication? In the above exemplar extract of a narrative and the reflections that follow, we have built an illustration which aims to highlight the merits of a new kind of narrative for IS case study research. This provides an alternative to the traditional case study of a consistent story, with chronological and space linearity and the characters and the interview citations supporting the story, without ambiguity, unresolved aspects, and so on. However, the readers’ job becomes greater in this new form as we suggest they should interpret the narrative themselves to develop contributions.

The role of the reviewer is also challenging. A ‘checklist’ approach to assessing such a paper would not be appropriate. But a reviewer might consider whether such a paper is likely to stimulate relevant and important interpretations that might lead to theory development or contributions to practice. For example, even though ours is a very short piece and limited to one aspect of our research into the Melibio project, we managed to elucidate four potential interpretations, reflections and questions which might lead to further research: ‘Is technology creating a culture of distraction?’, ‘in what sense is technology ubiquitous but unstable?’ ‘how do spatial arrangements make an impact during meetings and how do they influence the interactions and behaviors of participants?’ and ‘how does a technological entrepreneur lead a group towards the adoption of an ICT platform?’ … and there may well be other possible interpretations, observations and questions.

We recognize that the writing of the narrative is still authoritative since the researchers remain the authors and build the narrative. However, though this does not imply the role of the writer is any easier, the main implication of such a narrative genre for the case study is that the interpretation process is transferred from the narrator (researcher) to the reader. Many ambiguities and unresolved aspects still remain and different readers may come up with similar or very different interpretations that may be the result of influences such as their own education, world-view, experience and interests. There is scope for multiple possible interpretations. They may also research outside the narrative to support their own interpretation and they may have their own self-experiencing of the narrative to reveal other possible interpretations or facets of the case. Thus, we encourage journal websites to accept readers’ interpretations of the case so that a much richer and more holistic discussion can be shared.

The challenge of requiring readers to interpret the text might be resisted by many readers used to having the meaning made crystal clear. Our provocative stance goes against many taken-for-granted values and practices which science is built on related to finding a simpler truth. The reader might find inconsistencies in the styles in which the texts are presented as well as their meaning, with apparently missing ‘scenes.’ These can all be disconcerting. Although we do not claim that this alternative genre for the case study is superior to its conventional form, it is more engaging because the reader needs to be active in her role as ‘detective.
