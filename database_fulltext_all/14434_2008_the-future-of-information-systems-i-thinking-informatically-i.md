## Abstract

Many of those who are active within the academic field of information systems (IS) are constantly seeking a firm disciplinary basis for their endeavours. In many respects, such efforts are based upon a mistaken view of how disciplines are actually constituted, and the purposes that they serve. In many respects, it would be far more fruitful if those working within the field of IS were to accept a more fluid and contingent notion of a discipline; simultaneously recognizing the contested nature of many of the core concepts – particularly information, communication and technology. ## Introduction

In my book Thinking Informatically (Bryant, 2006), I sought to clarify and ground the concepts of information, communication and technology (ICT) using the admittedly awkward and increasingly ambiguous term informatics. In part, this was done to highlight and develop Donna Haraway’s definition of informatics as the study of ‘technologies of information and communication as well as the biological, social, linguistic and cultural changes that initiate, accompany and complicate their development’ (Haraway, 1985 – words in italics added by the author). More importantly, the title itself allowed me to evoke Zygmunt Bauman’s book Thinking Sociologically (Bauman & May, 2001), indicating my aim of building upon Bauman’s (1987; 1993a, b; 1998; 1999; 2000; 2001; 2002; 2004; 2006a, b; 2007) work. (The references include details of some of Bauman’s recent writings.) In what follows I offer a brief insight into Bauman’s work, particularly his concept of Liquid Modernity, and then outline the ways in which this needs to be taken into account within the field of information systems (IS), informatics or whatever term is thought to be most appropriate and evocative – or provocative. (In what follows I shall use Lynne Markus’ term ‘Academic IS’ rather than informatics. I discuss Markus’ use of this in my book, noting that she somewhat undermines it herself, but for present purposes it seems apposite.)

Bauman is widely regarded as the foremost sociologist, cultural commentator and political thinker of the last 20 years. Within Academic IS his writings are perhaps not as widely read as those of Ulrich Beck, Anthony Giddens and Bruno Latour; but I believe that it is high time that we started to explore the ramifications of his work; particularly his concept of Liquid Modernity.

## Liquid Modernity

Since 1990, Bauman’s work has centred on developing the concept and nature of Liquid Modernity, a phrase that has entered the conceptual lexicon of many disciplines. (A more detailed exposition can be found in Bryant et al., 2007 and Bryant, 2007.) Wikipedia, itself a paradigm example of Liquid Modernity, offers the definition of the term as ‘a kind of chaotic continuation of modernity’. Bauman summarizes it as follows: in Liquid Modernity ‘[T]ime flows; but it no longer marches on to any destination’. His concern is to stress that in our current phase of modernity the key characteristic is not merely a one-off stage of sweeping things away, but rather an obsessively continuous and all-encompassing process of jettisoning; the innovations of today become the detritus of tomorrow. It is no coincidence that one of Bauman’s bleakest books bears the title Wasted Lives.

The liquid modern world is one in which the agendas and strategies of the preceding stage of solid modernity are at best useless, and at worst impediments to any form of concerted action or intervention. This presents a challenge to Academic IS, itself the paradigmatic liquid discipline, whereby ‘the narrated reality and the narrated reason have developed in tandem, so that what at first was a technical reality with a technical reason has now become a dominant aspect of social life’. (This epithet is derived from Bauman’s characterization of a discipline or discourse, such as sociology; spawning ‘the narrated reality at one end and the narrating reason at the other’ (Bauman, 1992). An extended discussion of this idea in the context of IS can be found in Bryant, 2006, Chapter 1.)

## Academic IS – a flawed discourse

The concepts at the heart of Academic IS – ICT – are ambivalent and contested; the linkages and lineages to other disciplines equally fraught. Following Bauman, it can be argued that the discourse of Academic IS centred around ICT was ‘brought into being by the encounter between the awesome task of the management of organizational processes on a grand, corporate and societal scale and the ambitions of the modern state and corporation’ (in the original, Bauman, 1992, this is applied to sociological discourse – terms in italics added by the author).

Bauman notes that the outcome of this encounter between management and social ambition was the articulation of a ‘collection of engineering problems’. In similar fashion, early Academic IS too often adopted the mantle of an engineering discipline, and although there have been significant and sustained efforts to elude and transcend this legacy, the tendency remains and constantly reappears under new guises.

Joseph Weizenbaum has argued that information technology (IT) came ‘ready made’ into the context that demanded its use; ‘the remaking of the world in the image of the computer started long before there were any electronic computers' (Weizenbaum. 1984). By extension

Academic IS emerged in the wake of both the technology and this refashioned and welcoming world view, adopting significant aspects from the self-image of each. The result has been that IS, both in terms of proto-discipline and object of study, is inevitably caught up in an ambivalent relationship with modernity. Academic IS needs to transcend this initial embodiment which gave it the appearance of a solidly modern, engineering-mimicking and management-oriented endeavour, in favour of a project that seeks to provide a critical account of its own development, the context from which it sprang and the possibilities open for the future.

This means that we have to spend less time worrying about the disciplinary core, and more on finding ways of encouraging critical thought in Foucault’s sense of ‘the endeavour to know how and to what extent it might be possible to think differently, instead of legitimizing what is already known’ (quoted in Bauman, 1992). The invocation to think informatically is made specifically to bolster this aim.

To a significant extent Academic IS tends to be seen, and to see itself, as a discipline characterized by an ‘engineering-reformatory-managerial’ edge: with many senior figures constantly seeking a solid disciplinary basis, in the form of core concepts or theories or artefacts.

Bauman sums this up succinctly – ‘Only a discipline flawed as a discourse has to offer an apology, feels the need to justify its right to exist. y Concern with selfjustification has been, since the beginning, a conspicuous feature of infological discourse’ (Bauman, 1992, sociological in the original).

## Fantasies and mythic cores

In itself, this sort of critique is not new. As long ago as 1987, Boland (1987) argued that the central concept of Management Information Systems – information – remains elusive, and that ‘we as researchers have failed to address the essence of information in our work’. He highlighted what he termed the five information fantasies – pointing out that these fantasies ‘have two faces: a productively imaginative one, and an erroneous, selfdeluding one’ (1987). Unfortunately, as Boland acknowledged in 1987, the first face has proved considerably weaker than the second; an imbalance that has continued to the present day. Moreover, these fantasies apply not only to information, but also to the other two components of ICT – communication and technology.

In general terms, these fantasies lead to concepts of ICT that are (1) stolid and disembodied; (2) leading to their being seen in a narrowly defined rationalist manner such that they are readily amenable to hierarchical, commandand-control management; (3) precluding issues of power and difference; (4) largely ignoring their creative and interpretive natures and (5) where more is assumed to mean better, often with the eventual aim of perfection. The result is to prevent opening up the ‘possibility for thinking about the world in different ways’ (Bauman & May, 2001, p. 5).

In addition, there is a further factor that makes Academic IS a flawed discourse: it has little or no autonomy from other discourses; and in this sense it is similar to sociology. Drawing on the work of Foucault, Bauman demonstrates that disciplines cannot be defined in terms of what might appear to be the ‘obvious’ aspects such as ‘permanence of a thematic’ or ‘a welldefined alphabet of notions’; but, following Foucault, must be seen as discursive formations. ‘We sought the unity of discourse in the objects themselves, in their distribution, in the interplay of their differences, in their proximity or distance – in short, in what is given to the speaking subject; and in the end, we are sent back to a setting-up of relations that characterizes discursive practice itself; and what we discover is neither a configuration, nor a form, but a group of rules that are immanent in a practice’ (Foucault quoted in Bauman, 1992).

From this perspective, any discipline must be seen to constitute its topic and its practices: there is no preexisting corner of reality waiting to be claimed and explained by a specific discipline; nor is the discursive formation a ‘disturbing element’ which superimposes itself upon some ‘pure, neutral, atemporal, silent form’. The ‘incessant activity of discourse y spawns the narrated reality at one end and the narrating reason at the other’ (Bauman, 1992).

Furthermore, unlike disciplines such as physics and astronomy, Academic IS has no monopoly of experiences, or of expensive and complicated and generally inaccessible equipment. As such, scrutiny of IS research findings can easily be carried out by those outside the discipline. So, however, much IS-purists such as Benbasat & Zmud (2003) might try to defend some mythic core of the discipline – although for them it is not a mythic core, but a very real one; there is a necessary and inevitable engagement with a whole variety of other disciplines, which leads to profound problems of identity and demarcation. IS is a ‘flawed discourse’. The boundaries between it and other discourses are fuzzy – indistinct to the point of disappearance; and the topics at the centre of the discourse are claimed not only by other discourses but also by everyday ‘commonsense’. Indeed, with the ubiquity of the technology and the terminology, these claims have actually increased to the extent that in 1999 Markus could ask ‘What Happens if the IS Field as We Know it Goes Away?’ (Markus, 1999).

What we have as the current state of affairs in IS is a paradigm case of a discipline as a flawed discursive formation: constantly engaged in the Sisyphean task of having to justify its existence; fraught with debates on what constitute relevant research topics, and legitimate and valid research methods. Much as some might wish this state of affairs to be brought to an end in some conceptually sound and conclusive fashion, this is not going to occur. On the contrary, the discipline can be seen as an ‘incessant activity of discourse’ spawning the narrated realities and the narrating reasons.

## Thinking Informatically – from monologue to dialogue

Bauman notes that early sociology was monological, treating its subject matter as a series of objects, to be assessed, measured and regulated. The process of investigation was a monologue conducted by the active and articulate researcher with respect to the passive, silent topic of investigation, rather than a dialogue between two or more participants. With its origins in computer science and IT, the discipline of IS in its present guise emerged in a similar fashion, and still retains this identity, although now it is increasingly tempered by a dialogic orientation that challenges such assumptions. This owes much to the emergence of several varieties of and opportunities for contrary ideas, many emanating from within established IS institutions – including EJIS opinion pieces such as this one.

In its early phase, Academic IS, like sociology, developed as a series of efforts aimed at ameliorating the introduction of technology into the organizational work place, where it was seen as a prime source of increased efficiency and enhance effectiveness. As such, the discipline was largely seen as a method of enquiry derived from a quantitative approach based on empirical methods and measurement.

Using Weber’s term, Bauman states that there was an elective affinity ‘between the scientifically objective ambitions of the rising sociology and its managerial involvement’ (1992); a similar but more intense affinity applied to the ambitions of the emerging IS discipline in the 1960s and 1970s – and still pervades many aspects of the IS world.

In a fairly short time, this overwhelmingly monological and behavioural view was challenged; but even now a significant part of the IS community – however ethereal that might be – work largely with a view that centres on ‘managed social processes y that cast social reality as an object of a designed change y while disarticulating all other aspects’ (Bauman, 1992). These ‘other aspects’ include human subjectivity, and asymmetries of influence, power, control and deviation.

Academic IS is then a discipline constantly having to apologise for itself; continually having to reassert its right to existence and funding. The future of the discipline will be greatly enhanced if consideration is given to informatic thinking. The irony is that this requires that we move on from the concept of a discipline in terms of the erection and defence of boundaries. Following Foucault and Bauman, disciplinary practices have to be seen as fluid projects not as structural and structuring strategies. The task is endless; at its core is not a single, clear objective but an unremitting process of continual explanation, explication and dialogue.

There are no boundaries – although of course in terms of jobs, funds, resources and the like, someone, somewhere will make boundaries pay; so some form of disciplinary advocacy is demanded – but this is a very different project from the conceptual one.

Whatever the outcome, our aim must be to remain essentially permeable, with both a push and a pull to this permeability. The pull can be found in the constant borrowing of terms and models from other disciplinary discourses – initially engineering, but now increasingly from sociology, semiotics and cultural studies. The push refers to the ways in which terms such as information, IT, ICT and the like have become part of common parlance. The push usually comes after the pull, and in some cases the initial borrowing is returned with added richness.
