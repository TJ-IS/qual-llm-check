# The state of CRM adoption by the financial services in the UK: an empirical investigation

Bill Karakostas<sup>a,\*</sup>, Dimitris Kardaras<sup>b</sup>, Eleutherios Papathanassiou

<sup>a</sup>Centre for HCI Design, School of Informatics, City University, Northampton Sq., London EC1V 0HB, UK <sup>b</sup>Business Informatics Laboratory, Department of Business Administration,

Received 15 December 2002; received in revised form 29 August 2003; accepted 10 August 2004 Available online 8 December 2004

## Abstract

In recent years, organisations have begun to realise the importance of knowing their customers better. Customer relationship management (CRM) is an approach to managing customer related knowledge of increasing strategic significance. The successful adoption of IT-enabled CRM redefines the traditional models of interaction between businesses and their customers, both nationally and globally. It is regarded as a source for competitive advantage because it enables organisations to explore and use knowledge of their customers and to foster profitable and long-lasting one-to-one relationships. This paper discusses the results of an exploratory survey conducted in the UK financial services sector; it discusses CRM practice and expectations, the motives for implementing it, and evaluates post-implementation experiences. It also investigates the CRM tools functionality in the strategic, process, communication, and business-to-customer (B2C) organisational context and reports the extent of their use. The results show that despite the anticipated potential, the benefits from such tools are rather small. <sup>#</sup> 2004 Published by Elsevier B.V.

Keywords: CRM; e-Business; Financial services

## 1. Introduction

In response to the growing threat from nonfinancial and non-traditional competitors, established financial services providers (FSPs) are using information technologies (IT) to deliver new financial products or to complement traditional ones, such as deposit, investment, credit, and insurance services. Customer relationship management (CRM) holds the promise to achieve such corporate objectives in this highly competitive arena. It involves the continuous use of refined information about current and potential customers in order to anticipate and respond to their needs and draws on a combination of business processes and IT to discover knowledge about the customers and answer questions such as ‘‘who are the customers?’’, ‘‘what do they do?’’ and ‘‘what are they like?’’. With such effective use of information and communication technologies, organisations can offer their customer a variety of products, lower prices, and personalised service, at the same time. In order to market effectively to the individual customer, companies gather information from both internal and external sources, and use it to provide a unified view (or profile) of the customer for targeted marketing purposes. Therefore, the effective management of information and knowledge is central and critical to the concept of CRM for

 product tailoring and service innovation, e.g. websites tailored to customers needs, experience and other characteristics, and the development of mass customisation,

 providing a single and consolidated view of the customer,

 calculating the value of the customer,

 establishing a strategy for a multi-channel based communication with the customer,

 designing and developing personalised transactions.

Different definitions of CRM put emphasis on different perspectives. CRM’s technological perspective was stressed in [18,20,24], its knowledge management perspective was emphasised in [13], and its business re-engineering and continuous improvement perspective was presented in [3]. Web-based CRM (also known as e-CRM) means that the sources of customer-related data are collected from the customer interactions with the Web and Internet-based systems. From a technological view, it is argued that there are three phases in CRM lifecycle [23]:

 The integration of customer data from different sources.

 The analysis in order to acquire a deeper understanding and knowledge about the customer.

 The action for providing the required positive impact on customer relationships.

The philosophy of CRM [19] is based on:

 Relationship orientation.

 Customer retention.

 Superior customer value created through process management.

 IT as the enabling technology for the discovery and management of customer knowledge.

CRM encompasses the management of all possible ways that an organisation interacts with its customers. From receiving an order through the delivery of the products and services, CRM aims to co-ordinate all business processes that deal with customers and involves the collection, collation, and interpretation of customer data in order to define patterns of buying behaviour that can be used to support effective marketing programs [1,11]. The financial services industry had an early lead in CRM implementation as its transactions were essentially IT-based and contained valuable information about their customers [8,12]. Furthermore, most financial services companies have at least some kind of a customer-marketing database [2,15]. IT support for CRM systems varies in terms of the complexity, the difficulty in implementation, and the range of customer support. In its simplest form a CRM implementation may be a ‘‘Frequently Asked Questions,’’ (FAQ) page on the company’s Web site, a call-centre, an e-mail newsletter, or a customer application download facility. More complex implementations of CRM range from highly complex, customisable merchant servers, to process integration though EDI, to virtual communities. CRM implementations include internal systems, such as customer databases, systems for interaction with consumers, such as home banking, as well as systems directed to business customers, such as order processing via B2B portals on the Internet [7,9,14,16,21,22,25].

This paper is not concerned with the specific types of IT used in CRM implementation but investigates to what extent such systems have been deployed and used in FS organisations.

## 2. Key drivers of CRM

Traditional marketing approaches have tended to utilise macro and micro segmentation techniques, thereby classifying ‘types’ of customer in a market. However, given the unpredictability of customer buying behaviour, traditional marketing, especially in information-rich sectors like FSPs, is fast giving way to one-to-one marketing; its aim is to individualise the marketing effort. CRM is becoming a priority due to very powerful economic, technological, and social forces that have effectively made the traditional business models irrelevant in the contemporary business and technological environment; thus [5]:

 With the growth of e-business, customers are just a mouse-click away from competitors.

 Superior customer-related knowledge can generate more effective and less replicable differentiation strategies.

 Satisfied customers are less expensive and can become an effective advertisement channel.

 Customer retention provides additional revenue. It is argued that a 5% increase in customer retention can result in an 18% reduction in operating costs. Many banks think their customer profitability follows a Pareto type 80/20 rule: i.e. 80% of a bank’s profits can be attributed to 20% of its customers.

 Acquisition of new customers is five times more expensive than generating repeat business from existing customers.

 Financial institutions are facing strong competition, not only from existing players within the industry, but also from new entrants from other business sectors.

 IT, when appropriately utilised, can help retain customers by better managing customer-related knowledge and building stronger relationships [10]. Customer data is now recognised as an asset. Large financial service organisations that hold enormous amounts of data on their customers have become aware of the possibilities of using such data to build long-term relationships. To leverage the vast amount of data, many organisations are adopting more effective data storage and processing technologies.

However, an industry study conducted by the International Data Corporation and Cap Gemini showed that, despite the initial high expectations, only 12% of large European and North American companies have completed CRM systems. The majority are first generation systems that focus primarily on call-centres, while the second generation ones that enable market analysis and customer-linking activities are just emerging. According to this study, the average investment in CRM in the USA is \$3.1 million. Sixty-five percent of the 300 USA companies that participated in the study are aware of its capabilities while 28% are currently planning or implementing some application within its spectrum. Indeed, there is criticism regarding the effectiveness of CRM and of its contribution to long-term success and competitive advantage. However there are also data to support CRM’s popularity. By the end of 2002, the proportion of customer-centric organisations was expected to rise to 20–50%, while 75% of the businesses were expected to have integrated sales and IT functions and 85% to use data warehouses.

## 3. Research methodology

A questionnaire was developed and sent to a selected sample of 46 financial sector companies. The cover letter that accompanied the questionnaire presented a definition and examples of CRM and clarified its business and technological dimensions in order to minimise misunderstanding amongst the respondents. The generalisability of the study relied on the representativeness of the respondents. Therefore, a representative selection of banks, building societies, and fund management companies was made from a database of financial service providers. The selection included large organisations with a global presence and small to medium-sized entities, such as building societies located regionally and nationally. A total of 21 usable responses were received, obtaining a response rate of 45.7%, which is significantly higher that the usual postal surveys average rate.

The CRM literature suggests that successful CRM implementation requires an enterprise-wide integration of processes and a change in management focus and business performance metrics [4]. In [17], an ECRM framework was proposed; it promoted a broader view of CRM implementation. ECRM is an acronym made from the initial letters:

 E: for E-business – the integration of e-business activities.

 C: for Channel Management – the integration and interactivity of channels for customer access and distribution of products and services.

 R: for Relationships – built on service excellence, value, and Convenience.

 M: for Management of the total organisation and integration of the front and back office processes.

The questions in the study were designed to address CRM implementation from an enterprise-wide perspective, taking into consideration the ECRM framework and relevant literature. Therefore, business process integration and re-engineering, channel management, performance metrics, technological, and organisational issues were addressed from both a strategic and operational viewpoint and business and technological perspectives. The intention was to keep the questions as short as possible and to use simple, clear, concise and unambiguous language to aid the participants’ understanding. There was therefore no requirement for the respondents to do anything other than tick (check) predefined response boxes. A fivepoint Likert scale (‘‘Strongly’’, ‘‘Moderately’’, ‘‘Little’’, ‘‘Not At All’’, ‘‘I Do not Know’’) was used to help the respondents express the degree to which they agreed with each of the questions. Since no replies were received stating ‘‘I Do not Know,’’ this option is not shown in the tables. It is important though to mention that this is an indication of the respondents’ confidence in their answers. There were forty closed questions, divided into sections that addressed the following issues:

 What was the initial motivation for CRM implementation?

 What CRM tools functionality had been used at the strategic and processes level?

 What CRM tools functionality had been used for supporting communication channels?

 What CRM tools functionality was used for supporting different stages in the business-tocustomer (B2C) interaction?

 What were the criteria for selecting a CRM system?

 How did companies evaluate the performance of the CRM tools?

 What were the respondents expectations regarding the industry-wide CRM applications?

The instruments used to investigate the research questions were tested for their validity and reliability in order to assess the goodness. Convergent and Discriminant validity tests were not used as there is no theory that has been proposed to explain the implications of CRM tools introduction and integration. The content validity was examined by presenting the scales to academic and professional experts from the banking and consulting business sectors. Two pilot studies were conducted. The feedback from these was used to review and finalise the items of the scales and reword the questionnaire statements as necessary.

The reliability of the scales was examined using Cronbach’s coefficient alpha in SPSS 11.0 for Windows. The alpha coefficient (a) for each of the scales used to investigate the research questions of this study and the corresponding table where they are discussed in the data analysis are:

## 4. Limitations of the study

Our study focused only on the financial sector in the UK. Although conclusions for the whole industry may be drawn, the comparison of experiences across business sectors from different countries could show differences in CRM management and implementation issues.

## 5.2. What experiences were gained from using CRM tools at the strategic and processes level?

Data in Table 2 indicated the relatively high strategic expectations of the respondents regarding the potential of CRM: 96% of them strongly or moderately regard it as a source for competitive advantage. Also 81% of them agreed strongly and 19% moderately that it would assist in the coordination of the sale of products and services to their customers.

However, the strategic expectations were not always fulfilled in practice. CRM data was only being used in a minor way to assist organisations penetrate new markets: no respondent strongly felt that CRM had actually helped their organisation enter new markets. There was also no clear indication of wide use of CRM solutions in sales and marketing strategies. Similarly, 43% of the respondents made limited use of CRM-derived data to maintain or improve their market share as opposed to the 14% who acknowledged strong use. Moreover, neither the customer pre/post sales activity nor the creation of new products seemed to be unequivocally facilitated by CRM in practice. Only a small percentage supported its strong use, although there is an optimistic group that supports it ‘moderately’ in both areas.

Additionally, the anticipated impact of CRM on organisational business processes was not as expected, since the spread of responses to this issue was equal amongst ‘Strong’, ‘Moderate’, and ‘Little’. Indeed, no respondents strongly agreed that its use had reduced organisational operating costs. CRM enabled solutions were failing to deliver the expected reductions in the operating costs and to improve intra- and interorganisational transaction processing and the reconfiguring of organisational structures. Responses about the impact on organisational transaction processing were again surprising: 71% saw ‘little or no impact. Also, 81% of the respondents indicated that CRM has reduced the cost of inter-organisational transactions ‘little’ or ‘not at all’. Furthermore, the extent to which CRM has impacted the organisational structure was limited.

These findings lead to the conclusion that respondent organisations are not using their CRM tools to their fullest capacity or that CRM does not provide its anticipated benefits. Such findings are supported by other surveys, which suggest that financial institutions did not consider CRM to be a strategy, nor did they integrate it with their business.

## 5.3. What experiences were gained from using CRM tools for supporting communication channels?

The use of call centres was very popular amongst the respondents with 76% indicating their reliance on this communication channel. Approximately one quarter of the respondents relied moderately on email, but 57% tended to use it little or even not at all (19%). Five- and ten percent of the sample indicating a strong and moderate use respectively, of interactive digital TV (IDTV) as a communication channel, while 43% indicated their strong or moderate reliance on the permission marketing approach (Table 3).

There is no evidence to support the idea that FSPs develop a strategy that integrates and co-ordinates communication channels, which has been considered an important factor in reaping the benefits from CRM implementation.

## 5.4. What experiences were gained from using CRM tools for supporting different stages of the businessto-customer (B2C) interaction?

Of the respondents, 29% confirmed strongly and 48% moderately that they rely on CRM software in order to provide their customers with information about the availability of products and services. However, the degree to which customer-related data was disseminated throughout the organisations varied (‘Strongly’ 24%; ‘Moderately’ 43%; ‘Little’ 33%). More than half of the respondents used CRM ‘strongly’ or ‘moderately’ to either design or customise products/services for their customers needs. It was used also in varying degrees to respond to customers’ complaints. Fifty-seven percent of the respondents do not provide training for their CRM systems users, while 24% provided only ‘a little’.

## 5.6. How did companies evaluate the performance of the CRM tools?

Twenty-nine percent of the respondents acknowledged that their CRM system was capable of being cost justified in the same way as any other capital asset. But the other participant answers suggested that CRM systems were only marginally capable of being costjustified. This is consistent with a finding that 53% of the respondents made strong and moderate use of ROI indicators while 47% made little use of them at all.

Performance of CRM systems post-implementation was generally made by using metrics, though 14% indicated that they did not use them. The lack of a universal acceptance of metrics across the business sector is an indication of poor management.

## 6. CRM expectations

The greater majority of the respondents were of the view that their organisation’s dependence on CRM systems would increase over the next four years. Therefore, a wider coverage throughout the financial services sector is anticipated in the future by a total of 90% of respondents (Table 7).

There is a strong indication that CRM systems will grow in importance. This is probably due to the fact that FSPs now realise that competitive advantage will not be achieved exclusively through product differentiation.

## 7. Conclusions

CRM approaches and applications are key factors for modern businesses. In the FSP sector, an information intensive industry, knowing more about the customers could prove to be a source of competitive advantage. Our survey indicated optimism about CRM’s potential and future proliferation although the results supported findings from previous studies that financial institutions fail to adopt an appropriate approach to CRM implementation

Therefore, FSP’s need to:

 Integrate CRM data and applications with their existing IS infrastructure as well with their business processes for use in customer related decisionmaking and management.

 Develop a well thought metrics system, broader than just financial indicators, for CRM systems evaluation.

 Develop a customer-centric culture.

With respect to the strategic potential of collecting and analysing customer data, no one of the companies in the sample used any of this data to enter new markets. In an era of intensive competition, data mining is clearly under deployed. The development of new services and products is also an area that needs more attention. Clearly, the FSPs do not invest in CRM approaches and technologies to improve the core of their businesses. At the process level data shows a similar disappointing situation: it seems there is absolutely no effect on the internal processes and that operational cost, while limited, are the benefits.

With respect to CRM support for B2C interactions, FSPs did not seem to take into account any CRM input in customising services, managing complaints, or providing support to their customers. As a result there was only limited impact of CRM on customer satisfaction and loyalty. The current level of its application was restricted to giving information to customers with no contribution to the core of customer management and services development. Moreover, communication channels are poorly developed for FSPs who largely rely on call centres.

We therefore suggest that:

 The design of services should include CRM technologies as well. The analysis of customer profiles will provide valuable input for new services.

 The implementation of the services should be supported by changes in the organisational processes. Customers should be considered an integral part of business processes. Such changes should integrate all communication channels.

 The evaluation metrics of CRM approaches and systems should be the same as those used for process management, customer satisfaction, and strategy development.

 Integration with the customers should be a priority.

Our study also indicated that there are no major technical obstacles. It is rather a matter of supporting a change management program and introducing new approaches and technologies drawing on prior lessons.
