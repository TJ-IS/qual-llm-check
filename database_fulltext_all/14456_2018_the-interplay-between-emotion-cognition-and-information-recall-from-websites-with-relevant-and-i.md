## Abstract

Websites are increasingly used for information dissemination and web sites can include cues such as images that stimulate emotional responses in users and aid recall of information that can be utilized in decision making. However, these images may be more or less relevant in the context of the information provided. In such cases there is likely to be a complex interplay between users’ emotional and cognitive responses and the degree of information recall. This study used event-related electroencephalography (EEG) to shed light on these inter-relationships. Images providing emotional cues influenced emotions and EEG responses and these responses were in turn related to the degree of information recall. Importantly, the study shows that the neural correlates of emotion vary depending on whether the emotion-inducing image is relevant to the information provided or not and that these correlates are significantly related to recall.

Keywords: Neuro-IS, Electroencephalography, Event-related Potentials, Emotions, Cognition, Recall of Web-based information

## 1. Introduction

Websites are increasingly becoming a primary source of information for people across the globe [42]. The main objective of informational websites is to improve an audience’s knowledge of specific topics to enable more effective decision making. However, the increasing amount of information available on websites may have an adverse impact on the online users’ task of recalling information [26]. A low performance on the task of recalling web-based information may adversely affect the amount of knowledge that online users can acquire from these websites and, in turn, hinder information providers’ objective to increase their audience’s knowledge and better equip them for future decision making. Therefore, it is of interest to investigate how users can better retain web-based information.

Kelton, Pennington and Tuttle [29] present a theoretical framework and review based on Shaft and Vesey’s [56] extended cognitive fit model that shows how information presentation influences mental representations and thus decision making outcomes, in part indirectly through improved task knowledge for the decision maker. Further, it has been argued that “…effects of presentation formats are more important in the information acquisition part of the entire process than in the actual problem solving…” [16, p. 97]. Further, prior work shows that higher levels of information recall can be achieved by presenting information in different formats such as multimedia [26,36]; text illustrators [23]; lists versus matrix [9];knowledge maps [8]; pictures [24], The nature of product presentation has been shown to influence recall and has implications for consumer behaviour, a form of decision making [e.g., see 4]. The focus of this stream of research has been on investigating the influence of different information presentation formats that facilitates users' cognitive processing by providing complementary (relevant) information cues [26, 68]. However, a recent study has pointed out that

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Technology-Mediated Task Performance and Decision Making

Technology-mediated task performance (TMTP) refers to “performance on a given technology-mediated task, measured by task outcomes” [32, p. 707]. Reading web-based information to improve one’s knowledge of a specific topic is one form of a technology-mediated task, and the amount of knowledge gained by online users can be referred to as an indicator of TMTP [32].

Task performance such as information recall plays a vital role in knowledge acquisition from a given informational resource [36]. Knowledge that is acquired from information resources can then be utilized in decision making, as shown in the classic rational decision making model of Simon [59], where relevant knowledge is required in all three stages of the model: intelligence, design and choice.

## 2.2. Interface Design and Information Recall

Following prior literature, the focus of our study is on the interface design features, particularly features that represent information in different formats such as multimedia formats that include images and text and provide complementary information cues [23, 35, 36, 62] as well as inducing emotions through interface design features [50].The extended cognitive fit model of Shaft and Vesey [56] provides a highlevel theoretical base for our work as it links information presentation, recall and decision making outcomes. This model proposes that information presentation can affect decision making in two ways. First, information presentation formats affect the way information is processed in working memory. Second, information presentation formats indirectly affect decision making by influencing decision makers’ characteristics such as knowledge of a specific topic. Following Vessey (1991), the model proposes that a correspondence between task and information presentation lead to superior task performance by individual users. Kelton, et al. [29] find support for the extended-cognitive fit model in

review of the literature on the effects of information presentation format on judgment and decision making. For example, multimedia is proposed to have benefits for decision making as it allows for richer representations than simple text as in [36]. The theory of image-text comprehension suggests that when textual information is presented along with relevant images, a human’s cognitive system can create mental representations in the working memory which are most appropriate for the given task, leading to better task related knowledge and performance [55]. Kelton et al [29] note, however, that research into the efficacy of multimedia presentation in organizational contexts is sparse, with most research in educational settings.

## 2.3. Interface Design and Cognition

Some researchers who investigated the link between multimedia and information recall used cue summation theory and theory of information recall to propose that presenting the same information in different formats could increase the number of information cues that complement each other and lead to high levels of information recall [68]. Many of these studies have used graphics, matrix, and videos along with textual information to provide complementary informational cues. However, there is little research that uses pictures or images to induce emotions and provide additional informational cues despite the fact that images and text when presented together lead to effective use of cognitive resources [55].

According to the image-text comprehension theory [55] when written textual information is presented with an image, the human cognitive system actively starts creating mental representations for text and image that are best suited for the task at hand. It recognizes that the organization of verbal information (i.e., textual information) takes place in the verbal sub-system of working memory, whereas that of pictorial information takes place in the pictorial sub-system of working memory [2]. However, the model proposes that when mental representations are created for text and images processed at the same time, for the sake of integration of the text-based mental models with the components of the image-

based mental model, it is necessary that both the components of the textual and pictorial information being processed are present in working memory [55]. Therefore, according to this theory, the best use of human's cognitive resources can be achieved by presenting textual and pictorial information together that are relevant to each other (that is, provide complementary informational cues).

## 2.4. Interface Design and Emotions

Interface design features are not limited to graphics or videos, rather they may comprise several other features such as color [49], animation [67] and website images [10] [see for a review]. These design features in general and website images in particular have been found to be effective in providing complementary information cues by using images of products [26] and inducing emotions in online users by using soothing images such as cute animals or smiling faces [20, 50]. However, there is little research that investigates the influence of website images that induce emotions as well as provide complementary informational cues (an exception is Clements and Wolfe, 1997, cited in [29]. We believe the theoretical lens adopted in this study - an image-text comprehension model - allows us to examine the interaction of emotion and cognition in the context of the study.

## 2.5. Interaction of Emotion and Cognition

According to the nomological network for emotions for IS research proposed by [20], “cognitive processing and emotional experiences” are intertwined [p. 17]. The justification for this view is that emotions were evolved to serve specific functions – to facilitate the survival chances of early humans [55]. Therefore, when individuals come across a stimulus, they try to appraise it for its personal significance according to the levels of perceived facilitation or relatedness of the stimulus to the tasks at hand [54]. For example, an emotionally neutral stimulus may be perceived as positive if it helps individuals achieve their goals [43]. On the other hand, an emotionally positive stimulus may be perceived as less positive if it is irrelevant to the individuals’ goals or tasks at hand [13]. Despite the theoretical underpinnings for the interaction of cognition and emotions, our understanding of the

influence of this interaction effect is limited especially in the context of website design research and its impact on recall of web-based information.

Therefore, we believe that investigating the combined effect of information presentation formats that provide complementary informational cues as well as inducing emotions on knowledge acquisition addresses an important research gap, as identified by [29]. Further, responding to an important call by proponents of NeuroIS research [e.g., 51], we use a novel EEG technique to measure emotions. Hence, we provide a brief description of EEG and event related EEG potential in the next section.

## 2.6. Electroencephalography (EEG) and Event-Related Potential

Electroencephalography (EEG) measures electrical postsynaptic potential (i.e., the electric activity generated by communicating neurons) from the scalp. Event related potential (ERP) is “a depiction of the changes in scalp-recorded voltage over time that reflects the sensory, cognitive, affective and motor processes elicited by a stimulus” [27, p. 4]. In other words, ERP is stimulus/event locked EEG. The ERP waveform has three characteristics: (1) amplitude measured in micro volts, (2) polarity of the amplitude either positive or negative, and (3) latency, the duration of the ERP waveform. An ERP waveform is divided into ERP components which are specific to a time window in respect to the stimulus onset. For example, P1 is a component that lies between 60-130 milliseconds from the stimulus onset. It is called P1 because its amplitude is positive and the amplitude is highest around 100 milliseconds. ERP components such as P2 have been shown to represent emotional processes [21].

## 3.1. Interface Design, Cognition and Emotion

Theory of emotion posits that emotion-laden stimulus produces an emotional state that can be mapped on dimensions of valence and arousal [52]. Several studies have employed images to induce emotions [33]. We argue that inclusion of emotion-laden images on websites as well as any images that carry emotion-inducing quality can also evoke emotions in online users. Emotionally positive images will lead to high levels of valence (i.e., positive emotions). Emotionally negative images will lead to low levels of valence (i.e., negative emotions). Emotionally positive website design refers to the website design that induces positive emotions in online users where otionally negative website design refers to the website design that induces negative emotions in the online users. We adopted a dimensional view of emotions in this study. Valence refers to subjective feelings of pleasantness or unpleasantness while arousal refer to the subjective feelings of activation [13].It has been argued that both emotionally positive and negative stimuli can lead to higher levels of arousal compared with neutral stimuli [52].Image-text relevance and irrelevance refer to the degree of the perceived relatedness of website images to the information presented. Based on the above discussion, we propose the following

hypotheses<sup>1</sup>: Hypothesis 1: Emotionally positive website images will lead to higher levels of valence compared with emotionally negative and neutral website images for both image-text relevant and irrelevant websites (H1a). Emotionally positive and negative website images will lead to higher levels of arousal compared with emotionally neutral website images for both image-text relevant and irrelevant websites (H1b).

Applying the nomological network for emotions [20] and theory of emotion regulation [43], which explain the processes underlying interaction between cognition and emotions, we draw on the notion that goal-orientedness plays a vital role when it comes to processing website stimuli. When online users are performing some specific task, it is very important that website images should facilitate the accomplishment of that task and not hinder it [13]. Thus, emotionality of website images will be cognitively appraised in accordance with the levels of facilitation offered by them in carrying out the task at hand.

Further, it has been reported that when visual design features are to be incorporated into the websites, design features that are perceived by the online users to be hindering their goals, would cause the elicitation of negative emotions [20, 60, 57]. Therefore, it is important that design features should match online users’ tasks and goals [36, 61]. Goal-orientedness can occur when website users have a task of reading the information from given websites with the intention of being able to recall that information at a later stage. We propose that users, who are exposed to the website images relevant to the given text, will feel higher levels of valence (in positive website condition) and arousal than the valence and arousal of the participants who are exposed to irrelevant images. The logic of this proposed relationship is based in the theories of image-text comprehension model [55] and effects of information presentation formats [29, 56]. When textual information is presented along with image, people tend to find their relevance with each other [55]. The relevance of image with the text will provide

complementary informational cues leading to the presence of a cognitive fit (e.g., facilitating role of the complementary cues) [29]. However, if the website image are not relevant to the textual information, such facilitation will be absent. Based on the above discussion, we posit the following hypothesis. Hypothesis 2: Image-text relevance has a moderating effect on the relationship between positive website stimuli and valence and arousal, such that the relationship is enhanced for image-text relevant websites (H2a for valence, H2b for arousal).

3.2. Interface Design, Neural Correlates of Emotions and the Interaction of Emotions and Cognition The proponents of Neuro-IS research encourage IS researchers to use EEG for research involving emotion and suggest that identifying neural correlates of IS constructs is vital for IS research [14]. Eventrelated EEG potentials offer valuable insights into how people process emotional responses by capturing brain activity involved in the processing of emotional responses [11]. Various ERP components have been found to be sensitive to the emotionality of a stimulus compared to neutral stimulus [17]. Among these components are P1, N1, N170, N2, and P2. However, studies have shown mixed results about how well emotional stimuli affect P1, N1, and N170. N170 especially has been shown to be sensitive to facial expressions [68]. For example, some studies have found that P1’s amplitude increases for emotional images compared with neutral images [24, 58]. Others report that P1’s amplitude reduces for emotional images [48]. The situation is similar with N1 and N170 [8, 44]. However, P2 and N2have been reported to be stable indicators of emotional responses [37, 53]. Prior literature suggests measuring P2 and N2 at central sites of the human scalp (e.g., Cz) [66]. Several studies have found that the amplitude of P2 and N2 is larger for emotional stimuli than neutral stimuli [7, 23, 31]. Though there is no agreement among researchers that the effect of pleasant and unpleasant stimuli on the amplitude is equal, there is a wide agreement that the amplitudes of P2 and N2 will be larger for emotionally positive and negative stimuli when compared to neutral stimuli [21].

However, “The mechanisms of emotion and cognition are intertwined from early perception to complex reasoning” [46, p. 46]. It has been argued that not only can emotions affect cognitive processes such as attention and perception, cognitive functions can affect the experience of emotion, specifically “…cognitive interpretations are an important factor in the perception of emotion.” [46, p. 33]. There seems to be no agreement among researchers if cognitive evaluation takes precedence over affective evaluation, however, there seems to be agreement that both cognitive evaluation (e.g. aluating stimuli for its personal significance) and affective evaluation (e.g., evaluating stimuli for emotion inducing characteristics) are both intertwined.

For example, with trust-inducing stimuli, we can assume that a stimulus that is appraised to be facilitating the task at hand will be perceived as trustworthy while the stimulus that is appraised to be hindering the task at hand will be perceived as untrustworthy. Prior research has observed the ERP component of P2 when participants were exposed to a trustworthy stimulus [5] while N2 was observed with participants were exposed to an untrustworthy stimulus that did not fulfil participants expectations [67]. Following this finding, we assume that in image-text relevant condition, participants will show P2 while in image-text irrelevant condition, they will show N2. However, the influence of emotionality of the stimulus on the neural correlates will be as such that the amplitude of P2 in imagetext relevant and that of N2 in image-text irrelevant conditions will be larger than with emotionally neutral conditions. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: Emotionally positive and negative websites will lead to larger amplitudes of P2compared with an emotionally neutral website for image-text relevant websites (H3a, H3b). Emotionally positive and negative websites will lead to larger amplitudes of N2 compared with emotionally neutral website for image-text irrelevant websites (H3c, H3d).

3.3. Emotions, Neural Correlates of Emotions and Information Recall

According to theories of emotion from the evolutionary psychology perspective [43, 32, 28], early humans developed emotions to facilitate their survival chances. Positive emotions were developed to help them approach fitness maximizing events such as food sources or potential mates, while negative emotions helped them to avoid potential danger [28]. Another important aspect of the development of emotions was to help early humans to remember both fitness maximizing and impairing events and elements so that they could approach or avoid them in the future [41]. Further, literature from neuroscience [e.g., 22, 58] suggests that when information is presented in the context of emotioneliciting stimuli, the recall of that information at a later stage is enhanced because at the encoding stage, an emotional tag is added to the information. Therefore, recall of emotion-eliciting images has been found to be higher than with neutral images [25]. Even recall of words, which were presented in the context of emotion-eliciting sentences, has been reported higher than the recall of words which were presented in the context of neutral sentences [38]. Kock et al. [32] investigated the impact of negative emotions on information recall [42]. They found that the recall of participants who experienced negative emotions was higher than those who did not experience negative emotions. Further, in examining the relationship between emotional responses and recall of information, researchers have considered the “strength” of the emotional response as the driver, not whether it was a positive or a negative emotion [see 30]. Thus, when we consider the relationship between valence and information recall, our focus is on “valence strength” rather than “valence”. Valence strength depicts the distance from the central point of neutral valence, in both positive and negative directions. Hence, we expect that a strong emotion of unhappiness will have the same effect as a strong emotion of happiness on recall. Similar to valence strength, we expect arousal, which represents the intensity of the emotional feeling [13] and also affects the duration of the emotional experience [63], will have positive effects on the levels of information recall. Hence, we posit the following hypotheses. Hypothesis 4: Higher levels of valence

strength will lead to higher levels of information recall.Hypothesis 5: Higher levels of arousal will lead to higher levels of information recall.

ERP components (e.g., P2 and N2) have been reported to be reliable indicators of emotional responses [see 21 for a review]<sup>2</sup>. Prior studies have found that, when we read information presented in an emotional context, an emotional tag is added to the information while we encode it, which improves the recall of that information [25, 58]. ## 4. Methodology

We conducted laboratory experiments to test the proposed research model. We used a 3 x 2 mixed design model (3 types of emotional conditions (within-subjects) x 2 levels of perceived relevance of website images and text (between-subjects)). The emotional conditions were (1) positive, (2) negative, and (3) neutral website images. We manipulated the relevance of the website images by including either relevant or irrelevant images with respect to the text on the websites. We followed recommendations of Muller-Putz, Riedl and Wriessnegger [40] and Picton et al. [47] regarding participants, instruments, experimental protocol and stimuli, data pre-processing and data analysis.

## 4.1. Experimental Websites

We designed websites that provided health-related information to the general public with information on medicines and their effects on certain body functions. We chose health websites as experimental vehicles for two reasons. First, governments use these types of websites to disseminate health-related information online. Second, we believe information recall is an important factor in the context of medicines and their potential effects because it enhances people’s knowledge of useful and potentially life-saving information [65]. We used 180 websites that differed in images, medicine names, and

affected body function’s names. We chose images from the International Affective Picture Systems (IAPS) [33] and similar images from Google Images.

## 4.4. EEG Recording

We used an EEG device from BioSemi to record brain activity (amplification 20K and nominal bandpass 0.10 - 100 Hz). It involved 16 electrodes positioned according to the 10-20 international electrode placement system. The vertex electrode was used as a reference and digitized at 250Hz. We used two electro-oculogram (EOG) (one vertical and one horizontal) to record participants’ eye movements and blink artefacts so that they could be identified and eliminated. We cleaned and analysed the EEG data using EEGLab version 11.0.0 [12] and ERPLab. We filtered EEG signals with a 0.1 Hz high-pass and 30 Hz low-pass. We initially screened out eye blinks, saccades and other muscle artefacts using EEGLab by looking at the continuous data. We re-referenced the data to the average channel. We carried out all of these procedures in EEGLab. Then, using ERPLab event-markers for the emotionally positive, negative, and neutral website conditions, we extracted epochs<sup>4</sup> and assigned them unique numbers in order to analyse them collectively. We rejected artifacts using ERPLab’s artifact rejection function [37]. Next, we generated the averaged ERP waveform, and created the scalp distribution of ERP waveforms and amplitudes for ERP components. Stimulus-locked ERPs were extracted 200 ms pre-stimulus to 1000ms post-stimulus. The extracted ERPs were then segmented into six conditions (3 conditions for image-text relevant and 3 conditions for image-text irrelevant).

## 4.5. Participants

Forty two right-handed participants took part in the study. All participants were healthy and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. No participant took any medicine on the day of the study. We discarded the data from five participants because of excessive EEG artefacts caused by body movements. Therefore, we analysed the data from 37 participants (17 females: mean age = 23, SD= 3.94, 20 males: mean age = 22, SD = 2.80). Demographic information showed that all the participants were Internet users and went online daily to look for some type of information. We recruited participants via advertisements in a public university. Student subjects were recruited because they are primary users of health websites and as such are considered representative of the general population [18].

6. Discussion

## 6.1. Theoretical and Methodological Contributions

First, the support for our proposed set of hypotheses suggests that IS researchers can use interface design to induce desired emotional responses in users whether they are positive or negative emotions.

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

This is an important finding given that a great deal of emphasis has been given on user experience related variables such as emotions especially for the interface design of websites. Second, presenting complementary informational cues using images that can induce emotions have particularly important theoretical implications for interface design theory. For example, facilitating cognitive processing through diverse information presentation formats has been extensively studied [e.g., 36]. This study provides a new avenue for research by proposing that not only cognitive processing can be facilitated by providing complementary information cues, high levels of positive emotions can also be induced by combing interface design features that evoke emotions on one hand and facilitate a given task on the other. Third, the theoretically developed and empirically tested mediating role of emotions is a novel theoretical contribution. The study's findings suggest that combining textual information with emotioninducing interface design features such as website images that also offer complementary informational cues allows human brains to engage both cognitive and emotional processes and provides better user experiences (e.g., inducing desired emotions) and also improving task performance such as information recall at the same time.

Fourth, on the methodological front, this study responds to the call to use EEG to examine emotions [e.g., 51]. The findings of this study further elaborate the need of using Neuro-IS measures such as EEG. For example, in this study, the behavioral responses of participants did not show any difference between emotional responses for image-text relevant and irrelevant websites. However, EEG data revealed interaction of cognition and emotions such that the interface design of websites that facilitated the given task led to the evocation of P2 while the interface design that did not provide such facilitation led to the evocation of N2. We believe that this finding is important because it provides advice to NeuroIS researchers to be mindful in using the amplitudes of P2 and N2 as predictors of emotional responses and consider whether or not the emotion-inducing interface design features are facilitating the given task of participants.

Finally, this study also makes another important contribution by showing some support for a relationship between neural correlates of emotions such as amplitudes of P2 and N2 and information recall (see H6 and Table 5).

## 6.3. Managerial Contributions

This study demonstrates that information providers (e.g., governments) may use emotion-laden imagetext relevant interface design features such as website images that carry complementary informational cues to enhance online users’ task performance and in turn improve their knowledge for relevant decision making. Further, developers who are responsible for designing effective websites may also draw design guidelines from this study’s findings to enhance users’ hedonic experience by inducing desired emotional responses in them.

This study’s findings could also be applied to the advertising and brand management contexts. For instance, attracting the attention of online users and making them remember brand names have been of utmost importance to marketing and IS researchers [23]. Emotional responses induced by interface design features may not only increase the level of attention for interfaces, but may also lead to better retention of brand names or information presented besides those interface design features. Similarly, pharmaceutical companies and the companies that sell vitamins or supplements can also benefit from the study’s findings by using emotion eliciting interface design features which are relevant to the text being presented along with their brand or medicine names to improve the chances that online users will remember their products and brands in making purchase decisions, which in turn may fetch them more revenue.

## 6.4. Limitations and Future Research Directions

A limitation is that we used a static website, which does not allow participants to click on links and has very minimal interaction. Although prior IS research has used static web pages as experimental stimuli [e.g., 10, 13], we suggest that researchers should test the proposed research model using interactive

websites as experimental stimuli. A limitation of this study is the use of student sample. The use of students as participants has been widely discussed in Information Systems [45] and Management [34]. We think as Pew International report that majority of health website users fall between the age of 18 – 29 years old (Pew, 2013, p. 7) and because students also belong to that age group, the results of this study can fairly be generalizable to the young members of general public.

## 7. Conclusion

Although the role of emotions has been established for improving user experience and task performance, it has not sufficiently been examined in interface design research especially in online information provision. We contribute to website interface design theory particularly by investigating the mediating role of emotions for the interface design and task performance relationship. We propose that image-text comprehension theory, fitting within a more general framework of cognitive fit theory, offers an appropriate lens to investigate the interaction of emotion and cognition for interface design research. Specifically, we show that by combining design features that induce emotions and provide complementary informational cues, we can enhance user experience as well as task performance. An important finding is that neural correlates of emotions vary depending upon whether the emotioninducing-interface design features facilitate the given task or not. Further, we also show that use of Neuro-IS techniques such as EEG carries significant implications for research that involves emotions.

## ShirelyGregor

Shirley Gregor is the foundation Professor of Information Systems at the Australian National University, where she is a Director of the National Centre for Information Systems Research. Professor Gregor’s current research interests include the adoption and strategic use of information and communications technologies, intelligent systems and human-computer interface issues, and the theoretical foundations of information systems. Dr Gregor has led several large applied research projects funded by the Meat Research Corporation, the Department of Communications, Information Technology and the Arts, the Australian Research Council and AusAID. Professor Gregor spent a number of years in the computing industry in Australia and the United Kingdom before beginning an academic career. She obtained her PhD in Information Systems from the University of Queensland.

Dr Gregor’s publications include 4 edited books, 15 book chapters and over 100 papers in conferences and journals such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association of Information Systems, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, International Journal of Human Computer Studies, European Journal of Information Systems and Information Technology & People.

Professor Gregor was inaugural President of the Australasian Association of Information Systems 2002-2003 and was Region 3 (Asia/Pacific) Councillor for the Association of Information Systems 2007- 2009. She was a Senior Editor for MIS Quarterly 2008-2010 and was Editor-in-Chief of the Journal of the Association for Information Systems 2010-2013.

Professor Gregor was made an Officer of the Order of Australia in the Queen’s Birthday Honour’s list in June 2005 for services as an educator and researcher in the field of information systems and in the development of applications for electronic commerce in the agribusiness sector. In 2005 she was elected as a Fellow of the Australian Computer Society and in 2010 she became a Fellow of the Association for Information Systems.

## Saif Dewan

Dr Saif Dewan is a Postdoctoral Researcher at the Research School of Management at the Australian National University (ANU) and a visiting Assistant Professor at the University of Twente, Netherlands. He has obtained his PhD in Information Systems (IS) from the College of Business and Economics (CBE) at the ANU. He has performed important teaching and/or research roles at other local and foreign universities including the London School of Economics and University of New South Wales. He has published widely in IS and has presented his works in major IS conferences. His current research interests include human-computer interaction, technology-work nexus, mobile technologies and computational social science, big data analytics, auxiliary theory, and service innovation. He has also worked as an IT Consultant and Computer Programmer, and his clients included local and foreign Governments, including the Australian and New Zealand Government Departments, United Nations, and Samsung Corporation. He has been on the board of several Technology Startups, NGOs and private Schools over the years.
