# The acceptance and diffusion of the innovative smart phone use: A case study of a delivery service company in logistics

Keywords: Smart phone adoption Technology adoption Innovation diffusion Technology acceptance Delivery service providers

## A B S T R A C T

Technology adoption has been the topic of several theories. We empirically assessed and integrated four models to help in predicting smart phone adoption. The varying factors were combined and a survey was conducted in five sales branches of a delivery service company. Results of the analysis of the collected data show that the relationships amongst constructs were similar. Self-efficacy was a strong predictor of behavioral intention through attitude; after innovation, diffusion theory was added. Attitude towards smart phone adoption was found to be affected mainly by testability and organizational and environmental factors. We decided that an iterative approach to implementing smart phone adoption was effective. Managerial implications are discussed.

## 1. Background

Voice-Over-IP digital signals transmission, personal information management, wireless communications, satellite based transmissions, and text and multimedia messaging have become core features in today’s cell phones. These aspects, not only inspire new uses of phones, but create dependencies between devices.

In addition to voice and text communication, many phones also provide functions such as camera, personal digital recorder, Personal Digital Assistant (PDA), etc. Smart phones are equipped with features that were originally found solely in PDAs. This smart phone evolution has provided opportunities to both cell phone and PDA manufacturers. Smart phones have become available worldwide giving users a single solution for all personal communication needs.

## 2. Research objective

Several theoretical models have been offered to explain why people accept new technology. The objective of our study was to test four theoretical models in order to predict employee intentions in adopting the new smart phone technology in the logistics industry. Workers in this industry are generally not as computer savvy as people in other industries and this may influence their perception of the smart phone’s ease of use. Therefore, we thought that acceptance theories may have to be revised for this segment of business.

## 3. Logistics industry

The American Logistic Management Association defines logistics as aiming to satisfy customers’ demands by linking products and service information between customer and manufacturer, thereby providing highly efficient, low cost management of the flow of goods. Workers in logistics industry generally have less education and thus less opportunity to explore new technology than those in more professional industries.

## 3.1. Needs in the logistics industry

The logistics industry, while using supply chain management, experiences fluctuation in its business cycle since demands vary by season and by the state of the economy. Therefore, using technology to drive business innovation, enhance customer service, and utilize available resources has become a strategic necessity. The use of smart phones can speed information for decision making or to stabilize demands resulting in steadier longterm profits. Delivery persons equipped with a smart phone can improve service through embedded features that make it possible to answer queries differently than before, scan barcodes, and gather information on-site to enhance customer relationships.

One major factor revolutionizing logistics is the sharing of front-end technology for data collection and back-end technology for data analysis. Therefore, data captured at point-of-sale or during delivery is crucial. In the logistics industry, IT is used by professionals who have little or no computer education.

## 4.3. Model 2: the effect of self-efficacy

Self-efficacy is the individual’s decision to meet a goal through organization and execution [2]. It consists of two parts: prediction of the result (whether the action will lead to a result) and efficacy (whether he or she can successfully execute procedures to achieve the result).

## 4.3.1. The role of self-efficacy

Computer efficacy helps in predicting technology acceptance by explaining an individual’s willingness to learn [20], make online purchases [26], and use decision making systems [29]. Computer efficacy affects use intention through Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU) and Perceived Usefulness (PU). Our second model incorporated selfefficacy (Fig. 2) and added the following two additional hypotheses:

Hypothesis 6. Smart phone self-efficacy has a direct effect on perceived ease of use.

Hypothesis 7. Smart phone self-efficacy has a direct effect on behavioral intention.

## 4.4. Model 3: the innovation and diffusion of technology

The IDT model suggested that individuals would only choose to adopt a technology if five perceived characteristics were present [21]:

(1) Relative advantage: the new products’ superiority over the product it is attempting to replace.

(2) Compatibility, if the new product complies with the original object’s functionality, user values, needs, and past experience.

(3) Complexity, when the product’s features, if many and varied, diminish the user’s understanding of, or increase the difficulty in, using a new product.

Fig. 2. Research Model 2: TAM with self-efficacy.

(4) Testability, whether the new product performs successfully in testing by non-experts.

(5) Observable; the degree of difficulty in discovering or describing the tangible and intangible benefits of the new product.

Factors, such as compatibility and testability, have been found to be necessary precursors of technological diffusion [1,17]. However, they have not always operated in the same way. For example, ‘‘compatibility’’ has been measured as the compatibility between technology, life style, and technology use preferences [16,27] and as the compatibility between software and hardware systems [12].

Although IDT seems comprehensive, it can be extended to include factors such as task characteristics (task structure and uncertainty), individual characteristics (education, experience, and specialized skills), organizational factors (organizational structure and managerial support), and environmental factors (market strategies and pressure from competitors) [15] as illustrated in Fig. 3. Therefore, Model 3 introduces the following additional hypotheses:

Hypothesis 6. Compatibility has a direct effect on attitude towards smart phone adoption.

Hypothesis 7. Compatibility has a direct effect on smart phone usefulness.

Hypothesis 8. Observable has a direct effect on attitude towards smart phone adoption.

Hypothesis 9. Testability has a direct effect on attitude towards smart phone adoption.

Hypothesis 10. Task characteristics have a direct effect on attitude towards smart phone adoption.

Hypothesis 11. Individual characteristics have a direct effect on attitude towards smart phone adoption.

Hypothesis 12. Organizational characteristics have a direct effect on attitude towards smart phone adoption.

Hypothesis 13. Environment characteristics have a direct effect on attitude towards smart phone adoption.

## 4.5. Model 4: TAM with IDT and self-efficacy

Research has also generated several constructs to predict technology use intention. These have a unified meaning within their scope, but when joined they may present a comprehensive model, predicting the use of new technology. Therefore, the fourth model (Fig. 4) was intended to be comprehensive to predict smart phone use. One additional construct, self-efficacy, was added to model three to form the last model. The relationship of self-efficacy with the other TAM constructs has been proposed in our second model. The literature of the role of self-efficacy on attitude and behavioral intention has also been reviewed in the section for model two. Therefore, model four becomes an all-inclusive model that has all constructs and their relationships reviewed in the previous three models. In Fig. 4, the paths that start from selfefficacy to attitude and to behavioral intention are posited as Hypotheses 14 and 15, which are the same hypotheses numbered Hypotheses 6 and 7 in model two. The rationale for this renumbering is to ensure a smooth transition from model three to model four in terms of hypothesis numbers.

## 5. Research method

The survey questionnaire was developed based on existing instruments of IDT, TAM [28] and self-efficacy [5], most of which are broadly adopted in related studies. Questions in the questionnaire were modified to target smart phone use in the work environment with a special care on how the questions are phrased in order to avoid technical jargons, abbreviations and buzz words. This precaution is necessary to survey respondents with low education or technology preparedness, as it was recommended by supervisors of sales drivers in our prior interviews. Back translation was applied to make sure the translated Chinese does not violate the original meanings of the English questionnaire items. The questions were reviewed by content experts to ensure clarity and face validity.

## 6. Sampling

The sample was randomly drawn from employees directly involved in logistic activities in the five sales branches of the organization. These branches were chosen because they were in large metropolitan areas of Taiwan, with high volumes of sales. Because of top management commitment, problems during the survey administration were minimal. To ensure familiarity with the technology and to eliminate bias due to differences in features of available smart phone brands, a training session was offered to the respondents prior to the formal survey using a single brand of smart phone (Dopod 699). Trainees had an opportunity to try out features of smart phones and compare the usability with existing gadgets such as barcode readers, set-up boxes, GPS systems and other systems available in their work environment. Respondents were free to take as much time as they wished after the training session to experiment with the features of this model of smart phone.

## 6.1. Reliability and validity

Reliability of the factors was measured as both Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability. Table 2 shows that the Cronbach’s alphas calculated for this study ranged between 0.89 (for ‘‘Task’’) and 0.95 (for ‘‘perceived ease of use’’, ‘‘perceived usefulness’’, and ‘‘behavioral intention’’). The composite reliability values ranged between 0.73 (for self-efficacy) and 0.93 (for ‘‘behavioral intention’’ and ‘‘attitude’’). Reliability measured in both Cronbach alpha and composite reliability exceeded the minimum of 0.7 as suggested in previous studies [19]. Therefore, the results indicated satisfactory construct reliability.

To assess construct validity, we first conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). An 11-factor measurement model was estimated. Although the chi-square value for the model was significant $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 3 3 9 . 7 3 , \mathrm { d . f . } = 1 8 7 , p = 0 . 0 0 )$ , other fit indices show a good model fit (GFI = 0.93, AGFI = 0.86, CFI = 0.97, NFI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.05).

Convergent validity was specifically verified by following three criteria [8]: (a) all indicator loadings should be significant and exceed 0.7; (b) construct reliabilities should exceed 0.80; and (c) average variance extracted (AVE) calculated for each construct should exceed 0.50. The indicator loadings calculated in the CFA model ranged from 0.75 to 0.95, thus satisfying the first criterion stated above. Table 2 shows that Cronbach alphas exceeded 0.80, whereas AVE values exhibited were all greater than 0.50. Therefore, all three conditions for convergent validity were met.

## 8. Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we tested several structural models to predict smart phone use at five sales branches of a delivery service provider. Three main theories (including TAM, self-efficacy theory, and IDT) comprised the models in our study. The first model (only TAM) was expanded to the second model with the inclusion of selfefficacy. The third model considered TAM and IDT. The fourth model was a comprehensive version of all three theories. All models fit the data.

The TAM model that was tested showed that PEOU and PU both had a direct and positive effect on attitude. Both attitude and PEOU had a positive effect on behavioral intention (BI) to use the smart phone. PEOU’s positive role on PU was also confirmed. Although our model shows that PEOU had a weaker but significant effect on attitude than PU, PEOU’s effect on PU was surprisingly high. This finding differs from some existing studies (e.g., [13,22]), which found that PEOU’s effect on PU was weak. Considering the industry from which our sample was drawn, this finding may not be an unexpected surprise. In logistics and transportation, drivers reply heavily on the speed and accuracy of communication devices to stay effective. Under a high demand for speed and accuracy, PEOU can be largely related to the responsiveness of the system, ease of navigation and speed of data and voice transfer. However, such a high demand of system and user interface capabilities does not outweigh the role of PU on attitude. In our first model, PU still was the most significant antecedent factor on attitude.

Our second model adds self-efficacy into the first model to predict the overall effect of the expanded model on smart phone use. Existing relationships of TAM factors are once again confirmed with both PEOU and PU as the antecedents for attitude. Both attitude and PU are confirmed to be important factors that affect behavioral intention. Self-efficacy not only marginally affected behavioral intention, but also was a strong antecedent of PEOU. This offers some empirical evidence of the role of self-efficacy in conjunction with TAM. Existing research along this line does not seem to agree upon self-efficacy’s role in TAM. For example, selfefficacy is found to be an antecedent variable for both PEOU and PU in some studies [10], but the antecedent is strictly for PEOU in other studies [3,14]. Evidence is also available to support selfefficacy’s ‘‘negative effect’’ on PU and no effect on PEOU [4]. Chau speculated that the difference between his findings and Igbaria and

Liravi’s was that Chau’s study targeted a specific program use (i.e., MS Word), while Igbaria and Liravi aimed at measuring general computer use. In our current study, smart phone use in the delivery service industry by drivers and managers is an interesting issue. Smart phone technology by itself is as general a communication tool as many other communication devices, yet it is measured in our current study for a specific use context (i.e., communications for logistics tasks). Therefore, this distinct use of smart phones lies between the use contexts of Chau’s study and several others, including Igbaria and Liravi’s. This suggests that the relationship between self-efficacy and both PEOU and PU depends upon the context in which it is studied.

Model three is a mixture of TAM and IDT factors. Like previous models PEOU strongly affected PU. In fact, all relationships were confirmed except that PU from TAM and two IDT factors (compatibility and individual factors) were not significant predictors of attitude. Surprisingly, attitude was influenced more by the PEOU than by PU. However, PU in this model still has a relatively minor direct effect on behavioral intention to use smart phones.

The final model tested was a comprehensive model containing factors from TAM, self-efficacy, and IDT theories. Although PEOU and PU were predicted to have an effect on attitude just like they did in the first two models, their effect was not significant and was outweighed by several IDT factors. However, the same structure of self-efficacy being the antecedent for PEOU and then through PEOU to PU was still confirmed in this model. Self-efficacy had much larger effects on behavioral intention than it did in model two. As the comprehensive model shows, both attitude and self-efficacy had a similar weight toward behavioral intention. This suggests that smart phone adoption in the delivery service industry relies heavily on both (1) how users perceive the technology and (2) whether users consider themselves capable of using it (selfefficacy). This advocates that it is important for managers to design training programs so that trainees will develop positive attitudes about technological innovations and about their own capabilities for using those technologies. For example, training people to initially use only selected and essential features of smart phones may be a plausible approach to improve self-efficacy beliefs. After workers have mastered the initial small set of essential functions, training can proceed to the next stage that introduces workers with more advanced features of smart phones.

Even though few existing studies have found a positive relationship between self-efficacy and behavioral intention, we consider our finding of such a relationship an interesting one. A majority of technology adoption studies surveyed subjects with a high level of education who were familiar with advanced technology. As a result of this restriction of range, statistical relationships between self-efficacy and BI may have been attenuated. By contrast, our data included respondents with a wide range of educational attainment. Thus, our finding of a strong and significant relationship may be more reflective of the general population working in the logistics industry.

Another interesting find is the role that IDT’s compatibility construct plays in the integrated model. Our findings show that compatibility is an antecedent of PU, rather than PEOU, as reported in previous study. The various ways to operate the compatibility construct have been a source of confusion when interpreting results from existing studies. In the literatures, this construct has been developed to measure personal life style and preferences in some studies, while task and business requirements in others. Another camp of studies also considers compatibility as ‘‘system compatibility’’, measuring only how the technology of interest is compatible with the user’s existing software and hardware systems. As a result of these differences of how this concept is put into operation, its relationship with other constructs is expected to vary across studies. In a production environment, compatibility is better measured in a way that matches one’s business needs and the tasks at hand. Therefore, measuring the compatibility construct within the business context is applicable for our study.

Not all IDT factors had an effect on attitude in our comprehensive model. The only significant predictors of attitude were testability, task, organizational, and environmental factors. The task factor had a negative, but significant effect on attitude. One plausible explanation for this negative effect could be that the wealth of smart phone features might foster a positive impression of the technology’s usefulness, but such usefulness could be perceived as ‘‘complicated’’ and could likely overwhelm the study subjects. Simplification and demonstrated usefulness could combat potential resistance for adoption. It is unlikely that smart phone manufacturers will reduce the number of features already built into smart phone models, but companies wishing to adopt smart phones could take advantage of a staged adoption approach. Kwon and Zmud, suggest innovation implementation follow six sequential phases: initiation, adoption, adaptation, acceptance, use, and incorporation. Such an approach could be modified to reflect the implementation of features of technology, moving from simple and frequently-used features to more complex, rarely-used ones, as suggested above.

Staged adoption is certainly one way to ease pains through new technology adoption, but as our last model shows, the fundamental trigger for attitudinal change is how respondents perceived the needs (or the pressure) for adoption from the bigger environment (such as organization or outside environment). In other words, proving that a technology is easy and useful for one’s job is limited in affecting attitudes when the organizational culture does not complement such an adoption. Even the IDT constructs that reflect individual concerns for performance, work style or preference (e.g., compatibility and observability) did not receive support for their effect on attitude. This is contradictory to some studies. Our finding shows instead that the environment pressure was the most influential factor. This was followed by organizational issues (such as top management support, and user involvement). The heavy weight of these two constructs on attitudes suggests that managers implement their pro-technology adoption message within a supportive organizational environment.

## 9. Contributions

Technology diffusion is affected by the individual, organization, and social system in which the technology is employed. Some features of smart phone requires a certain level of skills to master. A user’s technology capabilities are crucial to their effective use of the technology. The context of the current study consisted of maledominant delivery personnel, some having little education and operating in a workplace needing accuracy, speed, and performance. This unique combination required a comprehensive model to capture social and individual interaction with the technology.

As we moved from the first to our last model, new factors were added. By developing and validating several models in stages we were able to visualize the variations in construct relationships from one model to the next. The final model offered an integrative validation of several theories. Although the result showed that constructs in the theories are related, they were not the same. For example, in our study, PEOU, was related to attitude in the first models, until contextual and self-efficacy variables were added in the final model.

## 10. Managerial implications

Most of the relationships in existing theories were confirmed, offering empirical evidence for their applicability in a different use domain and technology. Our empirical results highlighted the importance of good separation between technology and its features. Many modern technologies try to be ‘‘all-in-one’’ devices, with superfluous features added to increase its perceived value. However, smart phones are different from the many types of technologies tested in the early days. The technologies were simple and used for a small set of purposes while smart phone technology now provides personal information management, digital photos, and music and telecommunication tools. Our study offers insight in how to ease adoption implementation using a staged approach. Our study also provides empirical evidence that attitude toward smart phone use may be influenced by a sense of the organizational culture, policy, and external environment.
