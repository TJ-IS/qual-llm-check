# TURNBACK INTENTION: AN ANALYSIS OF THE DRIVERS OF IT PROFESSIONALS’ INTENTIONS TO RETURN TO A FORMER EMPLOYER<sup>1</sup>

Christian Maier Information Systems and Services, University of Bamberg, Bamberg, GERMANY {christian.maier@uni-bamberg.de}

Sven Laumer Chair of Information Systems, Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg Nuremberg, GERMANY {sven.laumer@fau.de}

Damien Joseph Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University Singapore, SINGAPORE, {adjoseph@ntu.edu.sg}

Jens Mattke and Tim Weitzel Information Systems and Services, University of Bamberg, Bamberg, GERMANY {jens.mattke@uni-bamberg.de} {tim.weitzel@uni-bamberg.de}

Recent statistics indicate that most organizations prefer to fill IT vacancies by rehiring IT professionals who previously worked in the organization. Less is known about what drives IT professionals to “turnback,” a term we define as returning to employment with a former employer. To explain this important and rarely considered IT job mobility behavior, we build on job embeddedness theory and on the concepts of shocks and job dissatisfaction from, among others, the unfolding model of voluntary turnover to develop the theory of IT professional turnback. We perform fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) of data collected from 248 IT professionals to draw conclusions about the intention among IT professionals to return to work for a former employer, and develop a midrange theory. Our results reveal two configurations contributing to high turnback intention and three configurations contributing to low turnback intention. Our model distinguishes between work shocks, personal shocks, and IT work shocks. IT shocks are a new category of shocks specific to the IT profession. We contribute theoretically by theorizing a behavior relevant to IT professionals and explaining attributes driving turnback intention.

Keywords: Boomerang employee, IT professionals, job embeddedness theory, shocks, job satisfaction, turnback, qualitative comparative analysis

## Introduction

Studies indicate that about 15% of employees have returned to a former employer at least once (Hannon 2015) and about 52% would be willing to do so (Nezich 2019). Rehiring former employees has several benefits for firms, including savings of \$20,000 in hiring costs, on average. Former employees are familiar with organizational processes and require less time to reach high proficiency and productivity (Keller et al. in press; Swider et al. 2017). Rehired former employees tend to be more committed, enthusiastic, loyal, and dedicated to the organization than new employees, making returning employees less likely to leave again (Shipp et al. 2014).

Rehiring former IT employees can serve as an important part of a viable IT HR strategy to address the chronic challenge of attracting IT talent in a competitive labor market. Compared to employees in most other roles in an organization, IT professionals are especially valued by firms for their ability to integrate technology-related competencies and businessrelated competencies to solve organization-related problems (Mithas and Krishnan 2008). In fact, firms value IT professionals’ integrative experience gained at other firms more than firm-specific IT experience gained at their own firm. This differential in the value of IT professionals’ competencies is rooted in the experiences gained from applying IT skills to specific business challenges at other organizations and being able to transfer those experiences into the rehiring organization (Mithas and Krishnan 2008). Rehired IT professionals can be a source of IT innovation for an organization when they combine competencies gained from working at another organization with their familiarity with the business challenges of the rehiring organization. One way to achieve this combination quickly is by rehiring IT professionals.

Given the benefits of rehiring IT professionals, there is surprisingly scant research examining why employees return to a former employer (Hom et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2017). This study seeks to answer the overall research question of why IT professionals return to a former employer by (1) clarifying the concept of “turnback,” and (2) developing a typology of configurations from a set of attributes that contribute to turnback intention.

This paper contributes to IS research in two main ways: by developing a theory using a set-theoretic configurational method and by identifying a typology or configurations of attributes that contribute to IT professional turnback intention. We examine turnback by applying job embeddedness theory (Lee et al. 2004). We also include specific well-established elements from the unfolding model of voluntary turnover (Lee et al. 1999; Lee and Mitchell 1994), including shocks and job dissatisfaction. We identify relevant attributes that drive IT professionals to leave their current employer and return to a former employer.

We posit that perceptions and behaviors are formed by integrating distinct attributes into a whole (Pelli and Tillman 2008). In essence, we argue that these attributes are perceived in a holistic, integrated fashion, with complex interactions among those attributes (Dane and Pratt 2007). In addition, we propose that the configurations of attributes that drive low turnback intention differ from the configurations that drive high turnback intention. To analyze these complex interactions of attributes driving turnback intention, we apply a set-theoretic configurational method and use qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) (Mattke et al. in press).

Our paper is structured as follows. First, we discuss related research from the field of turnover among IT professionals and explain our choice of job embeddedness theory and the concepts of shocks and job dissatisfaction, which are known from the unfolding model. We identify turnback as a unique field of research and discuss our research questions. We then present our methodological approach, our results, and an applicability check. Finally, we present the propositions and discuss their implications.

## Toward a Theory of IT Professional Turnback

We answer the broad research question of why IT professionals return to a former employer by introducing and conceptualizing turnback and identifying archetypes of attributes driving turnback intention.

## Turnback

In the following, we contrast turnback with the other forms of job mobility: entry into an employment arrangement, staying in an employment arrangement, and turnover to employment with another organization (Figure 1). Each of those job mobility behaviors focus on the relationship between an IT professional and an employer, but turnback has unique characteristics. We define turnback among IT professionals as the job mobility behavior of returning to employment with a former employer after having worked as an IT professional for one or more subsequent employers.

IT professionals’ turnback decisions include attributes related to multiple organizations, among others. There are attributes that drive IT professionals away from their current employer and attributes that draw them back to a former employer. This makes turnback more complex than other job mobility behaviors that include only attributes related to a single organization.

Entry: From an employee perspective, this is the initial job mobility in an employment arrangement. Many attributes determine whether a job seeker considers an employer to be attractive (Edwards 2009) and is thus willing to enter into an employment arrangement with an organization. General management research (Ng et al. 2007) reveals that job seekers assess job and organizational characteristics (e.g., pay, work environment), recruiter characteristics (e.g., competence), the recruitment process (e.g., justice), hiring expectancies, and perceived fit (e.g., personenvironment fit).

Job seekers’ assessment of these factors influences whether they will enter an organization and become its employee. In addition to the general attributes, IT research reveals that job seekers in the IT domain prefer to work for qualified managers with a fair and consistent leadership style, and for an organization that keeps promises, acknowledges performance, and offers training and promotional opportunities (Dinger et al. 2012). IT professionals may also consider the organization’s level of IT investment, IT strategy, and existing IT systems. These expectations then determine whether an IT professional considers the job to be attractive, applies, and ultimately joins an organization by accepting an employment offer.

Turnback and entry behavior both involve an IT professional taking up employment. However, an IT professional returning to a former employer is familiar with the organizational culture, work characteristics, and recruitment process. A new hire, on the other hand, is relatively uncertain about the work and the organization, such that they base their job acceptance decision on expectations. In contrast, turnback is grounded on actual past experience of having worked in the organization.

Staying: After entering a job, an IT professional is socialized into the organization’s culture. Job embeddedness theory (Mitchell et al. 2001) explains why an employee is more likely to remain employed with the current employer. Briefly, job embeddedness theory proposes that evaluations of fit with the organization and the community, links to others, and sacrifices potentially incurred upon leaving employment play a major role in individuals’ decisions to stay or not (Lee et al. 2004). The theory, supported by empirical research, finds that employees tend to stay when they have strong links with others, consider themselves to be a good fit with the job and community, and perceive certain sacrifices if they were to leave the current employer. Additionally, IT research finds that job characteristics, organizational commitment, and IT leadership styles are critical attributes in IT professionals’ decisions to stay with an employer (Eom 2015).

The obvious difference between turnback and staying is that the latter does not involve a job change. These two behaviors are, however, similar in that, in both cases, IT professionals desire to work at organizations where working conditions are known. Regarding staying, the IT professional wishes to continue working for the current employer. Regarding turnback, the IT professional wishes to work for a former employer again.

Turnover: Turnover reflects the IT professional’s conscious and deliberate willingness to leave an organization (Joseph et al. 2007). Numerous models and theories have sought to explain why workers voluntarily leave an employer (Hom et al. 2012). One prominent example is the unfolding model of voluntary turnover (Lee et al. 1999; Lee and Mitchell 1994). We refer to this model as the unfolding model in the remainder of this paper.

The unfolding model describes four psychological paths toward turnover. The central tenet of the unfolding model is that a jarring event, known as a shock, leads employees to reassess their current employment situation. The experienced shock sends employees down one of four evaluation paths toward turnover. In Path 1, a shock leads employees to enact preexisting plans and quickly quit their job. In Path 2, the shock violates the image of their current employer and leads employees to reconsider their organizational attachment and quit without searching for alternative job opportunities. In Path 3, the shock prompts image violation and leads employees to search for job alternatives before quitting their job. In Path 4, employees become increasingly dissatisfied and quit with or without having searched for alternative job opportunities (Holtom et al. 2005; Lee et al. 1999) such that job dissatisfaction is framed as a slow burn toward turnover.

In alignment with extant research, IS research has revealed that job-related attributes such as role conflict, organizational attributes such as pay and promotion opportunities, demographics such as gender, human capital such as education, job satisfaction, and perceived job alternatives influence IT turnover (Dinger et al. 2015; MacCrory et al. 2016; Maier et al. 2015a). Initial evidence shows that IT professionals are different from individuals in other occupations in terms of the paths taken toward turnover intention (Mourmant et al. 2009; Niederman et al. 2007). Notably, IT research emphasizes that certain reasons why an IT professional quits a job are unique to that IT professional (Niederman et al. 2007).

Both turnover and turnback involve leaving the current employer. However, the job destination of turnover is typically not included in its conceptualization (Joseph et al. 2012; Joseph et al. 2015). We conceptualize turnback by including its destination, i.e., returning to employment with a former employer. By doing so, turnback is grounded in a more complex interaction of attributes. In summary, we aim contribute to IT job mobility behaviors beyond entry, staying with, and leaving an organization by introducing turnback. Hence, we challenge the current conceptualization of quitting as an “end state” of the employee-employer relationship and the employment life cycle.

## Attributes Driving Turnback

Contemporary research agrees that shock or high levels of job dissatisfaction are among the reasons why employees leave an organization (Lee et al. 1999; Lee and Mitchell 1994). Furthermore, since turnback involves working at a known organization, the attributes driving turnback may be related to those of the stay behavior, where employees are in a familiar situation. Hence, job embeddedness theory (Lee et al. 2004; Mitchell et al. 2001) may be instructive.

Shock: In the employment life cycle context, a shock is defined as a jarring event that violates the values of an employee so substantially that they consider changing their behavior (Lee et al. 1999). Shocks break employees out of established routines and disrupt automatic cognitive processing (Morgeson et al. 2015; Morgeson and DeRue 2006). Experiencing a shock challenges the status quo. A shock can cause employees to recalibrate their perceptions about their current job (Holtom et al. 2012) and may increase their desire to quit (Wilson and Schooler 1991). Literature has identified two different forms of general shocks (Holtom et al. 2005): personal and work shocks. Personal shocks are caused by personal issues, such as those related to family, while work shocks are related to the general content, nature, and context of employment in an organization, such as a disagreement with the boss or a coworker or a new job offer.

IT work also has specific characteristics (Ang et al. 2015). The typical role of IT professionals is to acquire, develop, and manage IT resources; thus, certain shocks are unique to the IT profession (Niederman et al. 2007; Niederman and Sumner 2004). That said, such shocks arise from the nature, content, and context of IT-specific work—for example, shocks arising from IT-security work issues, IT project management issues, IT strategy issues, or IT infrastructure issues. More generally, we call these idiosyncratic shocks IT work shocks and suggest that they refer to jarring events grounded in the specific characteristics of the IT work context that are uniquely applicable to IT professionals. IT professionals are highly reliant on IT systems, as they must deal with these systems from different perspectives (system analysis, system development, system management). For example, an IT work shock might be perceived when the organizational IT system is hacked. One example of an IT work shock was when Apobank, a German bank for physicians and pharmacists, migrated their core banking system from Bank21 to Avalog. During the migration, there was a general system breakdown that lasted for days and interrupted processes and services. Employees, customers, and the press blamed IT professionals for the failure of the organizational processes.

While previous research has not specifically studied IT work shocks, there is some related research on role stressors (Joseph et al. 2007). Of the role stressors identified, role ambiguity is relevant to IT professionals (Moore 2000). IT professionals may experience role ambiguity—for example, when they are unsure about whether to focus on IT issues or other work activities (Ayyagari et al. 2011). Generally, the major difference between shocks and role stressors is that shocks are typically specific and episodic jarring events (Morgeson et al. 2015) whereas stressors are enduring and chronic (Galluch et al. 2015; Maier et al. 2019).

The above examples illustrate that certain shocks are only experienced by IT professionals and not by professionals in other occupations. We therefore distinguish IT work shocks, which are specific to IT professionals, from work and personal shocks, which can affect both IT professionals and professionals in other occupations. The experience of IT work shocks, work shocks, or personal shocks can drive IT professional turnback intention.

Job dissatisfaction is generally defined as a negative affective or attitudinal reaction to one’s job (Spector 1997). Most models of turnover include the degree of job satisfaction as a driver of turnover, identifying job dissatisfaction and shock as distinct attributes of turnover.

Specifically, greater job dissatisfaction is associated with higher levels of turnover (Hom and Kinicki 2001; Lee 1988; Zimmerman and Darnold 2009). In this paper, we theorize that job dissatisfaction can also drive IT professional turnback intention.

Job embeddedness theory postulates that the degree to which employees feel embedded depends on their fit with the organization and the community, their links to others, and the sacrifices they would incur if they were to leave the organization (Lee et al. 2004). Fit is defined as the perceived compatibility or perceived comfort with an organization and/or community (Mitchell et al. 2001). Fit depends on whether the goals and values of an employee match specific job attributes such as job knowledge or skills, and community attributes such as location, political climate, and entertainment options (Mitchell et al. 2001). Links are defined as the number of formal and informal connections an employee has within and to the organization and the surrounding community. Links include connections with other employees in the organization as well as off-the-job connections to local family members, friends, and social institutions. Sacrifice is defined as the “perceived cost of material or psychological benefits that may be forfeited from broken links [with the organization or community] by leaving a job” (Mitchell et al. 2001, p. 1105). Such sacrifices may be related to the organization—for example, the potential loss of valuable projects, job-related perks, contact to colleagues, and the loss of possible advancements—or to the community, such as the potential loss of a home, neighborhood, or friends outside the organization (Mitchell et al. 2001). In essence, job embeddedness theory posits that job embeddedness, in terms of fit, links, or sacrifices, reflects the degree to which an employee is enmeshed in the community and organization.

Turnover literature has firmly established the relationships between fit, links, and sacrifices (e.g., Lee et al. 2004; Mitchell et al. 2001; Zhang et al. 2012), providing evidence that strong fit, strong links, and high expected sacrifice are associated with a higher likelihood that an IT professional will stay with their current employer and a lower turnover intention.

Previous research on job embeddedness focuses on why employees stay with their current employer. We believe that the concept, theory, and boundary of job embeddedness can be extended to one’s former employers to explain why employees return to employment with a past employer. For example, after professional basketball player LeBron James returned to his former basketball team, he stated in an interview: “My relationship with Northeast Ohio is bigger than basketball. I didn’t realize that four years ago. I do now.” This suggests that job embeddedness with a former employer drove his turnback decision To study what contributes to IT professional turnback intention, we focus on both job embeddedness with the current employer and job embeddedness with a former employer.

Job embeddedness with the current employer: In line with Mitchell et al. (2001), an employee who is well embedded in an organization is less likely to quit and more likely to stay employed with their current employer than an employee who is less well embedded. Employees who are enmeshed in a social and institutional network (1) perceive a high fit with the job and the community, (2) have strong links and relationships with colleagues in the organization and/or in the community, and (3) would have to make significant sacrifices if they left the organization. This illustrates that job embeddedness theory acknowledges that nonwork attributes also determine quitting intentions.

Job embeddedness with a former employer: An IT professional considering returning to a former employer is already familiar with the people, processes, and policies of that former employer. IT professionals who intend to return are thus likely able to evaluate their degree of fit, links, and the sacrifices incurred if they were to return.

Fit: An IT professional who perceives high compatibility or a high level of comfort with a former employer or the surrounding environment may regret having left (Calisir et al. 2011). A strong fit might be the outcome of organizational socialization (Hall 1987), the IT used in the organization (Ayyagari et al. 2011), or the compatibility between the IT professional and the job or organization (Venkatesh et al. 2017). Regarding IT professionals, consider a former IT road warrior who worked on different projects in different places and then quit that job to work for another employer in a job with a fixed workplace and more static job responsibilities with better skill fit (Mithas and Krishnan 2008). If, with time, the employee comes to believe that a former job, employer, and community represented a better fit, the employee might develop high turnback intention and have low uncertainty concerning the fit with that organization and community.

Links: An employee who leaves an organization may maintain strong relationships, friendships, and other links with people associated with a former employer. Such social relationships are conduits through which socialization continues and employees receive updates on values, norms, and beliefs. Boundary-spanning activities (MacCrory et al. 2016), the breadth of interactions (Joseph et al. 2010), and change agents (Markus and Benjamin 1996) also influence how IT professionals’ links are established via interactions with others who work in the organization. Boundaryspanning activities mean that internal communications and meetings occur beyond an IT professional’s immediate department (Guimaraes and Igbaria 1992). Therefore, we argue that IT professionals will also communicate with colleagues with whom they have built relationships who still work for a former employer. These factors contribute to whether employees feel satisfied with working for an organization (Mitchell et al. 2001) and strong links might drive them to return to a former employer. Such strong links may also give the former employee access to job vacancy information. Internal recommendations from strong links with a former employer may also drive former employees to return to the former employer.

Sacrifice: Leaving an organization may lead to work-related losses, including losses related to job stability, advancement plans, and pension plans or investment opportunities. Leaving an organization may also involve community-related losses associated with relocation, such as the loss of nonwork friendships, finding a new home, and changing children’s schools and friends. IT professionals often experience an erosion of specific IT competencies typically related to the fit between task and technology when a customized software is used. Broken links are highly individual and decisions with negative impacts can influence employees’ emotions for a long time and significantly impact future behavior (Ito et al. 1998). Negative experiences associated with losses caused by leaving a former employer may, therefore, drive turnback intention. This is particularly relevant when skills are transferable and the cost of turnback is low. In summary, we consider both job embeddedness with the current employer and job embeddedness with a former employer to be factors driving IT professional turnback intention.

## Research Approach

Individuals are thought to consider their daily experiences in a holistic, configurational fashion (Campbell et al. 2016). We propose that turnback intention is shaped by complex interactions among multiple attributes experienced holistically as configurations.

We theorize that IT professionals do not experience specific attributes that push them away from the current employer and pull them back to a former employer separately but rather in a holistic, integrated fashion resulting in complex interactions among those attributes (Dane and Pratt 2007). These complex interactions can be viewed through two lenses. First, these complex interactions are a result of IT professionals integrating attributes based on two anchor points: attributes pertaining to their current employer (e.g., attributes of IT work, work-related and personal shocks, job dissatisfaction, and job embeddedness) and those related to former employers (e.g., job embeddedness), which collectively drive IT professionals to evaluate their turnback intention. Previous research on job mobility behaviors investigating turnover intention only considers one’s current employer and thus simplifies decisions to turnover vis-à-vis turnback intention.

Second, we evaluate attributes relevant for turnback intention in a two-step approach, known as configurational sensemaking (Campbell et al. 2016). First, IT professionals discern the presence or absence of shocks and job dissatisfaction, and evaluate their levels of job embeddedness with their current or former employers. Second, IT professionals integrate their evaluations on these attributes into a configuration that drives turnback intention. We differentiate between attributes that drive high vs. low turnback intention, as these drivers may be different and may not necessarily be inverse attributes.

To evaluate how complex interactions between attributes drive turnback, we posit a model of IT professionals attributes based on the discussion in this section (Figure 2). We use fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) to evaluate the complex interactions because it is suited for analyzing concepts (e.g., turnback) and it is also uniquely suited for retroductively identifying configurations. Each configuration reflects an ideal type and consists of multiple attributes. We propose different configurations representing a typology (Doty and Glick 1994) of multiple ideal types (Fiss 2011). We thus use a retroductive theory-building approach using well-known and widely used theories and contextspecific knowledge from related research streams to explain what factors contribute to turnback intention; we also collected empirical data and evidence to substantiate the elaboration of our theory. Furthermore, we create propositions rather than just relying on theoretical logic or direct observations. This approach is useful for explaining the why and how of emergent and extant relations and to propose a normative and prescriptive theory.

## Methodological Approach

## Data Collection, Sampling Strategy and Sample Characteristics

Our study samples IT professionals who left an employer at least once in their IT career and are currently employed at an organization. We invited a pool of 1,500 currently employed IT professionals who have held at least one other IT job over the last year to participate in our online survey. Within that pool, 253 IT professionals completed our survey and 248 submitted a valid response and fulfilled all formal criteria. We removed responses from participants that stated they were not currently working as IT professionals or were currently working for an organization and intended to go back to the same organization. Since we could not determine whether these responses were in error, whether participants wanted to work in another department, or whether they interpreted the question incorrectly, we decided to remove these responses.

## Measurement and Validation

In this section, we present the measures used in the survey and explain how we validated the data to ensure reliable results.

## Measurement

To capture IT professionals’ perceptions about work characteristics, job satisfaction, and job embeddedness with a former and current employer, we adapted measurement items that have been developed and validated in previous research to fit our research context. We developed new items to capture turnback intention. All measurement items are described in Table A1 (Appendix). We ensured that responses applied to one specific current employer and one specific former employer by asking participants to name their current employer as well as the former employer to which they considered returning. This reflects that our participants considered which employer they would return to ahead of time. We then fed the names of the relevant employers into all further questions.

Shock: To measure whether employees were confronted with a shock, we followed a two-step approach (Table B1 in the Appendix). First, we used an item from previous research (Mitchell et al. 2001) that asked: “Was there a particular jarring event that caused you to think about leaving?”

We specifically asked about a particular jarring event to make it clear that we were not interested in chronic issues, e.g., stressors. Second, if participants experienced a jarring event, we asked them to specify the nature of the shock event in an open text field. We removed a few participants from our final data sample because they did not specify the nature of the shock. We used their answers to identify types of shocks. We illustrate the process of abstraction in Table B1.

We used descriptive and interpretive coding (Myers 2009), which allowed us to identify three fundamental types of shocks: IT work shock, work shock, and personal shock. These three shocks are respectively related to the context of IT work, work in general, and the personal context (Table B2 in the Appendix). Reliability tests indicated that the coding is likely reproducible (Appendix B). We interviewed 12 additional IT professionals and 21 additional non-IT professionals using exploratory and confirmatory approaches to clarify that IT professionals were confronted with IT work shocks while non-IT professionals were not confronted with IT work shocks in the past. We thus conclude that IT work shocks are specific for IT professionals because these are not perceived by professionals working in other professions.

Job embeddedness: We used a seven-item global measure of job embeddedness developed and validated by Crossley et al. (2007) to ascertain fit, links, and sacrifice. This scale was modified to measure job embeddedness with the current employer and job embeddedness with the former employer.

## Validation

Table A2 (Appendix) presents the descriptive statistics and construct correlations. We found only one large negative correlation between job dissatisfaction and job embeddedness with the current employer. All other correlations were small or moderate in effect size. To validate the measurement model, we ensured content validity, indicator reliability, construct reliability, and discriminant validity (Bagozzi 1979). To ensure content validity, we either used well-accepted items from prior turnover research or developed new items for constructs that had not been previously examined, such as for turnback intention (Table A1).

The indicator reliability indicates the rate of the variance of an indicator that has its origins in the latent variable. To explain 50% of the variance of a latent variable by the indicators, the value must be at least 0.707 (Carmines and Zeller 2008). This is fulfilled (Table A2). To determine the quality at the construct level, we used composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) (Fornell and Larcker 1981). AVE should be higher than 0.50 and CR higher than 0.70, and both criteria are fulfilled (Table A2). Discriminant validity describes the extent to which measurement items differ from others (Campell and Fiske 1959). Hence, we include the square root of AVE in Table A2 on the diagonal of the bivariate correlations. Discriminant validity is determined to have been attained if these square root values are greater than the corresponding construct correlations (Fornell and Larcker 1981; Hulland 1999). The square root values indicate that discriminant validity is attained. The heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratio of correlations criterion detects a lack of discriminant validity more reliably than the Fornell-Larcker criterion. We used it to assess discriminant validity (Henseler et al. 2014). The absolute HTMT<sub>0.85</sub> criterion indicates that discriminant validity is not an issue (highest value is 0.81) in our research and that the measurement model is valid. Finally, we tested for common method bias and for the influence of nonresponse bias and concluded that neither are an issue for our data (Appendix A).

## Set-Theoretic Analysis with fsQCA

We take a configurational approach using fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) because the central thesis of this study is that IT professional turnback intention is driven by integrating distinct and different attributes into a whole (Pelli and Tillman 2008) through configurational sensemaking (Campbell et al. 2016). fsQCA is a set-theoretic configurational method of identifying ideal types that reflect a sufficient configuration of attributes driving high or low turnback intention. Since fsQCA is based on set theory, it enabled us to study how a configuration of attributes drives high and low IT professional turnback intention. We did not focus on the specific effect of attributes on an outcome, but rather followed the neoconfigurational approach (Misangyi et al. 2017) which reflects our assumption that causal combinations or configurations of attributes drive an outcome. In our case, we did not focus on shocks, job dissatisfaction, or job embeddedness separately or examine how much of the variance can be explained by e.g., using regression analysis. We show how these attributes simultaneously and collectively contribute to high and low IT professional turnback intention. This, in turn, enabled us to elaborate, build, and test typologies with ideal types, each including a sufficient configuration of attributes (Crilly et al. 2012; Fiss 2011; Mattke et al. 2021, in press; Misangyi et al. 2017).

Table 2 outlines a high-level overview of the basic principles of fsQCA and summarizes the key terminology of fsQCA in Table 2. At its core, fsQCA is based on set theory and is used to determine which attributes explain an outcome of interest using set membership scores. Multiple attributes taken together constitute a configuration. Therefore, fsQCA explains how configurations of those attributes contribute to an outcome by examining whether a configuration is sufficient to contribute to the outcome. In fsQCA, consistency refers to the degree to which the observations sharing a given configuration agree on displaying the desired outcome. Using fsQCA also enables necessary attributes within a sufficient configuration to be determined (Ragin 2014).

We analyzed our data using Version 3.0 of the commonly used fsQCA software (Ragin et al. 2016; Maier et al. 2020; Pflügner et al. 2020; Tan et al. 2016). The data analysis consisted of three steps: calibration, analysis for necessary attributes, and analysis for sufficient configurations for turnback intention, reflecting an ideal type contributing to turnback intention.

## Calibration

When fsQCA is used, attributes and outcomes must be calibrated in fuzzy sets. We used direct calibration with three membership anchors (Ragin 2008). Direct calibration is recommended for data measured on a Likert scale, and natural anchors on the Likert scale reflect the explicit meaning of the data gathered on the scale (Fiss 2007). We used the maximum, the median, and the minimum of the Likert scale as anchors. We did not calibrate based on the distribution of the data set, such as the mean value or percentiles because the resulting calibrated fuzzy sets would be idiosyncratic to our specific data set and replication with a different data distribution would yield a different solution. This approach helps to make our results generalizable (Wagemann et al. 2016).

Specifically, we assigned the anchor for full nonmembership the value 1, the anchor for the crossover point of maximum ambiguity the value 4, and the anchor for full membership the value 7, using these anchors in the direct calibration to translate each Likert scale value, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), into fuzzy sets ranging from 0 to 1. Accordingly, 0 indicates full nonmembership, i.e., a fuzzy value of 0 for turnback intention reflects a low turnback intention, and a fuzzy value of 1 indicates full membership— i.e., a fuzzy value of 1 for turnback intention reflects high turnback intention.<sup>2</sup> We followed this calibration method to transform all constructs using 7-point Likert scales (job dissatisfaction, job embeddedness with the current and former employer, turnback intention) into fuzzy sets. We also calibrated the binary constructs of shocks based on the described classification (Appendix B).

Following previous theory (Lee et al. 1999) positing that shocks are either present or absent, we calibrated the three shocks—IT work shock, work shock, personal shock—to either 1, reflecting the presence of the shock, or 0, indicating the absence of the shock. We also applied this procedure to the three types of shock to calibrate the results. To avoid fuzzy set values of 0.50, which cannot be handled with the fsQCA algorithm and would lead to a dropout of configurations including a 0.50 fuzzy set value, we followed recommendations (Fiss 2011; Mattke et al. 2021, in press) and added a constant of 0.001 to those values.<sup>3</sup>

## Analysis for Necessary Attributes for Turnback Intention

A necessary attribute must exist for the outcome to exist (Ragin 2014). In this study, a necessary attribute for high turnback intention always exists if an IT professional has high turnback intention. By the same logic, a necessary attribute for low turnback intention always exists if an IT professional has low turnback intention. Statistically, a necessary attribute exists when a single attribute exceeds the recommended consistency threshold of 0.90, indicating the degree to which observations with the same attributes share the same outcome (Ragin 2009).

## Analysis of Sufficient Configurations for Turnback Intention

Having determined that one particular attribute might be necessary for an outcome, we turn to what configurations of attributes drive high and low turnback intention. In our study, a sufficient configuration for high turnback intention is a specific group of attributes that, in combination, cause an IT professional to develop high turnback intention. Because of the set-theoretical nature of fsQCA, we can also examine sufficient configurations for low turnback intention, referring to a specific group of attributes that, in combination, may cause an IT professional to develop low turnback intention.

To identify sufficient configurations for high turnback intention, we first constructed the truth table of all logically possible configurations of the attributes, where each row represents one configuration. We then applied three thresholds in the truth table to reduce it to sufficient configurations. In line with previous research (Mattke et al. 2020) we used a consistency threshold of 0.85. The value used in this study is higher than the minimum consistency of 0.75 that is often seen as more appropriate (Ragin 2009). We applied a frequency threshold of 3, as recommended by previous research (Maier et al. 2020; Park et al. 2017). This threshold reflects the rule of thumb that the frequency threshold should be chosen so that more than 80% of the observations remain in the analysis (Greckhamer et al. 2018). Since we used fuzzy sets, following previous studies (Mattke et al. 2020; Park et al. 2017), we applied a proportional reduction in inconsistency (PRI) threshold of 0.75. The PRI indicates how consistent a configuration is for a high outcome (here: high turnback intention) without being simultaneously sufficient for a low outcome (here: low turnback intention). High values for PRI show that a configuration is sufficient for high turnback intention but is not sufficient for low turnback intention. We only used configurations that align with these recommendations as sufficient configurations for high turnback intention. To further simplify the remaining configurations, we applied the Quine-McCluskey algorithm, resulting in distinct sufficient configurations driving high turnback intention (Ragin 2008). We repeated this procedure to identify sufficient configurations for low turnback intention. For more details, see the complete truth table in Tables C1 and C2 in the Appendix.

## Set-Theoretic Configurational Analysis Results

In this section, we describe the results of the necessary attribute analysis and the analysis of sufficient configurations.

## Identifying Necessary Attributes for IT Professional Turnback Intention

High turnback intention: The analysis for necessary attributes for high turnback intention reveals job embeddedness with the former employer (consistency > 0.94) as a necessary attribute. This implies that employees need to be embedded with their former employer to desire to return to their former firm. The other attributes appear “not necessary” for the development of high turnback intention.

Low turnback intention: The analysis of necessary attributes for low turnback intention reveals the absence of IT work shock (consistency > 0.95) and the absence of personal shocks (consistency = 0.96) are necessary for low turnback intention.

## Identifying Sufficient Configurations for Turnback Intention

We created a truth table to identify sufficient configurations. All configurations of the analysis are graphically displayed in Figure 3. Our findings suggest that there are two sufficient configurations for high turnback intention (Figure 3: C1-C2). We see three sufficient conditions for low turnback intention (Figure 3: C3-C5).

High turnback intention: The raw coverage, which expresses the extent to which the configuration covers the data set (Ragin 2006), ranges from 0.09 to 0.12, indicating an adequate coverage for all configurations. The unique coverage, referring to the contribution of the configuration beyond what has been explained by other configurations (Ragin 2008), is equal to the raw coverage because there is no overlap with other sufficient configurations. The solution coverage that measures the proportion of the data set explained by the solution is 0.22. All values are well above zero, indicating an adequate contribution of each configuration. Solution consistency, which measures the degree to which all configurations together consistently contribute to IT professional turnback intention (Ragin 2008), is well above the minimum required consistency of 0.75 (Figure 3).

Our analysis reveals two sufficient configurations explaining why IT professionals develop high turnback intention. The first configuration (C1) represents IT professionals who experienced an IT work shock but no work or personal shock, are embedded with their current and former employer, and are dissatisfied with their job. IT professionals represented by this configuration are pushed away from their current employer when confronted with an IT work shock and dissatisfied with their job. These IT professionals are also pulled toward their former employer because they are still well-embedded with their former employer. Consequently, such IT professionals have high turnback intention when they are pushed away from their current employer and pulled toward their former employer. We therefore label this configuration the IT work shock-induced high turnback intention.

The second configuration (C2) presents an IT professional who experienced a personal shock, is not embedded with their current employer, but is embedded with their former employer. In addition, this employee is dissatisfied with their current job.

Such IT professionals are pushed away from their current employer due to shocks in their personal environment that cannot be mitigated by their current situation because they feel less embedded. However, they are pulled by their former environment where they are still highly embedded. Thus, we label this configuration the personal shock-induced high turnback intention.

Low turnback intention: The analysis for sufficient configurations for low turnback intention reveals three configurations (Figure 3). We conclude that the attributes driving low turnback intention are not the inverse attributes driving high turnback intention. Our causal asymmetry test underscores the importance of IT work shocks because IT professionals will have low turnback intention only when IT work shocks are absent. The first configuration contributing to low turnback intention (C3) is characterized by the absence of IT work shocks or personal shocks but the presence of a work shock. Although a work shock may push IT professionals away from their current employer, job embeddedness with the former employer does not play a role in the development of low turnback intention in this case. Thus, case, there is nothing that pulls the IT professional to the former employer. Similarly, job embeddedness with the current employer and job dissatisfaction are “don’t care” attributes. Thus, we label this configuration the general work shock-induced low turnback intention.

In the second configuration (C4), IT work shocks and personal shocks are absent and there is no job dissatisfaction. But the IT professional is embedded with the current employer. Hence, there are no attributes present that push the employee away from the current employer, and embeddedness with the former employer does not influence the formation of low turnback intention, as evidenced by the “don’t care” situation. We call this configuration the embedded with current employerinduced low turnback intention.

The third configuration (C5) consists of the presence of an IT work shock, the absence of a work shock, the lack of dissatisfaction with the current job, and embeddedness with the current employer. Concurrently, the pull factors are present because these IT professionals are embedded with their former employer. However, since they are not pushed away from their current employer, the pull effects of embeddedness with their former employer are not sufficient to develop high turnback intention. As such, these IT professionals continue to have low turnback intention. Thus, we call this configuration the embedded with both employers-induced low turnback intention.

Summary: These five sufficient configurations offer a typology with different ideal types, each including a sufficient configuration of attributes of IT professionals who develop high or low turnback intention.

## Job Search Behavior

We determined that our results are robust when considering alternative explanations. Given that the solution coverage is lower for high turnback intention than for low turnback intention (Figure 3), there may be additional contributing factors for high turnback intention. Up to this point, we have examined turnback intention rather than turnback behavior based on previous research that finds that intention remains the strongest predictor of turnover (e.g., turnover intention of turnover behavior) (Rubenstein et al. 2018). However, to further strengthen our propositions, we also included job search behavior as an indicator that an IT professional is currently looking for a new job or is prepared to do so. We tested whether job search behavior changes the results of the main analysis.

We reanalyzed the data with the same calibrations as mentioned above and conducted another fsQCA with job search behavior as an additional attribute (Figure 5). The results reveal that job search behavior is a necessary attribute (consistency > 0.92) in both identified configurations. Including job search behavior leads to slightly higher solution coverage as well as solution consistency, highlighting the importance of job search behavior for explaining high turnback intention.

## Toward a Theory of IT Professional Turnback

We develop theoretically based configurations of attributes that drive IT professional turnback intention in this study. Our results indicate that there are two equifinal configurations explaining why IT professionals develop high turnback intention and three equifinal configurations for low turnback intention. We conclude by offering a theory of IT professional turnback containing five propositions to explain and predict IT professional turnback intention. Our proposed theory may be classified as a type IV theory (Gregor 2006).

## High IT Professional Turnback Intention

## IT Work Shocks and Embeddedness with a Former Employer

Previous research informs us that IT professionals are confronted with different types of shocks, compared to employees in other professions (Ahuja et al. 2007). Our analysis confirms that IT work shocks are specific to the IT profession. Such IT work shocks challenge IT professionals but are not relevant in the same way for incumbents in other occupations. Our findings show that IT work shocks drive turnback intention (Figure 3).

IT professionals experiencing a configuration of attributes corresponding with the IT work shock-induced high turnback intention find themselves in an unpleasant situation (e.g., software development issue: “Bad software is suddenly deployed”) and would likely seek to withdraw from that situation. This unpleasant situation is reflected in dissatisfaction with their job with the current employer. Among such IT professionals, those who experience an episodic IT work shock are pushed away from their current employer. Low job embeddedness with their current employer facilitates the separation. Embeddedness with their former employer, a necessary condition, provides a proximate facilitator that eases job transition back into a familiar environment. Interestingly, there are interrelations between job embeddedness with the former and current employer. Perceived embeddedness with the former employer must be higher than perceived embeddedness with the current employer for turnback intention to occur (Figure 4). Taking these points as well as the presence of a job search (Figure 5) into consideration, we propose:

Proposition 1: IT professionals develop high turnback intention if they experience an IT work shock but no other type of shock, if they are dissatisfied with their current job, if there is no job embeddedness with their current employer, and if (and only if) there is job embeddedness with a former employer.

## Personal Shocks and Embeddedness with a Former Employer

Similarly, IT professionals experiencing a configuration of attributes in accordance with the personal shock-induced high turnback intention are in an unpleasant situation (e.g., illness: “My father got seriously ill”) and would likely seek to withdraw from that situation. The unpleasant situation is reflected by the presence of both the private and the professional domains: dissatisfaction with their current job and a personal shock, such as a death in the family, the illness of a significant other, or the birth of a child.

The low job embeddedness with the current employer implies that there is no link or fit with that employer or the current community that could provide support for the unpleasant situation triggered by a personal shock. Hence, such IT professionals are likely to search for a new job because there is no sacrifice incurred by leaving the current employer (Figure 5: C2\_JSB).

In parallel, job embeddedness with a former employer that is higher than job embeddedness with the current employer (Figure 4), a necessary condition, provides a proximate and easy job transition to the former employer. Given the higher level of job embeddedness with the former employer, IT professionals in this situation will likely form intentions to return to a familiar environment where they will be better able to manage the personal shock. Hence, we propose:

Proposition 2: IT professionals develop high turnback intention if they experience a personal shock but no other type of shock, if they are dissatisfied with their current job, if there is no job embeddedness with their current employer, and if (and only if) there is job embeddedness with a former employer.

## Low IT Professional Turnback Intention

## Work Shock, but Job Embeddedness with a Former Employer Does not Play a Role

IT professionals experiencing a configuration of attributes consistent with the work shock-induced low turnback intention perceive a work shock and find themselves in an unpleasant situation (e.g., layoffs: “There were many layoffs”). However, with this configuration, job dissatisfaction and job embeddedness with former and current employers may be either be present or absent. With this configuration, these attributes, taken together, are insufficient to drive IT professionals to return to a former employer. Although IT professionals in such a situation might want to escape the unpleasant situation, since job embeddedness with the former employer does not play a role in this case, there are no forces pulling them back toward employment with a former employer. Such IT professionals are, however, typical candidates for turnover, as evidenced by prior results from turnover research that finds work shock to be an antecedent of turnover (Niederman et al. 2007). Therefore, we propose:

Proposition 3: IT professionals have low turnback intention if a work shock is experienced and if (and only if) there are no IT work shocks or personal shocks.

## High Job Embeddedness with the Current Employer, but no Shocks or Job Dissatisfaction

IT professionals experiencing the configuration or profile of attributes relating to embedded with current employerinduced low turnback intention do not think about turnback and hence have low turnback intention. Such IT professionals are not dissatisfied with working for their current employer. In contrast, they are well embedded with the current employer and IT work shocks and personal shocks are absent. The low turnback intention of such IT professionals is in line with previous research showing that high levels of job embeddedness with the current employer and the absence of shocks and job dissatisfaction reflect a typical situation in which individuals will stay in the employment arrangement. Such IT professionals are typical candidates for retention (Lee et al. 2014). Thus, we propose:

Proposition 4: IT professionals have low turnback intention if there is job embeddedness with their current employer, if there is no dissatisfaction with their current job, and if (and only if) no IT work or personal shocks are experienced.

## High Job Embeddedness with Current and Former Employers, but no Shocks or Job Dissatisfaction

IT professionals experiencing a configuration of attributes matching embedded with both employers-induced low turnback intention have no reason to leave their current employer. Although there is still embeddedness with the former employer, such IT professionals do not perceive an IT work or work shock and are not dissatisfied with their job. Taken together, this configuration of attributes suggests that such IT professionals are happy with their current situation and would rather continue with their current work arrangements (Lee et al. 2014). Hence, we propose:

Proposition 5: IT professionals have low turnback intention if there is job embeddedness with their former and current employers, if there is no work shock and no dissatisfaction with their current job, and if (and only if) there is no IT work shock.

## Applicability Check

We performed an applicability check to test the importance, accessibility, and suitability of the results in terms of translation into practice (Rosemann and Vessey 2008). To do so, we interviewed six HR professionals from three different organizations (one FORTUNE 30 organization, one IT organization, one non-IT SME).

Importance: The representatives from the FORTUNE 30 organization report that the starting point for all efforts in rehiring employees was that “we were not able to fill IT vacancies, so we had to find new ways of recruiting … [so we] used our large alumni network to rehire IT professionals.” Representatives stated that rehires had the best cost-benefit ratio across all recruiting strategies, even after accounting for a financial incentive to persuade IT professionals to return. The IT organization representatives emphasized the benefits of rehiring IT professionals, stating: “it saves money, shortens the training period, increases commitment and brings in new IT knowledge.”

Accessibility: All representatives attested to the high degree of comprehensibility of our findings, especially the specific configurations. One representative concluded: “I see—and would agree—that it is our main task and responsibility [is] to keep in contact with IT professionals who have left the firm, to increase the probability of rehiring … this is clearly shown by the configurations.”

Suitability: The organizations’ representatives emphasized that the results differ regarding necessary and nonnecessary attributes. Noting that identifying job embeddedness with the former employer is a necessary attribute for rehires, one representative remarked: “the results provide a first step toward rehiring IT professionals.”

The applicability check conforms to the retroductive spirit of fsQCA.<sup>6</sup> Hence, there is assurance that the results are applicable to practice in terms of their importance, accessibility, and suitability.

## Discussion, Contributions, and Implications

In this section, we outline our contributions to research and theory, discuss our results in comparison to related research, and present the practical implications of our findings, the limitations of our study, and future research directions.

## Contributions to Theory and Research

The high relevance of turnback behavior for IT professionals and for rehiring firms is evident in our study and confirmed by our applicability check. For IT professionals, turnback places them in a familiar environment where they fit and have links to others in the organization and community. Rehiring firms enjoy savings from lowered hiring costs and time saved for an IT professional to be proficient and productive. This study contributes to the understanding of why IT professionals return to a former employer through the following points.

Turnover in the employment life cycle is not the “end state” in the employment relationship between an employer and an IT professional: Traditionally, IT professional research identifies three phases in the employment life cycle: entry, stay, and turnover (see discussion of employment life cycle above). This study extends the employment life cycle by introducing “turnback” as a fourth possible phase distinct from entry, stay, and turnover. By doing so, this study contributes to the discussion of what follows turnover.

We conceptualize the behavior of turnback as a job mobility involving returning to employment with a former employer after having worked for one or more different employers. With turnback intention as its proxy, we operationalize turnback intention and provide valid measures that can be used in further research. This study also adds to the broader conversation (e.g., Joseph et al. 2012, 2015) by expanding the IT job mobility criterion to include turnback, in addition to turnover, turnaway-within, and turnaway-between.

Developing a theory of IT professional turnback: We develop a typology consisting of two ideal types of attributes contributing to high turnback intention among IT professionals and three ideal types contributing to low turnback intention. Our theory suggests that high turnback intention is formed only when there is an attribute pushing the IT professional away and another attribute pulling the IT professional back to the former employer. Thus, this means that, on the one hand, if there is no push factor present, IT professionals will likely not intend to leave their current employer and return to their former employer. On the other hand, if there is no pull factor, e.g., the job embeddedness with the former employer is low, IT professionals are similarly unlikely to develop turnback intention; our findings show that this holds even when a push factor such as a shock is present.

Initial research in the stream of turnback (Shipp et al. 2014) has compared employees who return with those who do not, but there has been little understanding of the configuration of attributes that drive turnback intention. Our typology fills this research gap by identifying the precise configurations that drive high and low turnback intention.

Causality of turnback: A high-level theoretical contribution of this work is that it develops a theory that reflects the causality and causal complexity of different attributes constituting an outcome variable. This goes beyond the traditional linear approach of examining the net effect of different independent variables combined in some linear fashion on a dependent variable.

We map out the causality of turnback in the following three issues. First, we reveal mostly small or moderate correlations, as shown in Table A2. The lack of many correlations leads to conclusions of complex causality and, hence, the use of the configurational approach. Second, we reveal temporal precedence by considering attributes that started to influence an individual at different points in time. The timeline begins with the development of job embeddedness with the former employer and is followed by the development of job embeddedness with the current employer. At some point, a shock might occur while working with the current employer. Our approach also shows that job embeddedness with the former employer must be higher than job embeddedness with the current employer. Third, we control for alternative explanations by considering job search behavior as an additional variable that may explain why some IT professionals develop turnback intention. We found that job search behavior is a necessary attribute for developing turnback intention and this attribute increases the explanatory power of our analysis. IT professionals develop turnback intention only if they are currently searching for a new job.

We also establish the complex causality of turnback in the following three ways. First, we reveal the equifinality of turnback intention by finding several different configurations for IT professionals to develop high and low turnback intention (Propositions 1 to 5). Second, we establish asymmetric causation by showing that some attributes of turnback intentions are present in one configuration and absent in another. Most prominently, we see this with IT work shock. IT work shock may be present in one configuration driving high turnback intention but absent in another. Third, we show asymmetry between the outcomes by surfacing different conditions relevant for high but not for low turnback intention. In essence, low and high turnback intentions are formed by different configurations and are not in inverse configurations.

IT work shocks as specific to a particular profession: This study on why employees develop turnback intention identifies IT-specific attributes that drive IT professionals’ perceptions, intentions, and behaviors (Mourmant et al. 2009; Niederman et al. 2007). We contribute by showing that IT professionals face specific types of shocks, which we call IT work shocks, and that these shocks can drive turnback intention. In line with the concept of IT identity of reflecting “the extent to which an individual views the use of an IT as integral to his or her sense of self” (Carter and Grover 2015, p. 932), IT professionals appear to consider IT as central to their job because it gives meaning to their job and closely connects IT work shocks to whether they intend to continue working for an organization.

While our results emphasize the importance of IT work shocks, IT professionals in our sample also experienced work shocks. However, these work shocks did not develop into turnback intention. We observe that the presence of work shocks only influences IT professionals in a configuration (C3) that depicts a typical turnover situation where a work shock triggers an individual to leave their current employer (Niederman et al. 2007). As we are not able to provide both an empirical and theoretical explanation for why work shocks do not influence IT professional turnback, we encourage future researchers to further explore this finding. One possible explanation might be grounded in the reason that an IT professional left their former employer. This might reasonably have an influence on whether IT professionals have turnback or turnover intention. For example, IT professionals who left a former employer because of a work shock might not be motivated to return to the former employer should they experience a similar work shock while working for their current employer. In such cases, IT professionals may rather search for a new opportunity with a new employer.

Job embeddedness with a former employer as a characteristic attribute that is relevant and necessary for turnback: A substantial amount of general management research has used the concept of job embeddedness with a current employer to offer valuable insights into understanding why employees, in general, and IT professionals, specifically, stay at an organization (Mitchell et al. 2001; Lee et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2012). We extend that research stream in two ways. We show that IT professionals consider their level of job embeddedness with multiple employers. In particular, IT professionals return when job embeddedness with a former employer is higher than job embeddedness with the current employer.

Specific attributes drive turnback beyond those relevant for turnover: From a general point of view, our results offer theoretical arguments and an empirical validation that turnback is formed based on attributes different from those influencing other job mobility behaviors. While turnover and turnback are similar in that both involve an employee quitting a job, we propose that the two job mobility behaviors are formed differently. While turnover is typically formed by attributes that push the employee away from the current organization (Joseph et al. 2007), turnback also requires that an employee is pulled toward a former employer.

Ex-employees and returners as a new class of employees: We contribute by focusing on a new class of employees.

Specifically, this study indicates that employees returning to a former employer are equipped with unique and different knowledge about an employment opportunity. Employees returning to a former employer know what it was like to work for that employer, including work and organizational characteristics. In contrast, employees entering an organization for the first time know much less about these factors.

Specific insights in comparison to previous results from general management research: Prior general management research has discussed employees returning to a former employer in relation to their performance (Keller et al. in press; Swider et al. 2017) and turnback intention (Shipp et al. 2014). However, these studies have failed to explain the drivers of turnback among employees in general and IT professionals in particular. To fill this gap, we conceptualize turnback based on well-established concepts (i.e., shocks and job dissatisfaction) and theory (i.e., job embeddedness theory) and extend theory by investigating additional attributes (i.e., three different types of shocks, two different types of job embeddedness). We thus complement prior research by showing that returning to a former employer is accompanied by high levels of job embeddedness, which leads to turnback intention. We thereby we point to different sets of attributes related to turnback that are worthy of attention by future research.

## Implications for IS Practice

In the face of insufficient numbers of qualified entrants to the IT field, this research introduces recruiting former employees as a potential new source of IT professionals. We recommend<sup>7</sup> actions that organizations can take to attract former IT professionals to return after they quit. There are four different phases that each require different activities (Figure 6). The flight path of a boomerang can be used to illustrate the phases and explain organizational activities.

The first phase is the push phase, which is the period immediately after the IT professional submits their resignation. In that phase of detachment, the organization should accept the resignation in good faith and do so respectfully. For example, the organization should conduct an exit interview where both parties can express their views and discuss needed improvements. These insights may be key to attracting the IT professional back later. Despite the psychological distance created by resignation, it is important to establish a positive foundation for long-term embeddedness within the organization.

The second phase is the reach-out phase, which typically starts three to four months after the IT professional has left the organization. By then, the distance between the IT professional and the organization is stable. Even though the IT professional is working for another organization, the psychological distance may not increase further. The organization should actively increase the levels of embeddedness that the former employee continues to feel for that organization and seek to positively influence the employee’s turnback intention. An organization can strengthen former employees’ links to the organization and former colleagues by inviting them to join an internal alumni network and inviting them to attend formal or informal events such as sporting events. The organization could also focus on former employees’ fit with the organization and the community by informing them about ongoing developments and job openings through social networking sites, for example. In addition, to minimize the sacrifice incurred in leaving their current employer, hiring organizations can facilitate ex-employees’ return by supporting relocation, for instance. These tactics may also be used to evaluate whether former employees are currently satisfied with their current employer, how well they are embedded with their current employer, or whether they have experienced recent IT work or personal shocks. It is beneficial to wait several months because most IT professionals do not accept alumni network invitations immediately after quitting. They do not want to signal to their new employer that they still have a (strong) link to their former employer. Furthermore, after working for several months with their new employer, IT professionals may see that the grass is not greener there. Such activities can help former IT professionals maintain their links with the former organization and foster a continued feeling of embeddedness.

The third phase is the tipping point phase. After a period of stabilization, the distance between the IT professional and the organization may again grow smaller if factors begin to push the former IT professional away from their current employer. The organization typically notices this when the IT professional describes their increased dissatisfaction with working for the current organization or mentions a shock that has sparked thoughts of returning to the former organization. The organization must recognize these signs that the IT professional is considering leaving their current employer and still feels embedded in the organization, should continue to invite the IT professional to special events to maintain high embeddedness within the organization, and should actively communicate relevant open positions.

## Limitations and Future Research

This novel research into IT professional turnback intention paves the way for significant future research that might address the limitations of this study.

Uncover the link between turnback intention and turnback behavior: One limitation of this study is our focus on turnback intention rather than turnback behavior. Previous research has shown that hypothetical ideas such as behavioral intentions do not always lead to actual behavior. This is especially true for behaviors with far-reaching implications such as turnback behavior because hypothetical intentions minimize the uncertainty involved with moving, leaving behind the social environment, and establishing a new one, and risks such as being dissatisfied with new working conditions (Maier et al. 2015b). Although the intentionbehavior gap limits the implications of our results to a degree, the influence of the gap on turnback might be less significant than the gap on turnover (Joseph et al. 2007) because IT professionals returning to a former employer face less uncertainty than IT professionals venturing into unknown working conditions.

Investigate how IT professionals’ resignation motivations drive turnback intention: Do certain types of resignation motivations increase or decrease turnback intention and behavior more than others? Is resigning in response to a strong pull from outside more likely to drive turnback intention than resigning in response to a strong push via job dissatisfaction associated with stress factors, personal conflict, working conditions, lack of career advancement opportunities, or strategic or ethical differences? These potential research questions might be answered by applying Deci and Ryan’s theory of motivation (1985) and using a longitudinal multiplewave/diary study of IT professionals.

Reveal the degree to which IT work shocks, work shocks, and personal shocks drive turnback intention: How do IT professionals appraise shocks? What is the role of the criticality, urgency, and duration of a shock on IT professional turnback intention? To answer such questions, future research might integrate Morgeson et al.’s (2006) work on event characteristics, taking a qualitative process perspective.

Identify additional factors that drive turnback intention: How do various stress factors, role conflict, and role ambiguity influence dissatisfaction and turnover and, ultimately, drive turnback intention? How do interdepartmental and interorganizational employee communication patterns drive turnback intention? These potential research questions can be answered by taking a stress-theoretic perspective (Ahuja et al. 2007; Moore 2000) to studying role stress and a boundaryspanning perspective (Leifer and Delbecq 1978) to understanding information exchange. Research in that stream could take an individual-level, configurational perspective (Mattke et al. 2021) to reveal profiles of IT professionals who return vs. profiles of IT professionals who, instead of returning, seek a new employer.

Explain what happens after turnback: How do IT professionals’ beliefs about the job and the organization differ after turnback? In what ways does turnback influence the prevalence of loyalty, performance, job satisfaction, overall well-being, absentee rates, and resilience/burnout? Such potential research questions could be answered by applying belief-update theory (Hogarth and Einhorn 1992) to take a longitudinal approach to tracking how beliefs (Maier et al. 2015b) change across the employment life cycle including turnback and how returning influences IT professionals work-related behavior and well-being.

## Conclusion

This study offers a typology of attributes explaining why IT professionals develop intentions to return to a former employer after quitting. We call this intention to return to a former employer turnback intention and conclude that (1) turnover is not the end of the employee-employer relationship in the IT professional life cycle but rather can be followed by turnback; (2) the drivers of turnback intention may include attributes such as job dissatisfaction and personal or IT work shocks that push employees away from their current employer, as well as attributes such as perceived job embeddedness with a former employer that pull IT professionals back to a former employer; and (3) IT professionals are subject to a unique type of shock that non-IT professionals are not subject to, i.e., IT work shocks, which are distinct from general work and personal shocks. Finally (4), we establish the complex causality of turnback behavior arising from the equifinality, asymmetric causation, and asymmetry between outcomes. If turnover behavior involves searching for greener pastures, IT professionals who realize that the grass is not greener at a new organization may consider returning to a former employer to whom they still feel connected.

## Scale Development for Turnback Intention

Since previous turnover research has not focused on turnback intention, we could not use previously validated measurement items. We redefined related behavioral intention variables through lengthy discussions with scholars and IT professionals, some of whom had returned to a former employer. We validated the variables and items before using them in a survey following a two-step q-sort approach. We presented the newly defined concept of turnback intention and scale validation surveys to a panel of 38 scholars and IT professionals and requested that they assign the new items to one of the constructs. We gave them two fictive examples to illustrate how items should be assigned to the constructs. For example, we indicated that the fictive item “I will return to work for a former employer” should be assigned to turnback intention, a different behavioral intention (e.g., turnover intention) or a field named “no assignment” if the participants cannot assign it. We stressed the importance of assigning the items by carefully considering the definitions of turnback intention and the other behavioral intention variables. This procedure was repeated for every item. On the basis of 38 responses, we calculated ratios to which participants correctly matched the newly developed items to the construct turnback intention. As suggested in prior research (Nahm et al. 2002), we would have rejected items assigned correctly by less than 61% of the respondents but there were no such items in our case; we thus concluded that the identified items have high semantic coherence.

## Common Method Bias and Nonresponse Bias

We took steps to lower the risk of CMB and took procedural approaches to reduce the impact of CMB (Podsakoff et al. 2012). In our case, this meant ensuring that respondents were unaware of the nature of the study. We also ensured that responses were anonymous to reduce the pressure to respond in a socially desirable way. Finally, we used valid measures, all items were presented in a random order, and some of the items were reverse coded. To determine the extent of CMB, we ran three statistical tests. The Harman’s single factor test shows that only 13.4% of the variance of the data is explained by one factor. Then, we examined the correlation matrix. Extremely high correlations (r > 0.90) are an indicator of CMB but our correlation matrix did not indicate such high correlations. Finally, we determine the extent of CMB with the help of PLS by including a CMB factor into the model (Williams et al. 2003). The CMB factor explains an average delta R² of 0.0014. Without a CMB factor, the average R² is 79.2%. This indicates ratios of 1:565.7, i.e., no observable signs of CMB influence. Furthermore, we checked for the influence of nonresponse bias by splitting our data set into three equal-sized groups depending on when they participated in the survey. By comparing the demographic data and the attributes included in the research model with t-test statistics, we observed no significant differences between the first and the last participants. We also see that there are no significant differences for attribute responses in those groups, so we concluded that this was not an issue for our data.

## Classification of Shocks Based on the Survey Responses

Survey participants indicated whether they have experienced a shock that made them start to consider leaving their current employer. If so, we asked them to specify and explain the shock briefly or at length in an open text field. Based on these answers, two of the authors classified the shock. We analyzed the explanations using sentences and paragraphs as the unit of analysis. In an initial step, we checked all responses and removed those that did not fit the definition of shocks in terms of jarring events (section “Attributes Driving Turnback” and “Shocks” for further information). Only when both authors classifying the shocks agreed that a statement did not fit the definition of one type of shock did we indicate that the participant had not perceived a shock. When only one of the authors indicated a low fit with the definition, the statements were discussed in detail.

We then used descriptive coding (Myers 2009) to compare and contrast similar and different shocks in the given sentences and paragraphs to then group similar shocks (Table B1). Subsequently, we used interpretive coding (Myers 2009) to group the descriptive codes. We thereby acknowledge that shocks have different causes in one’s personal or work environments (Holtom et al. 2005) and incorporated specifics of our data suggesting that—on a different level of abstraction—we see shocks that are specifically IT related. To group conceptual similar shocks, we aggregated data across multiple individuals. This allowed us to identify three fundamental types of shocks: IT work shock, work shock, and personal shock. We defined the three shocks as shocks related to working with IT, to work in general, or to the personal context. As mentioned above, we differentiated between shocks that are specific to IT professionals in their work as IT professionals and shocks that are work specific but can occur in other occupations as well.

To ensure reliability, the coding process was done separately by two of the authors with 96% intercoder reliability and by one IT professional who was not part of the research team with intercoder reliability in this case of 95%. One of the authors coded the statements twice, once upon data collection and again approximately one year later with 98% intracoder reliability.

## Shocks for IT Professionals vs. Non-IT Professionals

We differentiate between the three types of shock in terms of IT work shock, work shock, and personal shock. To test whether IT work shocks are specific and only relevant for IT professionals, we performed post-survey interviews in two organizations. We interviewed 12 (Organization 1: 6, Organization 2: 6) IT professionals and 21 (Organization 1: 11, Organization 2: 10) non-IT professionals to determine whether (1) IT professionals were confronted with IT work shocks, and (2) non-IT professionals had not been confronted with those IT work shocks in the past. In short, our interviews were structured in two steps. In a first step, we asked participants exploratory questions about what shocks they perceived in the previous few years. In a second step, we confronted participants’ confirmatory responses with the shocks identified in our survey (Table B2) and asked whether they were confronted with that shock in the previous few years.
