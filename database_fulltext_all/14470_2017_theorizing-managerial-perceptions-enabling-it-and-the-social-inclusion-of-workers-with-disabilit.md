# Theorizing managerial perceptions, enabling IT, and the social inclusion of workers with disabilities

## 1. Introduction

An enduring challenge in information systems (IS) research is understanding how information technology (IT) can be best leveraged to advance the strategies of organizations. Diversity and inclusion are increasingly important organizational strategies woven into every aspect of the talent management lifecycle. A 2017 survey by Deloitte shows that the proportion of executives who view social inclusion (SI) as a top priority has grown 32% in the past 3 years (Bourke, Garr, van Berkel, & Wong, 2017). More diverse and inclusive organizations are believed to enjoy greater customer orientation, increased employee satisfaction, improved decisionmaking and greater ability to attract top talent (Hunt, Layton, & Prince, 2015; Rock & Grant, 2016). Despite growing recognition of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of diversity and social inclusion, organizations have failed to proportionately include people with disabilities (Chan et al., 2010; Institute on Disability, 2016; McDonnall, Crudden, & O'Malley, 2015; UNCRPD, 2008). A comprehensive study by the U.S. Department of Labor involving 3,797 U.S. managers found fewer than 20% employed workers with disabilities (Domzal, Houtenville, & Sharma, 2008). Managers have thus far been challenged to develop e<sup>f</sup>ective strategies for the inclusion of people with disabilities (Domzal et al., 2008; Erickson, Lee, & von Schrader, 2014a; Nota, Santilli, Ginevra, & Soresi, 2014; World Blind Union, 2010). What role can IS researchers play in helping organizations enact better strategies for the social inclusion of workers with disabilities?

Advances in enabling IT have dramatically increased the capabilities of workers with certain disabilities. Projects such as Microsoft's “Seeing AI”, Toyota's “Project Blaid”, NVDIA's “Horus”, Apple's talking iProducts, Google Glass, AIRA, and others demonstrate the high degree of commitment of the IT industry to rede<sup>fi</sup>ne disability through the use of enabling IT (Apple Inc., 2017; Beckett, 2016; Du<sup>f</sup>y, 2017; D. Lee, 2016; Smith, 2017). Some of this technology is relatively mature. Commodity devices such as smartphones, with their built-in screen readers, cameras, speech synthesizers and enabling apps comprise a mobile IT bundle have been shown to a<sup>f</sup>ord blind workers new capabilities (Babu & Heath, 2017). Organizational strategies toward diversity and inclusion stand to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from these developments. IS research is needed to inform organizations regarding how to leverage enabling IT to include workers with disabilities.

Current IS research on SI in the organizational context has largely focused on issues such as gender, racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic imbalance within the IT <sup>fi</sup>eld (Ahuja, 2002; Trauth, 2017). For example, Trauth and Graham (2015) studied if veterans with disabilities see themselves as future IT professionals. This stream of research focuses primarily on social and structural factors behind inclusion/exclusion practices, underemphasizing the role of IT as an enabler of SI. A second stream of SI research addresses technology enablement primarily as a design issue concerned with IT accessibility and usability for users with disabilities, rather than as a vehicle for SI (Cushman & Mclean, 2008; Trauth & Graham, 2015). There exists an opportunity for IS researchers to bridge these two streams and develop new SI theories on the role of enabling IT in advancing organizational strategies on diversity and inclusion regarding workers with disabilities.

The problem of IT-enabled social inclusion of workers with disabilities is socio-technical at its core. Many organizations have limited experience hiring and managing workers with disabilities (Domzal et al., 2008). The UN Commission on the Rights of People with Disabilities observes managers often fail to appreciate that when enabling IT is made available, workers with disabilities are able to work as effectively as their non-disabled colleagues in a wide range of industrial. commercial, managerial and professional jobs (2008, Article 27 section 2.2). In fact, research has shown that managers often invoke negative stereotypes when evaluating workers with disabilities (Crudden & McBroom, 1999; M. McDonnall et al., 2015; Ren, Paetzold, & Colella, 2008a; Stone & Colella, 1996). Negative disability stereotypes have a profound e<sup>f</sup>ect on managers expectations regarding the ability of a worker with disability to perform a job, and are a major barrier to their workplace inclusion (Crudden & McBroom, 1999; Crudden, Williams, Moore, & McBroom, 2002; Kirchner & Harkins, 1997; Louvet, 2007a). While disability stereotypes are not necessarily true, these are extremely resistant to change by discon<sup>fi</sup>rming information (Brewer & Miller, 1984; Stone & Colella, 1996). Though enabling ITs are rede<sup>fi</sup>ning what it means to be disabled, it is not known whether disability stereotypes held by managers are sensitive to discon<sup>fi</sup>rming information regarding the new capabilities these technologies might a<sup>f</sup>ord. New theorization is needed to account for managerial perceptions and their impact on IT-enabled workplace inclusion of people with disabilities.

This study explores how increased awareness of enabling IT can impact the disability stereotypes of managers. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it addressed the following questions: (1) how does increased awareness of enabling IT and the capabilities it can aford impact the stereotypical beliefs of managers toward workers with disabilities? And (2) does a better-informed stereotype increase the likelihood of their workplace inclusion? Workplace inclusion, in this study, refers to employment, participation in workplace activities, and the potentia for career advancement. The context of our investigation was blindness as a type of disability. There are several factors which motivate the selection of blindness as the context. First, much is known regarding the blindness stereotypes held by managers (American Foundation for the Blind, 2014; M. McDonnall et al., 2015; National Industries for the Blind, 2012; World Blind Union, 2010). Second, there has been explosive growth in the number of enabling applications available to blind smartphone users. AppleVis, a community-powered website dedicated to blind users of Apple products, has catalogued over 120 commercially available apps developed speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to empower blind people (AppleVis, 2016). Finally, > 160 million people around the world are blind or severely vision impaired (World Health Organization, 2010). Only 25% of the working age adults in this group are employed (Institute on Disability, 2016). Among those who are unemployed, nearly three-quarters express their desire to work (Ren, Paetzold, & Colella, 2008b). Research on the IT-enabled inclusion of blind workers is a potentially high-impact area of investigation. Unraveling this puzzle could help organizations leverage IT to reach a large and untapped pool of talent which has been historicall underrepresented in the workplace.

The research questions were investigated quantitatively using a quasi-experimental pre and post research design. Research participants included 235 managers of organizations spread across the U.S. Results showed that increased awareness of enabling IT and the capabilities it a<sup>f</sup>ords can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce the negative disability stereotypes held by managers. IT-enabled workers were deemed more capable, more independent, and easier to accommodate in the workplace than their unassisted peers. They were seen as a better <sup>fi</sup>t with the work and workplace. Participants professed greater willingness to include these workers in the work activities in their organizations. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest negative disability stereotypes held by managers are vulnerable to educational interventions.

The balance of the paper is organized as follows. The <sup>fi</sup>rst section provides a review of relevant literature on disability stereotypes and social inclusion of people with disabilities. Next, the conceptual model is presented with related hypotheses. Following this is a discussion of the methods. Next is a description of the data analysis and results. This is followed by the discussion, implications, and conclusion.

## 2. Review of literature

## 2.1. Stereotypes and social inclusion in the IS literature

There is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant body of IS research dedicated to understanding how cultural, social, and organizational stereotypes in-<sup>fl</sup>uence the social inclusion (SI) of underrepresented groups in the IT profession. IS research on SI often investigates the intersectionality of stereotypes about marginalized groups (e.g. gender, race and ethnicity) with stereotypes related to the profession. A number of studies have examined how gender stereotypes and stereotypes of IT work have discourage the entry of women into the IT workforce (Adya, Kaiser, Adya, & Kaiser, 2005; Ahuja, 2002; Annabi & Lebovitz, 2011; Clayton, Beekhuyzen, & Nielsen, 2012; Trauth, 2017). Identity factors such as ethnicity and race have also been examined in relation to participation in the IT profession (Cain & Trauth, 2013; Joshi, Kvasny, Unnikrishnan, & Trauth, 2016; McLaurin, Carte, & Randolph, 2015). For example, Kvasny, Trauth, and Morgan (2009) investigated power relations in IT education and work as experienced by black women who were both developers and users of IT. Morgan and Trauth (2013) examined the intersectionality of gender, race, and socio-economic factors in the use of IT for health information search and retrieval. Trauth, Cain, Joshi, Kvasny, & Booth (2016) investigated gender-ethnic intersectionality in their study of university students and their stereotypes regarding the IT profession. Despite this interest in stereotypes and their relationship with SI, the IS literature is largely silent on disability-related stereotypes and the inclusion of workers with disabilities. Based on a thorough review of IS literature, we found only two relevant articles. These studies dealt with the selfperceptions of military veterans with disabilities and their ability to “see themselves” in IT careers (Trauth, 2017). These studies focus on the perspective of an individual experiencing a disability, and not that of the observer (e.g. the manager of an employee with disability). Since managers serve as gate-keepers of a <sup>fi</sup>rm who decide whether an individual with disability may be included or not in their workforce, their perspective on workers with disabilities assumes critical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance for SI within the organizational context. As existing IS literature does not throw any light on this critical managerial perspective, we turn to literature from other disciplines to understand the linkage between disability stereotypes and inclusion/exclusion of individuals with disabilities within the organizational workforce.

## 2.2. Disability stereotypes and workplace inclusion

Disability stereotypes and workplace inclusion have garnered much researcher attention in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of Disability Studies. Workplace inclusion is used here as a subsumptive term which describes the employability, ability to participate in workplace activities, and potential for career advancement of individuals with disabilities. A central theme of many inquiries has been how human resource judgements are shaped by the a priori stereotypes held by managers (Crudden et al., 2002; Greenwood & Johnson, 1985; Louvet, 2007a, 2007b; Ren et al., 2008a; Stone & Colella, 1996). According to Stone and Colella (1996), managers make inferences about the attributes of individuals with disabilities based on disability stereotypes, and then assess the individual's quali<sup>fi</sup>cations based on the degree to which these attributes <sup>fi</sup>t the prototypical images of the work requirements. Managers automatically categorize individuals according to several disability subtypes (e.g., physically disabled, mentally disabled), then apply stereotypes associated with that disability sub-type to make inferences about the individual's capabilities. Work requirements and expectancies regarding the stereotypes shape their performance expectations from the individual with disability.

Managers often cling to unfounded concerns about individuals with disabilities, including false assumptions about their capabilities, performance levels, absenteeism, turnover rates, and the high costs of accommodation (Braddock & Bachelder, 1994; Lengnick-Hall, Gaunt, & Kulkarni, 2008). Some see individuals with disabilities as incompetent, unproductive, and dependent (Rohmer & Louvet, 2009; Scherbaum & Scherbaum, 2005). A large, nationally representative survey of hiring managers on disabilities and employment (Domzal et al., 2008) showed that uncertainty about cost of accommodations was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant managerial concern. Among di<sup>f</sup>erent types of disability, managers perceive blindness to be one of the most di<sup>fi</sup>cult to accommodate (B. A. Lee, 1996). Stereotypical attributes ascribed to blind individuals include inability to perform most jobs effectively, their need for costly ac commodations, and their dependence on lots of (sighted) assistance (Kwoh, 2013a; Lynch, 2013; M. McDonnall et al., 2015).

A basic criterion for assigning workers to a particular role or task within an organization is the expectation that they will be capable of successfully performing the assigned duties. This is often conceptualized in terms of <sup>fi</sup>t (Edwards, 1991; Kristof-Brown, Zimmerman, & Johnson, 2005; Sekiguchi, 2004). Fit can be broadly de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the compatibility between characteristics of an individual and various aspects of their work environment and/or the work to be performed. Theories of person-environment (PE) <sup>fi</sup>t have been prevalent in the management science literature for almost 100 years (Kristof-Brown et al., 2005). High degrees of <sup>fi</sup>t are seen as good predictors of individual performance. As is the case with all workers, the workplace inclusion of blind people is contingent on managers' perceptions of their <sup>fi</sup>tness or “<sup>fi</sup>t”. However, performance expectations for workers with disabilities depend on disability stereotypes and predictions of disability-<sup>fi</sup>t (Colella & Varma, 1999; Louvet, 2007a, 2007b). A meta-analysis of literature on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of disabilities on managerial judgements (Ren et al., 2008a, 2008b) showed clear negative e<sup>f</sup>ects of disability on performance expectations and decisions regarding inclusion/exclusion. Often, managers are unsure how the worker with disability will perform, and whether he/she would <sup>fi</sup>t with the position and/or organization. Workers with disabilities are typically evaluated as less desirable employees than workers without disabilities (Collela, De Nisi, & Varma, 1998; Gething, 1992; Loo, 2001; Ravaud, Madiot, & Ville, 1992; Stevenage & McKay, 1999). Managers have even greater concerns regarding workers who are blind (Fuqua, Rathburn, & Gade, 1984; Williams, 1972). They believe it would be di<sup>fi</sup>cult to recruit blind workers for commonly available positions (Gilbride, Stensrud, Evans, & Peterson, 2000; Inglis, 2006). Managers' lack of awareness and misperceptions regarding blindness is believed to be the major cause for the low workplace inclusion of blind individuals (Crudden et al., 2002; Crudden & McBroom, 1999; Kirchner & Harkins, 1997). Increasing the likelihood of workplace inclusion of people with disabilities will <sup>fi</sup>rst require interventions that reduce the negative e<sup>f</sup>ects of disability stereotypes held by managers.

Advocates for people with disabilities believe that greater awareness of enabling IT will help managers recognize the workplace potential of these workers (108th U. S. Congress, 2004; UNCRPD, 2008; World Blind Union, 2010). This stands in stark contrast to theorizations that negative disability stereotypes are resistant to change despite disconfirming information (Brewer & Miller. 1984: Stone & Colella, 1996). In an exploratory case study involving IT-enabled sightless workers of an Industry for the Blind, Babu and Heath (2017) reported that increased awareness of enabling IT could in<sup>fl</sup>uence managerial stereotypes in a positive direction. In particular, they found three stereotypical beliefs were impacted by the introduction of enabling IT, namely: worker capability, selfreliance, and self-accommodation. Managers in their study perceived IT-enabled blind workers as more independent, self-reliant and easier to accommodate. However, it is noteworthy that this research was conducted at a single organization predisposed to including blind workers. It remains unclear whether increased awareness of enabling IT would have the same impact in other organizations, particularly those which might be less “blind-friendly”. Quantitative research is necessary to understand the generalizability of the claim that the disability stereotypes held by managers are subject to change consequent to increased awareness of enabling IT, and to learn what impact, if any, this might have on the workplace inclusion of people with disabilities.

## 3. Conceptual model

In this research, workplace inclusion is investigated as a special case of social inclusion. The investigation concerns the blindness disability stereotype. Workplace inclusion is considered from the perspective of one key stakeholder group managers, who are the gatekeepers of inclusion within their organizations. The study draws on thematic issues identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior research to present a conceptual model of the blindness stereotype and workplace inclusion from the perspective of managers. The scope of conceptualization is on the stereotypical beliefs most logically impacted by greater awareness of enabling IT and the capabilities it might

a<sup>f</sup>ord blind workers.

## 3.1. Stereotypical fit of blind workers

This research is concerned with the perceived <sup>fi</sup>t of IT-enabled blind workers and its impact on the likelihood of their workplace inclusion. Studies have shown that managers struggle to accurately gauge the <sup>fi</sup>t of blind workers. For example, a recent study by McDonnall, O'Malley, and Crudden (2014) found a majority of managers (67%) could not identify how blind workers perform simple job tasks such as accessing a computer, using o<sup>fi</sup>ce equipment, or accessing preprinted materials. As a consequence, blind workers are often placed in low-skills jobs with limited opportunity for advancement (108th U. S. Congress, 2004; Catrin, 2005; World Blind Union, 2010). Lack of knowledge among managers is hardly surprising, as blindness is a low incidence condition among working age adults. In 2012, individuals with vision impairments constituted just 0.7% of the U.S. labor force (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2013). Disabilities literature <sup>fi</sup>nds managerial judgements toward workers with disabilities are governed by the disability stereotypes rather than more objective assessments of worker capabilities (Crudden & McBroom, 1999; M. McDonnall et al., 2015; Ren et al., 2008a; Stone & Colella, 1996). Further, disability stereotypes are nominally informed regarding enabling IT or the capabilities it might a<sup>f</sup>ord (Domzal et al., 2008; Ren et al., 2008b; UNCRPD, 2008).

In the case of blind workers, recent academic and practitioner research shows that enabling IT can increase their ability to interact with visual elements of their environment (Babu & Heath, 2017; Bilton, 2013; Csapo, Wersenyi, Nagy, & Stockman, 2015; Farr & Oreskovic, 2014; Sta<sup>f</sup>, 2013). We believe iincreased awareness of such augmented capabilities should improve manageria perceptions of <sup>fi</sup>t for IT-enabled workers. Building on the foundations described above, the following hypothesis is o<sup>f</sup>ered:

H1. Higher perceived capabilities of IT-enabled blind workers (IBW) will lead to higher perceived <sup>fi</sup>t

Another concern of managers is whether blind workers will be able to function independently. A survey of 400 Human Resources directors, managers, and hiring managers in the U.S. conducted by the National Industries for the Blind (2012) found 40% of respondents believed blind workers would require sighted assistance to perform their duties. Babu and Heath (2017) con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that self-reliance of blind workers was a managerial concern. Citing a CIO familiar with training and referring blind workers, they o<sup>f</sup>er the following:

Employers are concerned whether a blind worker can do the job or am I going to have to help them… If you're an organization that doesn't employ the blind, you don't know what blind workers can do or what you'll need to do for them.

Enabling IT has been shown to increase the functional independence of blind individuals (AppleVis, 2016; Parker, 2013; Sta<sup>f</sup>, 2013). Assuming the blindness stereotypes held by managers can be altered with discon<sup>fi</sup>rming information, IT-enabled blind workers may be seen as more functionally independent, and hence a better <sup>fi</sup>t for work and the workplace. This is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the following hypotheses:

H2. Higher perceived capabilities of IBW will lead to higher perceived functional independence.

H3. Higher perceived functional independence of IBW will lead to higher perceived <sup>fi</sup>t.

Many studies show that managers are uncertain regarding the ease of accommodating blind workers. Their perception is that blind workers will require expensive accommodations (Kwoh, 2013a; Lynch, 2013). We believe IT-enabled blind workers who are more functionally independent should require less accommodation. For example, IT-enabled blind workers have demonstrated their ability to independently read posters, bulletin boards, machine instructions and other print materials which otherwise required sighted assistance, a common form of job accommodation (Babu & Heath, 2017). Workers who need less accommodation are intuitively more likely to be perceived as a better <sup>fi</sup>t by managers. Based on this, the following hypotheses are developed:

H4. Higher perceived functional independence of IBW will lead to higher perceived ease of accommodation.

H5. Higher perceived ease of accommodation of IBW will lead to higher perceived <sup>fi</sup>t

## 3.2. Perceived fit and the likelihood of workplace inclusion

Performance estimates for blind workers are highly dependent on the blindness stereotypes (Louvet, 2007a; Stone & Colella, 1996). Comprehensive studies on managerial concerns related to hiring, advancing and retaining workers with disabilities show that managers believe these workers would be unable to perform most jobs in their organizations (Domzal et al., 2008; Erickson, Lee, & von Schrader, 2014b; M. McDonnall et al., 2015; National Industries for the Blind, 2012). Louvet (2007a, 2007b) suggests that the perceived worker <sup>fi</sup>t impacts performance expectations. However, enabling IT can a<sup>f</sup>ord blind workers new capabilities. Therefore, it is logical to believe that performance expectations from IT-enabled blind workers would be higher compared to their unassisted colleagues. Thus, the following hypothesis is developed

H6. Higher perceived <sup>fi</sup>tness of IBW will lead to higher performance expectations.

Performance expectations regarding workers with disabilities are strongly implicated in their workplace exclusion (Domzal et al., 2008; Kinicki, Lockwood, Hom, & Gri<sup>f</sup>eth, 1990; Kwoh, 2013b; M. McDonnall et al., 2014; National Industries for the Blind, 2012). Therefore, any increase in the performance estimates of managers regarding blind workers should increase their likelihood of workplace inclusion. Therefore, the following hypothesis is developed:

H7. Higher performance expectations regarding IBW will result in greater likelihood of workplace inclusion.

Based on the above discussion, we theorize that, in the case of workers with disabilities, enabling IT has a direct role in shaping the disability stereotypes held by managers as well as their propensity for workplace inclusion of such workers. This has not been theorized or tested before in the IS literature on SI. The empirical strategy to test the hypotheses is described in the section which follows.

## 4. Methods

This study adopted a quasi-experimental pretest-posttest design to test the hypotheses and theorized model. In addition the deltas of the dependent and independent variables were compared in the pre and post data to evaluate the impact of the treatment. The treatment was a brief video presentation in which a blind worker illustrated the use of enabling IT to perform two common workplace tasks. First, they read an unopened letter. Next, they identifying an unfamiliar piece of o<sup>fi</sup>ce equipment (i.e. a computer printer) and located information online regarding its operation. The target population for the study was U.S. managers and executives directly involved in worker selection and assessment in their <sup>fi</sup>rms. Data collection took place using an electronic survey administered by a third-party organization based on the demographic characteristics of the desired sample. In the pre-test, respondents were surveyed to measure their perceptions of blind workers absent technology. In the post-test, respondents were surveyed to measure their perceptions of blind workers using enabling IT. The delta between the pre and post-tests was measured with paired t-tests to understand the impact of the treatment on managers' perceptions.

## 4.1. Development of instruments

Survey measures for the six constructs hypothesized in the model were adapted from prior studies and contextualized to this research (Domzal et al., 2008; M. C. McDonnall, 2014; McDonnall et al., 2014; National Industries for the Blind, 2012). Multi-item scales were used to improve reliability and validity of measurement. All items employed semantic di<sup>f</sup>erential scales ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” or, in the case of Likelihood of Inclusion, “extremely unlikely to extremely likely”. Scale items are described in Appendix B. We adapted items from the U.S. Department of Labor Survey of Employer Perspectives on the Employment of People with Disabilities (Domzal et al., 2008) and the National Industries of the Blind Decision-Maker study (2012) to develop a three-item Likert scale which measured the perceived capabilities of blind workers in general. Perceived functional independence and perceived ease of accommodation were measured using items adapted from earlier work by McDonnall et al. (2014). Perceived <sup>fi</sup>t was measured using three items which contextualized the characteristics of blind workers to the respondent's own organization (Domzal et al., 2008; Gilbride et al., 2000; National Industries for the Blind, 2012). Performance expectation was measured using a four-item scale which examined expectations relative to workers without disabilities and also relative to work demands. This is consistent with other studies which treat performance expectation as a holistic judgment grounded in the blindness stereotype (Louvet, 2007a; M. C. McDonnall, 2014; National Industries for the Blind, 2012). Likelihood of workplace inclusion was measured using a three-item Likert scale which assessed the willingness of managers to hire blind workers and consider them for roles within their organizations (National Industries for the Blind, 2012).

## 4.2. Data collection and scale validation

The data used in the study were collected using a survey of U.S. managers and executives directly involved in the hiring process at their <sup>fi</sup>rm. Prior to distribution, the survey instrument was pretested in a pilot study. Data collection took place using an electronic survey administered by a third-party organization based on the characteristics of the sample desired (Anderson & Agarwal, 2011). The original sample consisted of 288 respondents. These were <sup>fi</sup>ltered to include only those individuals directly involved in worker selection and assessment in their respective organizations. In addition, reliability questions and attention <sup>fi</sup>lters were built into the survey to evaluate whether respondents had watched the videos, read the questions, and responded carefully. This resulted in 235 useable responses.

Data collection took place over a three-day period. In order to assess potential non-respondent bias, characteristics of earlyrespondents were compared to late-responders' using t-test statistics. Results showed no statistical di<sup>f</sup>erences between late-responders and early-responders, therefore supporting the assumption there was no response bias. The potential for common method biases exist in virtually all research but are well documented in cross-sectional studies which rely on self-reporting data. In order to minimize the threat of common method bias, the survey design arranged items to counter the order of the measurement of the dependent and independent variables (Lin & Huang. 2006). In particular, items for the dependent variable followed the items related to the independent ones whose order was randomized in the questionnaire In addition, we followed the procedural guidance ot Podsako<sup>f</sup>, MacKenzie, Leong-Yeon, and Podsako<sup>f</sup> (2003) in assuring respondent of their anonymity, assuring them that there were no right or wrong answers, and providing contextual information and de<sup>fi</sup>nitions to reduce any ambiguity. The presence of common method variance (CMV) was assessed using statistical measures as described in Appendix A.

## 5. Data analysis and results

Data analysis proceeded in three stages. First, we compared the means for the constructs in the theorized model to examine whether and how they had changed consequent to the treatment (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004). Next, we analyzed the hypothesized associations in the measurement model for both the pre-test (absent technology) and the post-test (IT-enabled blind worker) using partial least squares.

## 5.1. Comparison of construct means

To compare the delta in the constructs in the pre and post data, we aggregated item responses for each construct and compared the mean aggregate scores across pairs using a series of two-tailed t-tests (see Table 2).

Likelihood of Workplace Inclusion. the dependent variable in the study, increased from 10.5 in the pre-test to 11.3 in the post-test. supporting our expectation that increased awareness regarding the IT-enabled capabilities of blind workers would indeed positively impact managers' perceptions. Similarly, the delta for the other constructs moved in the expected direction as predicted by our hypotheses and prior studies. All of the comparisons were statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at $p \ < \ 0 . 0 5$ . These results justi<sup>fi</sup>ed further analysis to examine the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and strength of hypothesized relationships in the measurement model (Fig. 1).

## 5.2. Measurement model (pre-test)

Scale reliability and validity were assessed with a con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis using partial least squares (PLS) approach. PLS is a structural equation modeling technique that simultaneously assesses the reliability and validity of the measures of theoretical constructs and estimates the relationship among these constructs. The PLS technique was chosen based on its disregard for the constraint of multivariate normality, less restrictive requirements for sample size, and the ability to appropriately estimate the error variances of latent constructs measured by relatively few items (Gefen & Straub, 2005; Gordon, Dakshinamoorthy, & Wang, 2006; Petter, Straub, & Rai, 2007; Wang & Hou, 2015). Analysis was performed using SmartPLS 2.0 on the raw data sets and modeling all scale items as re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis was performed to examine the validity and reliability of the constructs.

## 5.3. Structural model and hypothesis testing (pre-test)

We employed the PLS bootstrapping procedure in order to obtain inferential statistics for all of the model parameters. Results support the hypothesized constructs and relationships in the model. ## 6.1. Discussion

Overall, our empirical results show the disability stereotypes held by managers are subject to change when managers are made aware of enabling IT. Our results show that education can rede<sup>fi</sup>ne managers' perceptions regarding workers with disabilities. Our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question asked how does increased awareness of enabling IT and the capabilities it can aford impact the stereotypical beliefs of managers toward blind workers?” As expected, managers saw IT-enabled workers as more capabilities, more functionally independent and easier to accommodate (see Table 1). ## 6.3. Implications for practice

There are many stakeholders who have demonstrated their commitment to solving the challenge of workplace inclusion of people with disabilities. Organizations are striving to create more diverse and inclusive work environments. Similarly, nearly three-quarters of people with disabilities who are not working want to <sup>fi</sup>nd a job (Ren et al., 2008b). Governments around the world have enacted legislation aimed at increasing the workplace inclusion of people with disabilities. The developer community has made great strides in developing enabling IT for people with disabilities. Similarly, The IT industry has created products with synthetic senses which allow people with disabilities to more fully interact with their environment. Clearly, there are a number of important stakeholders who are motivated to address the challenge of workplace inclusion for people with disabilities. Our research shows one way to help convert their motivations into tangible results.

In light of the advances in enabling IT, we take the view that it is no longer acceptable for managers to rely on under-informed stereotypes regarding the potential of workers with disabilities. Therefore, educational interventions are needed to help di<sup>f</sup>use knowledge of the IT-enabled capabilities of workers with disabilities into the workplace. In this research, we found even a brief exemplary demonstration of the capabilities of an IT-enabled worker with disabilities was su<sup>fi</sup>cient to change the hard held beliefs of managers and positively impact their willingness to include them in the activities in their organizations. Targeted interventions are needed to educate managers about the functional independence, capability and ease of accommodating workers with disabilities. Also, we recognize the need to integrate information on enabling IT into the IS curricula used to prepare future IS managers. Particularly, perspective IS managers should understand how IS extends the capabilities of workers with disabilities, how it a<sup>f</sup>ords them functional independence in the workplace, and how it can reduce the need for additional accommodation. In this way, they can play their role in supporting organizational strategies regarding diversity and workplace inclusion of people with disabilities.

## 7. Conclusion

Advances in enabling IT are increasingly associated with a wide range of social and organizational change. However, no perfect technology exists to solve all the challenges faced by workers with disabilities. Part of the motivation for writing this paper was to invite the IS research community to engage and help determine what IS possible.

This research investigated the potential of an emergent enabling IT to improve the workplace inclusion of people with disabilities. It showed how awareness of the enablements a<sup>f</sup>orded by IT can change minds regarding blindness as a disability, and reduce the negative impact of blindness stereotypes held by managers on the likelihood of workplace inclusion of blind individuals. Going right at the root of the problem of workplace disenfranchisement prioritized by the U.N. Convention on Rights of People with Disabilities, we highlighted the critical role of enabling IT in overcoming the perceived liabilities invoked by stereotypes associated with disabilities. The study <sup>fi</sup>nds even an exemplary demonstration of IT enablement served as an e<sup>f</sup>ective intervention for changing managerial attitudes toward workplace inclusion of people with disabilities. This assumes signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in light of two major impediments identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in literature in bringing about workplace inclusion. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is that disability stereotypes which shape managerial attitudes have been assumed to be intractable. The second is that negative managerial attitudes are a major cause of the high underrepresentation of workers with disabilities in organizations. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings prove that such impediments are not invincible.

An important <sup>fi</sup>nding of this research was that managers viewed IT-enabled workers with disabilities in a di<sup>f</sup>erent frame than those without enabling IT. Overall, they assessed the IT-enabled worker to be more desirable for their <sup>fi</sup>rms. Clearly, IT-enabled workers with disabilities belong to a di<sup>f</sup>erent league in the minds of managers, perhaps somewhere between unassisted workers with disabilities and mainstream workers without disabilities. As smartphones and other kinds of smart technologies have become commoditized and more readily available in most economies, enabling IT as a driver for workplace inclusion assumes signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. More work is needed to develop appropriate interventions aimed at di<sup>f</sup>using enabling IT and raising awareness of workplace enablements they a<sup>f</sup>ord people with disabilities. As consequence of this, we believe IS managers have a central role to play in sup porting organizational strategies on diversity and inclusion.

While the context of our investigation was blindness stereotypes, the theoretic model presented in this paper can easily be extended to examine workplace inclusion for other categories of disability and other forms of enabling IT. The model can guide future research to inform the strategies of policy-makers, vocational and rehabilitation organizations and referral agencies to shape interventions aimed at swaying managerial attitudes and behaviors toward workplace inclusion of people with disabilities. Further, the model can guide research to inform job skills trainers and other rehabilitation agencies in developing appropriate training regimes to produce IT-enabled workers. Taken together, such interventions represent appropriate <sup>fi</sup>rst steps, and involve the right stakeholders, to positively impact the wicked social problem of workplace disenfranchisement of people with disabilities.

Lastly, it is our best hope that this paper provokes other IS researchers and opens out a research stream on the role of enabling IT as a vehicle for social and economic inclusion. Situated at the con<sup>fl</sup>uence of organizations, IT, and people, the IS community is uniquely positioned to investigate this potential.

Within our organization, accommodating IT-enabled blind workers would not require job-task redesign.
