# Is it Your Fault? Framing Social Media Inclusion and Exclusion Using Just World Theory

Stacie Petter,<sup>1</sup> Laurie Giddens<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Wake Forest University, USA, petters@wfu.edu <sup>2</sup>University of North Texas, USA, laurie.giddens@unt.edu

## Abstract

Individuals who have experienced harm (also known as victims) by people, organizations, or adverse events sometimes use social media to share their experiences with others, search for information, and find social support. While some observers offer support and engage in inclusion toward victims on social media, other observers blame victims for their plight and participate in revictimization and exclusion. Victim blaming, which can lead to social exclusion, disproportionately impacts those in society who are already at risk for exclusion and may perpetuate existing racial, ethnic, gender, and economic inequalities. This research provides a theoretical framework to identify reasons why observers engage in such wide-ranging responses to victims on social media. Specifically, we consider social inclusion and exclusion at the confluence of a social phenomenon (victim blaming), a theory (just world theory), and an information technology artifact (social media) among a specific type of actor (observers). Our theoretical framework of social media inclusion and exclusion is informed by just world theory and considers how social media functionalities can promote social media inclusion or exclusion. We also offer research questions to stimulate future research related to social media inclusion, social media exclusion, and just world theory.

Keywords: Social Media Inclusion, Just World Theory, Social Media Exclusion, Victim Blaming, BJW, Belief in a Just World

Arlene Bailey was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on March 29, 2021 and underwent three revisions. This paper is part of the Special Issue on Technology and Social Inclusion.

Trigger warning: This article provides vignettes and examples of social media inclusion and exclusion associated with individuals who are victims of racism and nonconsensual dissemination of intimate images.

## 1 Introduction

Kevin Pak<sup>1</sup> is a second-generation Korean American who owns a restaurant and relies on social media to communicate with his current and potential customers. During a wave of anti-Asian American sentiment within his local community, Mr. Pak’s restaurant was vandalized as individuals painted slurs on the walls and broke several windows at the front of his restaurant. Mr. Pak posted a message on Instagram with a picture of the damage and announced:

We closed our restaurant this week to make repairs after being vandalized. We hope to be back serving your favorite dishes next week. Stay tuned!

Mr. Pak’s Instagram account was immediately flooded with responses with some being supportive, such as:

Thank you for sharing your amazing Korean cooking with the community. Can’t wait to enjoy your braised short ribs when you reopen!

Other responses included sentiments, such as:

Quit taking jobs and resources from people who are from here. Sounds like you got what you deserve. Maybe you will go back to China.

Mr. Pak can’t help but notice the varying reactions, ranging from support to blame.

For many individuals, social media is a critical component of their everyday lives. People use social media to share their experiences with others, search for information, connect with friends, and find social support (Eichhorn, 2008). Social media also enables individuals to obtain positive benefits for themselves and society, such as promoting personal health and wellness (Nabi et al., 2013), facilitating social change (Vaast et al., 2017), and encouraging civic engagement (Tim et al., 2017). After experiencing hardship, individuals may utilize social media to gain access to fundamental human needs such as support (Lu & Hampton, 2017) and a sense of belonging (Schneider et al., 2017). For example, victims, individuals who have experienced harm by others or an adverse event, can obtain support during a difficult time when observers respond to their hardship through comments or reactions to posts via social media. In the opening vignette, some patrons expressed support for Mr. Pak by sharing their desire for his restaurant to reopen.

Yet, victims do not always encounter positive experiences from observers on social media. When an individual shares their victimization on social media, some observers engage in revictimization by responding with negative responses, such as derogatory comments that blame the victim or support the offender (Wellman et al., 2017). Mr. Pak was first victimized offline when vandals broke windows and painted racist slurs on his restaurant. Revictimization occurred online when social media users engaged in victim blaming and suggested that Mr. Pak deserved to receive harm (e.g., through the destruction of his property) because of his race and ethnicity.

Social inclusion enables individuals to have similar and equal opportunities to participate in society, while social exclusion limits individuals or groups from fully participating in society (United Nations, 2016). The act of blaming and disparaging victims has important implications for understanding social inclusion and exclusion. Victim blaming disproportionately impacts those in society who are at risk for exclusion and may perpetuate existing racial, ethnic, gender, and economic inequalities (Dukes & Gaither, 2017; Gravelin et al., 2019; Heimer, 2019). By blaming a victim for their plight, individuals deny victims of justice, dignity, and well-being. Thus, we argue that the act of victim blaming perpetuates the exclusion of certain groups from engaging fully in society. If a victim is perceived as being responsible for the harm that comes to them, observers may feel absolved from their duty to intervene (Lerner, 1980) or make efforts to include individuals or groups who have been excluded. Research indicates that observers who witness someone being victimized online tend to perceive the victim as less human (Park & Park, 2015), and observers may perceive victims as being undeserving of respect (Stubbs-Richardson et al., 2018) or deserving of punishment (Rudert & Greifeneder, 2019).

Social media has the potential to exacerbate the effects of victim blaming (Hou et al., 2017). When an individual shares a victimizing event in an offline context, the number of observers who may engage in victim blaming are generally limited to those in a similar geolocation who may have personal knowledge of the victim or the event. However, when an individual discloses a victimizing event on social media, those observing the victim’s post or those who post victimblaming comments can be observed by a much larger audience (Shin & Hampton, 2021). For instance, half of adult Facebook users have over 200 friends (Smith, 2014).<sup>2</sup> Furthermore, an observer can easily share or repost another person’s victimizing experience or victim-blaming comment on social media, which then increases the number of people observing victim blaming. Harm is perpetuated when the victim cannot delete or control the spread of the incident online. Since victims belonging to certain minority groups are more likely to experience victim blaming than those belonging to non-minority groups (Dukes & Gaither, 2017; Zaleski et al., 2016), an opportunity exists to explore how and why social media can provide a sense of belonging and inclusion to some people while also excluding and revictimizing others.

A central theory providing insight into the antecedents, contexts, and impact of victim blaming in offline settings is just world theory (Lerner & Miller, 1978). Just world theory contends that individuals make sense of injustices in the world by assuming that victims receive the outcome that they deserve (Lerner, 1980). The theory explains how individuals rationalize and cope when observing or encountering victims who are subject to unjust or unfair suffering (Lerner, 1980). Extensive research has utilized just world theory to examine why observers perceive and respond to victims positively or negatively (e.g., Alves et al., 2019; Bègue, 2002; Correia & Vala, 2003; Hafer, 2000; van den Bos & Maas, 2009). We propose that just world theory also provides insight into the inclusion and exclusion of certain groups in society. On the one hand, as individuals are victimized, some observers of injustice will respond by derogating or blaming the victim, a response that further exacerbates the exclusion of individuals and groups. On the other hand, observers of injustice might respond with the support and inclusion of individuals and groups who have experienced harm. Just world theory has been predominantly theorized within offline contexts with few exceptions (e.g., Hollonquest, 2014; Hou et al., 2017; Shin & Hampton, 2021). Social media has altered how individuals engage with one another (Wong, 2017; Shin & Hampton, 2021); therefore, this article posits an extension of just world theory as a foundation for a theoretical framework to encourage new research within information systems to examine the reactions and behaviors of observers when they encounter victims on social media and how observers’ responses contribute to social media inclusion and exclusion.

This article explores social inclusion and exclusion at the confluence of a social phenomenon (victim blaming), a theory (just world theory), and an information technology (IT) artifact (social media) among a specific type of actor (observers). We develop a theoretical framework to stimulate new research on social media inclusion and exclusion behaviors by considering the integration of just world theory and social media functionalities. This research connects the concepts of social media inclusion and exclusion to the rich research tradition related to just world theory. Using this theoretical framework, we identify opportunities for new research initiatives and theorizing that investigate important phenomena regarding the role of social media in promoting social inclusion and exclusion. Our theoretical framework incorporates existing knowledge related to information systems, just world theory, social media functionalities, and social inclusion and exclusion.

This research offers several contributions to social inclusion and information systems research. First, we introduce victim blaming as a social inclusion issue within the information systems literature and provide a theoretical lens to examine the phenomenon in a social media context. Second, we integrate just world theory and social media functionalities to offer a means for scholars to consider why observers may respond differently in online settings and platforms compared to offline contexts. Third, we provide a theory-driven conceptualization of the factors and processes that can encourage individuals to engage in social media inclusion and exclusion, which provides insight into a complex social phenomenon and a social media artifact. This theory-driven conceptualization explicates the complex social phenomena of inclusion and exclusion and the IT artifact of social media by providing theoretical insight and a sociotechnical perspective of social media inclusion and exclusion. By theorizing the problem (Majchrzak et al., 2016) of social media inclusion and exclusion, our theoretical framework provides an anchor for future research on inclusion and exclusion in information systems. As we theorize the problem, we offer potential research questions related to the study of social media inclusion and exclusion.

This paper is structured as follows. We first provide background on the concepts of social inclusion and exclusion and define the terms social media inclusion and social media exclusion. We also provide an overview of the major tenets and concepts within just world theory that relate to this article. Then, we explain the role of bright and dark functionalities of social media in the context of just world beliefs and social media inclusion and exclusion. In the next sections, we present a theoretical framework of social media inclusion and exclusion and a research agenda to further investigate the phenomenon. Finally, we identify the limitations of our theoretical framework and delineate the contributions of this research.

## 2 Social Inclusion and Social Exclusion

The United Nations defines social inclusion as “the process of improving the terms of participation in society for people who are disadvantaged on the basis of age, sex, disability, race, ethnicity, origin, religion, or economic or other status, through enhanced opportunities, access to resources, voice and respect for rights” (2016, p. 20). Simply stated, social inclusion is “the ability to participate fully in one’s social world” (Bailey et al., 2020, p. 1). Individuals who experience social inclusion have the ability to access a range of social institutions to support their needs, such as employment, healthcare, education, government, and social support, among others.

In contrast to social inclusion, social exclusion is “a state in which individuals are unable to participate fully in economic, social, political and cultural life, as well as the process leading to and sustaining such as state” (United Nations, 2016, p. 18). Experiencing social exclusion is involuntary, and those experiencing social exclusion may have economic, health, educational, or other disadvantages that inhibit their ability to fully participate in their social world (Gore &

Figueiredo, 1997). Individuals also experience social exclusion when they cannot voice concerns, engage with one another, or expect to be treated with dignity and respect (United Nations, 2016).

Socially included individuals experience many hidden benefits. For instance, Klanfer, a French social commentator, explained that society assumes that those who are excluded from society are personally irresponsible, while those who are included within society are viewed as having higher levels of personal responsibility (Allman, 2013). Gillies (2005, p. 12) states that “societies have a tendency to normalize the sins of the included while penalizing the sins of the excluded.” Thus, socially excluded individuals do not receive the same benefit of the doubt as individuals who are socially included.

Complicating the study and consideration of social inclusion and exclusion is that individuals may experience both social inclusion and exclusion simultaneously (Jackson, 1999). Social exclusion is considered multidimensional because there are many ways in which a person may be excluded from participating in society (Rodgers, 1995). As information systems have become more pervasive in all elements of society (e.g., economic, health, government, political, and social, among others), new opportunities are arising to consider how specific information systems contribute to social inclusion and exclusion in today’s digital world (Bailey et al., 2020).

## 2.1 Social Media’s Impact on Social Inclusion and Social Exclusion

Many individuals choose to participate in society by using social media. Social media are defined as “computer-based tools (such as websites and apps) that enable people to create and share content with other people and/or participate in a community” (McKenna et al., 2017, p. 88). Since social media platforms enable individuals to connect with other individuals and groups, the networks established and maintained via social media have elements that are different from other types of social networks or interactions (Kane et al., 2014). Many social media interactions do not have offline equivalents, such as friend requests, moderators who can alter or censor communication, or the ability to observe how individuals respond or do not respond to social media posts (Sobczak, 2017). Theories developed or studied in offline contexts may apply differently in a social media setting because of the differences in the types of interactions that occur offline versus online (Preece & Maloney-Krichmar, 2005).

Social media have a range of functionalities that can be a benefit for some users and yet a source of harm to others. Kietzmann et al. (2011) created a framework to explain the functional building blocks of social media that organizations can leverage to engage with their customers. As criticisms and concerns about social media developed, Baccarella et al. (2018) altered the original framework to identify how the same social media functionalities that provide organizational benefits can harm others. These social media functionalities identify technical characteristics of social media as an IT artifact to explain how functionalities can enable benefits or create harm to users. Table 1 provides the definitions, positive implications, and negative implications of the seven social media functionalities identified by Kietzmann et al. (2011) and Baccarella et al. (2018).

To elaborate on how social media functionalities offer help or harm to individuals, we offer two initial examples. First, the social media functionality of conversations <sup>3</sup> enables users to engage and interact with others. Potential harm occurs, however, when individuals become part of conversations that include misinformation, disinformation, and trolling behaviors. In another example, as individuals share more about themselves with others, they may choose to reveal their personal identity to gain a sense of belonging or acceptance. Yet harm may occur both online and offline if others exploit a person’s online social media identity to gain access to information for personal gain.

Social media functionalities enable this information technology to be both helpful and harmful to individuals, rather than being just one or the other (Baccarella et al. 2018). It is worth noting that the differences in social media functionalities and their use across platforms may affect how individuals engage with others via social media. For instance, an analysis of comments on news stories related to sexual assault victims within social media forums found that there were higher levels of incivility on some platforms than others (Stevens et al., 2021). Therefore, considering social media functionalities offers one way for scholars to examine how and why there are differences in interactions across social media platforms.

Many scholars have focused on the negative outcomes associated with social media, such as social media exclusion. According to Sobczak (2017), social media exclusion occurs when individuals are impeded from accessing information on social media. This definition of social media exclusion needs further expansion since there are additional tactics individuals can use to exclude others within social media networks, such as limiting one’s access to people or groups (Timeo et al., 2020).

Some individuals limit others’ participation on social media when they degrade, shame, or give others a limited ability to voice their concerns. Therefore, we define social media exclusion as behaviors that prevent an individual’s or group’s access to information, people, or groups and limit one’s dignity within a social media environment. Based on our definition, the observer engaged in social media exclusion by blaming Mr. Pak for being vandalized and encouraging him to “go back to China.” The poster is sharing these statements on social media to negatively affect Mr. Pak’s reputation and demonstrate that Mr. Pak is not accepted within the community. While individuals or groups may engage in many different behaviors to promote social media exclusion toward another individual or group, we focus on three specific behaviors previously studied in online contexts: ostracism, cyberbullying, and stigmatization.

## 2.2 Social Media Exclusion Behaviors

Ostracism is being ignored or excluded (Williams, 2007b) or receiving negative attention, often without explanation (Williams, 2007a). Individuals who are ostracized on social media experience negative effects on their sense of belonging, self-esteem, meaningful existence, and well-being (Schneider et al., 2017). <sup>4</sup> Ostracism in a social media context not only denies an individual access to people, groups, or information, but it also limits the dignity of an individual. Research has also shown that observers who watch someone being ostracized are more likely to dehumanize the victim (Park & Park, 2015). Specific acts of ostracism can vary by social media platform (Vorderer & Schneider, 2017) and can include unfriending an individual, removing a person from a group, or ignoring posts or messages. When a group or person with whom an individual has a relationship ignores or dismisses a person on social media, observers often know the identity of the ostracized victim in an online context. As observers watch or engage in ostracizing a victim, the victim’s reputation can be negatively affected.

Cyberbullying refers to aggressive behaviors toward a vulnerable or weaker target that occur online repeatedly by an individual or group (Chan et al., 2019). This type of behavior can limit the dignity of an individual and will often coincide with restricting one’s access to information, individuals, or groups. While cyberbullying among adolescents has been frequently studied (e.g., Casas et al., 2013; Fenaughty & Harré, 2013), some adults engage in this behavior as well (e.g., Chan et al., 2019; Lowry et al., 2016). Given that cyberbullying is an exclusionary behavior that occurs online, research has already identified several social media functionalities that affect cyberbullying behaviors. When individuals are not required to disclose their identity on social media, this can influence their likelihood of engaging in cyberbullying behaviors (Chan et al., 2019; Lowry et al., 2016). The types of relationships between those engaging in cyberbullying behavior and the target can affect the level of distress experienced by the target (Fenaughty & Harré, 2013). As individuals make their presence known on social media to larger numbers of people, it increases their potential of becoming a target (Chan et al., 2019).

Stigmatization is the attribution of an undesirable or discrediting label within social settings that negatively affects how an individual perceives themselves or how others perceive the stigmatized individual (Boudewyns et al., 2015; Goffman, 1963). Stigmatization can be overt or subtle toward the stigmatized person (Bos et al., 2013), and stigmatization may be internalized through self-blame or shame or may result in negative social reactions from others (Kennedy & Prock, 2018). The act of stigmatization constricts an individual’s dignity and can result in observers engaging in additional negative behaviors, such as ostracism and cyberbullying. A critical discourse analysis of internet memes shared via social media describes the stigmatization associated with poverty or the need to rely on welfare (Dobson & Knezevic, 2017); those who disclose their poverty status via social media may find their reputation negatively affected. Those with health conditions that are considered stigmatizing (e.g., sexually transmitted diseases) are less likely to engage in conversations with others about the topic on social media (Boudewyns et al., 2015).

When observers engage in ostracism, cyberbullying, stigmatization, or other exclusionary behaviors via social media exclusion, their victims can have detrimental short-term and long-term effects. For example, individuals who perceive that they are being ignored or excluded via social media through fewer likes or comments on social media posts experience higher negative emotions (Timeo et al., 2020). Adolescents, who often have a strong need to belong, can feel ignored and excluded on social media and experience negative impacts on their emotional wellbeing (Timeo et al., 2020). Scholars have found that individuals who believe others are ignoring or excluding them via social media experience negative attitudes about the subject matter of the post (Vinuales & Thomas, 2021). Social media not only enables individuals to engage in social media exclusion but also facilitates observation of the social media exclusion by others, which can lead to new observers dehumanizing and devaluing the experiences of victims (Park & Park, 2015).

## 2.3 Social Media Inclusion Behaviors

In contrast to social media exclusion, some individuals engage in social media inclusion behaviors online. We define social media inclusion as behaviors that enable individuals or groups to access information, people, or groups and allow one to maintain dignity in a social media environment. Individuals who experience social media inclusion behaviors may find social media to be a platform that offers support during difficult times, a community of like-minded individuals, and a means to participate fully in their social world. There are a range of behaviors that are consistent with social media inclusion, and we focus on three forms of social support offered on social media: tangible aid, companionship, and emotional support (Lu & Hampton, 2017). Observers may engage in one or all of these social media inclusion behaviors after observing a victim sharing a traumatizing event on social media.

Tangible aid is the offering of financial assistance, care, or other material resources to an individual during a time of need (Cohen & McKay, 1984; Lu & Hampton, 2017). In the opening vignette, one of the observers shares their willingness to be present by posting public support (online) and patronizing Mr. Pak’s restaurant (offline) as a form of tangible aid. Observers could offer tangible aid by posting a link on social media to a crowdfunding site to provide funds for Mr. Pak to repair his restaurant. In another example of tangible aid, a female United States Marine shared her story about a lack of justice in a sexual assault case wherein a fellow Marine distributed images of her without consent (Cooper & Steinhauer, 2021). Her post went viral on social media as observers shared her story among groups along with posts on Twitter that mentioned and identified top U.S. Department of Defense officials to provide wider visibility of the victim’s plight. After the United States Secretary of Defense viewed the post, the United States Marines issued a statement on Twitter, publicly indicating they would meet with the victim and senior members of her command offline. In addition, social media promoted conversations in which prosecutors who observed the posts offered to share information, resources, and strategies to support the victim.

Another form of social media inclusion behavior is companionship, which is the ability to participate in activities with others (Lu & Hampton, 2017). The high level of presence made possible through social media enables observers to engage in social support activities such as companionship. Social media enables individuals to express their willingness to engage in relationships with people or groups online, and these interactions can encourage offline interactions as well. A study of Chinese migrants in Singapore revealed that migrants sought companionship and other forms of social support online to complement offline support (Chen & Choi, 2011). When observers offer their companionship to a victim, they are engaging in social media inclusion behaviors that offer access to individuals and groups and maintain and promote dignity toward the victim.

Observers offer emotional support by expressing caring, concern, feedback, and understanding (Cutrona, 1990; Lu & Hampton, 2017) for victims. Social media provides an opportunity for those who may be traditionally stigmatized (e.g., HIV/AIDS patients) to create or join groups of similar others to promote and encourage emotional support among participants (Chen & Shi, 2015) through relationships and conversations. Sexual assault survivors utilize social media platforms, such as Reddit and Twitter, and hashtags (i.e., #MeToo, #NotOkay) to access and offer emotional support (Hosterman et al., 2018) through groups. By disclosing traumatic experiences, many sexual assault survivors receive encouragement, advice, and understanding (Hosterman et al., 2018). Although receiving emotional support is critical, observers who share or provide emotional support online also experience benefits. Cancer patients sending supportive messages to other cancer patients online improved their own coping strategies (Namkoong et al., 2013). As such, social media provides a medium for emotional support and emotional expression, thereby allowing observers to demonstrate their desire for the victim to maintain their dignity. Since each observer may have a different reaction to a victim’s experience, this study recognizes that a victim may encounter both social inclusion and exclusion simultaneously (Jackson, 1999). After a victim posts a message online, one person or a group of observers on social media may offer emotional support for the victim, while another person or group of observers may blame the victim through stigmatization. In the opening vignette, observers engaged in both social media inclusion behaviors and social media exclusion behaviors toward Mr. Pak. Some observers offered emotional support and tangible aid through their sharing, identity, and presence after reading his post on social media, while other observers engaged in stigmatization through sharing. The same social media functionalities used to engage in supportive and social media inclusion behaviors toward victims can also support victim blaming and social media exclusion through stigmatization. One approach to understanding when and why observers may choose to engage in victim support or victim blaming is through the lens of just world theory. In the next section, we discuss the major tenets of just world theory and its insight into victim blaming.

## 3 Theoretical Lens: Just World Theory

Just world theory, asserts that “individuals have a need to believe that they live in a world where people generally get what they deserve” (Lerner & Miller, 1978, p. 1030). This theory, sometimes referred to as belief in a just world (BJW) or the just world hypothesis, explains how individuals rationalize the fates of themselves or others by making judgments regarding the consequences of an event. Although many individuals acknowledge that life events are not always fair, having a sense of general fairness in the world enables individuals to maintain a level of perceived control over their outcomes (Lerner, 1980). This perception permits individuals to have an orderly view of the world that allows individuals to pursue long-term goals. Individuals with stronger beliefs that the world is generally fair or just toward them and others tend to better cope with situations that evoke anger (Dalbert, 2002) and more easily forgive others and themselves (Strelan, 2007) than those who believe the world is not fair.

Individuals have an innate need to believe that the world is fair in order to cope with and interpret life events (Lerner, 1980), but the strength of one’s belief in a just world can vary. This variation in belief in a just world can be affected by one’s religious beliefs (Rubin & Peplau, 1975), openness to experience (Jost & Hunyady, 2005), and internal locus of control (Hafer & Sutton, 2016), among other correlates. Personal belief in a just world is an individual’s perception that the world is generally fair toward them (Dalbert, 1999), while general belief in a just world is an individual’s perception that the world is generally fair toward others (Lipkus, 1991).

Events can threaten one’s personal or general belief in a just world, which occurs when an individual becomes aware of a circumstance that is difficult to reconcile with the idea that people tend to get what they deserve. When an individual’s belief in a just world is threatened, particularly for someone with a strong individual or general belief in a just world, it can create a strong need to restore the perception of fairness in the world (Lerner & Miller, 1978; Lerner, 1980). The innocence or non-innocence of the victim, the length and degree of suffering, and the ability to exact retribution on a victim’s perpetrator can all threaten one’s personal or general belief in a just world. Individuals tend to view innocent victims more positively than non-innocent victims, and conversely, individuals perceive non-innocent victims more negatively than innocent victims (Correia & Vala, 2003). The threat to one’s belief in a just world can be lessened if it is possible to restore justice to the victim by punishing the attacker (Dawtry et al., 2020) or engaging in an action to provide support for the victim (Kenrick et al., 1976). If justice cannot be restored to a victim, an observer may have a strong reaction to this disruption of one’s perception of a just world by engaging in strong emotions toward the victim, such as fury, outrage, and indignation (Lerner, 1980; Lerner & Miller, 1978). As such, an individual who observes an injustice and cannot restore justice may respond with exclusive behaviors.

The conceptual model, illustrated in Figure 1, offers one approach to understanding just world theory. Individuals have different strengths of beliefs about whether the world is generally fair toward them or others that are developed based on their prior experiences, personality traits, and other beliefs and character traits. When an individual observes a triggering event, they become an observer, which can threaten their general or personal belief in a just world. Then, the observer responds through beliefs or actions to restore their perception of fairness in the world. If someone perceived as being innocent and not deserving of suffering experiences an injustice, this can violate an observer’s natural sense of order that the world is just and fair. Observers who encounter an innocent, suffering victim realize that they may too become a victim of injustice (Lerner, 1998). When an observer’s just world beliefs are challenged, they will seek to rectify the situation by intervening to restore justice. If observers are unable to restore justice to the victim, then they will respond cognitively by aligning the situation with their belief in a just world (Dalbert, 2002). To illustrate this cognitive response to injustice, consider the following vignette.

Sofia recently ended her relationship with her longtime boyfriend, James. Immediately after Sofia had a difficult conversation with James to break off the relationship, Sofia changed her relationship status to “single” on social media and messaged three of her closest friends privately about the conversation.

Later that evening, Sofia sees messages on social media from an unknown person that includes a link tagging her. Sofia clicks on the link, which begins to play a video. It doesn’t take long for her to realize that the video is an intimate, explicit video of her and James, which is now available online. While Sofia’s face and body are clearly visible to the camera, James’ identity is obscured through camera angles and digital editing.

Sofia reads some comments on the social media post that tagged her and finds comments, such as:

“She was awful to her ex! Serves her right that someone would post this video.”

“What do you expect when you record everything you do?”

Sofia notices some private messages from a couple of friends, who share:

“Are you okay? I doubt you wanted this moment shared with the world.”

“I have a friend who works at the local police department. Do you want me to see if you can get a restraining order or file a report?”

Sofia immediately feels a mixture of emotions. She feels anger as she realizes this private moment is now public. Sofia also feels betrayed. James never asked her if he could record (or share) their most private, intimate moments.

When people view the post and video of Sofia, they become an observer of a potentially triggering event that could threaten their personal or general beliefs in a just world. Each observer’s response to the triggering event is affected by their just world beliefs. Just world theory suggests that observers perceiving Sofia as innocent (i.e., she did not post the video, she did not know the video was recorded) would experience discomfort because these individuals realize that they could experience a similar situation in the future (i.e., victim of dissemination of non-consensual explicit images). Some observers may find reasons to blame the victim for their plight (Dalbert, 2013), which is known as the derogation effect (Dalbert, 2013; Furnham, 2003; Lerner, 1998). In the vignette, some observers blamed, or derogated, Sofia for the non-consensual video being posted, insisting that she is responsible for the private moment being shared publicly because she must have consented to being filmed. The derogation effect is a response that is triggered when an observer relates to the victim’s circumstances (i.e., believes the situation could happen to them) and the observer cannot restore justice to the victim (Lerner, 1980; Lerner & Simmons, 1966). When the derogation effect occurs, the observer belittles or diminishes the victim to reclaim a sense of justice in the world (Hafer, 2000; Harber et al., 2015; Lerner & Simmons, 1966).

Derogation effects, or victim blaming, is a response that becomes prominent when observers encounter innocent victims who experience continuous or prolonged suffering after a harmful event (Hafer, 2000), when the perpetrator of harm has not been brought to justice (Hafer, 2000), or when the observer cannot restore justice to the victim (van den Bos & Maas, 2009).

Researchers have used just world theory to explain the derogation effect in response to AIDS patients (Kogut, 2011), impoverished populations (Moreno‐Jiménez, 2016), and rape victims (Foley & Pigott, 2000), all of whom are vulnerable to social exclusion. Unfortunately, when victims are subjected to derogation effects, victims suffer from the original victimizing event and also from revictimization if they are blamed for their plight (Janoff-Bulman et al., 1985).

Victim blaming also removes the attention of society from the perpetrators or culprits of victimization and other social ills and focuses attention on the victim, further enhancing power differentials in society (Stubbs-Richardson et al., 2018). When individuals observe victims who are blamed for their experiences (e.g., a rape victim is blamed due to clothing, presence at an event, or other behavior), an observer who is later victimized in a similar manner may be dissuaded from seeking social services or support or disclosing a crime to law enforcement out of fear that they will be blamed as well (Ahrens, 2006; Barr & Murphy, 2020). Thus, victim blaming undermines new victims’ ability to seek and obtain support if they experience a victimizing event.

Although one’s belief in a just world is associated with negative outcomes (i.e., the derogation effect), their belief in a just world shields them from the reality of harsh, unjust life circumstances (Strelan, 2007). Thus, belief in a just world is not a negative belief system, but deep-rooted beliefs that individuals have a strong need to protect. Individuals will protect their just world beliefs when threatened with injustice as a protection mechanism against the idea they could experience the same fate (Furnham, 2003; Lerner & Miller, 1978) and respond by blaming or derogating the victim. Outside of social psychology, just world theory has been applied in political science (Bénabou & Tirole, 2006; Moreno‐Jiménez, 2016), criminal justice (Dalbert & Filke, 2007), management (Johnston et al., 2016), and marketing (White et al., 2012; Wilson & Darke, 2012) research. However, this lens is underutilized in information systems research, even though it can help explain why individuals engage in inclusive or exclusive behaviors on social media.

## 4 Theoretical Framework for Social Media Inclusion and Exclusion Research

Just world theory offers a lens to consider how and why observers engage in social media inclusion and social media exclusion online. In this section, we present a theoretical framework of social media inclusion and exclusion that incorporates relevant concepts from just world theory, bright and dark social media functionalities, and social inclusion and exclusion to identify four distinct research areas related to social media inclusion and exclusion. The theoretical framework considers how social media functionalities influence or are influenced by the just world beliefs of social media users, triggering events observed online by users, observers’ evaluative responses to triggering events, and observers’ social media inclusion and exclusion behaviors (shown in Figure 2).

In addition to theorizing about the sociotechnical phenomenon of social media inclusion and exclusion, this framework also informs just world theory, which has been widely studied in offline contexts (Hafer & Bégue, 2005; Lerner & Miller, 1978) and is only just beginning to be studied in a social media context (e.g., Hollonquest, 2014; Hou et al., 2017; Shin & Hampton, 2021). Much of the research examining just world theory manipulates contextual factors related to the triggering events that threaten one’s belief in a just world, such as the degree of innocence of the victim (Lerner & Miller, 1978), the degree or length of suffering (Correia & Vala, 2003), if a victim’s attacker was brought to justice (Dawtry et al., 2020), and the ability to restore justice to a victim (Hafer & Bégue, 2005). Other research has considered whether beliefs in a just world can be primed or influenced through interventions to encourage more emotional or rational responses to a scenario (van den Bos & Maas, 2009).

Additional research is needed to examine the boundary conditions of just world theory in a social media context to understand and explain how and why observers respond to victims who share their experiences. Studies indicate that victim blaming is facilitated by social media through actions such as retweeting (Hou et al., 2017; Stubbs-Richardson et al., 2018), sharing photos or videos of victimizations online, and allowing comments from individuals from within and outside the victim’s social network (Wellman et al., 2017). Although research shows that social media facilitates victim blaming, there is promising research indicating that social media also facilitates victim support (Shin & Hampton, 2021; Stubbs-Richardson et al., 2018). Our theoretical framework of social media inclusion and exclusion and just world theory provides an anchor to examine what, why, and how social media platforms are used to include or exclude individuals or groups. The goal is to inspire future research that explores the nuance of beliefs, triggering events, and responses to injustice that inform observers’ engagement in social media inclusion and exclusion behaviors.

We developed our theoretical framework by examining the three streams of research discussed in section three: social media functionalities, social media inclusion and exclusion, and just world theory. We then utilized the conceptual model of just world theory (see Figure 1) to identify areas of overlap among social media functionalities and social media inclusion and exclusion. To reduce the complexity of this exercise, we focused our efforts on the beliefs and responses of observers. The areas of overlap provide the building blocks or basis for researchers to examine areas of interaction between a complex social phenomenon and information technology. The result is the identification of four distinct areas of research related to how observers react and respond when seeing a post about a victimizing event on social media.

The goal of this framework is to provide a way of thinking about whether observers engage in social media inclusion and exclusion behaviors at a grand level of abstraction. Consistent with the tradition of other theoretical frameworks, such as Baird and Maruping (2021), our theoretical framework does not want to limit future research to only one theory, method, or perspective. The theoretical framework is specific enough to stimulate research that considers just world theory and social media inclusion and exclusion within the context of a specific IT artifact (social media) and actor (observers). However, the theoretical framework is also general enough for scholars to still leverage the framework while altering various components, such as considering social media affordances (rather than functionalities) or the impact of social media inclusion and exclusion on victims (rather than observers). In this section, we expound on four research areas depicted in our model and identify specific research questions to inform future inquiry. We also offer more specific guidance for researchers using the theoretical framework.

## 4.1 Research Area 1: Social Media Functionalities and Just World Beliefs

Limited research has examined how individuals’ just world beliefs are formed and shaped over time by one’s experiences and events. Research has found that certain personal attributes, such as socioeconomic status, can influence one’s personal and general just world beliefs (Hou et al., 2017). In offline contexts, studies have examined whether external stimuli can affect one’s just world beliefs. For instance, a crosscultural study found that those watching fictional stories on television tend to have stronger just world beliefs (Appel, 2008). Another study explored how to prime or encourage individuals to use intuitive or logical mindsets when evaluating a victimizing experience that may threaten their just world beliefs (van den Bos & Maas, 2009). Social media functionalities also have the potential to amplify, temper, or alter an individual’s personal or general just world beliefs (Shin & Hampton, 2021).

On average, users spend over two hours on social media per day (Armstrong, 2021). Users engage with social media to maintain and create relationships, find affinity groups, share experiences, engage in conversations, and obtain news, among other reasons. As individuals engage in these activities, they can read or respond to large numbers of social media posts. If some posts include victims sharing their experiences, it is unknown how these posts may be internalized or how they might affect one’s beliefs in a just world over time (Shin & Hampton, 2021). Viewing social media posts about victimizing experiences could cause individuals to revisit their perceptions of the world being just; if someone observes that others suffer for no known reason, then they may be forced to acknowledge that a similar fate may await them (Lerner & Miller, 1978). Online platforms and an individual’s interaction methods affect their awareness of desirable and undesirable life events, which in turn affects one’s belief in a just world (Shin & Hampton, 2021).

Researchers can explore a range of questions, such as whether individual social media users have similar just world beliefs as members of groups in which a user might be affiliated on social media. Moreover, exposure to certain conversations or news feeds may expose individuals to general just world beliefs shared online, which may or may not impact their personal just world beliefs. Just world beliefs may also propagate online based on a user’s reputation on social media. For instance, it is unknown whether celebrities, with millions of followers, influence other users’ just world beliefs. Given the limited research regarding how just world beliefs are formed or influenced in a social media context, it is unknown whether just world beliefs are impacted by the use of presence on social media. ## 4.2 Research Area 2: Social Media Functionalities and Triggering Events

After a social media user views a post about a victimizing event, they become an observer. The social media post can trigger the observer to question or reevaluate their just world beliefs. Prior research on just world theory typically triggers an observer to question their just world beliefs by presenting a person with an experimental scenario (Correia & Vala, 2003), a news story (Hafer, 2000), or a real-life, offline, scenario (Regan, 1971) that identifies a victim and victimizing event. However, increasing research is exploring how social media posts can evoke a similar threat to one’s just world beliefs (Hou et al., 2017; Shin & Hampton, 2021; Stevens et al., 2021). Regardless of how one learns about a victimizing event, the observer makes cognitive evaluations about the victim and the victimizing event that could introduce threats to the observer’s just world beliefs. This section explains the need for future research to examine how social media functionalities influence how observers perceive triggering events posted online.

In the opening vignette when Mr. Pak describes the vandalism of his store on social media, observers’ just world beliefs could be threatened depending on their perceptions of the victim and the event. When cognitively evaluating the situation, observers are likely to have access to more information from social media than from offline contexts. Knowing the identity of the victim can change how observers perceive the victim or the event (Kogut, 2011), and social media functionalities are likely to reveal the identity of the victim (i.e., Mr. Pak). Observers who know Mr. Pak or have relationships with others who know him may perceive the social media post (i.e., triggering event) differently than they would if the innocent victim were a stranger.

An observer may also consider the victim’s reputation when viewing the social media post and making a cognitive evaluation about the victim and victimizing event. Observers may perceive a victim’s reputation on social media differently based on the number of people following the victim’s social media account, the number of reposts of the victimization, responses by other observers on social media, or the identity of other observers responding to the victim’s post on social media. Furthermore, if Mr. Pak belongs to a group known by the observer or has relationships with others known by the observer, the observer may feel an increased sense of connection to the victim, which may affect how the triggering event affects the observer’s belief in a just world. In contrast, if the observer has a negative opinion about the reputation, groups, or relationships of the victim that are identifiable via social media, the information shared through social media functionalities may affect if and how the observer is triggered when learning about a victimizing event.

Studies in offline contexts have examined how observers perceive an individual who expresses belief in an unjust world during an interview or in a writing sample. In these studies, observers tend to view individuals who suggest that the world is unfair more negatively (Alves et al., 2015; Alves et al., 2019). There are subgroups of people who experience an unjust world because of systemic racism and biases; however, if these individuals express these beliefs about an unjust world to others, observers are more likely to view these individuals as less desirable in a social context (Alves et al., 2015). This leads to additional questions about how observers perceive victims who have shared their unjust world beliefs on social media. Observers who have stronger beliefs in a just world are more likely to have negative perceptions of the victim’s reputation. Social media allows negative expressions about a just world to be observed by larger numbers of people who may belong to social and demographic groups that have largely experienced a just world. As a result, not only is it possible for observers to have negative perceptions of the reputation of a victim who perceives the world to be unjust, but it may also be possible that observers may transfer the negative perceptions of the victim’s reputation to the victim’s groups and relationships. ## 4.3 Research Area 3: Social Media Functionalities and Observers’ Evaluation of Triggering Events

Just world theory suggests that observers with stronger beliefs in a just world attempt to reconcile unexplainable events that happen to victims with one of two reactions: restoration of justice (victim support) or derogation of the victim (victim blaming) (Lerner & Miller, 1978). If an observer cannot restore justice to a victim by offering support, they may engage in victim blaming, which enables them to reconcile what injustice or harm they have observed with their just world beliefs. Victim blaming or victim support is a cognitive, evaluative response that may or may not be explicitly acknowledged or proclaimed by the observer.

Social media functionalities may influence observers cognitive evaluation of a triggering event. Factors, such as how a victim shares their experience on social media, the victim’s presence on social media before or after the event, or how the victim or others engage in conversations about the victimization could affect an observers’ propensity to blame the victim or offer support. Consider the vignette about Sofia. Since Sofia’s victimization (i.e., non-consensual dissemination of intimate images) was shared online by others, observers may be more likely to want to restore justice to her since

she may appear to be an innocent victim. However, public conversations about Sofia on social media may signal to some observers that she often recorded or shared explicit videos. Prior research has demonstrated that social media comments have the potential to affect one’s attitudes about a subject, particularly when the observer identifies with those commenting (Walther et al., 2010). Depending on how a victim’s experience is conveyed online, observers may perceive the victim as being responsible (or not) for their victimization. <sup>5</sup> In offline contexts, victims whose identities are known and who are perceived as having some level of responsibility for their plight are less likely to receive support (i.e., a desire to restore justice) from observers (Kogut, 2011). Furthermore, individuals who identify as being a member of a disenfranchised, or stigmatized, group may be perceived differently than those who are not part of these groups. Observers may feel anger about a victimizing event (and even toward the victim), but not all will engage in negative actions that promote social media exclusion (Hou et al., 2017). Research has questioned how social media posts can cultivate and trigger individuals’ perceptions of a just world (Shin & Hampton, 2021). ## 4.4 Research Area 4: Social Media Functionalities and Engaging in Social Media Inclusion and Exclusion

Depending on how the observer evaluates the triggering event (i.e., feelings of victim support or victim blaming), the observer may engage in social media inclusion or social media exclusion behaviors toward the victim. In the context of social media, an observer may or may not post a comment that either supports or blames a victim (or group) experiencing harm (Hou et al., 2017). We posit that social media functionality influences how observers engage in social media inclusion behaviors and social media exclusion behaviors.

One way for an observer to reduce the threat to their belief in a just world is to restore justice to a victim (Lerner, 1980), which can be performed through a range of behaviors that are consistent with social inclusion. Experimental studies examining just world theory have studied whether observers will donate to suffering individuals or groups (Kogut, 2011; Regan, 1971), otherwise known as tangible aid, which is an inclusive behavior that demonstrates support for a victim. Social media provides other alternatives for individuals to restore justice with positive, inclusive actions toward the victim, such as defending the victim (e.g., Stubbs-Richardson et al., 2018) and providing companionship or social support (Shin & Hampton, 2021). Several studies have noted that groups of observers will provide tangible aid offline after a victim shares their needs online via social media (Li et al., 2019).

As victims share their victimization on social media, some observers demonstrate their victim support through actions such as posts, likes, and comments. The vignettes demonstrate that observers may engage in inclusion behaviors by offering their presence and identity publicly (as in the case of Mr. Pak) or privately (as in the case of Sofia). On many social media platforms, the users’ presence is indicated online by showing one’s status (e.g., Facebook, Instagram, and LinkedIn indicate users who are currently active online) or by publicly or privately sharing support for a victim. The social presence of victims and observers may influence whether other observers engage in behaviors associated with social media inclusion toward victims. The victim’s reputation may improve or hinder the type and amount of support provided by observers. A potentially interesting area of inquiry would be to consider how social media allows observers to engage in social media inclusion behaviors that are not possible offline. For example, likes, comments, and shares are facilitated at a much larger scale by social media. However, early research on just world theory found that people tend to stop caring about a victim after their guilt has been absolved through some action (Mills & Egger, 1972). It is unknown if clicking a “like” button, reposting a message, or other forms of public support on social media may limit how an observer engages in social media inclusion behaviors.

Alternatively, when observers blame victims for their plight, this leads to revictimization (Correia & Vala, 2003) and is associated with social media exclusion. The amount to which observers believe in a just world affects the likelihood of liking or reposting hostile comments about an event (Hou et al., 2017). Observers may engage in one or more social exclusion behaviors toward a victim, such as ostracism, cyberbullying, or stigmatization. Observers engage in punitive ostracism when a victim is shunned or ignored because the observer perceives that the victim has done something wrong and deserves consequences (Rudert & Greifeneder, 2019). As observers identify targets of ostracism online, observers perceive the victim more negatively (Park & Park, 2015). Social media enables individuals to witness the lack of presence of observers in supporting a victim, which can be a form of ostracism that occurs via social media. If a group of observers ignores or dismisses a person who is victimized, they are further victimizing and excluding the victim who is sharing their experience on social media.

Ostracism can create a further reinforcing cycle of social exclusion through additional ostracism or more aggressive behaviors, such as bullying (Bos et al., 2013). The reputation of the observers engaging in cyberbullying may lead to other observers engaging in additional cyberbullying or stigmatization of the victim. As observers share their stigmatization of the victim via social media, others may engage in cyberbullying or ostracism. Furthermore, when observers engage in social media exclusion behaviors, other observers can view the original victimization and revictimization that is shared online, which can lead to further engagement of social media exclusion behaviors by some observers. It is worth noting that social exclusion behaviors may occur offline or online in that observers’ actions may negatively affect the victim’s ability to fully participate in society. ## 4.5 Research Approach for Social Media Inclusion and Exclusion

Considering just world theory in the context of social media inclusion and exclusion provides new research opportunities. Although we use a theoretical lens of just world theory and bright and dark social media functionalities to inform our research questions, we encourage researchers to utilize a wide variety of information systems and social theories to explore social media inclusion and exclusion. In our theoretical framework, we used social media functionalities established in prior literature. However, the use of other theories such as social capital theory (Huang et al., 2019), information systems affordances (Leonardi & Vaast, 2017; Majchrzak et al., 2013), or CARE theory (Leidner & Tona, 2021) would also provide additional insights related to the phenomenon of social media inclusion and exclusion. Information systems affordances might be particularly useful as a theoretical lens to identify how technology is appropriated for both positive (social media inclusion) and negative (social media exclusion) purposes. CARE theory is also a beneficial lens through which to view social inclusion and exclusion research because it focuses on the impact of information systems on human dignity, a core concept in inclusion and exclusion. In addition, our theoretical framework and research agenda do not consider how victims respond to their own plight and how this affects their posts on social media. Therefore, the framework could be altered and extended to consider the role of other actors related to social media inclusion and exclusion, such as victims.

Although prior research of just world theory relies heavily on experiments, other research methods including qualitative methods, secondary data analysis, social media analysis, and design science used within information systems could contribute additional understanding of social media inclusion and exclusion. Given that the IT artifact under study is social media, social networking analysis, text analysis techniques, design science, and social media analytics will provide unique insights into social inclusion and exclusion on social networking platforms. There are many opportunities within information systems to consider just world beliefs and the implications thereof in a social media context. Expanding just world theory to online contexts also expands the type of data traditionally utilized to explore just world theory (experiments and surveys) to large, secondary datasets.

## 5 Conclusion

If we want to encourage more inclusive behaviors within our society, we must acknowledge that individuals’ victimization and observers’ reactions to victims can be a source of exclusion and harm. Some information systems, such as social media platforms, can amplify the victimization of individuals within society and can promote prosocial behaviors, such as social media inclusion, or harmful behaviors, such as social media exclusion. Just world theory provides useful insights into why individuals may blame victims for the harm that has befallen them, further excluding these victims from justice, dignity, or well-being, rather than providing support and inclusion. Much of the research examining just world theory considers an offline context, and there is more to learn regarding how just world beliefs are perpetuated, extended, or exhibited in online contexts, such as social media. Social media has the potential for observers to support victims by engaging in social media inclusion behaviors, which can promote additional observers to engage in social inclusion behaviors toward victims both online and offline. In particular, we introduce victim blaming and victim support as important social inclusion and exclusion issues to study within information systems research. We provide a theoretical framework and offer a foundation to explain how and why victim blaming and victim support are important topics for understanding social inclusion in the context of technology. By integrating just world theory and considering the role of various social media functionalities, we demonstrate that theories and phenomena studied in offline contexts can be extended and applied to yield new insights in online contexts. This research also contributes to information systems and social inclusion and exclusion theory by theorizing the problem (Majchrzak et al., 2016) of social media inclusion and exclusion. We develop a theoretical framework of social media inclusion and exclusion that incorporates the theoretical underpinnings of both the social phenomenon and the technical artifact in question. We provide a holistic view of a phenomenon by utilizing a duality approach wherein we consider bright and dark social media functionalities of social media and both social media inclusion and exclusion behaviors. We also respond to calls to further social science research exploring how minority groups are blamed for being disadvantaged and victimized (Heimer, 2019).

Research investigating social media inclusion and exclusion is important since social media changes the nature and impact of social inclusion and exclusion. Social media allows for a high degree of visibility into others’ lives, creating opportunities for observers to become desensitized to others’ suffering as we try to make sense of “why bad things happen to good people.” As human beings, we innately use our beliefs in a just world to rationalize why events occur and to protect ourselves from feeling vulnerable to injustices that occur within the world. The scalability and visibility of social media have the potential to amplify these effects. Social media inclusion or exclusion behaviors create cycles of further social inclusion or exclusion, both online and offline (Shin & Hampton, 2021).

For the purpose of this article, we intentionally limited our discussion to examining how just world theory affects how observers respond and interact with victims on social media. As such, we did not consider alternative theoretical perspectives that explain how observers of victimization engage with victims. Additionally, while just world theory has been applied to consider how victims and observers respond to victimization, our theoretical framework and research agenda largely consider the opportunities for research related to social media users who observe expressions on social media about victimization and how inclusion and exclusion manifests on social media. Because we focus on the theoretical lens of just world theory, the phenomenon of victim blaming, and the IT artifact of social media, additional research could examine different theoretical lenses, related phenomena, or alternative IT artifacts to investigate social inclusion and social exclusion in the context of information systems research.

In setting the boundaries for this research, there is more to explore beyond what is discussed in this article. For example, we did not consider the types of victimization or the different ways that observers can learn about a victim’s experiences within the context of social media (Giddens & Petter, 2020). Our research does not define who is a victim other than any individual who perceives that they have experienced a traumatizing event. The examples provided in the vignettes describe individuals who are visible victims of racism and non consensual dissemination of intimate images, but there are individuals who perceive themselves as victims for more subtle or controversial reasons. For instance, one vignette focuses on Sofia’s experience via social media, but we do not discuss whether observers might have perceived James as the victim since Sofia ended their relationship. Observers’ support of James may ultimately blame and exclude Sofia. Furthermore, our theoretical framework does not consider how social media inclusion or exclusion behaviors translate to offline social inclusion or exclusion. Additionally, while some may assume that our theoretical framework implies that a person will either experience social media inclusion or social media exclusion, we recognize that the same person can experience both inclusion and exclusion during the same victimizing event (consistent with Jackson, 1999). We simplified our framework and discussion of social media inclusion and exclusion as dichotomies, but there is an opportunity to examine the interaction between social media inclusion and exclusion in future research. We encourage further research exploring the juxtaposition and interplay between social media inclusion and exclusion behaviors as well as how social media inclusion and exclusion affect offline social inclusion and exclusion. As additional research examines social media inclusion and exclusion, we look forward to the development of refined definitions, new constructs, and theories related to this phenomenon.

We want to inspire future research investigating the phenomena of victim blaming and social media inclusion and exclusion to offer insights into policies and practices that govern behavior and use on social media platforms. Ideally, sites should have user policies and features that encourage social inclusion while discouraging social exclusion. Some content producers, such as Popular Science and Reuters have removed comment sections from their websites because of the deluge of toxic comments and the increasing demand on publishers’ resources required to moderate comments (Ellis, 2015). Research on victim blaming, victim support, just world theory, and social media functionalities can have implications for organizations beyond social media platforms that seek to navigate the balancing act between protecting freedom of speech and protecting citizens from online abuse and encouraging positive, inclusive behaviors toward victims.
