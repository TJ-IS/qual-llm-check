# Persuading Chronically Ill Patients to Subscribe to Medical Services in Physician-Driven Online Health Communities

Yan Li,<sup>1</sup> Haiyun (Melody) Zou,<sup>2</sup> Juhee Kwon,<sup>3</sup> Yulin Fang<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Information School, Central University of Finance and Economics, China, yl\_yanli@cufe.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, UK, Melody.Zou@wbs.ac.uk <sup>3</sup>Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, China, juhee.kwon@cityu.edu.hk <sup>4</sup>HKU Business School, University of Hong Kong, China, ylfang@hku.hk

## Abstract

While patient-driven online health communities (OHCs) have long been a fixture of the online healthcare landscape, physician-driven OHCs are becoming increasingly popular. Drawing on the persuasive communication literature, this study takes a physician’s perspective to examine how physicians signal their professional expertise and caring demeanor through online responses and influence patient medical service subscription decisions. By analyzing textual and image data collected from a leading physician-driven OHC, we found that regarding message framing, caring messages (emotional appeals) significantly boosted subscriptions among chronically ill patients, while technical messages (rational appeals) did not. The results were found to be the opposite for source framing: authoritative reputation (rational appeals) had a significant effect but smiling images (emotional appeals) did not. The results also punctuate the importance of congruence between message and source and between text and image in both rational and emotional appeals. Our findings make significant theoretical contributions and offer guidance for physicians seeking to optimize their OHC participation to enhance chronic disease care.

Keywords: Online Health Communities, Physician-Driven OHC, Persuasive Communication, Medical Service Subscription, Emotional Appeal, Chronic Disease, Image Mining

## 1 Introduction

Shifting from patient-driven online health communities (OHCs), physician-driven OHCs that enable direct communication between physicians and patients have received increasing attention in recent years (Khurana et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020). These physician-driven OHCs empower patients by offering broader access to physician consultation and extended care choices, helping to reduce health disparities caused by geographic location (Goh et al., 2016). Receiving medical advice online also provides patients with cost-effective care, which is invaluable for those with chronic diseases suited to self-management or treatment options in non-hospital venues.

In addition to supplying free question and answer (Q&A) services, physician-driven OHCs have started to launch paid medical subscription services (e.g., weekly/monthly plan for messaging/phone-call/faceto-face consultations), catering to patients’ needs for long-term personalized consultation (Zhang & Zhang, 2024). OHC platforms that offer a range of paid subscription services present multifaceted benefits for three primary stakeholders: patients, physicians, and platform providers. Along with the convenience of consulting physicians online, chronically ill patients with self-management needs can benefit from the repeated in-depth, private, personalized cost-effective conversations with physicians offered by such online platforms. Physicians can also utilize these platforms to signal the quality of their care while gaining recognition and compensation for their efforts. These OHC offerings further serve as a sustainable business model, providing platform providers with a promising and stable financing source.

Even though physician-driven OHCs are gradually becoming an important tool to supplement offline medical care, research from the physician’s perspective—the primary healthcare service provider in OHCs—is insufficient. First, while the extant research has examined the positive outcomes of physicians’ online responses for patients—such as improvements in patient health, well-being, and satisfaction (Liu et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2015)—it has rarely investigated how such online responses can benefit physicians. For example, offering medical subscriptions on such platforms can allow physicians to signal and share their expertise, motivating and rewarding their investment of time and effort on OHC platforms (Guo et al., 2017). Moreover, offering medical subscriptions on such platforms could also benefit physicians by expanding their patient base and bolstering their reputation (Khurana et al., 2019).

Second, while it has been widely acknowledged that chronically ill patients require both informational and emotional support, few studies have explored how physicians, as opposed to peer patients (Chen et al., 2019a; Yan & Tan, 2014), can help patients meet these needs—especially their emotional needs—through online responses. With the rise of physician-driven OHCs, patients now have greater opportunities to evaluate individual physicians directly, rather than relying primarily on institutions (Lu & Wu, 2016). This can allow patients to form more holistic evaluations of physicians based on a broader range of online information, beyond traditional rational criteria such as clinical titles that had previously constrained judgments in offline settings. This offers new opportunities for physicians to signal not only their professional competence (rational) but also their caring demeanor (emotional)—i.e., their “bedside manner,” comprising qualities such as caring, trust, respect, and empathy—which patients often specifically seek when choosing a physician (Hao & Zhang, 2016; Lu & Wu, 2016). Third, the prior literature examining physicians’ online responses has generally focused on the rational aspect of physician care, using surface-level measures (e.g., the quantity or quality of text) (Khurana et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019), with less emphasis on capturing the richer content delivered by physicians’ use of both textual and image cues. However, patients are keenly interested in the verbal and nonverbal evidence provided by such online cues.

To fill this gap in the literature and advise physicians on how to demonstrate their professional competence and caring demeanor, this study aims to study the effects of rational and emotional appeals in physicians online responses (in both text and image forms) on chronically ill patients’ subscription decisions. Thus, drawing on two streams (message/source, rational/emotional appeals) from the persuasive communication literature (Johnson et al., 2018; Kotler & Keller, 2008; O'Keefe, 1990), we theorize a congruence between the message/source and rational/emotional appeals in the physician-driven OHC context. Specifically, we propose that physicians’ technical messages (domain-specific knowledge) and authoritative reputation (clinical title) serve as the message and source in rational appeals, respectively, while their caring messages (comforting language) and smiling profile image serve as the message and source in emotional appeals. We further propose a congruent effect on the interaction between the message and source (i.e., message × source in rational/emotional appeals) of physicians on chronically ill patients’ medical service subscriptions.

Examining the physician’s perspective, we crawled 426,354 Q&A responses from 653 physicians along with their profile information (clinical title and profile image) in a physician-driven OHC. Using term identification techniques, we identified the rational and emotional appeals in responses and analyzed their effects on the number of medical service subscriptions initiated by chronically ill patients. Our results reveal that caring messages in emotional appeals and authoritative reputation as a source in rational appeals were positively associated with patient subscriptions, whereas technical messages in rational appeals and a smiling profile image as a source in emotional appeals were not. Notably, we found that congruence between message and source appeal is important, as the effects of messages in rational and emotional appeals were stronger when paired with corresponding sources. Our physician-centered analysis—drawing from a diverse and comprehensive sample of archival data—provides strong statistical power and generalizability, especially from the physician’s perspective. However, our ability to delve into the psychological mechanisms driving these interactions from the patient’s perspective was somewhat limited.

This study makes several contributions to research and practice. First, it contributes to the OHC literature by drawing attention to the neglected physician’s perspective and providing understanding on the new medical subscription services. Drawing on the persuasive communication literature, this study systematically investigates the effects of physicians’ online responses on patient medical subscription behaviors with a holistic consideration of source and message in rational and emotional appeals. Second, it operationalizes chronically ill patients’ informational and emotional needs from physicians, as opposed to peer patients, complementing the deficiencies of patients’ rational-dominated criteria for choosing physicians in the offline context. Third, this study utilizes advanced text and image mining techniques to analyze the rational and emotional appeals of physicians’ textual responses and profile images. It also provides empirical evidence for recent information system (IS) research on text and image mining in physician-driven OHC settings and enriches the boundary discussion on the use of human images and facial expressions. Lastly, it makes meaningful practical contributions to addressing the challenges that chronically ill patients face with physicians at different institutional ranks. By examining how physicians can best frame their message and source via effective rational and emotional appeals, we propose a more efficient utilization of OHC platforms and medical resources to further address global trends in chronic disease and healthcare disparities.

## 2 Theoretical Foundation

## 2.1 Physician-Driven OHCs and Medical Service Subscription

Physician-driven OHCs have become increasingly popular in recent years, as they fulfill an important need by enabling direct and intensive online communication between physicians and patients. A physician-driven OHC is defined as an online community created and maintained by physicians, emphasizing their active role in patient engagement (Liu et al., 2020). One of the common formats in such OHCs is Q&A, where patients can post questions and seek physicians’ answers to their health concerns for free, thus improving medical care accessibility and comprehensiveness (Khurana et al., 2019). As a form of physician-generated content, physicians’ online responses are designed to disclose credible medical information before initiating a longterm paid medical service subscription while also facilitating comparisons that can assist patients in evaluating the physician’s services (Khurana et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019).

Our scrutiny of the OHC literature suggests three research gaps that need to be filled to achieve our research objective. First, there is relatively limited research from the physician’s perspective on how their online responses affect patients’ likelihood of initiating a medical service subscription. Online platforms have accumulated a vast amount of data on physician-patient communication, leading to an increasing number of studies focusing on the impact of physician-patient interaction texts (e.g., Chen et al., 2019a). However, early studies predominantly analyzed this phenomenon from the patient’s perspective—evaluating, for instance, how response quantity and quality affect patients’ satisfaction and well-being and the patient-physician relationship (Chen et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2015). Recent studies have started to shed light on physicians’ motivations in engaging in such responses and interactions (Chen et al., 2023a; Meng et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023) and on how these interactions could potentially benefit physicians (Khurana et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019) and impact patient decision-making (Meng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023) (Appendix A). We provide a comprehensive view in this study. Understanding medical service subscriptions from the physician’s view highlights the benefits or rewards that physicians can gain from such subscriptions and from their participation in OHCs, which is a key factor motivating their continued involvement (Guo et al., 2017). This insight is crucial for understanding how to better promote physician engagement in OHCs. Given that physicians are the only source of professional expertise in these communities, their continuous participation is essential for both user growth and the sustainability of OHCs (Meng et al., 2021). Therefore, research from the physician’s perspective is not only valuable but also represents a critical branch of OHC studies.

Second, the literature has fallen short in explaining the emotional aspect of physicians’ responses in OHCs, which indeed constitutes a major part of patients’ decision-making when choosing a physician. This lessunderstood topic thus serves as a complement to the predominantly rational perspective in the physiciandriven OHC literature. While the social and emotional support that patients seek from peer patients is wellunderstood (Yan & Tan, 2014), patients also look for physicians’ caring expressions and emotional commitment in physician-patient communications (Burstein et al., 2022). This is aligned with the findings indicating patients’ preference for caring providers who exhibit the following bedside manner characteristics: (1) communicating in a friendly, sympathetic, and understanding style (Victoor et al., 2012); (2) involving themselves in the decisionmaking process regarding care (Bernard et al., 2006); and (3) establishing a friendly atmosphere (Kolstad & Chernew, 2009). Thus, this study tries to fill this gap in the research by identifying the emotional aspects of physicians’ online responses.

Third, prior studies, based mostly on the rational view, have primarily treated physicians’ online responses as a unitary construct—one type of behavioral information—by measuring it using standard methods involving the quantity, word length, or readability of the information (Khurana et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020). However, given the huge variety of online responses in OHCs, these approaches may not adequately identify the multifaceted aspects underlying online responses and how they influence patients’ behaviors (Chen et al., 2019a; Khurana et al., 2019). Indeed, prior studies have produced inconsistent empirical results—for example, while Khurana et al. (2019) found that physicians’ online responses have a significant effect on patients’ care decisions, Zhang et al. (2019) found them to be insignificant. Moreover, a caring bedside manner and the ability to facilitate a friendly atmosphere are not easily observed in online settings, where nonverbal evidence is scarce (Cyr et al., 2009; Watson, 2008). We therefore propose that combining rational and emotional perspectives and text and image data can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effect of physicians’ online responses on patients’ likelihood of initiating medical service subscriptions from the physician’s perspective.

## 2.2 Rational and Emotional Appeals in Persuasive Communication

Persuasive communication has been widely applied in the communication, marketing, and psychology disciplines and examines how individuals’ attitudes and behaviors are influenced through communication— essentially, “who says what to whom” (Albarracín & Johnson, 2018; Hovland et al., 1953). A major stream of this literature focuses on message and source through central and peripheral routes, represented by the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) and heuristicsystematic model (HSM) (Chaiken, 1980; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). Another important stream of the literature focuses on rational and emotional appeals (also referred to as cognitive and emotional appeals) (Fabrigar & Petty, 1999; Kotler & Keller, 2008; Wang et al., 2016).

In this study, we focus specifically on the informational and emotional needs of chronically ill patients and posit that message and source in persuasive communication can be expressed via rational and emotional appeals. Rational appeals stimulate individuals’ cognitive processing by communicating the benefits of a product, person, or service and persuading them through evidence, facts, and reasoning (Kotler & Keller, 2008). By offering detailed information and evidence, such communication can help eliminate potential uncertainty associated with information asymmetry and improve credibility and persuasion levels (Panda & Mishra, 2013). Emotional appeals, on the other hand, rely on descriptive, sensory-based language and imagery to evoke positive or negative emotions during information processing (Chaudhuri, 2002) to impact individuals’ attitudes and their subsequent evaluations and behaviors (Cyr et al., 2009).

## 2.2.1 Message

Message framing via rational and emotional appeals has been extensively studied, including in IS contexts (Chen et al., 2019b; Kotler & Keller, 2008; Wang et al., 2016). A well-articulated message via a rational appeal on product/service merit has been found to be more convincing due to its high information quality, hence affecting user beliefs on usefulness (Bhattacherjee & Sanford, 2006). Recent studies have confirmed that certain language can trigger individual reasoning and processing of a message’s rational appeal (Chen et al., 2019b). In physician-driven OHCs, physicians can persuade patients via rational appeals in the form of technical messages, which comprise domain-specific medical terminology reflected in physicians’ online responses (Jackson, 1995). Technical details and the use of such terminology, as evidence-based information, are often given in response to patients’ inquiries. These rational-based communications can assist patients in understanding the unknown or complex aspects of their disease, conveying the physicians’ professional competence and reliability in a logical manner (Li et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2020).

Messages in emotional appeals are typically easier to recall and believed to be more persuasive because they involve emotion, which promotes a sense of familiarity or intimacy with the communicator and encourages individuals’ interest in the subject and response behaviors (Fox & Amichai-Hamburger, 2001). Such emotion may also be “contagious” in that it can be quickly transferred from the communicator to the recipient (Small & Simonsohn, 2008). In physiciandriven OHCs, physicians convey emotional appeals largely through caring messages, reflecting their good bedside manner by using comforting language to convey trust, respect, and empathy in their online responses (Kuruoglu et al., 2015). Being a caregiver is one of the most important qualities of a professional medical service provider. In the nursing literature, reflecting the direct, one-on-one treatment of patients, caring is defined as a value and an attitude that has to become a will, an intention, or a commitment, that manifests itself in concrete acts (Burstein et al., 2022; Watson, 2008). We thus define a caring message in physician-driven OHCs as communication through an online response that conveys the physician’s sincere concern for and emotional commitment to a patient’s well-being.

## 2.2.2 Source

Communication can be especially persuasive if it comes from a credible source (Goldsmith et al., 2013; O’Keefe, 1990). When trying to evaluate the credibility of a caregiver, patients are impacted by both rational and emotional appeals. Expertise is a critical aspect of source credibility and is often highly persuasive rational appeals (Hovland et al., 1953; Wilson & Sherrell, 1993). Expertise refers to the extent to which the communicator has the capability to make correct assertions in the message (Sternthal et al., 1978). Research has shown that individuals are likely to accept a message from an expert source when they internalize the source’s belief or viewpoint into their own value system (Kelman, 1961; Wilson & Sherrell, 1993). Furthermore, expertise has been found to be effective in endorsing source credibility in the context of online consumer reviews and e-health platforms (Karahanna et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2017).

In physician-driven OHCs, physicians’ authoritative reputation has been widely adopted as an indicator of professionalism and competence in the healthcare system (Guo et al., 2017). In previous literature on OHCs, physicians’ authoritative reputation has mainly adopted variables from offline contexts as the proxy, such as the clinical title or hospital level, which is evaluated by an authoritative organization. When faced with a vast amount of data in informationasymmetrical online environments, users tend to make decisions based on authoritative information. Domainspecific identity-based information enhances the physician’s online credibility and the persuasiveness of the source via rational appeals (Guo et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2016). Hence, even in physician-driven OHCs, patients tend to consider authoritative reputation when comparing services and evaluating service quality across providers (Guo et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2019).

Another key aspect of source credibility is trustworthiness, which can be persuasive through inducing positive attitudes via emotional appeals (Kelman & Hovland, 1953; Sternthal et al., 1978). Trustworthiness in communication refers to the degree to which an audience perceives the assertions made by a communicator to be valid (Hovland et al., 1953; Pornpitakpan, 2004). In the online environment, human images are commonly used to create social presence and cultivate trust or personal appeal (Cyr et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2016). Research has shown that facial expressions, in particular, are especially effective in conveying emotional information about service providers and influencing evaluations of service quality (Chen & Wyer, 2020; Cyr et al., 2009; Deng & Ravichandran, 2020). In particular, the caregiver’s smile has been recognized as a vital reflection of one’s personality and emotional competence in establishing rapport, which is critical in building a trusting care relationship (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2006; Houston et al., 2018).

Thus, in physician-driven OHCs, the physician’s smiling profile image is a salient visual cue that can allow patients to evaluate the source credibility via an emotional appeal (Fagerstrøm et al., 2017). Specifically, a patient who views a smiling physician in their online profile will be more likely to think they can provide sincere and family-like care services beyond professional treatments (Woo & Chan, 2020), which is especially important when other social cues are absent (Cheng et al., 2020). Patients may also attribute the smile of a physician to their intrinsic motivation in treatment (i.e., providing compassionate, personalized care) and may infer source credibility from the image based on its emotional appeal. By creating a more intimate connection to the physician, a smile image can serve as a potent signal that can persuade patients that a physician will provide highquality care.

## 2.2.3 Congruent Effects Between Message and Source

Research has long established that both message and source are critically important in persuasion (Bhattacherjee & Sanford, 2006; Wilson & Sherrell, 1993), showing that individuals might not trust a message from a less credible source, even if it contains comprehensive factual information (Sussman & Siegal, 2003). Lack of a credible source may also bias individual information processing and affect the recipient’s evaluation and decision-making processes, whereas source credibility can inject confidence into the individual’s judgment and make the message more persuasive (Ismagilova et al., 2020; Pornpitakpan, 2004).

Extending the discussion, we propose that communication may be more persuasive if the message and the source are matched in their appeal (i.e., both aligning with rational or emotional appeals) (Capelli et al., 2012; Johnson et al., 2018). Advertising studies have found that a congruent effect between the source and product characteristics is desirable for relaying positive emotion and enhancing purchase intentions (Knoll & Matthes, 2017; Misra & Beatty, 1990). In rational appeals, where individuals focus on objective facts and characteristics, the endorser is paired with the endorsee, who makes sense of the information in the context of using their existing knowledge about the endorser (e.g., source characteristics) (Misra & Beatty, 1990; Till & Busler, 2000). In contrast, in emotional appeals, endorsees focus on the effect of the appeal and then transfer it (e.g., favorable attitude) to the endorser (Knoll & Matthes, 2017; Misra & Beatty, 1990). Individuals’ needs in terms of rational and emotional processing also affect the persuasiveness of different types of messages on attitude changes, where rational appeals relate to the practical and objective attributes of the product, while emotional appeals target qualities such as taste or appearance (Haddock et al., 2008). Thus, based on the congruent effects hypothesis, congruence between highly relevant characteristics of the source and message will make the appeal more persuasive.

## 3 Hypotheses Development

## 3.1 Rational and Emotional Appeals of Messages: Technical and Caring Messages

Medical services are associated with high uncertainty because the quality of care is not easily observed prior to engagement (Howden & Pressey, 2008). This uncertainty is especially heightened in the online context because patients may be disadvantaged in terms of being able to understand their disease or locate information when attempting to verify the credentials of an online physician (Khurana et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2014). The technical messages that physicians provide to patients in OHCs help fill this knowledge gap. In responding to patients’ needs for informational support, a physician can convey their expertise and ability to educate patients on their health condition by using a higher ratio of medical terms to describe the symptoms and causes of a disease, which can in turn influence subscription decisions (Liu et al., 2020). Even though the medical terminology may not be fully understood by patients, using such terminology may lead patients to conclude: “I don’t understand you but I trust you” (Xu, 2021). Sharing medical expertise and technical knowledge by using more technical terms can convince patients that this physician indeed has the knowledge to effectively treat their disease (Yan & Tan, 2014). We thus hypothesize:

H1: Technical messages in a physician’s online response (operationalized as the ratio of medical terms) are positively associated with the likelihood of a chronically ill patient initiating a medical service subscription.

In the chronic disease context, active self-care makes up a large part of patients’ health management, which often causes considerable pressure and stress (Li et al., 2022). Patients may need to cope with doubts, fears, challenges, or negative thoughts on a daily basis and may even experience more difficulties developing or maintaining relationships with others (Yan & Tan, 2014). Such patients may have a stronger need for a communication style that is friendly, caring, and understanding, and may be more likely to open up and talk about themselves if physicians demonstrate these characteristics in their online responses (Schmid Mast et al., 2008; Victoor et al., 2012). Establishing a friendly rapport can make patients feel like they are being treated fairly in the healthcare partnership rather than passively receiving instruction/treatment (Kolstad & Chernew, 2009; Lee & Hong, 2016; Liu et al., 2020). Physicians’ caring messages in OHC settings could also evoke patients’ positive emotions and confidence in the treatment. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: Caring messages in a physician’s online response (operationalized as the ratio of caring-related words) are positively associated with the likelihood of a chronically ill patient initiating a medical service subscription.

## 3.2 Rational and Emotional Appeals of Source: Authoritative Reputation and Smiling Profile Image

Due to information asymmetry, patients often evaluate physicians as the primary healthcare information source when making a subscription decision. A physician’s authoritative reputation (i.e., clinical title) shown in their profile signals their expertise and will thus influence patients’ care expectations (Liu et al., 2016). Moreover, a medical expert’s professional standing is likely to trigger stereotyping (i.e., status equals capability), which will affect patients’ information processing and behaviors (Luo et al., 2013). Indeed, it is not surprising that patients are more likely to book consultations with highly reputable physicians, whose domain-specific knowledge and professional commitment are perceived as vital for successful treatment (Liu et al., 2016; Lu & Wu, 2016). Hence, we hypothesize:

H3: The authoritative reputation of a physician (operationalized as their clinical title) is positively associated with the likelihood of a chronically ill patient initiating a medical service subscription.

We further posit that the physician profile image can affect patients’ subscription decisions. In particular, a service provider’s smile in their profile image could impact patients’ evaluation of the services they provide (Fagerstrøm et al., 2017). First, individuals are more attracted to smiling people because they appear to be approachable, positive, and easy to get along with, satisfying the patient’s need for emotional support (Ekman, 1993). Second, by expressing positive emotions through, for example, a smile, a service provider can create a similar response in viewers, who would thus form a parallel response and expectation of service quality. Indeed, providing “service with a smile” is a common standard in service industries (Chen & Wyer, 2020; Woo & Chan, 2020). Third, individuals may infer a non-self-interested intention from a service provider with a smiling profile picture that exudes empathy. When making a subscription decision, in addition to responding to their persuasive communications, patients will likely seek to ascertain a service provider’s intentions and trustworthiness (Deng & Ravichandran, 2020; Reinhard et al., 2006). Politeness and honesty conveyed by an uplifting facial expression (e.g., smile) can address this concern (Deng & Ravichandran, 2020). In the case of healthcare for chronically ill patients, positive emotions expressed by the physician’s smile may be interpreted by patients as a genuine intention to help them and may reassure them that the physician has a caring nature ( Reinhard et al., 2006). Hence, we hypothesize:

H4: The smiling profile image of a physician (operationalized as the smile intensity) is positively associated with the likelihood of a chronically ill patient initiating a medical service subscription.

## 3.3 Congruent Effects Between Message and Source via Rational and Emotional Appeals

The literature has proposed that for integrated marketing communications, the message and source should match in tone and style (Kamins, 1990; Kotler & Keller, 2008). A congruence between source and message characteristics has been found to enhance consumers’ favorable attitudes and purchase intentions (Knoll & Matthes, 2017; Misra & Beatty, 1990). We hence propose that a message will be more persuasive if it aligns with its source in terms of its rational or emotional appeal.

When processing a physician’s technical messages in rational appeals, patients will likely also examine the source, notably the physician’s authoritative reputation. Patients with limited knowledge about their chronic disease may not have the capability to verify whether a physician’s response is helpful (i.e., appropriate or sound medical advice). In this situation, an authoritative reputation is an objective indicator of a physician’s expertise and experience and provides useful input for a patient’s decision-making when considering a service subscription (Guo et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2016). Physicians’ higher-level clinic title and hospital status in the medical service context implies they have been recognized by authoritative institutions that have confirmed their expertise (Guo et al., 2017). Hence, in physician-driven OHCs, a strong authoritative reputation can further enhance the reliability of technical messages in patient interactions. Using this logic, patients making care decisions are more likely to be convinced by technical messages from physicians with a good professional standing. We thus propose:

H5: A physician’s authoritative reputation positively moderates the relationship between their technical messages and the likelihood of a chronically ill patient initiating a medical service subscription.

Ensuring that patients’ needs for emotional support can be satisfied when receiving caring messages from the physician is central to OHC subscription behavior. Because the physician’s smiling profile image conveys a salient signal of sincere care and respect, it may bolster patients’ perception that the text of the message they are receiving is caring and heighten the emotional potency of the message (Woo & Chan, 2020). Furthermore, since smiles have been shown to be “contagious,” (Barger & Grandey, 2006; Woo & Chan, 2020), the physician’s smile could even be transferred to patients, causing them to engage in positive emotions themselves and leading them to transfer the positive emotion to the message. This will cause them to evaluate the service quality favorably, which will in turn affect their subscription decision (Chen & Wyer, 2020; Misra & Beatty, 1990; Parasuraman et al., 1985). Based on congruent effects, the emotional appeal of the message will interact with the emotional appeal of source and thus enhance the persuasive effect on patients’ subscription behavior. We therefore propose the following hypothesis and summarize the research model in Figure 1:

H6: A smile in a physician’s profile image positively moderates the relationship between their caring messages and the likelihood of a chronically ill patient initiating a medical service subscription.

## 4 Methodology

## 4.1 Data Collection and Processing

We collected data from a popular physician-driven OHC in China, where Q&A is one of the primary forms of interaction. We chose this platform because of the large volume of physician-generated content in their Q&A section, the high level of physician activity, and the availability of medical service subscriptions. On this platform, physicians initially offer free medical advice in response to patient inquiries. Based on these responses, patients can decide whether to subscribe to the physician’s medical services. Figure 2 shows several physicians responses to a patient’s question, all of which are publicly available (left-hand column). The patient interested in pursuing care with a physician can click on their profile, which directs them from the Q&A section to the medical service subscription section, where they can view diverse medical service options (right-hand column).

After crawling data from this OHC, we constructed panel data of chronically ill patients (e.g., diabetes and thyroid disease) from 2015 to 2016. We focused on this time period because, at this point, the platform had reached a stabilized development phase, with no external intervention affecting its primary outcomes.

The process of data collection and pre-processing is visualized in Figure 3. First, we gathered physicians’ profiles (including clinical titles and profile images) from the registration section, response data from the Q&A section, and subscription data from the medical service subscription section. After removing missing data (e.g., physician posts without images), we were left with 426,354 patient-physician interaction texts from 2,035 physicians. Among them, 653 physicians offered medical service subscriptions and received a total of 77,449 subscriptions over the period of 106 weeks. Second, we identified medical terminology and caring words used in physicians’ online responses. Third, we analyzed the physicians’ profile images to assess their smile intensity. These processes allowed us to construct variables and integrate them into the final dataset at the physician level. By aggregating the data weekly at the physician level, we obtained a final sample of 4,319 observations.

## 4.2 Measurement

Medical service subscriptions: We measured medical service subscriptions by counting medical services subscribed to at a weekly level (Lin, 2002). That is, for physician i, we calculated the total subscription number of medical services subscriptions at time t+1.

Technical messages: Prior studies have widely adopted the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS)—a repository of biomedical and health-related terminology developed by the US National Library of Medicine—as the lexicon to analyze text in English. In this study, given that the texts in the Q&A section were all written in Chinese, we used the THU Open Chinese Lexicon (THUOCL), developed by Tsinghua University, one of the bestrecognized Chinese lexicons produced for the healthcare domain (Han et al., 2016). To improve reliability in identifying technical words in physicians’ online responses, we combined THUOCL with the word segment results from our datasets as our lexicon. Using the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) program, we calculated the number and ratio of technical words in physicians’ online responses. This approach has been widely used in previous studies. In our study, the ratio of technical words in online patientphysician interactions was calculated to measure physicians’ informational support or patients’ health literacy levels in online consultations (Chen et al., 2019a, 2020).

Caring messages: This study defines caring messages as messages delivering physicians’ concerns or emotional commitment to a patient’s well-being through their online responses (Burstein et al., 2022).

The linguistic expressions of individuals can yield profound insights into their psychological states. Occasionally, these insights are quite evident and straightforward. For example, a physician’s frequent use of reassuring phrases like “do not worry” or “do not be afraid” suggests they are understanding and caring, seeking to ease worry or negative emotions and encouraging positive behaviors. As the physicians’ use of such expressions allowed us to reliably gauge their approach to patient care, we counted the number of caring words in physicians’ online responses to assess their attributes using LIWC to calculate the number of caring messages in physicians’ online responses. Developing a lexicon specific to the medical field is crucial, as we aimed to measure a domain-specific variable in OHCs. After segmenting the Chinese text of physicians’ online responses, we invited coders representing different perspectives to label the caring words (Appendix B). We then used LIWC to calculate the number and ratio of caring words in the message each physician posted in the Q&A section. A coding example of caring words can also be found in Table 1.

Authoritative reputation: A physician’s clinical title (e.g., chief physician, associate physician) is a strong indicator of their authoritative reputation. It is primarily granted by governmental or professional entities and reflects the physician’s expertise and overall status in the organization and thus serves as a rational appeal for the source (Guo et al., 2017). We constructed a categorical variable to represent different levels of authoritative reputation, as shown in Table 2.

Smiling profile image: This study uses the smile on a physician’s profile image to capture the emotional appeal of the source. To extract smile features, we employed a cloud machine learning approach using the Microsoft Azure API interface, <sup>1</sup> which has been widely adopted as an effective method for photo analysis (So & Oh, 2018). This program automatically identifies objects, faces, and sentiments in each photo, allowing us to detect and quantify the intensity of the physician’s smile. We analyzed images of 653 physicians from this interface and obtained smile intensity scores ranging from 0 to 1.

Control variables: As described in Table 2, we included Q&A responsiveness, hospital location, and physician attractiveness as control variables. Since physicians’ participation in the OHC may have affected patients’ willingness to subscribe, we measured their Q&A responsiveness by calculating the time difference between patients’ questions and physicians’ answers in the Q&A section (Khurana et al., 2019). Since big cities are expected to possess greater medical resources (e.g., more experienced physicians and financial support from the government), hospital location plays an important role in patients medical subscription decisions (Mosadeghrad, 2014). We divided physician location into six categories based on China’s national urban hierarchy. We also controlled for physicians’ physical attractiveness beyond their smile in their profile image. Physical attractiveness is defined as the traits or characteristics attributed to a person that evoke a perceived sense of pleasure, satisfaction, or social status (Ohanian, 1990). To measure this, we applied the training model from Fan et al. (2017) and Liang et al. (2018), utilizing the label distribution learning model to predict physicians’ facial attractiveness scores.

## 5.2 Robustness Check

This study employed three approaches to test the robustness of the main results. First, we employed an alternative model—the system generalized method of moments (system GMM)—which uses the lagged differences of endogenous variables as instruments (Arellano & Bover, 1995). Second, we applied alternative measurements for the main variables by analyzing the word counts of technical and caring messages in physicians’ responses, instead of their ratios, as was done in the main analyses. Third, we conducted a difference-in-differences (DID) model to verify the causal relationship between physicians’ online responses and patient medical service subscription (Appendix C).

System GMM estimations: The results from the alternative models in Table 7 align with those from the main models in Table 5. To ensure the reliability of our system GMM estimates, we verified that two key conditions were satisfied through the following tests: (1) a test for serial correlation in the differenced error terms, and (2) the Hansen test for overidentifying restrictions, which evaluates the overall validity of the instruments (Hansen, 1982).

The inability to reject the null hypotheses for both tests supports the robustness of our system GMM models. First, the first-order serial correlation tests (AR(1)) using the Arellano-Bond method are significant in Table 7, indicating the need to check for second-order serial correlation. Although deeper lags are necessary as instruments for testing second-order correlation, the AR(2) tests failed to reject the null hypothesis at $p <$ 0.05, meaning there was no second-order serial correlation in the differenced error terms. Second, we applied the Hansen test as part of our system GMM procedure. The results confirm that we failed to reject the null hypothesis, indicating that the instruments were valid. These specification tests demonstrate that the Arellano-Bond system GMM estimator was appropriate for our data and yielded unbiased estimates.

Alternative measures with technical and caring word counts: To further strengthen the reliability of our empirical results, we conducted additional robustness checks by applying an alternative approach to measuring technical messages. Instead of using ratios, we opted to quantify the technical and caring messages by counting the number of relevant words in each response. This method allowed for a more granular assessment of message content, with a focus on the absolute number of technical or caring words rather than their proportional representation. By shifting to a word count-based approach, we aimed to more accurately capture the intensity or volume of these message types. The results of these robustness checks confirmed the consistency of our findings across both the original ratio-based method and this alternative measurement approach (Table 7).

## 6 Discussion

## 6.1 Main Findings and Discussion

Building on the persuasive communication and physician-driven OHC literature, this study developed a research model that examines how rational and emotional appeals in message and source affect patients’ medical service subscriptions. Our results (Tables 5 and 7) yielded some interesting findings.

First, after analyzing the content and appeal of physicians’ online responses, we found that emotional appeals (i.e., caring messages) in message framing were effective in influencing chronically ill patients’ medical subscription decisions, but rational appeals (i.e., technical messages) were not. Counter to the wellestablished effect of message framing using rational appeals, this surprising finding—that the effect of technical messages on patient medical subscription was not significant—can be interpreted in view of the respective appeals in the healthcare context of chronically ill patients. That is, based on the conceptualization of rational appeals, chronically ill patients need to engage with deep cognitive processing and more mental resources to comprehend technical messages, which may be challenging due to limited health literacy or cognitive capacity, especially when compounded by fear and anxiety related to their disease (Liu et al., 2020). Finding themselves unable to fully process the technical messages in rational appeals, patients may instead rely on intuitive cues, such as authoritative reputation (source in rational appeals) within the same category of rational appeals (Bhattacherjee & Sanford, 2006; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). Our findings also support the contention that authoritative reputation (source) is more effective in affecting subscription decisions than technical messages (message) in rational appeals.

On the other hand, if patients are unable to fully process technical messages in rational appeals, they may also shift their attention to another form of appeal in messages—caring messages. These messages, grounded in emotional appeals, are processed through the emotional system. Emotional appeals are often more effective because the emotions they contain can automatically activate the motivation system, allowing individuals to devote more mental resources to processing the message (Lang & Yegiyan, 2008). Furthermore, emotion is contagious—when patients receive a caring message from a physician, they may quickly feel the empathy conveyed through emotional transfer (Small & Simonsohn, 2008; Woo & Chan, 2020). Considering the long course of their disease, chronically ill patients—who may be more likely than other patients to seek emotional support such as understanding, empathy, and companionship in the course of their treatment—may be deeply affected by such expressions of care from health professionals or be more easily persuaded by caring messages (Gallagherlepak et al., 2009). Such patients may be more likely to respond positively to physicians after receiving sincere expressions of concern through emotional appeals (Huang et al., 2019; Schmid Mast et al., 2008). Our findings therefore confirm that caring messages (messages in emotional appeals) are more effective in persuading chronically ill patients to subscribe to medical services than technical ones (messages in rational appeals).

Second, after considering the source in both textual and image forms we found that the source in rational appeals (i.e., authoritative reputation) was more effective than that in emotional appeals (i.e., smiling profile image) for increasing medical service subscriptions. This interesting finding—that a smiling profile image had no significant effect on patient medical subscriptions—can perhaps be attributed to the strong source effect and the distinct mechanisms by which human images and facial expressions are used in the e-health context. Following the previous explanation, the chronically ill patients examined in this study would likely have taken the most direct heuristic cue when processing the source effect, expertise (in rational appeals), which has been found to have the strongest source effect in persuasive communication (Wilson & Sherrell, 1993). Especially in the healthcare context, authoritative reputation is a primary criterion that patients use when choosing a physician, as it conveys not only the physician’s experience, affiliations, accreditations but also their access to the critical medical resources, networks, and equipment necessary for effective treatment (Guo et al., 2017; Wu & Lu, 2017).

Meanwhile, recent IS research has demonstrated that the use of human images and smile expressions in profile pictures is effective in generating monetary transactions (Deng & Ravichandran, 2020). We did not find this to be the case in our study, however, and suggest that the trustworthiness mechanism functions differently across various contexts. The studies above used e-commerce platforms such as Airbnb, crowdfunding, and medical crowdfunding. In such settings, individuals must interact with unknown online vendors and hosts and may risk financial losses (e.g., when investing in a deceptive project) or even their personal safety (e.g., by staying with an untrustworthy host) (Featherman & Pavlou, 2003). Thus, in such cases, human images can help reduce social uncertainty and smile expressions can be used to signal honesty and friendliness (Cyr et al., 2009; Ert et al., 2016).

In physician-driven OHCs, however, the risk of engaging inept or disreputable physicians is minimal, as their professional status is verified by the platforms and can be cross-validated through public profiles from their affiliated institutions. Moreover, trustworthiness (represented by a smiling profile image in our context), as another aspect of source credibility, may partially overlap with expertise (represented by authoritative reputation). This overlap may explain the weaker persuasive effect of the smiling profile image on patient subscriptions in the source effect, as the images provide less new information (about trustworthiness) regarding source credibility (Hovland et al., 1953). Consequently, patients will shift their attention to other selection criteria when choosing a physician (Hovland et al., 1953; Liu & Goodhue, 2012; Zou et al., 2023). Research has also found mixed results regarding the influence of trustworthiness on purchase decisions, while the impact of expertise remains consistent (Clow et al., 2008), supporting our explanation. In summary, our findings suggest that authoritative reputation (rational appeals through expertise) is more effective in persuading medical service subscriptions than a smiling profile image (emotional appeals through trustworthiness) in the e-health context.

Third, our results indicate the importance of congruence between the message and the source, evident in both rational and emotional appeals. This (intuitive) finding is consistent with existing persuasive communication literature, which indicates that beyond the main source effect, the source can reinforce the message’s impact through an interaction effect (Johnson et al., 2018). Our contribution lies in extending this concept by examining the congruence between the source and message of appeals through the integration of both text and image data in the healthcare context, where high levels of expertise and emotional reassurance are essential. In rational appeals, for example, a credible source significantly enhances the persuasiveness of the message (Li et al., 2019). Furthermore, this aligns with the “halo effect” theory, which posits that a high reputation enhances perceived service utility and influences purchasing behaviors (Burke et al., 2018).

Our study also demonstrates that, beyond the appeal conveyed through tone and style, the congruence between physicians’ texts and image (e.g., caring messages paired with a smiling profile image) and between verbal and nonverbal cues, enhances the persuasiveness of physicians’ online responses (Gillis & Nilsen, 2017). A counter example is the nonsignificant interaction effect between technical messages and a smiling profile image, despite the assumption that this pairing might address patients’ informational and emotional needs. However, human cognition is naturally inclined to avoid dissonance and favor congruence, and image-text consistency increases processing fluency (Festinger, 1957). For instance, a smiling face may appear incongruent or unsettling when paired with a serious text using substantial technical terminology to describe a disease, and may thus not effectively motivate patients to subscribe to medical services.

## 6.2 Theoretical Implications

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it systematically analyzes the effects of physicians’ online responses on online medical service subscriptions. While previous studies have proposed that physicians’ online responses can enhance patient satisfaction, well-being, and physician-patient relationships, which mainly benefit patients (Lu & Wu, 2016; Zhang et al., 2019), recent research has increasingly focused on the rise of paid OHC services (Khurana et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019). This shift highlights a gap in understanding the effect of physicians’ online responses on patients’ subscription decisions from the physician’s perspective. Our study fills this important gap and our findings can be used to better motivate physicians, the critical healthcare providers in OHCs.

Second, our study provides new insights by enhancing the understanding of the effects of emotional appeals derived from the physician’s informational and emotional support in physician-driven OHCs. The emotional perspective has received intensive attention in prior OHC literature, which has mainly discussed emotional support derived from peer patients sharing their experience (Yan & Tan, 2014), whereas the physician-driven OHC literature has largely focused on indicators of patients’ choices from a rational aspect (Guo et al., 2017). Building on the persuasive communication literature, this study is one of the first to combine rational and emotional appeals with the two routes of message and source processing. This approach provides a more comprehensive and holistic perspective on how the informational and emotional needs of chronically ill patients can be effectively addressed by physicians on OHC platforms. Furthermore, this study contextualizes the message and source in emotional appeals from physicians, demonstrating how the physician’s bedside manner can be effectively manifested in the e-health context. This study also contributes to the literature by highlighting the emotional aspect of physicians’ online textual responses and profile images while also offering guidance for future research on how physicians can better utilize and balance rational and emotional appeals to enhance message and source framing in physician-driven OHCs (Cheung et al., 2012).

Third, in addition to analyzing physicians’ online textual responses, this study explores the role of human smiles in profile images—commonly used visual cues on online platforms. By applying advanced methods to assess physicians’ smiles, the findings reveal that while a smile alone does not directly influence patients service subscriptions, it has a significant impact when combined with caring messages. This combination resembles the traditional bedside manner in the offline context, fostering patient confidence. Examining the use of smile images in specialized physician-driven OHCs, compared to general e-commerce platforms, we provide valuable insights into how image cues can be leveraged in future IS research, particularly considering the distinct roles images play across different contexts. As image mining techniques are rapidly advancing and human images, along with facial expressions, are increasingly attracting scholarly attention, this study offers a fresh perspective on their role in online healthcare interactions and OHC subscription behaviors (Chen et al., 2023b; Deng & Ravichandran, 2020; Fagerstrøm et al., 2017).

## 6.3 Practical Implications

This study has important practical implications for both physicians and OHC platform providers. Specifically, our findings provide a better understanding of how physicians can engage in OHCs to gain medical subscriptions and new patients. First, contrary to the common practice of physicians using technical terminology to demonstrate expertise, our findings suggest that this approach is less effective in converting into medical subscriptions, compared to the more straightforward use of authoritative reputation (e.g., clinical title). However, our research proposes a remedy and also provides insights into how physicians can optimize their engagement in OHCs. By focusing on both rational and emotional appeals in terms of message and source, physicians can signal both their professional competence and caring demeanor, appealing to patients’ needs for informational as well as emotional support. Second, caring messages in the emotional appeals were found to be effective for physicians at all ranks to approach patients. although our findings confirm that clinical title substantially influences patients’ choice of a physician in the online context, we suggest a “silver lining” for physicians with low-status titles: expressing friendliness and accessibility using caring messages in emotional appeals, which we found to be an effective method for physicians at all ranks to attract patients. Moreover, our study results on congruent effects also punctuate the importance of considering rational/emotional congruency in texts and images. For example, although smiling may be viewed as a less serious expression and thus less appropriate for presenting credentials to attract patients—a smile in a profile image coupled with caring messages in physician responses to patient questions can indeed improve the efficacy of persuasion.

This study offers practical guidance for OHC platform providers on how to design more compelling and appropriate health communication interfaces and advises physicians on how to shape their platform engagement. For instance, our results confirm that caring messages attract patients more than technical messages. OHC platform providers could advise physicians on how to best utilize appropriate language or effective communication tools to encourage more patient subscriptions—for example, by enabling emojis. On the other hand, we also urge the OHC platforms to be cautious about overemphasizing authoritative reputation (which can contribute to unequal popularity among physicians) and balance reputation with other aspects of the physician (e.g., platform endorsement on the physician) that can help attract patients (Zhan et al., 2024).

While the relationship between technical messages and service subscription was found to be insignificant in our study, we wish to emphasize physicians’ ethical responsibility in supporting and enhancing patients’ health and health literacy for the benefit of society. As medical experts, physicians have a duty to support and assist patients, with their professional motivation often rooted in a strong sense of occupational responsibility (Yang et al., 2023). Additionally, the technical messages in online exchanges can enhance patients’ health literacy, potentially leading to better health outcomes. Therefore, providing high-quality technical messages in OHCs is a crucial patient benefit (Chen et al., 2019a).

## 6.4 Limitations and Future Research

This study has limitations that suggest directions for future research. First, text analysis has limitations due to the inherent ambiguity of natural language, where words carry multiple meanings depending on context. Moreover, method performance is impacted by cultural variations, potential biases in training data, and challenges in addressing emerging topics not presented in the training dataset. These factors underscore the importance of continually refining and considering diverse linguistic and cultural contexts in text analysis applications and using pretrained large language models to learn patterns in human speech that can more accurately identify the information being transmitted in text. Similarly, our image analysis also faces limitations due to potential biases in the algorithms used, such as underrepresentation of certain demographic groups in the training data. These limitations, similar to those in text analysis, may affect the accuracy and generalizability of the results.

Second, patient characteristics, such as disease risks and health literacy, may play an important contingent role. Due to the complex and interacting factors of disease treatment, the high risks associated with specific diseases can influence a patient’s perceived importance of technical knowledge in physician comments (Lu & Wu, 2016; Zhao et al., 2022). For example, cancer patients may focus more on the ability of physicians to cure them (Lu & Wu, 2016). On the other hand, health literacy encompasses an individual’s understanding of health-related information and their ability to apply it effectively (Nutbeam, 2008). This suggests that patients with higher health literacy are better equipped to understand complex health messages. Consequently, they are more likely to recognize the value and relevance of technical messages, enhancing their ability to achieve positive outcomes when engaging with advanced technical content. Since this study emphasizes patients with chronic diseases in physician-driven OHCs and does not measure health literacy, future studies could further explore contingent variables to better understand the role of technical knowledge in OHCs from the patient’s perspective. Additionally, examining the dyadic effect—how physician characteristics align with patient traits—could offer deeper insights into the effectiveness of technical messages.

Third, while our study offers valuable insights through a robust empirical analysis of a large dataset of physicianpatient interactions, the absence of qualitative data limits our ability to explore the finer psychological mechanisms driving these interactions from the patient’s perspective. Nevertheless, our physician-focused analysis—utilizing a large and diverse sample of physicians and patients— delivers significant statistical power and generalizability, particularly from the perspective of physicians. This comprehensive dataset allows us to identify broad patterns and trends in physician-patient interactions that smaller or more controlled studies might not capture. Despite the absence of qualitative insights, our study makes a substantial contribution by uncovering critical dynamics within these interactions that have broader implications for healthcare delivery. Future research could address this gap by shifting focus to the patient’s viewpoint and employing alternative methodologies. These methodologies would enable a deeper exploration of the psychological factors at play, offering a richer understanding of the motivations and perceptions that drive patients’ medical service subscriptions, thereby complementing and expanding on our physician-centered findings.

## 7 Conclusion

This study examines how physicians’ online responses influence patient subscription behaviors by holistically considering rational and emotional appeals of message and source in both textual and image formats, using advanced machine learning techniques and econometric analyses. We believe that this study offers a sound foundation for understanding how physicians can effectively signal their professional competence and caring demeanor in OHCs. This study outlines strategies derived from both rational and emotional appeals that physicians at various levels can adopt. By refining these strategies, physicians can enhance their communication effectiveness, improve chronic disease care and delivery, and achieve economic benefits along with broader professional recognition. Consequently, this study contributes to the efficient utilization of OHC platforms and medical resources while addressing global trends related to chronic disease and health disparities.

## Appendix B. Manual Coding for Caring Words

Increasing numbers of OHC studies have started to utilize term identification methodologies. Due to the lack of a suitable lexicon for caring-related expressions, we manually coded caring expressions in physicians’ online responses. First, we assigned coders to the task of categorizing sentences as either caring or non-caring. The sentences identified as caring were then segmented into individual words. Coders refined the word list to create a comprehensive Chinese caring lexicon.

## Manual Coding Methods for Caring Words

The manual coding process involved four coders: where two with research backgrounds in healthcare took the patient’s perspective and the other two, medical students, took the physician’s perspective. We used two methods in the coding process to label the data segments: emotion coding and hypothesis coding (Saldana, 2009). First, we invited the researcher coders to review the data from the patient’s perspective. After randomly selecting about 5,048 physicians online responses from our Q&A dataset for caring message coding, the coders followed the hypothesis coding scheme guidelines to code the response text.

## Guidelines for Caring Message Codes Scheme

Hypothesis coding applies a predetermined list of codes “developed from a theory/prediction about what will be found in the data before they have been collected or analyzed” (Saldana, 2013, p. 147), serving as a pre-hypothesis method of analysis. We developed the coding scheme for caring messages in online responses based on the caring theories of Watson (2008), Swanson (1991, 1993), and Martensson et al. (2020).

Specifically, Burstein et al. (2022) defined caring in health communication as physicians’ sincere concern for and emotional commitment to a patient’s well-being, as expressed through communication. Also, Watson (2008) defined caring as having a value and an attitude that has to become a will, an intention, or a commitment, that manifests itself in concrete acts, which include five dimensions (i.e., trusting relationship, anticipates, comforts, explains and facilitates, accessibility). Watson’s caring theory served as the theoretical foundation for the coding scheme development of Martensson et al. (2020). Swanson (1991, 1993) and Martensson et al. (2020) provided specific phenomena and examples of what it means for nurses to practice caring.

Based on these definitions, we developed a coding scheme for assessing the caring expressed in physicians’ responses. In physician-driven OHCs, online responses to patient questions in the Q&A section deliver physicians’ attitudes toward and solutions to health problems, whereas caring messages communicate their bedside manner (i.e., politeness, respect, trust, sincerity, and concern) and overall expressions of concern for patients’ health (Gallagher-lepak et al., 2009). We adopted the measurement and evaluation of care based on data and caring concept content from Watson (2008) and Martensson et al. (2020), which focused on verbal communication and non-verbal behaviors. We also gave coders evaluation criteria and examples in online verbal communication to provide verbal communication guidelines for identifying care in online responses in Chinese. Details of operational definitions and examples of caring and noncaring codes in English are shown in Tables B1 and B2.

## Coding Process

The first two researcher coders were invited to classify the text independently to determine whether they showed caring messages or not. Based on the hypothesis coding process, they first learned from the code scheme guidelines (Table B1) to guide their evaluation of the response text. As this study aims to identify whether physicians’ online responses express caring (emotional expression), we used emotion coding to label the response texts as caring messages or noncaring messages.

Emotion coding labels the emotion participants experience or recall (Saldana, 2009), with emotion being defined as “a feeling and its distinctive thoughts, psychological and biological states, and range of propensities to act” (Goleman, 1995, p. 289). In the physician-driven OHC context, physicians convey an emotional appeal through caring messages by using comforting language that reflects good bedside manner (Kuruoglu et al., 2015), which is more easily expressed in offline settings.

Accordingly, following hypothesis and emotion coding, if the coders perceived caring in the physicians’ online responses and textual messages that corresponded to the operational definitions of caring (Table B1), they labeled the text as a caring (or non-caring) message accordingly. For instance, when a coder had a sense of mutuality and reciprocity when observing the response, they labeled it as a caring message. Meanwhile, as each physician’s response was anchored to a particular question, their way of showing caring varied in each response.

To examine the validity of this coding scheme, the two coders independently labeled 1,000 Q&A responses as a caring or non-caring message. The Cohen’s kappa measure used in our coding scheme is 0.76, indicating satisfactory interrater reliability (Straub et al. (2004). They then manually coded the remaining 4,048 response texts (2,024 for each coder). In total, 2,536 out of the 5,048 physician responses were categorized into the caring group. The manually coded 5,048 response messages were used for further evaluation.

## Caring Words Identification

We filtered text identified by the coders as caring messages, performed Chinese word segmentation on the sentences using pkuseg, and eliminated stop words. We excluded medical terms from the segmented results and then conducted word frequency statistics on these results. The original coders refined the segmentation results by removing words that did not meet caring standards, creating a caring words lexicon that provided a basis for calculating the number and ratio of caring words in the physician’s response texts.

## Robustness: New Coders from the Physician’s Perspective

To enhance the robustness of the coding perspective of the first coder group (with healthcare research experience taking the patient’s perspective), we invited the second coder group (medical students taking the physician’s perspective) to label the text independently using the same coding process. Altogether, we had four codes to represent both patients’ and physicians’ perspectives.

By comparing the coding results from the four coders in distinct groups, we observed that the evaluation consistency within the researcher group was 0.76, while among the medical student coders, it was 0.71. We then segmented all texts labeled as caring messages and performed a word comparison. The resulting consistency, measured at 0.74, signified the robust validity of the coding process. ## Appendix C: Difference-in-Differences (DID) Model

To address the different endogeneity mechanisms of the selection process and to reduce the impact of unobservable variables and measurement bias, this study employed the widely adopted time-varying difference-in-differences (DID) method to control for the effect from unobservable variables that vary with time and individuals.

Since the time spent engaging in Q&A responses varied with the physician, the standard DID was inappropriate for this study. We therefore used the time-varying DID method to examine the relationship between Q&A questions that were answered and medical service subscription. Widely used to eliminate the effect of unobservable variables that vary with time and individuals, DID allowed us to control for omitted variables while examining the relationship between physician online responses and patient medical service subscriptions. This data further supports the argument that physician online responses will motivate patients to purchase medical service subscriptions.

Specifically, this study followed the time-varying DID methods from Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) and Beck et al. (2010) to estimate the relationships among our main variables. This method has been adopted by the IS literature to estimate the treatment effect of various time points indicated, such as in Liu et al. (2021). The regression model is:

$$
Y _ {p t} = \alpha + \beta D _ {p t} + \delta X _ {p t} + A _ {p} + B _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {p t} (1)
$$

For Equation (1), this study selected a quarter as the time unit to balance each time point’s sample size. Hence, t is a time variable ranging from 1 to 8. $\mathrm { Y } _ { \mathrm { p t } }$ is a measure of medical service subscriptions that physician p received in time t. $A _ { p }$ and $B _ { t }$ are the vectors of dummy variables that account for time and individual fixed effects, respectively. $X _ { p t }$ is a set of physician-level time-variant control variables, and $\varepsilon _ { p t }$ is the error term. $D _ { p t }$ is a dummy variable that equals 1 in the time t after physician $p$ first answered a patient’s question in the Q&A section. Therefore, this study’s interest, coefficient β indicates the impact of physician online responses on medical service subscriptions. A positive and significant β suggests that these responses significantly bring more medical service subscriptions. In contrast, a negative and significant β indicates that physician online responses decrease medical service subscriptions.

Study results shown in Table C1 confirm that physicians’ online responses are significantly positively associated with medical service subscription $( \beta = 0 . 8 6 5 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which indicates they will promote medical service subscriptions. Furthermore, authoritative reputation is also positively associated with medical service subscription $( \beta = 0 . 1 5 2 1 , p <$ 0.001), which is consistent with the effect of authoritative reputation in our main results. These analyses further support our main results.
