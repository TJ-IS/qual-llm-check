## A B S T R A C T

Online agent bidding is seen as a win–win solution for both bidders and auctioneers in online auctions. However, it has not been as widely adopted as expected. Based on information systems (IS) habit literature, we developed the agent bidding habit and use model (ABHUM) to investigate the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence bidders’ agent bidding use. We propose that perceived bidding agent effectiveness, agent bidding winning experience and bidder rationality are the antecedents of agent bidding habit. Together with perceived bidding agent effectiveness and bidder rationality, agent bidding habit also in<sup>fl</sup>uences agent bidding use. The social presence of other agent bidders is a moderator in the model. We con<sup>fi</sup>rm our model in the context of Taobao, which is the largest electronic online market in China. Our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide new insights for both researchers and practitioners in this <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## 1. Introduction

Online bidding has become one of the most popular forms of ecommerce. Consumers can easily purchase numerous products online, at a price they have negotiated, without stepping out of their homes. Unlike traditional auctions, online auctions usually are of longer duration, with a deadline set beforehand, to attract more potential bidders from the entire Internet. The newer features of online auctions have resulted in the emergence of new bidding strategies, such as agent bidding and snipe bidding, which, in addition to traditional bidding strategies,<sup>1</sup> have attracted researchers’ interest [1–5].

Agent bidding refers to the use of a software bidding agent to bid in online auctions. The agent software bids at the minimum level required to outbid the current highest price until the bid exceeds the reservation price set beforehand by the bidder [6]. Its advantages of automation and <sup>fl</sup>exibility can reduce the time and cost associated with tracking and monitoring an auction [1]. In addition, agent bidding can bene<sup>fi</sup>t both auctioneers and bidders, unlike snipe bidding, which favours only the bidders.<sup>2</sup> Therefore, researchers have recommended agent bidding to both bidders and auctioneers [7,8]. However, agent bidding has not been as widely adopted as originally expected. A recent study showed that snipe bidding is still the major bidding strategy of Taobao bidders [4].

Some studies have attributed the reluctance of bidders to use agent bidding to their lack of understanding of the working process<sup>3</sup> [1] or their intention to bid with <sup>fl</sup>exibility of changing strategy from time to time [9]. However, few studies have empirically investigated the underlying rationale behind agent bidding use or ways to cultivate this behaviour, particularly related to how a previous habit of agent bidding use affects continual agent bidding use. Based on the current research gap, we propose the following research questions:

1. What are the antecedents of agent bidding use? Does agent bidding habit affect agent bidding use?

2. What are the antecedents of agent bidding habit? Do perceived bidding agent effectiveness, agent bidding winning experience and bidder rationality affect agent bidding habit?

3. Does the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence of other agent bidders affect the relationship between agent bidding habit and use?

Based on the information systems (IS) habit literature, we propose the agent bidding habit and use model (ABHUM), which is based on the assumption that perceived bidding agent effectiveness, agent bidding habit and bidder rationality all in<sup>fl</sup>uence agent bidding use. In it, we propose that perceived bidding agent effectiveness, agent bidding winning experience and bidder rationality are the antecedents of bidding habit. We also add the situational factor to the model: the social presence of other agent bidders as a moderator between agent bidding use and its two antecedents, perceived bidding agent effectiveness and agent bidding habit.

The remainder of this paper describes the background and further motivation on this literature, followed by our research model. We then explain how we tested our model with 261 Taobao users in the People’s Republic of China. Our model was largely con<sup>fi</sup>rmed, leading to several important contributions. This study contributes to the body of online auction research by speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focusing on agent bidding strategy and its antecedents. The con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the effect of agent bidding habit on use indicates the important role played by habit, echoing the recommendation to focus on unreasoned actions in IS studies, especially for continual behaviours [10]. Exploring the antecedents of habit also enriches theory development in this area. Furthermore, this study’s research <sup>fi</sup>ndings can help online auctioneers to understand their customers. In addition to improving bidding agent software and cultivating agent bidding, habit is also very important in increasing agent bidding use. Promotions of agent bidding can attract new users and retain current ones; however, targeting experienced agent bidders is more effective in forming their agent bidding habit. Once the habit is formed, agent bidding use becomes natural. In this way, both bidders and auctioneers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the wide adoption of agent bidding.

## 2. Research background

## 2.1. Agent bidding in online auctions

Research on online auctions has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several bidding strategies, among which agent bidding was recommended by researchers to bene<sup>fi</sup>t both bidders and auctioneers to sustain the development of online auctions [2,7,8]. Agent bidding <sup>fi</sup>rst appeared when auction websites provided some bidding agent (or proxy) to assist bidders. It refers to the use of a software bidding agent in online auctions. A reservation price is set beforehand, and the bidding agent helps one bidder outbid others until the current price exceeds the reservation price. Bidders may choose thirdparty software bidding agents or the bidding agent service provided by the auction website [11].

The wide adoption of agent bidding in an English auction<sup>4</sup> would make it similar to a second-price one.<sup>5</sup> The reservationprice a bidder sets in the bidding agent is similar to a price sealed in an envelope. The difference between agent bidding and a second-price auction is that the winner using agent bidding pays slightly more than the second-highest bid (usually the second-highest bid plus the minimum bid increment) [12], of which the mechanism is similar to a sealed second-price auction (also known as a Vickrey auction [13]). This bidding model alleviates bidder concerns regarding overpaying for products. Agent bidding also confers the advantage of maximising consumer surplus [1]. It can keep bids lower than the reserve price, hence resulting in a lower loss of consumer surplus. Moreover, a study on online, single-unit auctions indicated that agent bidding can help save bidders money and give them more chances to win [4]. Rogers et al. [6] developed a model for analysing the effects of a bidding agent and a minimum bid increment on the total auction revenue of eBay—when bidders adopt agent bidding, there is an optimal value for the minimum bid increment at which the auctioneer’s revenue is maximised [6].

In addition, agent bidding provides more <sup>fl</sup>exibility for bidders, as they do not have to wait online near the end of the auction to bid. A reservation price can be set beforehand to reduce the time and cost associated with tracking and monitoring the auction; this model is especially attractive for those who value their time [1]. A special software bidding agent that has a time-tracking feature can facilitate other bidding strategies, such as snipe bidding [11].

Finally, agent bidding guarantees successful bids because it avoids the network traf<sup>fi</sup>c jam (such as snipe bidding) at the end of auctions, ensuring that higher bids are not missed, which bene<sup>fi</sup>ts both bidders and auctioneers. It can also help auctioneers to encourage more potential bidders to participate in their auctions and reduce the undesired phenomenon of too few bids. Therefore, the wide application of agent bidding is critical for auction websites that wish to retain their customer base. However, researchers have found that this bidding strategy is not dominant in eBay as expected [14]. Some have explained that the low adoption of agent bidding may be due to the fact that bidders do not fully understand the process or to a bidder preference for the option to revise their reservation price at will [9]. However, these reasons have seldom been empirically investigated.

In fact, the existing literature lacks agent bidding-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investigation. Most prior research has investigated agent bidding together with other bidding strategies. However, a few studies have empirically investigated the antecedents of agent bidding use, such as the effect of agent bidding habit. Moreover, the existing literature on agent bidding lacks theoretical support, because most of it is data-oriented. Therefore, this study contributes to the literature by investigating the effect of agent bidding habit on agent bidding use, based on the previous IS habit literature.

## 2.2. Habit and its applications in IS research

As a research construct, habit can be traced back to 1890, when the philosopher William James [15] <sup>fi</sup>rst described its signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in daily life. Habit is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the behavioural tendency to repeat responses, given a stable supporting context [16], or as learned

sequences of acts that become automatic responses to certain situations or stimuli [17].

Research shows that to cultivate a habit it is necessary to repeat or practice a behaviour several times—that is, related experiences are prerequisite to habit formation. Stable contexts are also needed for habit formation, and it is typically necessary to repeat a behaviour at least once [16]. The underlying reason for this requirement is the fact that stable contexts enable individuals to make appropriate responses with minimal attention; in stable contexts, a behaviour is performed automatically once a habit has been developed [16]. This means that sporadic conscious intention and minimal thought are required to initiate action [18]. Hence, when behaviour is driven by habit, it becomes non-re<sup>fl</sup>ective, suppressing conscious intention to behave differently, because habitual behaviours require little cognitive processing and intentional control. In other words, habit can reduce response time. As James [15] remarked, ‘habit simpli<sup>fi</sup>es the movements required to achieve a given result, makes them more accurate and diminishes fatigue’ (p. 112).

Some researchers have noted the important role of habit. For example, Triandis et al. proposed a model of interpersonal behaviour in 1977 (further developed in 1980), in which they synthesised the effects of intention, habit and situation conditions on interpersonal behaviour. Intention is also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by other factors, such as social norms, self-identity and perceived consequence and affect. One of the biggest contributions of Triandis et al.’s model to behavioural studies is that it highlighted the importance of habit, in addition to intention. Although developed for interpersonal behaviour, it drew attention to unreasoned actions in addition to reasoned ones, which have been widely applied to the continual usage of information systems.

In IS research, habit has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the extent to which people perform behaviours (use IS) automatically because of learning’ [19,p. 705]. In a study of the use of a business-toconsumer (B2C) website, Liao et al. [20] found that in addition to perceived usefulness and trust, habit was a key driver of continual use. Similar results were found in an online consumer retention study [21]. Hong et al. [22] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed habit as a key determinant of behavioural intention or use behaviours of agile information systems, and Venkatesh et al. [23] con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of habit among mobile Internet consumers in their acceptance and use of technology. The incumbent system habit is so strong that it might even become an obstacle to the acceptance of new systems [24]. Limayem and Cheung [25] demonstrated that habit could moderate the path from IS continuance intention to actual usage, and some researchers even treated habit directly, as a pattern of behaviour rather than a determinant, because they believed continual use at different points of time could re<sup>fl</sup>ect the power of habit [26–28].

Although the literature on IS usage has paid attention to the role of habit, most of the studies were conducted based on the traditional theory of planned behaviour, or reasoned action, even though some researchers suggested that these theories may not provide the best theoretical foundation for IS continual usage studies [10]. The role played by habit in IS continual usage may be much more strong than initially thought by IS researchers [29].

Therefore, in this study, we focus on the habitual rather than the intentional factors of agent bidding use, which is a continual behaviour in online auctions. In line with previous research, we believe habit is a highly important factor in choosing online bidding strategies. We also examine the antecedents of agent bidding habit and the moderator between agent bidding habit and use. In the next section, we propose the agent bidding habit and use model (ABHUM) to better explain and predict the use of the agent bidding strategy in online auctions.

## 3. Proposing the agent bidding habit and use model (ABHUM)

To examine the relationship between agent bidding habit and use, we propose the ABHUM, which is based on previous IS habit literature. When investigating the acceptance of auction websites, some scholar built a model of its antecedents, which include: 1) incentives to use an auctioning system, 2) experience with auctions, (3) social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and (4) the habitual use of auctioning systems [30]. We adapted the exiting model to agent bidding use by developing the factors of (1) perceived effectiveness of agent

Within the ABHUM, the perceived effectiveness of agent bidding, agent bidding winning experience and agent bidding habit apply to general situations; by contrast, the social presence of other agent bidders and agent bidding apply to specific auctions. ## 3.1. The antecedents of choosing agent bidding As the bidding strategy

We propose that, in the online bidding context, bidder perception in the bidding agent’s effectiveness positively affects acclimation to and adoption of the agent. That is, the more effective or useful a bidding agent is, the easier it is to use. The well-known technology acceptance model also indicates that perceived usefulness positively affects the intention to use and subsequent behaviour of technology use [32–34]. A positive perception of a bidding agent may foster bidders’ further use of agent bidding. Therefore,

H1. Perceived bidding agent effectiveness is positively associated with agent bidding use.

The Triandis [35] model indicated that habit is an important factor in shaping behaviour. Again, several studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed habit as an important predictor in the continual used of IS [20,22,23] (see Section 2.2). Researchers believe that habit deserves strong consideration in systems use and adoption models [29] and have advocated for empirical studies that place a much greater emphasis on unplanned, or unreasoned, action [10]. We believe that habit also plays an important role in the online bidding context in this study. To keep it on the same level with other variables in the model, we have conceptualised habit as a determinant of use behaviour rather than a particular behavioural process. When bidders form a habit of using the bidding agent, the chance of using agent bidding the next time is increased, because having a habit implies non-re<sup>fl</sup>ective processing [18]. Therefore,

## H2. Agent bidding habit is positively associated with agent bidding use.

Traditional auction theories assume that all bidders are rational. However, in reality, people are not fully rational [31]. Some people are more likely to make rational decisions in accordance with the principles of economics, while others exhibit irrational behaviour more often. It follows that researchers should investigate the effects of bidder rationality when it comes to bidding strategy, including agent bidding use. Agent bidding, as a bidding strategy that effectively maximises consumer surplus and saves monitoring time [1], would be widely adopted if every bidder acted in a fully rational manner. However, some people may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by irrational factors and by bounded rationality, hence leading to a lack of agent bidding adoption. The less rational a bidder is, the less likely it is that he/she uses bidding agents. Therefore,

H3. Bidder rationality is positively associated with agent bidding use.

## 3.2. The moderating effect of agent bidder social presence

It has long been believed that online technologies facilitate socialisation and enhance social presence in the context of online auctions [36]. On the other hand, online bidding is rarely a solitary experience. Other bidders’ participation creates a competitive climate that in<sup>fl</sup>uences bidder behaviour. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in this study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne agent bidder social presence as the virtual presence of the other agent bidders. The virtual presence of bidders (e.g., their photos, instant messaging, and auction proxy, etc.) has been found to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect both bidding strategy and market outcomes in online auctions [37]. Accordingly, agent bidder social presence is believed to affect agent bidding use. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, their social presence may moderate the effects between the antecedents (including bidding agent effectiveness and bidding agent habit) and the outcome (agent bidding use), but in different ways.

The social presence of other agent bidders is usually revealed by their bids in the auction. When one bidder is aware that other bidders are using a bidding agent to submit their bids, this should con<sup>fi</sup>rm his/her perception in the bidding agent’s effectiveness. The con<sup>fi</sup>rmation further strengthens the positive effect of the perceived effectiveness of the bidding agent on agent bidding use. Hence, the bidder becomes more likely to use the bidding agent, which is consistent with the herd effect phenomenon (in which individuals in a group mimic each other’s strategies, either consciously or subconsciously) [38,39]. Therefore,

H4a. Agent bidder social presence positively moderates the relationship between perceived bidding agent effectiveness and agent bidding use.

Some recent studies found that the relationship between habit and behavioral intention/actual behavior usually was moderated by some demographic/background factors [23,40]. For example, the relationship between the community participation habit and behavior was found to be moderated by the members’ membership and tenure [40]. Age, gender, and experience together were also found to moderate the effect of the habit of using information technology on the behavioral intention and actual behavior [23]. We generalize this pattern into our study, proposing the important situational factor in agent bidding, social presence of other agent bidders, as the moderator between agent bidding habit and use. Online auction is like a game against other bidders and therefore, bidders may change their agent bidding strategy from their habit when facing different rival bidders.

In online auctions, bidders might also be aware that more bids could increase the <sup>fi</sup>nal price of an auction [41]. Although more bids using bidding agents con<sup>fi</sup>rm the effectiveness of this strategy, bidders might still worry about the <sup>fi</sup>nal price and, as a result, reconsider their bidding strategy (perhaps choosing a strategy other than agent bidding). In such cases, the effect of agent bidding habit may be reduced. Hence, the high social presence of other agent bidders might cause the avoidance effect, in which bidders try to avoid the winner’s curse [13]. If so, this avoidance effect could undermine the positive relationship between habit and strategy. Therefore,

H4b. Agent bidder social presence negatively moderates the relationship between bidding habit and agent bidding use.

## 3.3. The antecedents of agent bidding habit

As mentioned, habit formation is affected by the degree of satisfaction and the frequency of previous behaviour [19]. With respect to online auctions, we hypothesise that the above factors affect a bidder’s habit of using a bidding agent. According to the expectation–con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory applied in technology use contexts, the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of one expectation leads to user satisfaction [42–44]. Generally, whether users are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the system they employ depends on the effectiveness of the system and the users’ subjective cognition of whether their expectations were met, and this has been shown to be true with digital agents in general [45]. Hence, the characteristics of the bidding agent system, especially its effectiveness at the level of user interaction, should largely determine user satisfaction. The satisfaction should then facilitate the formation of the corresponding habit of using the system [46] (i.e. agent bidding habit). Therefore,

## H5. Perceived bidding agent effectiveness is positively associated with agent bidding habit.

In terms of the frequency of use, we propose that agent bidding winning experience—especially the positive experience of winning via agent bidding—affects the formation of agent bidding habit. Similar patterns have been shown by social psychologists in other economic “winning” contexts. The experience of winning (or almost winning) generates emotional and physiological arousal, the tension and excitement of which can lift self-esteem and create habit or even addiction towards the behaviours that created the arousal [47,48]. For example, in the context of gambling, experiences with winning fuel habit formation and even addiction [47,48]. Interestingly, almost winning or near misses can have the same effect, as they increase the prospects of future success in people’s minds [48]. Such addictive behaviours caused by “winning” are seen extensively in traders who experience success in <sup>fi</sup>nancial market trading, from online investing to hedge fund management [49,50]. It has been shown that the ease, anonymity and accessibility of online technologies can worsen such habits and addictions and serve as underlying catalysts [51,52]. Consequently, we predict that bidders who have more experience winning when using a bidding agent online are more likely to repeat the same strategy and behaviours, gradually forming a habit. Therefore,

## H6. Agent bidding winning experience is positively associated with agent bidding habit.

The transformative learning theory indicates that individual rationality might negatively affect the formation of a habit through constant re<sup>fl</sup>ective thinking and behavioural changes [53]. We propose that the same principle holds in the online bidding context. Rational bidders exhibit greater readiness to change or adjust their behaviour, which makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to form a habit, because repetition of a particular behaviour is a prerequisite for habit formation [16,19,54]. Rational bidders’ re<sup>fl</sup>ective thinking, which is associated with individual rationality, may hinder the creation of agent bidding habit formation by causing one to rationally evaluate whether or not to use it. Therefore, we propose that:

H7. Bidder rationality is negatively associated with agent bidding habit.

## 4. Methodology

In this study, we examined the speci<sup>fi</sup>c agent bidding use of bidders. All the data collected in this study are related to Chinese bidders participating in the Taobao marketplace, the biggest ecommerce platform in China. Most of the data were collected through an online questionnaire, which was designed to gather online bidders’ subjective evaluations of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c auction in which they had just participated individually. Additional objective data were collected from the auction. The subjective and objective data were combined to test the proposed model and to reduce measurement bias. LISREL was applied to test the measurement model and SmartPLS to test the path model. Partial least squares (PLS) regression has been deemed appropriate when testing a new model for which an established nomological network does not exist in the literature [55,56].

## 4.1. Measure development

To build measures with construct validity, we reviewed the previous IS and social psychology literature. All measures were reviewed by experts and pilot tested prior to <sup>fi</sup>nal implementation. Most of the measures employed in the questionnaire were composed of multiple statements to which the respondents were asked to respond using a seven-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The exceptions were the measures of winning experience and social presence. The former was composed of one question that asked how many auctions respondents had won using the agent bidding strategy; this question was designed to measure the respondents’ agent bidding winning experience. Agent bidder social presence was measured by the number of agent bids made by other bidders in the auction, according to objective observation (all the agent bids were marked in the publicly available bidding list, as indicated in the sample in Appendix B).

The measures of perceived bidding agent effectiveness were based on a study by Pavlou and Gefen [57], who measured the perceived effectiveness of a feedback mechanism by investigating its accuracy, availability, effectiveness and reliability. They also measured the perceived effectiveness of escrow services through the guarantee they provide in terms of function. Based on the characteristics of the bidding agents, we used effectiveness, reliability and service quality, and functional guarantees as the dimensions for the measure of perceived bidding agent effectiveness.

The measures for assessing agent bidding habit were taken from [19], changing the research context from the World Wide Web to the bidding agent. The three items used to measure bidding habit were obvious choice, natural behaviour and automatic behaviour.

Bidder rationality was measured in this study using an instrument developed by Epstein et al. [58]. Among the 10 items measuring intuitive–experiential and analytical–rational thinking styles, the <sup>fi</sup>ve items of the latter were selected to measure bidder rationality. With two reverse-coded items, the instrument mainly measured the degree of bidder preference for complex and challenging thinking.

Because no multi-item measure for agent bidding use exists in the literature, we developed one based on descriptions in the previous literature [1,6]. Three items—one measuring the preference for using a bidding agent, one the extent of agent bidding and one the reliance on a bidding agent—were developed.

The questionnaire was pretested by three IS professors with expertise in survey research and auctions. Their feedback was used to improve the questions’ content validity. The questionnaire was then translated into Chinese by one author. The other author helped to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the translation to ensure content validity of the Chinese version. A pilot test of the translated questionnaire was performed using 35 online bidders. Cronbach’s alphas were calculated to test the reliability of the questionnaire using their responses. All the values were above 0.7. Because the last item of bidder rationality had a low item-to-total correlation (<0.5), it was dropped from the questionnaire. We also conducted interviews with the respondents after the questionnaire was completed to get feedback on the questionnaire’s wording and clarity. Revisions were then made to reduce ambiguity and improve readability.

## 4.2. Data collection

In this study, we examined online bidders’ speci<sup>fi</sup>c agent bidding use in one of their auctions, rather than their overall bidding strategies. Therefore, we collected data from bidders through one auction in which they had participated. We randomly selected recently completed auctions on Taobao.com so that bidders would likely be able to vividly recall the details of their recent auction. First, the bidders near the end of the bidding list of each auction were examined (please refer to the sample in Appendix B). To avoid fake identities, only the individuals whose identities had been previously veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by Taobao.com were contacted. A total of 809 bidders were contacted through the online instant messenger WangWang. Along with the invitation to participate, we sent a brief introduction to the study, including its research objectives and requirements, to each of the selected bidders. The bidders who responded positively were asked to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that they use agent bidding and are able to recall the bidding situation of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c auctions they had participated in. They were then asked to complete an online questionnaire hosted by a professional online survey <sup>fi</sup>rm (www.surveymonkey.com). A reward of RMB 10 was paid for each successfully completed questionnaire. After two rounds of reminders, 261 bidders successfully completed the questionnaire without any missing data, yielding a strong response rate of 32.6%. Data was then collected from the auctions in which the bidders participated to calculate social presence. Again, this construct was measured by counting the bids submitted by the bidding agents of other bidders in the auction. Importantly, the standardised value was used to reduce the contextual variation that could have resulted from different auctions.

## 4.3. Respondents

We <sup>fi</sup>rst tested for non-response bias by comparing the gender, age and education of those who responded to our questionnaire on time and those who responded after the deadline. No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences were found between the groups, suggesting no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant non-response bias. Among the 261 respondents, 102 were male and 159 were female. Their age distribution was as follows: 31.1% were below 24 years old, 59.0% were 25–38 years old and 9.9% were over 38 years old. A total of 80.8% had a university education, 10.7% had completed high school and 8.5% had less than a high school education. In terms of income, 28.4% of the respondents made below RMB 2000 each month, 60.2% earned between RMB 2000 and 6000 each month and 11.4% made more than RMB 6000 each month. Students formed the biggest percentage of the respondents (20.7%), followed by IT workers (14.6%), salespersons and market workers (13.0%) and government and public employees (11.5%). Most of the respondents (73.1%) had won between one and 20 auctions; only 10.2% had never won an auction.

## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Measurement model analysis

Content validity, convergent validity and discriminant validity were examined to validate the measurement model. Content validity was established using measures from the literature and reviewing these measures with IS professors and experienced online bidders to ensure consistency between the items and variables. We performed con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis using LISREL 8.51 to assess the measurement model. The overall model <sup>fi</sup>t was assessed using <sup>fi</sup>ve goodness-of-<sup>fi</sup>t indices: the chi-square (164.3, df = 71), non-normalised <sup>fi</sup>t index (0.96), comparative <sup>fi</sup>t index (0.97), standardised root mean residual (0.057) and root mean square error of approximation (0.071). The results of these indices met the recommended criteria, suggesting an adequate <sup>fi</sup>t of the measurement model and convergent validity. Convergent validity was also assessed by examining Cronbach’s a and the average variance extracted (AVE) of the scales. As shown in Table 1, the Cronbach’s a values ranged from 0.819 to 0.936, all of which were higher than the recommended threshold of 0.7. In addition, all the variables’ AVEs (underlined and bolded in the diagonal cells of Table 1) were above 0.6, higher than the recommended threshold of 0.5 [59].

Discriminant validity was assessed by comparing the AVEs and the covariance shared by the variables. As shown in Table 1, the covariance shared by each pair of variables (off-diagonal) was smaller than the AVEs of each variable. Hence, discriminant validity was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed [59]. Common-method variance (CMV) was examined using Harmon’s single-factor test using con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis [60]. In the model, all the items of the multi-item measures were targeted to a single factor. The model <sup>fi</sup>t indices (chi-square/df = 1716.46/77, RMSEA = 0.30, NNFI = 0.50, CFI = 0.58 and standardised RMR = 0.19) indicated a bad model <sup>fi</sup>t, which also suggested that no serious CMV was likely in our study. The multicollinearity of the variables was examined by the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF). The resulting VIF values for all the variables were excellent (between 1.009 and 1.219).

## 5.2. The path model

After the assessment of the measurement model, PLS was applied to test the model and hypotheses. SmartPLS version 2.0 [61] was run, and 500 resamples were used for the bootstrapping analysis. All the data were centralised to reduce any multicollinearity, and the analysis results are shown in Fig. 2. The results showed that the explanatory power of this research model is high, with the R<sup>2</sup> of the dependent variables of agent bidding at 46.8%. All the hypotheses were supported at p 0.10, p 0.05 and p 0.01, except for H5. In line with similar articles [62–66], we included p = 0.10 for partial signi<sup>fi</sup>cance because this is preliminary research on a new theoretical model.

## 6. Discussion

Again, we tested our proposed research model on user-to-agent bidding with 261 users of Taobao in China. As expected, our model supported most of the hypotheses, indicating the important role of habit in agent bidding use. However, the negative relationship between bidder rationality and agent bidding habit was not supported. We believe there may be another positive effect of bidder rationality that counteracts the hypothesised negative effect. In fact, rationality and habit are not exact opposites, especially when it comes to good habits that can be considered rational. With respect to such habits, rational re<sup>fl</sup>ective checks can con<sup>fi</sup>rm the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of continual engagement in the habit rather than spurring a change in behaviour. Based on the results of this study, we believe that rational bidders identify both the negative aspects of agent bidding habits (such as raising the price by too many bids or leaking one’s reservation price to others) and positive ones (such as reducing monitoring time and increasing convenience). Because of these opposite and simultaneous effects, we concluded that the results show no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between bidder rationality and agent bidding habit.

## 6.1. Implications

This study contributes to the literature on online auctions. First, it focuses on an important, yet neglected, bidding strategy—agent bidding. It also con<sup>fi</sup>rms the factors that may affect bidders’ use of an agent bidding strategy. The study also validates the application of habit to online auction research, which enriches the development of theory related to online auctions. Furthermore, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study can help researchers to understand various bidder behaviours in online auctions. In addition to improving bidding agent software, cultivating among bidders the habit of using such software is very important.

This study’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings also provide valuable insights that help practitioners (e.g. online auction sites similar to Taobao) to increase bidders’ adoption of bidding agents. First, our results highlight the critical need to continually improve bidding agents, because perceived effectiveness of a bidding agent has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and direct effect on its adoption. Software developers and designers may consider developing a new generation of bidding agents, such as intelligent systems that can make recommendations to bidders [69], participate in and bid across multiple auctions [70] or consider the time feature in submitting bids. It is also important to create bidding agents that embrace the human–computer interaction concepts of usability, trust-building and familiarity, which have been shown to increase online agent adoption [71].

Second, this study can help practitioners to understand the importance of cultivating the agent bidding habit. The standardised coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between agent bidding habit and agent bidding use (0.528) suggests that practitioners may need to help bidders to establish this habit. Initially, advertisements and offers of training in the use of bidding agents could be used to attract new users. However, the retention of old users, especially those who have a great deal of winning experience, would be more effective. Auctioneers may consider providing discounts/promotions for more experienced bidders who are most likely to continue to use their bidding agents. In that way, their agent bidding habit may be encouraged.

Third, rational bidders are more likely to use a bidding agent. Bidder pro<sup>fi</sup>les may need to be created to allow auctioneers to learn more about their customers and to provide relevant service. Auctioneers could consider administering a questionnaire to get information directly from their customers, or they could examine their bidders’ online bidding habits.

## 6.2. Limitations and future research

One limitation of our study is the accuracy of some of its measures. For example, we used agent bidding winning experience because it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for bidders to recall all the auctions in which they have used agent bidding strategy. However, this design may neglect the possible effect of an auction losing experience. In the future, more data should be collected to investigate such an effect. In addition, when we measured agent bidding winning experience, we used the number of previous wins. The use of the absolute value needs to be further evaluated. In the future, the percentage of wins or additional subjective self-report items could be included to complement the current measure. Another example is social presence, which uses the numbers of agent bids of other bidders. Although we tried to collect data from bidders who had bids near the end of the auction to make sure they had the chance to examine the bids of others, we still had to assume that they had paid attention to other bidders’ bidding behaviour, which may not have always been the case. Some subjective items of social presence could be applied to future studies to ensure the validity of the measure.

Second, we did not consider other learning theories, such as operant conditioning and social learning theory, which could provide alternative explanations. Likewise, we did not include certain motivational or interpersonal factors in our model, such as the various intrinsic motivations that may be key drivers of behaviours involving online technology [34,44,72]. Hence, we believe further consideration of motives, especially on the lesserknown intrinsic level, could help to explain some of the missing variants of agent bidding use (e.g. enjoying the process of winning in an auction). Future research should extend our model to study the relationship between motivation and agent bidding strategies.

Third, this study collected data from a single auction website, Taobao, in a Chinese context. Therefore, caution should be taken when generalising the results of this research to online auctions outside China, in which cultural differences might moderate various factors, such as the development of habit and the perception of bidding agent effectiveness. IS and e-commerce research, for example, has established that Chinese culture exhibits a different decision-making and trust-building mind-set (e.g. collectivism and guanxi orientation) in using technology to interact with others, as compared to Western cultures [73–75]. This fact alone has been a challenge to Taobao, because Chinese people lean towards relationship-based economic transactions [76] and are hence traditionally hesitant to conduct economic transactions with strangers [77].

Finally, although it is now generally assumed that trust is a prerequisite for online agent use [71,78], it could be useful to add agent and institutional trust to the baseline model, especially when considering cross-cultural differences or when considering design artefacts that increase online bidding agent use.

## 6.3. Conclusion

This study enriches the understanding of online bidding use from the perspectives of cognitive learning and habit shaping; it also provides a validated conceptual model (ABHUM). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm that perceived bidding agent effectiveness, agent bidding habit and bidder rationality signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the use of an agent bidding strategy. Agent bidding winning experience also in<sup>fl</sup>uences agent bidding habit, and agent bidder social presence moderates the relationship between the two in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factors (i.e. perceived bidding agent effectiveness and agent bidding habit) and agent bidding use. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings shed light on the importance of cultivating bidders’ agent bidding habit in online auctions and provide insights into online bidding, which many auction websites are currently exploring.
