# Two Worlds of Trust for Potential E-Commerce Users: Humans as Cognitive Misers

Ben Q. Liu

Department of Information Systems, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon, Hong Kong SAR, ben.liu@cityu.edu.hk

Dale L. Goodhue

MIS Department, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, Georgia 30606, dgoodhue@terry.uga.edu

n this paper we consider the impact of trust on a new visitor’s intention to revisit a website, but instead of Iusing the typical expectancy-value theories as our conceptual basis, we look at the issue from the perspective of cognitive complexity and “humans as cognitive misers.” Starting with the suggestion that it is cognitively taxing to distrust, we propose that in order to conserve on cognitive resources, once a new visitor has convinced him or herself that a website is “trustworthy enough,” that user will drop trustworthiness from their concerns and only consider other characteristics of the website (e.g., task-technology fit, aesthetic appeal, etc.) in determining their revisit intention. This leads to what we call a “trust tipping point” and two different worlds of trust. Above the tipping point revisit intention is constructed in one way, and below the trust tipping point it is constructed in a quite different way. This perspective results in very different recommendations for web site designers as to the likely payoff from improving task-technology fit, aesthetic appeal, or trustworthiness, depending upon where their existing website stands relative to the trust tipping point. To test our hypotheses we used data from 314 student website users, and expanded a technique called piecewise regression (Neter et al. Applied Linear Statistical Models, 4th ed.) to allow us to analyze data as two different linear surfaces, joined at the tipping point. We found good support for our assertions that users operate differently above and below a trust tipping point.

Key words: accessibility-diagnosticity; bounded rationality; humans as cognitive misers; piecewise regression; task-technology fit; trust; web aesthetics

## Introduction

Consider the following questions about two hypothetical online vendors of laptop computers, both of which presumably have limited resources for website refinement: Should ZipBuy.com invest to increase its new users’ average trustworthiness perceptions, say from 6 to 6.5 (on a 7-point scale of trustworthiness)? If it did, would it increase the likelihood that those new users would revisit the site? Should JoesTech-Buy.com invest to raise new users’ average trustworthiness perceptions, from 5 to 5.5? If it did, would it increase the likelihood that those new users would revisit their site?

Certainly the answers to the above questions depend upon how potential customers create their intentions to revisit a website. For example, to what extent are they worried about being cheated on the transaction versus whether the website helps them find out about the most relevant laptops and their characteristics. Rather than answering such questions based on the more typical expectancy-value framework (e.g., theory of reasoned action, or theory of planned behavior), we propose answering them based on the notion of humans as “cognitive misers” (Feldman and Lynch 1988). Constantly worrying about the possible negative consequences of a trust violation is cognitively taxing. In general, and in considering behavior relative to a website specifically, humans presumably have a tendency to economize on cognitive effort by focusing on only some of the potentially important aspects of their situation. The suggestion is that they focus only on those aspects that are instantaneously most relevant to their decision, and that are available or easily retrievable from memory.

Returning to the laptop computer website example, we believe that when a potential laptop buyer doubts the trustworthiness of a website, his most immediate concern will be something like: “Will I be sorry if I use this website?” and the trustworthiness of the site is the most relevant characteristic to his concern. Furthermore, when trustworthiness is in doubt, tasktechnology fit (TTF) of the website, i.e., the extent to which the website facilitates the accomplishment of a user’s tasks<sup>1</sup> (Goodhue and Thompson 1995), may not be very relevant to a consumer’s immediate concern. For example, if our potential buyer is worried about whether the website is trustworthy, he may not devote a lot of attention to determining TTF, such as whether the website helps him find the best laptop for his needs.

On the other hand, when people are satisfied that the website is trustworthy enough, differences in the level of trustworthiness may not matter much. As a potential purchaser’s trust in a website increases, we argue that at some point (a “trust tipping point”) the potential purchaser accepts the website as trustworthy enough, stops worrying about the website cheating him, drops trustworthiness from his mental calculus, and devotes his attention to other reasons to use or not use the website, for example, its TTF. As we will argue, this “two worlds of trust” perspective is quite different from the reigning expectancy-value view of how revisit intentions are constructed, and leads to consequential differences in the way users create their intentions to revisit a website, depending upon whether their trustworthiness assessment is above or below a “trust tipping point.”

If true, this could have important implications for website designers. The prevalent expectancy-value models of trusting intention would suggest that investing to increase either the apparent trustworthiness or the TTF of a website would always strengthen consumers’ intention to revisit a website. The “two worlds of trust” perspective would suggest that above some tipping point, investment to increase the apparent trustworthiness of a website might well have no effect on reuse. Similarly, if a website’s trustworthiness was in doubt, improving TTF might bring no benefit.

There are certainly important questions raised by the “two worlds of trust” argument. For example, how much trust is enough? What set of attribute values in a website is sufficient to engender the requisite amount of trust? Is the tipping point about the same for all consumers? We do not attempt to answer all such questions in this paper, but we do present evidence that consumers seem to operate in a fashion consistent with a trust tipping point. More specifically we seek to answer the following question: Will consumers form their intentions differently depending on whether their assessment of the website’s trustworthiness is above or below some tipping point?

This study makes a second contribution beyond developing the “two worlds of trust” model. It is the presentation of an additional possibility (multidimensional piecewise linear regression) in the set of methodological alternatives for testing the “two worlds of trust” model. Our model can be visualized as two flat surfaces connected at the tipping point. Each surface represents a mental calculus for creating intention to revisit the website. Piecewise regression allows us to estimate the tipping point in multidimensional space, and test for the different effects of the relevant variables on different sides of the tipping point.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review the trust literature and position the current study in the literature. The third section develops the hypotheses about the trust tipping point and how that tipping point leads to two different mental calculi. We then present the methods and data analysis to test our hypotheses. We conclude the paper with a discussion on the research findings and their theoretical and practical implications.

## Positioning This Study in the Trust Literature

A steady stream of literature has examined trust in the electronic environment. Although the context of this work includes more varied trust realms, the biggest part of it examines trust in a website. Much of this work can be seen as focusing on three general questions in the online domain: what are the antecedents of trust, what is the makeup of the trust construct, and what are the consequences of trust.

In the first category (the antecedents of trust), researchers have examined the impact of a number of factors, such as site quality (e.g., perceived ease of use, usefulness), site design (e.g., navigation, visual, information presentation), customer feedback, personalization, trust-assuring arguments, vendor reputation, institutional safeguards, social presence of the website, past experience (e.g., whether the consumer had experienced a “psychological contract violation”), familiarity with the website, general propensity to trust, etc. (see Appendix A). Another important dimension of this work looks at the impact of culture on trust and its impacts, with the suggestion that though the impact of trust may not differ across cultures, its antecedents probably do (Benbasat et al. 2008). From this body of work on antecedents we might conclude that a great many factors can influence a consumer’s trust in a given website. As of yet, researchers have not identified a parsimonious set of factors that are the primary determinants.

In the second category are studies that sought to understand the trust construct and its relationship to other related constructs such as distrust and risk. Perhaps most interesting in this category is the question of whether trust and distrust are two ends of the same continuum, or two different constructs that have different antecedents and different consequences. Some theoretical work argues that trust and distrust exist simultaneously as two separate constructs (Lewicki et al. 1998). Recent empirical work supports this notion, with evidence that trust and distrust are distinct and only modestly correlated constructs (McKnight and Choudhury 2006). Dimoka (2010) addressed this question using functional neuroimaging in trust versus distrust situations. Although from questionnaire data she found no psychometric evidence that trust and distrust were different constructs, neuroimaging data showed that distrust situations were processed in the part of the brain dealing with strong emotion and fear of loss, while trust situations were processed in the part of the brain dealing with reward, prediction, and resolving uncertainty. We will return to Dimoka’s findings when we consider the implications of our findings.

In the third category, where our own interest primarily lies, researchers have focused on how trusting beliefs are integrated into intentions to engage in trust-related behaviors. Our reading of this body of work is that most studies (across all three categories above) do include in their models a relationship between trusting beliefs and some behavioral consequence. It appears that intentions to interact with a website are clearly affected by trust, in addition to the traditional TAM constructs of usefulness and ease of use. Also, there are interesting suggestions that the impact of trust on intentions may be partly mediated by perceptions of risk, or that trust may have an impact on perceptions of usefulness, etc.

There is also the recognition that an individual’s trust in a website may develop over time. Many studies focus on initial trust (see Appendix A), i.e., trust in an unfamiliar e-vendor, one with whom the consumer has not engaged in trust-related behaviors such as purchasing (McKnight et al. 2002b). While a consumer has no first-hand experience with the vendor, trust is essential to overcome the perceptions of risk and to persuade him or her to transact with the vendor (McKnight et al. 2002b). With time and experience, the impact of trust may lessen and the impact of other factors such as usefulness may increase (Gefen et al. 2008). This is suggested by Gefen et al. (2003b) who found that for potential users (as opposed to users who had purchased from the particular website in the past), trust is a predictor of purchase intentions, but usefulness is not. This contrasted with their findings for experienced users for whom both usefulness and trust were predictors of intentions, but with trust having a much smaller value for experienced users than with potential users. Since our paper also focuses on potential users, these findings are particularly interesting to us. They are somewhat perplexing when looked at through the lens of linear expectancyvalue theories, perhaps more explicable when looked at using a longitudinal model, but they might also be consistent with our perspective of two worlds of trust.

Undoubtedly, the expectancy-value theories are the most popular theoretical lens in the online trust literature (see Appendix A). For the most part, in the trust literature and in work on intent to use information systems in general, these theories assume that people’s behavioral intentions are formed through an exhaustive assessment of all salient characteristics of the system. By far the majority of these assume a linear model of salient characteristics predicting intent, though there have been some studies that investigate quadratic and/or interaction effects (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2003, Venkatesh and Goyal 2010).

Our study focuses on the role of initial trust in creating intention to revisit a website. We draw on the “humans as cognitive misers” perspective assuming that humans operate under bounded rationality. For example, when deciding whether to revisit a website, they focus on those aspects most relevant to their immediate concern rather than engaging in an exhaustive processing of all potentially salient characteristics of the website. Following this line of thinking, we develop our “two worlds of trust” model for potential users in the next section.

## Theory Development

## Truth-Bias in Person, Greater Skepticism on the Web, and the Trust Tipping Point

Interestingly, extensive research has suggested that humans are often “truth-biased” in the offline setting; i.e., they tend to believe messages from other individuals, and are very poor at detecting when those individuals are not telling the truth (Levine et al. 1999). It has been suggested that perhaps such a bias is a useful heuristic—if most of the messages people hear are probably truthful, a truth-bias heuristic is an effective way to reduce cognitive complexity in human interactions (Millar and Millar 1997). Believing another person’s representations is less cognitively demanding than doubting them. Anderson et al. (1999, p. 395) suggest that “To disbelieve requires an extra effort 0 0 0 0 The truth-bias then is the default setting. It can be overcome only if the perceiver has ample motivation and sufficient cognitive resources to do the work of disbelieving.” However, it has also been shown that truth-bias is weaker (1) when the relationship between two parties is not well developed (Stiff et al. 1992) or (2) when the two parties communicate through less rich media (Boyle and Ruppel 2005).

It is worthwhile considering whether this truthbias also applies to web transactions. A little reflection suggests that the context of the interaction is an important factor. Many of the detection studies investigating the truth bias involved either face-to-face speech, or the viewing of an individual making statements on a video tape and did not involve an actual transaction. In everyday conversation between individuals it may well be true that most utterances are truthful, so a truth-bias might be cognitively efficient and functional, especially where mistakes are not too costly. But as the context changes, the default truthbias is almost certainly modified. For example, when dealing with a used car salesman, one might expect some embellishment of the truth; when approached on a street corner by someone offering to sell a Rolex watch, a certain amount of skepticism is almost certainly the default response.

Where does the context of a transaction on the web fit on this continuum? How different is it likely to be from the context of transactions in an unfamiliar brick and mortar shop? In fact it seems widely assumed that consumers have significant concerns about the trustworthiness of Internet websites as suggested by the following quotes: “There is, however, considerable evidence that users perceive significant risks and uncertainty in interacting with web-based vendors” (McKnight et al. 2002b). “In the context of e-commerce, because most transactions are conducted across large geographical distances, sight unseen, consumers are often very concerned that selling parties may not adhere to their transactional obligations.” (Kim et al. 2009).

We suggest therefore, that when considering transactions on the web, humans tend to be less truthbiased, and more cautious than they would be in shopping in a brick and mortar establishment. For whatever reason, it does seem that in potential website transactions, knowledgeable people are well aware that dishonest or incompetent online vendors could easily misrepresent their abilities or their intentions on a website, and therefore, potential buyers need to be vigilant and skeptical about the trustworthiness of a website. We would go further to suggest that such a shift from a truth-bias to a more cautious stance may explain why trusting beliefs are of such interest to researchers studying website intentions.

This extra skepticism may incur significant cognitive costs because there are so many aspects to worry about: product malfunctioning, monetary losses, privacy breaches, identity theft, spyware, “Trojan horse” worms, and viruses that can be unleashed simply by browsing a site. On the other hand, humans as “cognitive misers” tend to reduce cognitive complexity in judgment and decision-making whenever they can (Lynch et al. 1988). We believe that the tension between appropriate but cognitively expensive skepticism on the one hand, and the human desire to reduce cognitive complexity on the other, leads to a trust tipping point—once a person believes that a website is “trustworthy enough,” he or she stops worrying about the potential negative consequences. In other words, when trust reaches the tipping point, people simplify the remaining cognitive process by ignoring trustworthiness from that point forward. Once dropped from the cognitive process, differences in perceived trustworthiness will no longer affect the intention to revisit the website.

## The Psychological Origin of the Trust Tipping Point

Schwarz and Bohner’s (2001) work on attitude construction provides a psychological explanation for the trust tipping point. They argued that people’s attitude depends not only on beliefs about the target, but also on the beliefs about a standard of comparison or a reference point to which the target is compared. When forming an attitude towards a target, people retrieve potentially relevant information that is chronically accessible in memory or temporarily accessible due to contextual influence. The retrieved information will be used to form a mental representation of the target stimulus but also a mental representation of the reference point.

In the context of online trust, the standard of comparison may originate from the memory of a known trusted website, or from an ideal view of a trustworthy website. For example, when visiting a DVD rental website, a person assesses trustworthiness by asking questions such as “is this a known reputable firm like Netflix,” or “does this website display third party seals and customer feedback as a trustworthy DVD rental website would?” or “does this website have a money-back guarantee, or no-hassle return policy as Amazon.com does?” Similar to the “anchoring and adjustment” heuristic (Tversky and Kahneman 1974), comparison with a reference point as opposed to computing an absolute judgment of the trustworthiness of the site serves to reduce cognitive complexity.

When the uncertain website is close enough to the reference point or even goes above and beyond what the reference point does, users are, in a sense, relieved to be able to simplify the cognitive complexity of their task by categorizing the website as trustworthy. This allows them to stop worrying about negative outcomes, stop gathering and interpreting clues about trustworthiness or its opposite, and to continue with the now simpler cognitive task of evaluating a product on a trustworthy website. When a website compares too negatively with the reference point, people will continue to feel uncomfortable trusting it and are forced to continue the cognitively difficult process of deciding whether or not to trust the ambiguous site.

The trust tipping point is the point at which the characteristics of the uncertain website are close enough to the reference point that consumers accept as trustworthy and drop trustworthiness from the list of attributes they need to cognitively process. If there is such a tipping point as described above, we would expect to see differences in the impact of trust below and above that tipping point:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> For a new-to-the-user website, below the tipping point, trust in the website will significantly predict website revisit intentions, but above the tipping point, trust in the website will not significantly predict website revisit intentions.

## Further Differences in the Mental Calculus Above and Below the Trust Tipping Point

The tendency to reduce cognitive complexity leads humans to make decisions based on highly accessible information in memory, as suggested in the famous “availability heuristics” proposed by Tversky and Kahneman (1974). Feldman and Lynch’s (1988) accessibility-diagnosticity framework further suggests that the likelihood that any piece of information will be used as an input to judgment or decision is determined not only by its accessibility in memory but also by its “diagnosticity.”

Diagnosticity refers to the relevance of a belief to the focal behavior (Cohen and Reed 2006, Feldman and Lynch 1988). This concept assumes two key things: goal directedness and a principle of cognitive economy, as suggested in the following quotes: “No more information is retrieved for use in attaining a processing objective than is sufficient to allow the objective to be attained (Wyer and Srull 1986, p. 331)” and “We view the consumer as a ‘cognitive miser’ who attempts to make the decision using whatever information is salient. Other relevant inputs are retrieved from long-term memory or are sought externally only if the original salient information is insufficiently diagnostic to attain the task objective” (Lynch et al. 1988, p. 171). Therefore, because of the tendency to reduce cognitive complexity, an accessible belief will not be utilized if it is not relevant (or “diagnostic”) to the current processing object, because the increased cognitive complexity is not justified by the likely improvement in decision-making.

## Below the Tipping Point

We have suggested that above the tipping point, a user assumes the website is trustworthy and quits worrying about trust. However, below the tipping point the consumer has not necessarily decided that the website is untrustworthy. Rather, below the tipping point the user is still unsure of the trustworthiness of the site.<sup>2</sup> Until the consumer can convince herself that the website is trustworthy enough, the focus of the mental calculus has to be on whether the website can be trusted. In this situation consumers face a fairly difficult and onerous cognitive task. What cues on the website might be sufficient to overturn the fear of loss? One cue that is immediately accessible to consumers viewing a website is its aesthetic appeal and careful design (Cyr et al. 2009, 2010). The perception of web aesthetics (i.e., the extent to which a website is visually attractive and pleasing) is generated quickly and effortlessly, with very little cognitive load. Lindgaard et al. (2006) suggest that reliable judgments about web aesthetics can be made within 50 milliseconds.

In addition to being highly accessible, we propose that high aesthetic appeal has a signaling effect on perceptions of the website. It is reasonable to assume that to consumers, a carefully and effectively designed website signals intensity of purpose on the part of the company behind the website, and this is consistent with the legitimacy of the site. This signaling effect is often automatic and unconscious (rather than cognitively difficult), operating through the intuition system of the mind (Morewedge and Kahneman 2010).

High aesthetic appeal may also lead (again quickly and effortlessly) to a generally positive affective response to the website, or what Hassenzahl (2004) calls assessments of “goodness.” Therefore, the signaling effect is a broader cognitive and affective reaction than the specific cognitive trusting beliefs examined in this study (i.e., competence, integrity, and benevolence). Note that the signaling effect may also increase people’s trust in the website (as will be discussed later in the paper), but because it is automatic, effortless, and emotion-laden, it can directly inform the intention to revisit a website above and beyond the mediation of trust. All of this suggests that consumers can assess the aesthetic appeal and to some degree the “goodness” of a website with minimal cognitive load, and this makes it likely it will be taken into account in decisions about the website.

We are not suggesting that below the trust tipping point, an aesthetically appealing website will be enough to convince the user to revisit the site irrespective of other factors. Rather, if the website is poorly or unimaginatively designed, this signals to the consumer a lack of intensity of purpose on the part of the company behind the website, or more generally a lack of “goodness.” When this lack of “goodness” is combined with uncertain trustworthiness, there is little reason to continue to deliberate on whether it is a good website to do business with. In this case the user’s assessment of trust will be lower than the tipping point but not necessarily at the lowest level, aesthetic appeal will be low, and the intent to revisit will be quite low. On the other hand, if aesthetic appeal is high, but trust is still below the tipping point, users will continue to be uncertain about revisiting, and will have middle range (rather than low) intentions to revisit. We are suggesting that below the tipping point, intention to revisit varies primarily between neutral and negative. Therefore:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> For a new-to-the-user website, below the trust tipping point, the aesthetics will significantly predict website revisit intentions.

As long as trust is below the tipping point, the processing of TTF information will be affected, as described below. The principle of cognitive economy suggests that until skepticism about the trustworthiness of the website is resolved, it would be inefficient to attend to TTF information, since that determination involves many factors that have to be weighed against each other, an especially difficult cognitive process. If it is determined that the website is not trustworthy enough, no amount of TTF will be enough to change the decision not to revisit the website. Thus, unless and until the trust issue is resolved positively, TTF is not diagnostic to (will not affect) the decision to revisit the website. If the consumer’s conclusion is that trust is below the tipping point, it is already clear to that consumer that all cognitive effort spent evaluating TTF will be wasted. Therefore cognitive economy suggests that individuals will drop TTF from their cognitive calculations, when trust is below the tipping point. Only if the consumer gets serious about revisiting the website (because trustworthiness has been resolved positively) would TTF become diagnostic. Thus if trustworthiness is still uncertain, the level of a consumer’s intention to revisit will be determined by other factors than TTF. Therefore:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> For a new-to-the-user website, below the trust tipping point, TTF will not significantly predict website revisit intentions.

## Above the Tipping Point

Above the trust tipping point the website is deemed “trustworthy enough.” In this case, in order to conserve cognitive resources, consumers cease to worry about whether they can trust the site. One might say that they revert to the more cognitively comfortable truth-bias stance. Now the important question becomes whether the website provides relevant and tailored information, makes online transactions easier than traditional means, etc. In other words, on the high trust side of the tipping point TTF, even though cognitively difficult to assess, becomes critically diagnostic and thus will be used as input to decide whether to revisit the website.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> For a new-to-the-user website, above the tipping point, TTF will significantly predict website revisit intentions.

Above the tipping point of trust, consumers still respond to differences in website aesthetic appeal. With a trustworthiness assessment above the tipping point, consumers will be quite interested in the question of TTF, but a website’s aesthetic appeal still has an impact in two possible ways. First, if two websites are both seen as being trustworthy enough, and both have about the same level of TTF, it is quite reasonable to assume that the more appealing website will be the one the consumer will gravitate toward. Secondly, as stated earlier, website appeal is almost effortlessly assessed, and could have an automatic and unconscious signaling effect of “goodness” in general. Thus aesthetic appeal is likely still a component of a consumer’s mental calculus in creating intention to revisit a site. Therefore:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> For a new-to-the-user website, above the tipping point, web aesthetics will significantly predict website revisit intentions.

Hypotheses 1 through 5 together imply that there are “two worlds of trust.” On different sides of the tipping point, TTF, and trust aspects of a website operate in distinct ways to predict intent to revisit a website (see Figure 1).

## Relationships Between Trust, TTF, Web Aesthetics: A Process View

We recognize that the relationships between web aesthetics, trust, and TTF are also of interest to many. Here we briefly consider these relationships in the context of a process<sup>3</sup> that is implicit in the above arguments. On first viewing a website, the initial question that must be resolved by a consumer interested in transactions on the web, is whether the site is trustworthy enough to transact with. As suggested earlier however, web aesthetics is immediately and easily recognized by the consumer. Initial trust, often determined by “trust drivers” such as brand strength, privacy and security seals, etc. (see Bart et al. 2005), can also be assessed fairly quickly because of the ease of detecting the presence of these trust drivers on the website. In contrast, TTF takes time to assess because people have to explore or actually use the website to make the assessment. Until a site has been accepted as trustworthy enough, consumers will spend little cognitive effort trying to assess TTF, because it is not diagnostic to the question of trustworthiness, and is immaterial if the site is not trustworthy enough to transact with.

In this initial phase, high aesthetic appeal leads immediately to a positive affective reaction and a sense of “goodness” for the website, which could color and sway the successive trust assessment, even though trust is also importantly determined by many “trust drivers.” At the end of the initial phase, if the consumer is still uncertain about a site’s trustworthiness, and aesthetic appeal is low, the consumer will fairly quickly reject the site as not worth dealing with. If the consumer is still uncertain about a site’s trustworthiness, but aesthetic appeal is high, the consumer will remain uncertain (neutral) about revisiting the site. In either of these cases, the consumer will leave the website, without ever having seriously evaluated the site’s TTF. If asked at this point about the TTF of the site, the low trust or low aesthetic appeal and the associated negative affective reaction would probably spill over to the as-yet not-very-carefully evaluated assessment of TTF.

Only if perceived trustworthiness of the site is high enough for the consumer to label the site as trustworthy, will the consumer move into the second phase. Here the emphasis shifts to the more cognitively complex issue of the TTF of the site. At this point differences in levels of perceived trustworthiness (all are above the tipping point) are no longer diagnostic and are ignored. Aesthetic appeal still operates in this “trustworthy enough” realm, but TTF becomes the focus of the consumer’s cognitive efforts, and is now the key diagnostic factor in the decision of whether or not to revisit the site. If the consumer is asked at this point for their level of trust, it may be that their answer would be colored by the recent focus of their efforts, and the now, most important factor overall, level of TTF.

We have above recognized that these relationships between web aesthetics, trust, and TTF could have different strengths and even different directions of influence above and below the trust tipping point. Given the extra complexity of the required analysis, and the fact that our focus is primarily on the determinants of behavioral intention, we have not hypothesized nor concentrated our analyses on these “upstream” relationships. We do explore the possibility of different strengths in these relationships above and below the tipping point in a post-hoc analysis (see Appendix C6 in the online appendix<sup>4</sup>).

## Methodology and Research Results

## Instrument Development

We used survey data from student web users to test our hypotheses. TTF and web aesthetics were both operationalized using the WebQual instrument<sup>5</sup> (Loiacono et al. 2007). WebQual is a well-tested instrument that can provide both wide- and finegrained measures for evaluating organizational website quality.

We modeled the TTF construct as a secondorder formative construct. Distinct dimensions of TTF were related to two types of tasks: collecting information and carrying out a transaction. Dimensions relating to collecting information include “information-fit-to-task,” the ability of the site to provide relevant information that fits users’ task, and “tailored information,” the degree the site provides customized information through search fields. Dimensions relating to carrying out a transaction include “online completeness,” the ability of the site to complete necessary transactions online, and “relative advantage,” the benefits of completing transactions over the web as compared to traditional means.

“Web aesthetics” was also modeled as a secondorder formative construct. Its dimensions include whether the website is perceived to be visually appealing, innovative enough to set itself apart from other sites, emotionally appealing so as to encourage continued browsing, and compatible with the image projected by the firm through other media (so as not to create dissonance which might discourage further use of the site).

Trust has been conceptualized as a second-order construct with three dimensions: integrity of the information provided on the website, competence of the website to perform required functions, and benevolence of the vendor represented by the website (e.g., McKnight et al. 2002a). However, empirically the discriminant validity of the three dimensions is somewhat in doubt, especially when subjects have had limited exposure and insufficient time to develop more nuanced beliefs about the vendor (McKnight et al. 2002a). Given that this also described our subjects, we followed McKnight et al. (2002b) and operationalized trust as a single reflective construct with questions pertaining to all three originally hypothesized dimensions.<sup>6</sup> The questions were drawn from Bart et al. (2005) and Pavlou et al. (2007), but were refined to focus on the user’s perceptions of the website, even if the concern was the company behind the website.

The revisit questions were based on WebQual, (Loiacono et al. 2007) but were modified and expanded slightly. The “response time” construct from WebQual was included as a control variable. The final instrument included 13 lower-level constructs (four TTF, four web aesthetics, and three trust dimensions, plus response time, and intention to revisit). The actual questions are shown in Appendix B1.

## Data Collection

We used an online survey to collect data from undergraduate students at a southeastern university, based on their reactions to one of 12 actual websites. We selected the 12 websites so that they varied along the lines of the different dimensions of our constructs. These 12 websites are sites that this group of college students might actually use—three sites each of four different types: DVD rental, book, CD, and air ticket booking (see Appendix B2). The intent was to have realistic differences between the websites, enhancing the possibility that different websites would have very different profiles across the 12 dimensions.<sup>7</sup>

To ensure that subjects had sufficient web experience, we used undergraduate students enrolled in a college-wide required MIS introductory course that included studying and accessing the web. Participation was voluntary and students could withdraw from the study at any point of time. The incentive for participation was extra course credit (they could also gain that credit by completing an alternate assignment).

Participating students were instructed to visit the study website at their own convenience. Once they had logged in at the study website, each student was randomly directed to one of 12 websites. They were given an appropriate context (e.g., “Imagine it is your friend’s birthday and you are searching for a good gift—a CD”), and were asked to explore their designated website for at least 10 minutes as if they were considering a purchase. After exploring their designated website, subjects were directed to an online questionnaire that measured the 12 website assessment constructs as well as questions measuring intentions to revisit the website. All questions were formatted in a 1–7 Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.”

361 students participated in the study and 325 responses were usable (90.03%). We assessed nonresponse bias by comparing the demographics (age and web experience) of subjects who gave complete responses and incomplete responses. The t-test comparisons showed no significant differences between the two groups. We further dropped 11 of the 325 responses because our focus is on potential (not actual) users, and these 11 subjects had actually purchased something from their assigned website. For the final sample of 314 subjects, the average age was 20.1. Their average web experience was 7.5 years. The gender distribution was 51% male and 49% female. The average of subjects’ familiarity with their assigned websites was 1.8 on a 1–7 scale.

We also recorded the time each subject spent on the task (website exploration and online survey), which can be used as an indicator of whether a subject took the task seriously. Results showed an average time of 26.7 minutes. It took approximately 25 minutes for one researcher of this study to complete the task. This enhanced our confidence that subjects took the task seriously. We also performed a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) to test whether scores on the 12 WebQual constructs and the revisit construct, differed across the 12 different websites. They did,<sup>8</sup> with a significance level of p < 00001.

We first analyzed measurement validity for the firstorder dimensions of our major constructs and then we validated the second-order constructs. See the Technical Appendix C1 for details. From this analysis, we concluded that our measures demonstrate good psychometric properties. Since we used a regressionbased method to test our model, we calculated construct scores for trust, TTF, and web aesthetics. The second-order formative construct scores for TTF and web aesthetics were calculated in two steps: first, we calculated construct scores for the sub-dimensions by taking the straight average of their indicators. Since all sub-dimensions are reliable reflective constructs, the average of indicators is a reasonable representation of these dimensions. ## Piecewise Regression Model

Piecewise regression, also known as spline regression, is a tool that is commonly used to test structural change, i.e., when the effect of a variable changes as the variable reaches a tipping point. An intuitive approach to test structural change is to split the sample at the trust tipping point and run regressions on the split samples separately. However, two weaknesses of this approach make it unattractive. First, the tipping point is unknown. Instead of specifying the tipping point a priori, we wish to estimate it from the data. Second, the split sample approach leads to a possible “jump” between two regression lines (see Figure 2(a)). Clearly this “jump” is problematic as a description of how an individual might respond to gradually increasing levels of trust—it is unlikely that revisit intention would plummet just as trust increases a slight amount above the tipping point, even if it was reasonable to assume revisit intention would then rise at a slower rate as trust continued to increase. By imposing one or more continuity restrictions, piecewise regression connects the two separate regression lines at the tipping point while their slopes are allowed to be different on either side of the tipping point (see Figure 2(b)).

Another alternative to approximating structural change such as that represented in Figure 2(b) (i.e., when the impact of a variable changes abruptly at a tipping point), is quadratic regression. Piecewise regression is often more effective than quadratic regression for two reasons. First, quadratic regression is not flexible enough to capture highly asymmetric structural change, for example, a sharp transition at irregular intervals (Marsh and Cormier 2001). Second, quadratic regression often runs into multicollinearity as squares and products are added (see Appendix C5, Tables C5.1 and C5.2). There is also the concern that quadratic models are often harder to interpret, especially for practitioners.

Piecewise regression has been used in many different disciplines. For example, it has been used in ecology to identify the critical point in ecological processes (Toms and Lesperance 2003), in economics to model the structural change in wages with respect to education and work experience (Murphy and Welch 1992), in management to study the impact of resource investment and deployment decision congruency on firm performance (Sirmon and Hitt 2009). The basic piecewise regression model is explained in many widely used textbooks (e.g., Neter et al. 1996, Greene 2003). More detailed discussion on this method can be found from Marsh and Cormier (2001).

## Summary of Results

Although the results in Table 2 support all our hypotheses, the Wald test comparisons of the differences in path strengths above versus below the tipping point show only partial support. As shown in Figure 3, the clear difference between the above and below impacts of TTF is strong support for H3 and H4. However, even though the impact of trust on intention is significant below and not significant above the tipping point, the lack of a statistically significant difference between the two coefficients suggests caution in accepting H1. However, the reduction of trust is in the direction hypothesized, and there are clear differences in the impact of TTF and aesthetic appeal above and below the tipping point.

In fact, though the different impacts of aesthetic appeal above and below the tipping point were not hypothesized (we only suggested that it would be important both above and below the tipping point), in retrospect this difference seems quite reasonable. We suggest that below the tipping point, when trustworthiness is in doubt and cognitively difficult to ascertain, low aesthetic appeal is very accessible and becomes a signal for a “bad” website, in the absence of any other persuasive information. Thus below the tipping point, aesthetic appeal is the strongest relevant signal to users about the website. Above the tipping point, aesthetic appeal, though still quite accessible and relevant, must in a sense “share the stage” with TTF, another quite diagnostic characteristic. It is therefore reasonable that trust’s relative impact is less.

Overall we conclude that the data is quite consistent with the idea that consumers use different calculi to arrive at revisit intentions above and below the trust tipping point, and more specifically they do this in ways that are generally consistent with the predictions from the “two worlds of trust” model.

## Theoretical Implications

Our findings have several theoretical implications. First, a weakness of the expectancy-value theories is the cognitive complexity (for humans) of calculating attitudes as a guide to behavior. The notion of humans as cognitive misers suggests that humans may do at least three things not envisioned in expectancyvalue theories. First, potential users may compare a new website with their image of a trustworthy website rather than making absolute judgments. Second, they may calculate intentions differently when a website compares favorably with that trustworthy site image, versus when it compares unfavorably, because they will be worried or concerned about different things. Third, in calculating intentions users will selectively drop any nondiagnostic dimensions from their calculations, depending upon those dimensions’ evolving relevance to their immediate concern.

This is quite different from expectancy-value theories, where trusting beliefs combine with all other relevant constructs such as perceived usefulness (PU) to create trusting intention (e.g., Gefen et al. 2003a). Statements such as the following are common: “What this suggests is that while both are important, PU is a stronger predictor than trust, at least for experienced repeat customers of an e-vendor” (Gefen et al. 2003a) or “These results imply that purchasing services on the Internet depends not only on the operational characteristics of the website, its PU and PEOU (perceived ease of use), but also, and possibly to a greater degree, on consumer trust in the e-vendor”

(Gefen and Straub 2003). The implicit belief is that whatever model explains the relationship between various predictors (trust, usefulness, etc.) and revisit intentions, it operates the same way across all values of trust, at least for a given class of users.

Gefen et al. (2003b) did suggest that the impact of trust on revisit intentions might change over time. More specifically, they suggested that as users gained more experience with a website (by purchasing from it), their greater experience would lessen the impact of trust, and increase the impact of usefulness (similar to our TTF factor). In fact, they showed results for potential versus experienced users that mirror fairly closely our results for users below versus above the trust tipping point. Because all our users are without experience on the website they visited, but still demonstrated essentially the same pattern of results that Gefen et al. (2003b) saw, it raises the interesting possibility that the differences seen by Gefen and colleagues might have had as much to do with different levels of trust as they did with different levels of experience.

The idea of “two worlds of trust” is also a useful perspective through which to view the debate about trust and distrust as opposite ends of a continuum versus as two distinct constructs (e.g., Gefen et al. 2008, Benbasat et al. 2010). Dimoka’s (2010) interesting results are certainly consistent with the distinct constructs hypothesis: trust seemed to activate areas of the brain associated with reward, prediction, and resolving uncertainty, while distrust activated areas concerned with intense emotions and fear of loss. However, note that Dimoka found that “the corresponding psychometric data could not clearly distinguish between trust and distrust 0 0 0” Dimoka (2010, p. 388). Our work leaves open the possibility that trust and distrust are truly opposite ends of a single continuum, but that how humans respond to a particular situation depends upon whether that continuum score is above or below some tipping point of “trustworthy enough.” If a site is not trustworthy enough, humans have to worry about being harmed. If it is trustworthy enough, users forget worry and try to predict the most likely positive outcome. Our model and our results are consistent with both Dimoka’s (2010) findings and Kahneman and Tversky’s (1979) idea of loss aversion. Both of the above suggest a clear break at some tipping point, which we note is not consistent with either the linear or the quadratic model.

## Practical Implications

Our findings also have implications for practice, and could have a major impact on the way website designers allocate their resources between making their sites more trustworthy versus making them more useful or more aesthetically pleasing. When trust is low, strong TTF may be ignored by consumers! Companies without brand-name recognition or some other factor establishing their trustworthiness need to be especially focused on trust building, and creating appealing websites—since these two factors are what will motivate a user to not quickly reject a site when trust is low. Studies by Pavlou and Gefen (2004) and Stewart (2003) suggest ways that trust can be increased for less well-known websites. The possibilities include: demonstrating a relationship with a trusted website by including links; signaling that the site is associated with a trusted context (for example, even a picture of a brick and mortar store may help); or becoming part of a community of sellers that enjoys trust either from its own performance or from oversight by a trusted third-party intermediary.

On the other hand, if a website engenders enough trust to reach the tipping point, developers of websites should probably not spend too much money trying to move their customers’ perceptions of trust beyond that level. For companies with strong brand names (a key driver of initial trust), it may not be helpful to spend resources attempting to increase trust.

Finally, though we have looked exclusively at the impact of trust in a website, there is also the question of how “the two worlds of trust” perspective might apply when we consider trust in other targets. For example, McKnight et al. (2011) have looked at trust in a technology (such as Excel) as opposed to trust in a person or a website. It seems logical to propose that the trust tipping point phenomenon might also apply as users consider whether to utilize an unfamiliar technology.

## Methodological Implications

Our study has a methodological implication. The data analysis results show that the multidimensional piecewise regression is a viable technique for testing a model with structural change such as the “two worlds of trust.” There are certainly other IS domains where users trying to ease cognitive complexity may avoid an absolute scoring of attributes and instead make comparisons to some reference point they have in mind. In such cases there may well be different calculi applied above and below some tipping point, driven by the tendency to drop nondiagnostic characteristics from consideration to simplify the decision. For example, if a new corporate system is perceived as at least as easy to use as the old system, users may be very receptive to arguments about how potentially useful the system is to the corporation. On the other hand, if the system is harder to use than the old system, users may focus (worry!) much more about the impact on the difficulty of their jobs than on the potential benefits to the corporation. We believe that the notion of humans as cognitive misers evaluating new options based on some reference point, and the resultant possibility of tipping points, could contribute to a much more accurate portrayal of the way behavior is generated in many information system contexts.

Quadratic or higher-order polynomial regression is currently a major tool for testing nonlinear relationships in our field (e.g., Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). As shown in the model comparison, for the trust data in our study the quadratic model did not reasonably represent two surfaces connected at the tipping point. There are two possible reasons for the poor performance of the quadratic model in our study. First, the transition in the slope change may be abrupt and the smooth quadratic surface may not be able to capture the abrupt transition. Second, the tipping point lies toward the end point of the trust range. This leads to an asymmetric two worlds of trust that cannot be easily represented by a symmetric quadratic surface. Regardless of why the quadratic model failed to give useful results in our study, the results show that piecewise regression can be quite useful for testing this type of structural change.

Since it is fairly straightforward to specify and estimate a multidimensional piecewise regression model, this technique could be readily applied in other IS research. Researchers need to first specify a model with all the relevant variables and a dummy that indicates different segments (e.g., below or above the tipping point). They then need to impose the continuity restrictions that the value of response variable must be the same for both segments at the tipping point. The resulting model can be estimated using either maximum likelihood or least squares.

## Limitations and Future Research

There are several limitations in our study that suggest further research. First, given the significant impact of the trust tipping point on user behavior, it is important to answer the question of where that tipping point is. The current study inferred the tipping point value from data instead of directly measuring or manipulating it. Future studies might use two techniques to understand the nature of the tipping point. The first one is the priming technique widely used in psychological studies (e.g., Mandel and Johnson 2002). Researchers may prime subjects on pre-specified trust tipping points and observe the effects of the primed tipping points on their behavior. The second technique is the process-tracing method such as verbal protocol analysis used in decisionmaking literature. This method does not rely solely on statistical tests of variance, but also examines what initial information is accessible in short-term memory and how subjects use that and other factors of interest to arrive at decisions.

A second limitation is that we assumed that different individuals have about the same trust tipping point value on a 1–7 scale. We believe that this is a reasonable assumption. Certainly the numeric rating on the 1–7 scale is subjective. It is clear that the same numeric rating can mean different things for different individuals. However, the scale has distinct endpoints (“strongly agree” and “strongly disagree”), with an implicit “neither agree nor disagree” at the midpoint of 4. We would argue that no matter how demanding an individual’s image of a “trustworthy enough” reference website is, that individual would subjectively interpret the questionnaire scale so that such a website would be above 4 and below 7. More specifically, we suggest that all individuals will probably consider something in the range from 5 to 6 as what they would rate a trustworthy enough website. Our estimated tipping point of 5.5 puts us halfway between 5 and 6. Future work could examine the question of whether there are different reference websites (or imagined websites) for different individuals, and how those different reference sites relate to the current scale for trust.

Third, the present study has left unexamined a number of causal relationship issues. We have not dealt with the issue of causal impacts between TTF, trust, and web aesthetics, and how those might change on different sides of the tipping point. Our analysis does not preclude different relationships between TTF, trust, and web aesthetics under high and low trust. Therefore our findings suggest a need for additional research on the different nomological net leading to trust under different trust conditions. In addition, we have not dealt with the suggestion from the empirical data that web aesthetics matters more in situations of low trust than of high trust. This is also a potentially fruitful area for future research.

Fourth, while piecewise regression provides a good approximation of user behavior, we do not suggest that a user’s response function changes in an instant as trust increases from below to above the tipping point. It does seem plausible that there is a level of trust where the user ceases to be concerned about website incompetence, opportunism, or malfeasance. It is quite plausible that there is a significant shift at that level of trust, with behavior being very close to linear away from that tipping point in either direction, though with different slopes on either side. However, behavior very close to the tipping point could be studied to understand this better.

Finally, the dependent variable—intention to revisit a website—covers a broad domain. People may revisit a website for information acquisition, transaction making, or both. Different purposes of revisiting a website may expose people to different types of risks and require different levels of commitment in the decision-making. Although revisiting a website is not as definitive a behavior as making a transaction, it is a prerequisite for making a purchase, and the failure to intend to revisit the website almost precludes making a purchase. While we feel it is an effective indicator of a behavioral response to the various characteristics of the website, future studies may investigate how the two worlds of trust model would change when we examine the more specific behavior of making a purchase.

## Conclusions

The current study aims to explain the role of potential users’ trust in creating intention to revisit a website from a bounded rationality perspective—humans as “cognitive misers.” We formalize our thinking in the “two worlds of trust” model (with different mental calculi operating above and below the tipping point). Drawing on the simple two-dimensional piecewise regression model, we specifically develop the multidimensional piecewise regression to test our model. We show that the new multidimensional piecewise regression is a viable technique that can be used to test models with structural change.

Returning to the example in the introduction of the paper, our findings suggest that ZipBuy.com should not invest in increasing the trustworthiness of its site, since such an investment is not likely to lead to more revisits. If they wish to invest, they should probably invest in improving the TTF of their site. On the other hand, JoesTechBuy.com should definitely invest in the trustworthiness of its site. Only in this way will they rise above the tipping point of trust, and remove doubt from their potential users. Our data analysis not only supports the “two worlds of trust” model, but also shows its superiority to the linear expectancy model. Most importantly our model’s different recommendations for web designers make intuitive sense and are well worth consideration by those web designers.
