Bridging the Divide: A Qualitative Comparison of Information Security Thought Patterns between Information Security Professionals and Ordinary Organizational Insiders

## ABSTRACT

Organizational insiders have considerable influence on the effectiveness of information security efforts. However, most research conducted in this area fails to examine what these individuals believe about organizational security efforts. To help bridge this gap, this study assesses the mindset of insiders regarding their relationship with information security efforts and compares it against the mindset of information security professionals. Interviews were conducted with 22 ordinary insiders and 11 information security professionals, which effort provides insight into how insiders gauge the efficacy of recommended responses to information security threats. Several key differences between insiders’ and professionals’ security mindsets are also discussed.

## KEYWORDS

Behavioral information security; risk assessment; qualitative analysis; organizational insiders; security professionals; protection motivation theory

## 1.0 INTRODUCTION

Information security is a major concern for most organizations (Cook 2002; Goel and Shawky 2009; Siponen and Willison 2009), and is a key focus of information technology (IT) spending, despite recent economic downturns (Loveland and Lobel 2011). The security market grew 12% in 2010 to US\$16.5 billion—an increase of 6 percent compared to 2009 (Contu and Cheung 2010)—and is expected to exceed \$125 billion globally by 2015 (GIA. 2010), due in part to security issues stemming from organizational insiders’ behavior (ITRC 2009; Warkentin and Willison 2009). Ordinary organizational insiders (i.e., insiders) are part-time employees, full-time employees, temporary workers, and consultants who have access to an organization’s information and/or information systems (Posey et al. 2013; Shaw et al. 1998). These insiders pose considerable intentional (D'Arcy et al. 2009; Posey et al. 2013; Straub 1990; Warkentin and Willison 2009; Willison and Backhouse 2006; Willison and Siponen 2009) and accidental (Im and Baskerville 2005) security risks to firms and are extremely costly (ITRC 2009). Despite this evidence, over 70% of security professionals who responded to a global information security survey in 2012 were confident that their approaches are effective (Loveland and Lobel 2012).

Technical methods have traditionally received the most attention from information security professionals as the primary means of preventing breaches (Adams and Blandford 2005; Dhillon and Backhouse 2001; Dhillon and Torkzadeh 2006; Siponen and Willison 2007). However, over-reliance on technical solutions and authoritarian mandates leads to security practices that users find ineffectual, because these methods cannot solve the underlying behavioral causes of the problems (Choobineh et al. 2007; Fagnot 2008; Posey et al. 2013; Schneier 2000; Stanton and Stam 2006). Worse, recent evidence suggests that if authoritarian approaches go too far, they can backfire, causing insider-related problems to increase rather than decrease (Posey et al. 2011a). Accordingly, to understand the effects of user behavior on information security, researchers and practitioners must incorporate behavioral frameworks from disciplines outside of computer science and electrical engineering that examine human perceptions, beliefs, motivations, and behaviors (Choobineh et al. 2007; Im and Baskerville 2005; Posey et al. 2013).

Another potential issue in the extant security research is that much of the knowledge base concerning the

#

behaviors of organizational insiders has been accumulated largely from the opinions and experiences of information security and technology professionals (Loch et al. 1992; Richardson 2007; Straub 1990; Whitman 2003). Although these information security professionals, who themselves represent an advanced breed of insiders, provide a valuable perspective, they could suffer from an elite bias (Myers and Newman 2007) due to their removal from the day-to-day tasks and vulnerabilities that ordinary organizational insiders face (Albrechtsen and Hovden 2009; Stanton and Stam 2006). Conversely, insiders make daily decisions in their routine jobs that directly impact their organizations’ security (Albrechtsen 2007; Albrechtsen and Hovden 2009; Posey et al. 2013). Regardless of the technological security measures in place, success or failure of the organization’s security efforts relies on the diligence and knowledge of these insiders (Rhee et al. 2009). Effective security requires that information security professionals create policies and technologies that ensure security and are consistent with insiders’ work practices (Adams and Blandford 2005). At the same time, insiders must share the goals of the information security professionals and understand the required security practices. With this in mind, a high likelihood exists that information security professionals do not understand ordinary insiders as well as they may think. If true, this gap would indicate that existing security practices are disconnected from the reality of organizational insiders’ day-to-day jobs.

More explicitly, research on risk and risk perceptions provides evidence that expert and general public opinions of risk do not align well, and that risk perceptions are inherently subjective (Krimsky and Golding 1992; Slovic 1987). This misalignment is derived from the fact that experts tend to view risk on the bases of probability calculus and formal risk assessment, whereas lay people form risk perceptions based on intuition, which is linked tightly to emotion and affect (Peters et al. 2006; Slovic et al. 2004). These dichotomous approaches have been termed analytic system (i.e., risk as analysis) and experiential system (i.e., risk as feelings), respectively. The first is based on mathematics and logic, and the second is based on the ‘goodness’ or ‘badness’ of a decision (i.e., the affect heuristic) (Slovic et al. 2004; Slovic and Peters 2006). Both, however, are vital in assessing and combating risk:

There is wisdom as well as error in public attitudes and perceptions. Lay people sometimes lack certain information about hazards. However, their basic conceptualization of risk is much richer than that of the experts and reflects legitimate concerns that are typically omitted from expert risk assessments. As a result, risk communication and risk management efforts are destined to fail unless they are structured as a two-way process. Each side, expert and public, has something valid to contribute. Each side must respect the insights and intelligence of the other. (Slovic 1987, p. 285)

Given that organizational security and risk have many commonalities (Peltier 2005; Straub and Welke 1998; Sun et al. 2006), and much of the opinions regarding security fail to account for public (i.e., organizational insider) perspective, we used this opportunity to elicit and compare the perspectives of information security professionals and organizational insiders, using an analysis of semi-structured interviews with 33 individuals from various organizations and industries. Previous research typically views organizational insiders as security threats that must be controlled through organizational mandates (e.g., Boss et al. 2009; D'Arcy and Hovav 2007; D'Arcy et al. 2009; Dhillon and Moores 2001; Hovav and D’Arcy 2012; Myyry et al. 2009; Straub 1990). In contrast, we examined insiders’ beliefs and motivations that positively influence their organizations’ security from both perspectives. We used protection-motivation theory (PMT) (Rogers 1983) to understand these groups’ perceptions of how insiders become motivated protective agents against organizational information security threats.

To proceed, we will first review PMT’s theoretical foundation. Then, we will discuss our approach to examining the perceptions that insiders and security experts have about security behaviors and their antecedents from a PMT-based framework. We did so by interviewing ordinary insiders and organizational information security experts, and then analyzing the results using thematic coding. We found important insights into the differences of how insiders and security experts view organizational security matters. We will discuss these findings, along with their contributions to research and practice, and close by discussing the limitations and future research opportunities that emerged from this study.

## 2.0 THEORETICAL FOUNDATION: PROTECTION MOTIVATION THEORY

PMT is a general theory of persuasive communication with an emphasis on understanding cognition, attitudes, and behavioral intentions of individuals in response to threat information—especially threats received via fear appeals (Boer and Seydel 1996). A fear appeal is a message designed to increase awareness of an environmental threat, in order to motivate the target to take a course of action to reduce the fear of the threat, and ultimately, harm stemming from the threat (Johnston and Warkentin 2010b). PMT suggests that two types of

#

behaviors are possible when an individual faces a threat: adaptive and maladaptive behaviors (Rogers 1983). Adaptive behaviors are those activities that successfully lessen the effects of the threats, and are hence beneficial in subduing the danger; maladaptive behaviors only reduce the fear individuals experience, rather than the danger presented by those threats (Rogers and Prentice-Dunn 1997). As a result, maladaptive behaviors often lull individuals into a false sense of safety because they do not eliminate the danger—they focus on downplaying the fear that should alert them to the danger. Examples of adaptive behaviors in a security context include the installation and utilization of anti-malware software, data backups, and adhering to organizational data-retention policies. Examples of maladaptive behaviors in this context include avoidance (e.g., “I try not to think about security threats on the Internet”), fatalism (e.g., “I will be attacked by a hacker regardless of whether I do anything about it or not”), and religious faith (e.g., “I think the best way to deal with the security of my organization’s information resources is to pray to God and ask Him to handle it”), among others.

Engagement in either of these behavior sets in an organizational context is preceded by the individual’s assessment of both positive and negative factors associated with the security threats and potential subsequent reactions. These two assessments are termed the threat appraisal and coping appraisal processes. Table 1 describes these assessments proposed by PMT and their effects on protective, adaptive intentions. During the threat appraisal process, individuals weigh both the intrinsic and extrinsic rewards they could potentially receive for not engaging in protective actions against the likelihood and severity of the threat or danger under consideration. If the rewards are more beneficial than the threat vulnerability and severity are detrimental, then the individual is less likely to act in a protective manner (Rogers and Prentice-Dunn 1997).

The second appraisal process—coping appraisal—is composed of the individuals’ beliefs in the efficacy of the recommended response(s) to minimize the threat effectively, their self-efficacy to engage in the recommended response(s) adequately, and any potential costs incurred with acting in the protective behavior. If individuals believe they are capable of engaging in the effective, recommended responses, then they are likely to do so. However, when the perceived personal-response costs exceed the expected benefits, the individuals’ motivation to protect themselves from the threat decreases (Maddux and Rogers 1983).

In recent years, information security researchers have utilized the PMT framework to understand individuals’ adoption of protective technologies (Gurung et al. 2009; Lee and Larsen 2009; Liang and Xue 2009; Liang and Xue 2010; Woon et al. 2005) and employees’ adherence to protective actions within their organizations (Herath and Rao 2009; Siponen et al. 2007; Workman et al. 2008). These studies have established that PMT provides a robust foundation to explain the protective process that individuals engage in when they believe themselves or their organizations to be susceptible to information security threats. What is largely lacking, however, is an indepth comparison of the differences between ordinary organizational insiders and information security professionals understanding of the major building blocks provided by PMT.

## 3.0 METHODOLOGY

Again, rather than provide additional relationship validation among the components specified by PMT’s rigorous theoretical foundation in an information security context, our overall goal is to identify the differences

between the two groups’ (i.e., experts and insiders) understanding of each of these components. Specifically, we conducted a series of 33 confidential, semi-structured interviews with ordinary organizational insiders (n = 22) and information security professionals (n = 11) from a wide variety of organizations and industries across the United States. Table 2 summarizes our interviewees by their job roles, industries, and years of professional experience. Participants were recruited from various groups including academic forum boards and local professional information security organizations. No two interviewees belonged to the same organization. The inclusion of participants from such an array of industries and positions provides a greater degree of generalizability of the research findings than those stemming from studies examining employees from a single position, organization, or industry. Furthermore, the management-level security professionals represent experts at both middle- and executive-management levels, thereby providing another degree of generalizability. This approach is referred to as a ‘maximum variation’ sampling strategy, which helps to derive common patterns and document diverse variations among groups (Miles and Huberman 1994; Patton 1990). Among other benefits, this approach helps in documenting uniqueness and yields “important shared patterns that cut across cases and derive their significance from having emerged out of heterogeneity” (Patton 1990, p. 235)

The open-ended questions designed for the interview protocol (see full protocol in Appendix A) were based on extant PMT research so that they involved each concept of the theory, as summarized in Figure 1. When necessary, probing follow-up questions were asked of the interviewees, to provide for additional, rich insights. Each one-on-one interview was conducted via telephone and lasted an average of 30 minutes. All interviews were conducted by the first author, and all interviewees gave permission for the conversations to be recorded under conditions of strict confidentiality. Over time, the interviewees’ responses within each group became homogenous for the individual PMT components, so no further interviews were deemed necessary (Lincoln and Guba 1985). The interviewees were guaranteed that their statements would be confidential, which is essential in eliciting honest responses to sensitive topics (Bennett and Robinson 2000).

## 4.0 FINDINGS FROM THE PMT-BASED INTERVIEWS

We explain our findings following the questions concerning both the threat appraisal and the coping appraisal processes. We begin with our general overall questions, then cover the responses to the more PMT-specific questions depicted in Figure 1.

## 4.1 THREAT APPRAISAL FINDINGS

Organizations simply cannot expect any of their employees to protect against unknown security threats, so our first general question to each group was: “what do you believe to be main security threats to your organizations’ information and/or computerized information systems?” Table 3 shows the major security threats that were identified by the traditional organizational insiders and security professionals. Of the themes elicited from insiders, external threats, such as hackers (39%) and Internet threats (e.g., viruses, Trojans, and worms) (22%), were viewed as the top information security threats. Even the insiders felt that they and their co-workers were major security threats, a perception that supports previous research on the insider threat (Moore et al. 2008; Ponemon 2009). As Table 3 shows, these self-incriminating perceptions account for 36% of the themes elicited in this area—with unintentional and intentional forms accounting for 19% and 17%, respectively. The insiders’ insights about their peers include the following examples:

The main drawback is your irresponsible employees. That’s what it boils down to—it’s not anybody really going that extra mile to do what they’re supposed to do. (Organizational Insider, Finance Manager, Financial Services)

People within the company talking to people outside of the company—I mean non-electronically divulging information. Also, maybe if someone’s doing work at home, like an engineering project that they’re working on from home, if their home network isn’t secure, it could cause a security problem. I think that the reason being is that there’s a lot of profit in selling people’s information. (Organizational Insider, Radiology Technician, Medical)

## 4.1.1 Threat Appraisal (Rewards): What are the potential rewards for not engaging in protective behaviors?

As part of the mental calculus suggested by PMT, individuals take into account any potential benefits for not performing protective actions against threats purposely, or maladaptive responses. Maladaptive responses occur when insiders choose non-protective responses that help reduce perceived threat, but that actually fail to reduce the occurrence and/or effects of the actual danger (Rippetoe and Rogers 1987; Rogers and Prentice-Dunn 1997)— thus harming the individuals and their organizations. Two themes dominated these discussions regarding insiders’ protection against information security threats (see Table 4). The first theme was extrinsic rewards, such as financial benefits (77%). For example, one respondent stated:

I have clients that competitors would like to know information regarding what we do with a particular client, which would then better position them to gain that client’s business. The competitor may be willing to pay me for this information. (Organizational Insider, Collections Agent, Financial Services)

These forms of rewards are extrinsic maladaptive rewards, which are potential benefits based on personal, extrinsic motivations that encourage an individual to engage in non-protective responses that can result in organizationally harmful processes and outcomes (Posey et al. 2011b).

The second major theme was intrinsic rewards in the form of satisfaction for retaliating against the organization for some perceived injustice or discrimination (15%). Such retaliatory behaviors have been found to be a method that insiders use to “balance the scales” of justice (Ambrose et al. 2002; Skarlicki and Folger 1997) and might help provide evidence as to why individuals choose to “get back” at someone or something through computer system abuse (Willison and Warkentin 2009). The following quote provides insight into these behaviors:

You know for us, I think the biggest reason [for avoiding protective action] would be someone who had some sort of agenda or some type of complaint against the organization or an individual within that organization. Or they just wanted to get back at the system, they were angry. For some reason—that could be the only reason, because it’s clear to all of us in our organization that to do something like that would be unbelievably damaging. (Organizational Insider, Pilot, United States Armed Forces)

These forms of rewards are intrinsic maladaptive rewards, which are potential benefits based on personal, intrinsic motivations that encourage an individual to engage in non-protective responses that can result in organizationally harmful processes and outcomes (Posey et al. 2011b).

4.1.2 Threat Appraisal (Vulnerability): What makes the organization more vulnerable to security threats?

PMT explains that individuals assess two aspects of threats prior to engaging in protective behaviors: threat severity and threat vulnerability. ## 4.1.3 Threat Appraisal (Severity): What makes one security threat more severe than another?

Again, both threat vulnerability and threat severity are core to the threat appraisal process. Threat severity is the perceived degree of harm associated with an organization’s information security threats, should they happen (Herath and Rao 2009; Johnston and Warkentin 2010b). Threat severity increases fear arousal, which should increase protection motivation (Rogers and Prentice-Dunn 1997). Namely, people are less likely to take shortcuts with security measures if they believe the consequences are severe.

Table 6 summarizes the threat severity themes that emerged. When asked about factors relating to threat severity, insiders most often mentioned that security threats leading to a loss of sensitive or private customer information increased the threats’ perceived severity (34%). Although the professionals also mentioned these severity factors, it was with considerably less frequency (11%). A similar pattern occurred for the results in fines or lawsuits: insiders felt that these caused security threats to be more severe (24%) than did the professionals (15%). The professionals mentioned loss in organizational reputation slightly more often than the insiders did (22% vs. 17%), and both groups mentioned loss of competitive edge with equal frequency.

## 4.2 COPING APPRAISAL FINDINGS

Coping appraisal refers to adaptive responses and one’s ability to cope with and avert a threat (Maddux and Rogers 1983). PMT predicts that in the coping appraisal process, the individual will evaluate whether the adaptive response behavior is able to overcome the threat, whether the individual is able to perform the indicated response, and whether the response costs are too high or not (Johnston and Warkentin 2010a; Maddux and Rogers 1983).

These three PMT coping-appraisal components are termed response efficacy, self-efficacy, and response costs behavior, respectively (see Figure 1).

## 4.2.1 Coping Appraisal (Response Efficacy): How do insiders make judgments about appropriate responses to security threats?

PMT states that individuals must believe that a recommended response will be capable of effectively minimizing a threat before an individual will be motivated to act in the suggested manner. That is, they must have response efficacy. In an organizational security context, response efficacy is one’s degree of belief that following an adaptive response will be effective in reducing the associated security threats (Herath and Rao 2009; Johnston and Warkentin 2010a). When insiders were asked how they knew certain responses to information security threats would be effective, they overwhelmingly reported that they had little knowledge concerning proper security actions unless they received such information from their organizations (see Table 7). Two of the themes mentioned by the insiders were the results of their organizations’ official efforts to educate insiders on security threats: training and reports on the tests (e.g., physical documentation) of the organization’s security measures. Together, these items accounted for 67% of the themes mentioned by insiders.

## 4.2.2 Coping Appraisal (Self-Efficacy): What factors increase insiders’ self-efficacy to protect their organizations from information security threats effectively?

Not only is response efficacy a necessary component of the coping appraisal process, but insiders must also believe in their own capability to engage in the recommended response(s). Specifically, they must have self efficacy. In this context, self-efficacy is an insider’s “judgment of personal skills, knowledge, or competency about fulfilling the requirements of the” desired adaptive responsive (Bulgurcu et al. 2010, p. 529; Herath and Rao 2009; Johnston and Warkentin 2010a). Unless self-efficacy is present, insiders might choose to withdraw from the threat rather than act according to the suggested protective behavior(s). Furthermore, insiders knowing what to do in the wake of a potential threat and insiders knowing that they can do what they should do to protect the organization are not synonymous. Insiders’ beliefs that they can perform the necessary protective actions (i.e., self-efficacy) might be the most important PMT element, because it most strongly predicts intentions to engage in adaptive behaviors (Maddux and Rogers 1983; Pechmann et al. 2003).

A total of 46% of the themes mentioned by the professionals were either training or experience related (Table 8). Another 18% of the professionals’ themes were related to insiders having sufficient time to follow the security guidelines carefully. These results suggest the professionals’ optimistic view that insiders can effectively protect the organization from security threats once provided with security protocols training and the time to follow them. However, consistent with Albrechtsen and Hovden (2009), the insiders took a less optimistic view of the value of training. For the insiders, the internal confidence to adequately protect their organizations from security threats is largely related to the insiders’ individual traits (i.e., factors that cannot be trained easily). ## 4.2.3 Coping Appraisal (Costs): What response costs do insiders perceive when protecting their organizations?

Despite the efficacy of the recommended responses and the insiders’ perceived efficacy to perform these actions, such actions likely have drawbacks or negative side effects—some of which might be so high as to negate the positive influences of the responses and self-efficacies on the protection motivation levels. These response costs are the final component of PMT’s coping appraisal process (Rogers and Prentice-Dunn 1997). Formally, response costs are the insiders’ expected, overall negative outcomes that result from engaging in the desired adaptive behavior. The insiders emphasized many times that following information security protocols within their organizations places considerable constraints on accomplishing their daily tasks. One example was slowing productivity down, by making small and simple tasks take much longer than they should. Another constraint identified by insiders was the perceived change in the work environment, whereby information security efforts made the atmosphere stiff and uncomfortable. Other common themes for response costs were inconvenience (e.g., the suggested behaviors can be irritating or frustrating), difficulty in following all of the rules mandated by the organization, the perceived irrelevance of the recommended responses, and the perceived financial cost to the organization, due to a decrease in initial customer satisfaction (see Table 9).

## 5.0 DISCUSSION

## 5.1 CONTRIBUTIONS TO RESEARCH AND PRACTICE

The results from our interviews reveal both consistencies and inconsistencies between ordinary organizational insiders’ and information security professionals’ perspectives. Although the consistencies are promising, the major points of divergence between the groups regarding positive, protective human behavior within their organizations deserve attention. It is this divide that likely leads to compromises of information security efforts within many organizations, because each party fails to understand the other’s perspective completely. Notably, the ‘correctness’ of the groups’ perspectives is not necessarily vital to closing this gap; rather, embracing these differences and designing efforts to unify the unique perspectives is likely vital.

As a first step to better understanding information security threats from the insider’s perspective, the finding that insiders perceive hackers and Internet threats, like viruses, worms, and Trojan horses, as the most likely security menace is not surprising. Therefore, our research provides evidence that insiders are not ignorant that their actions can have a substantial effect on internal information security efforts. Security professionals should thus feel comfortable approaching insiders about their possible negative security role given that they are already largely aware of the importance of their actions. It would be important, however, for professionals to state that most of these actions are thought to be unintentional rather than intentional so that insiders do not feel prejudged and ‘guilty until proven innocent’ when approached.

Although the insiders understand that they can be a major negative threat to their organization’s security, only two factors really appear to motivate them to not engage in protective activities (i.e., act in maladaptive manners). First, insiders understand that the information created, stored, and utilized by their firms is of considerable monetary value. This information could easily be accessed and sold to competitors and could act as an extrinsic reward for maladaptive behavior. Second, insiders believe that purposefully withdrawing from protective action is a way to seek retribution for some form of perceived injustice on the part of the organization or its members. These findings support previous research (Willison and Warkentin 2009) and indicate that organizations should be very conscious of how they treat their members, since decisions not initially related to information security can potentially lead to considerable security concerns.

Given our findings relative to this and other PMT concepts, information security professionals should be able to help outweigh at least some interest in such financial gains for not protecting the organization. Insiders should be provided with information regarding which and how organizational members (e.g., fellow coworkers, families, customers) could be adversely affected by their actions, up to and including loss of job, retirement possibilities, and personal identities. This approach aligns well with insiders’ reliance on ‘risk as feelings’ and plays to their focus on effects to ‘others’.

Although it is not necessary that the insiders agree completely with the experts on what threats exist (and vice versa), it is important that the insiders are aware of the threats for which they exercise some control. The differences between insiders and professionals in terms of hackers and inadequate system configuration are not likely to be a problem, because users are not likely to be involved in the security measures against these threats.

#

However, the insiders are likely to form a key part of the defenses against Internet threats (e.g., laptops, mobile devices, and home computers, especially) and social engineering.

The solution, presumably, to educating insiders is through fear appeals that take the form of training, newsletters, emailed bulletins, and other similar methods. Unfortunately, as Albrechtsen and Hovden (2009) found, official communications about security threats seem to have little influence. Perhaps this is because those efforts failed to take into account the ‘risk as feelings’ perspective. The mass media, friends, and co-workers appear to have greater influence. This finding might explain why the insiders viewed external threats, such as hackers and Internet threats, as the top threats. These issues receive considerable media attention, and most people know someone who has been the victim of a virus or spyware. Information security professionals, on the other hand, have access to the statistics of the number of external attacks, viruses, and so on, that the organization actually experiences, and can evaluate the relative risks more accurately, hence their focus on ‘risk as analysis’.

Our findings also confirm that insiders feel a sense of personal responsibility, or obligation, in protecting their organization’s information resources. Of particular importance to security education, training, and awareness (SETA) instructors is that insiders express the need, and their willingness, to protect against information loss, especially if the loss directly impacts other individuals (e.g., co-workers, customers). Again, SETA programs are needed that underscore these relationships by placing more emphasis on the insiders’ responsibility to protect the information of ‘others’ rather than to position the insiders’ responsibility solely on protecting the organization’s information.

In addition to this finding, these instructors must also realize that their interactions—both formal sessions and disseminated internal documents—with insiders are a determinant of the value of protective actions (i.e., response efficacy). Should the instructors perform inadequately, the insiders will believe that ‘all is well’ with their current practices if security breaches are not made known through public notices. This assumption is quite a dangerous one to make, since many breaches might never be discovered (Straub and Nance 1990), and many of those that are discovered might never be formally reported to the authorities (Brooker et al. 2007; Cavusoglu et al. 2004; Goel and Shawky 2009) or to organizational members. Therefore, information security professionals should be

#

willing to share information about threats because not doing so will present a façade of adequate protection, which could lull insiders into a false sense of security.

Even with adequate SETA efforts, organizations might experience difficulty with insiders protecting information resources because the insiders do not feel capable of engaging in all of the prescribed activities (i.e., self-efficacy). For example, some individuals believe that their lack of organizational skills prohibit them from being as meticulous as their organizations demand. Others might feel that they or their co-workers are simply too lazy to follow every protocol. These traits aside, individuals might not engage in protective activities if they believe they do not have enough time to do so. Insiders often weigh two options: (1) getting some of their daily tasks completed in a secure manner, and (2) completing all of their job tasks in a mostly secure manner. This trade-off is a harmful perspective, because it indicates that insiders often separate information security from their daily job tasks in such a way that the completion of one must be done with only a partial fulfillment of the other. Thankfully, however, our findings show that most insiders view internal security efforts as constraining and a bit inconvenient rather than irrelevant.

A key takeaway for information security professionals then is that communication to insiders must include clear messages that security is everyone’s job. Under no circumstances should uncertainty exist regarding whose responsibility it is to protect the organization. Additionally, it would serve organizations well to examine how much of their employees’ time is spent on organizational tasks. Our findings suggest that when employees are overloaded with ‘work’, lapses in security are more likely to occur. Employees who thus feel overburdened are more likely to engage in the dangerous tradeoff mentioned above. Given the stated influence that fellow coworkers have on insiders, however, seeing coworkers engage in protective behaviors is likely to limit such thinking since an individual is able to view his/her colleagues doing their job securely given similar environment demands. Managers should design jobs that are manageable, and when demands do become high, they should make sure that a ‘security champion’ receives high visibility within each department.

Finally, information security managers should be aware that insiders are motivated to protect information resources by factors other than just fear of sanctions for noncompliance. In fact, the major motivating factor for

#

insiders is making sure that their organizations succeed. An insider’s commitment to his/her organization is thus likely a major contributor to protective security behaviors. Insiders are also motivated by pride in themselves and their professions, and a willingness to manage others’ information in a manner that they would want others to do for them (i.e., the “Golden Rule”). Surprisingly, incentives—both financial and verbal—appear to have a minimal influence as a motivating factor. These findings indicate that insiders are motivated to engage in protective behaviors by factors that underscore the negative and positive effects of noncompliance and compliance, respectively. Organizations should use this information to redesign their SETA efforts so that they can be more effective.

## 5.2 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH OPPORTUNITIES

Despite these important findings, we must acknowledge several limitations that point to interesting future research opportunities. First, several studies indicate that culture matters greatly in technology use (Lowry et al. 2011; Posey et al. 2010; Zhang et al. 2007); thus, it would be beneficial to elicit qualitative responses from insiders in more than one country. Previous research has addressed this limitation to some degree (Albrechtsen and Hovden 2009), but this previous effort was not based on a theoretical framework. In order to determine how PMT can be effectively applied within organizations more completely, future research will need to explore PMT’s facets from a multicultural standpoint. Security research has already begun to explore the role that culture plays in adopting protective technologies (Dinev et al. 2009), and similar influences could be found in insiders’ motivations to protect their organizations from security threats as well.

Another limitation is that PMT assumes rational agents, because they are assumed to appraise the threat and possible coping methods in dealing with the threat. However, this is not necessarily always a valid assumption. Humans do not always act rationally, and can be influenced by factors such as habit (Moody and Siponen 2013; Vance et al. 2012) and low self-control (Hu et al. 2011). There is also the consideration of what exactly happens when fear generated by a fear-appeal goes too far and exceeds a person’s coping capabilities. These factors were not considered as they are outside the scope of PMT, but a likelihood exists that such factors could be used to significantly extend PMT or similar models.

#

Another interesting limitation is that although PMT evolved from dual-process models, it is a single-process model focused solely on explaining the formation of adaptive intentions (i.e., protection motivation) and behaviors. Despite the fact that maladaptive rewards are shown as part of the PMT nomology, maladaptive behaviors are not expressly modeled in the PMT foundation. In dual-process fear appeal models, from which PMT evolved, both adaptive and non-adaptive behaviors are considered. A contemporary version of such models is the extended parallel process model (EPPM) by Witte et al. (1992; 1994). Further research can thus consider using EPPM to examine adaptive and maladaptive behaviors in contexts where both are likely to exist.

A final limitation, which points to future research possibilities, is that we have not provided quantitative validation of our qualitative findings. We thus urge researchers to explore the strength of each identified theme in contributing to the individual facets suggested by PMT, and encourage them to evaluate whether and how PMT can be extended with our findings shown in Table 10. Specifically, we believe that the insiders’ commitment to their respective organizations and personal pride are key indicators to protective activity within the workplace. We would caution, however, that the use of financial incentives might likely be a negative reinforcement agent via misalignment with overall purpose, where individuals engage in the suggested behavior not because of the protection it provides but rather the financial gains they receive from their activity. Such negative impacts of financial incentives have been previously documented (Fleming 2011; Frey and Jegen 2001; Gneezy et al. 2011). Likewise, due to the nature of small-sample qualitative research, it is likely that the responses in Tables 4-7 and their associated interpretations are based on the interviewees' implicit assumptions about the sources of security threats, and not solely about the rewards, vulnerabilities, severity, and so on. Thus, for example, if an expert was thinking about external hackers versus unintentional employee mistakes, then his/her responses would be anchored accordingly.

## 6.0 CONCLUSION

It is clear that the traditional view of IS security as simply a technical concern is flawed. Security is both a technical and behavioral matter. Aside from the detrimental behaviors that arise from conflicts in job design and security expectations, insiders choose behaviors based on social influence. Insiders rely on each other for

#

knowledge, important information, and increases in self-efficacy. Insiders also feel very loyal to other individuals with whom their organizations conduct business and collect information from (i.e., ‘risk as analysis’). Security professionals should strive to leverage this dependence and loyalty in the design of security training programs.

# APPENDIX A. INTERVIEW PROTOCOL

# Employee Interview Protocol

You have been invited to participate in research conducted by [University] that examines the roles that organizational insiders enact in the protection of information and computerized information systems within their organizations. The goal of this research is to identify behaviors that individuals within your organization perform or might perform to help care for the information that is relevant to the daily functions of your organization. These individuals may be full-time employees, part-time employees, consultants, or temporary workers who might have authorized access to your organization’s information and/or information systems. These behaviors are under the control of the individuals and have the intention of protecting organizationally relevant information and/or the computerized information systems that accumulate, store, manipulate, and/or disseminate this information.

There are no right or wrong answers to the questions in this interview, and your participation is voluntary. All of your answers will be held in strict confidentiality and will only be reviewed by the researchers. This conversation will be audio recorded to ensure accuracy in subsequent transcription efforts. I will not mention your name or the name of your organization during the interview as well as afterwards to ensure complete confidentiality. In the event you feel uncomfortable about being recorded, please inform me, and we will continue the interview without audio recording. Again, all of your answers will only be analyzed by the researchers and will be held in strict confidence. It is expected that this interview will last approximately 30 – 45 minutes and will consist of 19 open-ended questions. While you will receive no monetary reward for your participation, you will have the opportunity to receive a detailed executive summary of the interview results.

I understand that your time is extremely valuable, and I appreciate your efforts in helping me in this important research endeavor. Without the contribution from you and other professionals like you, this research could not be a success. Your answers will help guide the development of programs to help other working professionals manage their efforts in protecting important information and computerized information systems within their organizations. Thank you again for your participation.

Do you agree to participate?

Thank you. I will now begin the interview by asking you a series of general questions regarding your current job.

1. In what type of organization are you employed, and what is your current occupation?

2. How long have you been employed in this job?

3. Could you briefly describe what you do in your job?

4. Could you describe the computerized information systems you use to perform those tasks and how much of your working day is spent using them?

5. If this is not your first professional job, could you briefly list the previous occupations you have had and/or the type of organizations you have worked for? In addition, please state the total number of years of professional working experience that you have.

As I stated earlier, this research investigates the protection of information relevant to the daily functions of your organization. Examples of such information might include, but is not limited to: firm revenues, customer billing data, medical records, inventory records, etc. Therefore, information that assists your organization in its daily operations is of interest. We are also interested in determining how individuals inside your organization can protect the computerized technology with which this information is collected, retrieved, stored, manipulated,

and/or disseminated throughout your organization.

6. What do you believe to be the main security threats to this information and/or computerized information systems?

7. Are some of those threats more severe than others? If so, what factors help you make this determination?

8. What factors make or could make the information and/or computerized information systems more susceptible to those threats?

9. If this information and/or computerized information systems within your organization were not successfully protected by you and/or your fellow co-workers within the organization, what harm could be done?

Now that you have discussed the threats to your organization’s information and/or computerized information systems, I will ask you specifically how you and/or your fellow co-workers within your organization can personally help protect this information and the computerized information systems that store, accumulate, manipulate, and/or disseminate this information. Once again, there are no right or wrong answers.

10. How can or do you and/or your fellow co-workers within your organization attempt to protect the information and/or the computerized information systems from those threats you mentioned? In other words, what behaviors or actions can you and/or your fellow co-workers within your organization personally do to protect the organizationally relevant information and/or the computerized information systems? Please take as much time as you need to respond.

11. Are these protective actions or behaviors you just stated all similar, or do differences exist among them? If differences do exist, what separates some from others?
