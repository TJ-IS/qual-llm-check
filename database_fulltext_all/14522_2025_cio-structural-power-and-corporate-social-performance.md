# CIO Structural Power and Corporate Social Performance

Cong Feng,<sup>1</sup> Pankaj C. Patel,<sup>2</sup> Scott Fay,<sup>3</sup> Huigang Liang,<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Business Administration, University of Mississippi, USA, cfeng@olemiss.edu <sup>2</sup>Villanova School of Business, Villanova University, USA, pankaj.patel@villanova.edu <sup>3</sup>Whitman School of Management, Syracuse University, USA, scfay@syr.edu <sup>3</sup>Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, USA, hliang1@memphis.edu

## Abstract

Drawing on the structural power framework and the resource management theory, we examine the influence of chief information officer (CIO) structural power on corporate social responsibility. Our empirical analysis shows that CIO structural power enhances corporate social performance (CSP) by fostering an innovative organizational culture. In addition, we find that CIO structural power amplifies the positive relationships between financial resource availability and CSP, as well as between employee productivity and CSP. These findings remain consistent across multiple robustness tests. By highlighting the multifaceted impact of CIO structural power on CSP, we aim to advance understanding of CIOs’ strategic role in corporate social responsibility.

Keywords: Chief Information Officer, Structural Power, Corporate Social Performance, Corporate Social Responsibility, Innovative Culture, Financial Resource, Labor Productivity

Daniel Chen was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on March 17, 2023, and underwent three revisions. Cong Feng is the corresponding author.

## 1 Introduction

Corporate social responsibility (CSR) has been increasingly recognized as a potent corporate strategy to create value for stakeholders (Kang et al., 2016; Sun & Govind, 2022). By aligning business practices with stakeholder expectations, CSR enhances corporate reputation and legitimacy, fosters consumer trust, mitigates business risks, and builds long-term competitive advantages (Brown & Forster, 2013; O’Riordan & Fairbrass, 2014; Servaes & Tamayo, 2013). Improving corporate social performance (CSP) serves as a tangible means for organizations to demonstrate their commitment to societal and environmental objectives. However, achieving superior CSP requires organizations to address key CSR-related challenges, such as fostering an innovative culture to develop creative solutions for societal and environmental issues, orchestrating organizational resources to maximize their impact on

CSR goals, and embedding these efforts into organizational strategies and operations.

Information technology (IT) plays a critical role in overcoming these challenges by enhancing employee connectivity and communication, organizational knowledge management, boundary spanning, information processing, innovation, and collaboration and coordination (Dewett & Jones, 2001; Kohli & Grover, 2008; Li & Kettinger, 2006; Tarafdar & Tanriverdi, 2018). Such streamlined internal operations and external collaborations hold central importance in the realm of CSR; specifically, the efficiency, synergy, connectivity, and innovation driven by IT could lead to better CSP (Chuang & Huang, 2018; Malaquias et al., 2016; Nishant et al., 2017). However, realizing the transformative potential of IT in the CSR domain requires strategic leadership capable of aligning IT capabilities with organizational objectives.

As the executive responsible for IT strategy and operations, the chief information officer (CIO) could provide the strategic leadership necessary to align IT capabilities with organizational goals (Feng et al., 2021). CIOs hold formal authority within the organizational hierarchy, known as structural power, that enables them to shape organizational decision processes and allocate resources strategically (Preston et al., 2008). Such structural power is vital for leveraging IT capabilities to integrate CSR initiatives into core business practices, ensuring they are operationalized effectively and aligned with broader organizational priorities. Notably, many industry reports (summarized in Appendix Table A1) underscore the CIO’s growing role in the CSR domain, with initiatives such as advancing sustainable technology strategies and integrating environmental, social, and governance (ESG) practices with IT operations.

Despite the growing recognition of CIOs’ potential contributions to CSR by practitioners, academic research has largely overlooked their role in advancing CSP, resulting in a significant lack of scientific evidence in this area. While the importance of the upper echelons in influencing CSR-related outcomes is a well-established notion in the literature (Reimer et al., 2018), existing studies on CSR leadership have primarily focused on boards of directors, CEOs, and other TMT members. For instance, prior CSR studies have explored the impact of the board of directors, analyzing factors such as the influence of outside directors (Johnson & Greening, 1999), female directors (Macaulay et al., 2018), and the board’s managerial control (Coffey & Wang, 1998) on CSP.<sup>1</sup> Other CSR research has centered primarily on the CEOs (e.g., Manner, 2010; Tang et al., 2015; Waldman et al., 2006), with a recent shift towards examining the roles of other TMT members such as the chief marketing officer (Sun & Govind, 2022) and the chief sustainability officer (Fu et al., 2020). However, the specific role of CIOs in leveraging IT capabilities and resources to enhance CSR remains underexplored.

To bridge this gap, we draw on existing theories and the extant literature to contend that the CIO’s unique position as both a technological leader and a strategic influencer within the TMT allows them to align IT capabilities with CSR objectives and drive CSR initiatives effectively. Unlike other TMT members whose roles may be confined to specific functional areas, empowered CIOs often act as cross-functional integrators (Smaltz et al., 2006), connecting various departments through the strategic use of IT. We conceptualize the potential of CIO structural power to influence CSP through two key mechanisms: fostering an innovative organizational culture and enhancing the effectiveness of resource management.

First, as highlighted by Preston et al. (2008), structural power legitimizes the CIOs’ role within the TMT, enabling them to influence key organizational processes and priorities. This influence allows CIOs to embed creativity, adaptability, and forward-thinking into organizational practices and foster an innovative organizational culture, which is essential for addressing complex social, environmental, and sustainability challenges (Costa & Opare, 2024). In other words, structural power grants CIOs the strategic decisionmaking authority necessary to drive organizational culture changes, which play a crucial role in shaping CSP (Galbreath, 2010; Wan et al., 2020).

Second, CIO structural power enhances CSP by amplifying the effectiveness of resource management. Resource management theory (Sirmon et al., 2007) emphasizes that the value of resources lies not only in an organization’s possession but in how they are orchestrated and managed by executives to achieve strategic goals. Empowered CIOs, leveraging their structural authority and IT expertise, can strategically allocate and integrate financial and human resources with technological solutions to align with CSR objectives. This positions CIOs as cross-functional stewards, ensuring that IT-enabled organizational resources are deployed effectively to improve their impact on CSP.

Building on the two conceptual pathways outlined above, this research seeks to answer two key questions: (1) whether and how CIO structural power is associated with CSP through enhancing innovative culture, and (2) how CIO structural power amplifies the effectiveness of organizational resources, specifically financial resource availability and employee productivity, in influencing CSP. To test these relationships, we extracted a rich dataset from multiple databases (e.g., Kinder, Lydenberg, and Domini; Execucomp; Compustat; the corporate culture dataset by Li et al., 2021; and InformationWeek 500). The resulting sample consisted of 5,576 firm-year observations corresponding to 1,110 unique public companies across 64 SIC 2-digit industries from 2006 to 2013. This diverse sample enabled a comprehensive examination of CIO structural power in the CSR context. Our empirical analysis revealed that CIO structural power not only indirectly improves CSP through enhancing innovative culture but also amplifies the effectiveness of financial resources and employee productivity in driving CSP.

In this paper, we contribute to the growing literature on CIOs by documenting the multifaceted roles of CIO structural power in relation to an important noneconomic organizational outcome, namely, CSP. We provide empirical evidence of an underlying mediation mechanism linking CIO structural power to CSP, specifically through the enhancement of an organization’s innovative culture. Additionally, we demonstrate the moderating role of CIO structural power in amplifying the effect of certain crucial organizational resources on CSP enhancement, furthering our understanding of how structural power can be leveraged in corporate settings.

In addition, this research substantiates the growing advocacy by practitioners (see Appendix Table A1) for the active involvement and contributions of CIOs in CSR activities, emphasizing their critical role in the success of these initiatives. Our empirical findings further validate these industry perspectives and demonstrate the strategic importance of CIOs in advancing CSR efforts. By showcasing the unique contributions of CIO structural power, we provide managerial insights into how CIOs can drive meaningful, non-economic outcomes for organizations.

Moreover, we contribute to the TMT literature by studying an essential characteristic of CIOs, namely, their structural power. Beginning with Finkelstein’s (1992) identification of the key influence of structural power, many studies have attempted to understand how structural power is associated with organizational outcomes. We identify an important TMT member that the board of directors should consider endowing with more structural power to drive or amplify CSP. Lastly, by integrating the structural power perspective (Preston et al., 2008) with resource management theory (Sirmon et al., 2007) to study the phenomenon of CIO structural power, we explain how the unique position of the CIO can significantly contribute to an organization’s strategic CSR objectives.

## 2 Hypothesis Development

Researchers have highlighted the importance of tracing a company’s CSR initiatives to its upper echelons, as this can help to pinpoint the fundamental factors driving the company’s social involvement and performance (Sun & Govind, 2022). Anecdotal evidence suggests that CIOs can act as facilitators to bridge the gap between a firm’s IT capabilities and strategic CSR goals and integrate CSR initiatives within the broader business strategy (Marchand & Barrington, 2013). In what follows, we argue that CIO structural power is critical for driving CSP through improving a company’s innovative culture and that CIO structural power can amplify the effectiveness of financial and human resources in improving CSP (see Figure 1).

## 2.1 CIO Structural Power, Innovative Culture, and CSP

## 2.1.1 CIO Structural Power

According to the structural power perspective (Finkelstein, 1992), CIO structural power refers to the formal authority given to a CIO by an organization. Such structural power provides CIOs with the latitude to engage in strategic decision-making, enabling them to address complex and unstructured organizational problems while influencing organizational priorities, aligning IT initiatives with strategic objectives, and initiating IT-driven solutions to contribute to firm-wide performance (Feng et al., 2021; Preston et al., 2008). Structural power also enables CIOs to share their opinions and concerns during board and TMT meetings, allowing them to draw enough attention from their TMT peers to develop a shared understanding regarding emerging IT and non-IT related issues (Chen et al., 2021b). Without sufficient structural power, CIOs become less effective at swaying organizational agendas and less influential among their peers (i.e., other TMT members), their subordinates, and external stakeholders (Chen et al., 2010; Finkelstein, 1992). Table 1 provides a list of representative empirical studies that explore the effect of CIO structural power on various organizational (mostly ITrelated) variables such as IT governance, IT assimilation, IT governance, CIO leadership, IS strategic alignment, CIO capability that drives firm competitive strategy, and firm financial performance.<sup>2</sup> Structural power legitimizes the decisions (and subsequent actions) of CIOs and authorizes them to craft, initiate, and deploy the underlying IT strategies needed to fulfill organizational goals.

## 2.1.2 CIO Structural Power and Innovative Culture

Innovative culture is widely recognized as an important driver of innovative behavior, organizational innovations, and overall performance (Carrillat et al., 2004; Michaelis et al., 2018; O’Connor, 2008). According to Bolton et al. (2013), leaders can profoundly influence corporate culture by setting clear direction and promoting coordination to overcome misaligned interests and drive collective action. Vera and Crossan (2004) distinguish between general leaders and strategic leaders, identifying the latter as those occupying top positions within an organization who are empowered to make strategic decisions and shape corporate culture.

As key TMT members with a deep business understanding and enterprise-wide perspective (Muller, 2013), structurally powerful CIOs are distinctly positioned to serve as strategic leaders. Equipped with strategic decision-making authority and the ability to align IT with broader business priorities (Preston et al., 2008), CIOs with substantial structural power become important architects of a corporate culture that emphasizes innovation across the organization. Indeed, practitioners indicate that effective strategic IT-business alignment requires CIOs to adopt continuous improvement practices and an innovative mindset to navigate the ever-changing operational environment (Clewlow, 2023). As strategic leaders, empowered CIOs can leverage this mindset to embed values of adaptability and creativity into the broader organizational culture (Muller, 2013), and implement governance and IT policies that enhance information sharing, cross-functional collaboration, and routine practices (Feng et al., 2021), all of which can promote innovative cultural values (Dewett & Jones, 2001; Li et al., 2021). By leveraging their strategic decision-making authority, CIOs essentially act as cultural change agents.

Indeed, existing research has highlighted that CIOs can institutionalize values and practices that support innovation throughout the IT function and broader organization (Watts & Henderson, 2006). Chun and Mooney (2009) further supported this by demonstrating that CIOs who evolve into executive-level leaders (i.e., with more structural power) are often those who drive business innovation and are tasked with using IT to implement innovation and process changes across the entire organization. As shown by Chen et al. (2021b), CIOs positively influence IT innovation within an organization, with CIO structural power amplifying the effectiveness of their efforts in promoting innovation. Peppard et al. (2011) similarly described powerful CIOs as key TMT members who can transfer and embed information capabilities into the whole organization for

IT-enabled competitive differentiation. Leidner et al. (2010) also emphasized that CIO strategic leadership, which is closely tied to structural power, is a key antecedent to organizational IT innovation. Together, these studies underscore that CIO structural power is vital for cultivating a stronger innovative culture within organizations. By utilizing their strategic decisionmaking authority, CIOs can influence key stakeholders, secure resources, and implement innovation-supporting practices across the organization.

## 2.1.3 Innovative Culture and CSP

Previous studies have found that the essence of a company’s CSP is significantly influenced by corporate culture (Galbreath, 2010; Wan et al., 2020), which serves as a guiding principle for employees and represents a system of shared values and norms for organizational members (Li et al., 2021; O’reilly & Chatman, 1996). Indeed, several scholars have argued that corporate culture allows firms to embrace duality (Gulati & Puranam, 2009; Iranmanesh et al., 2021), such as balancing profitability with social impact or shortterm gains with long-term sustainability. When a firm embarks on CSR activities, a particular aspect of corporate culture, namely innovative culture, aids in creative problem-solving and forward-thinking approaches (Naranjo-Valencia & Calderon-Hernández, 2018), allowing the firm to integrate CSR initiatives seamlessly with its existing business operations.

A strong innovative culture can act as a stabilizing and adaptive force that enhances a company’s strategic flexibility (Bock et al., 2012), enabling the reconfiguration of organizational resources towards CSR-related initiatives and the management of diverse CSR demands while maintaining focus on both financial and non-financial objectives. Building on this foundation, such an innovative culture empowers firms to foster creative solutions to competitive threats and cultivate a mindset of continuous improvement and experimentation (Amabile & Khaire, 2008; Bock et al., 2012), allowing them to develop products and practices that can maintain economic viability while contributing positively to CSP.

Essentially, a strong innovative culture creates an environment that encourages tackling complex challenges and fosters ingenuity and originality within an organization (Hood & Koberg, 1991), all of which are relevant in the CSR context for creating sustainability-oriented business practices (Siqueira & Honig, 2019). Without the support of a strong innovative culture, CSP initiatives may struggle, as organizational members may not fully engage with CSR activities (Slack et al., 2015) or may place unbalanced weight on their assigned daily tasks without making a meaningful connection to the organization’s overarching CSR goals. This is perhaps because committing to CSR is an expensive and lengthy process that may not yield immediate benefits (Branco & Rodrigues, 2006). Worrying about failure due to the lack of a strong innovative culture, coupled with elevated initial costs of CSR and pressure from investors to enhance short-term performance, necessitates resilience from the TMT and a firm-wide commitment to CSR. This commitment at an organizational level can be bolstered, propelled, and fortified by a strong innovative culture. Thus, we argue that corporate culture is crucial to improving CSP.

## 2.1.4 CIO Structural Power, Innovative Culture, and CSP

As argued previously, CIOs who possess significant structural power play a pivotal role in shaping innovative culture. Their decision-making and leadership approach, particularly in integrating IT and ethical practices into business operations, establishes the organizational ethos of social responsibility. Drawing upon their strategic decision-making authority derived from structural power (Preston et al., 2008), CIOs can instill values that foster an innovative culture, which is essential for enhancing an organization’s social responsibility.

Effective CSR development and deployment require continuous integration, processing, and improvement of both internal and external information flows. Organizations today face the dual challenge of aligning the non-economic objectives of CSR with the economic goals of broader business strategies (Siltaloppi et al., 2021), which necessitates balancing competing stakeholder priorities across economic and non-economic interests. Addressing these complexities demands innovative solutions. The effective management, integration, and optimization of information play a critical role in fostering a culture of innovation within an organization that ultimately enables the translation of these processes into effective CSR strategies.

As integrators and relationship architects (Smaltz et al., 2006), CIOs can bridge the IT-social responsibility gap. Their frequent interactions with various stakeholders, functional areas, and strategic business units (Chen et al., 2021b; Feng et al., 2021; Marchand & Barrington, 2013) provide the foundation for cultivating a culture that embraces creativity, experimentation, and forwardthinking. As reported by CIO & Leader (2013): “IT infrastructure in a company provides ample scope and opportunity for an enterprise technology decision-maker [CIO] to take the lead in becoming the CSR crusader.” With significant strategic decision-making authority (derived from structural power), a CIO can influence the ethos towards innovation, ensuring that the IT strategy supports not only operational efficiency but also the development of novel solutions to complex CSR challenges, such as green IT initiatives, supply chain transparency, digital inclusivity, and ethical data management. Conversely, when CIOs lack sufficient structural power, they (and the IT departments) have limited influence on other stakeholders and reduced decision-making authority to undertake strategic initiatives. This diminished influence may hinder their ability to embed a strong innovative culture within the organization, thereby reducing their effectiveness in leveraging IS to facilitate CSR deployment. Summarizing all the preceding arguments, we argue that the positive relationship between CIO structural power and CSP is mediated by the innovative culture that the CIO helps to cultivate:

H1: CIO structural power is positively associated with CSP through the mediation of innovative culture.

## 2.2 The Moderating Effect of CIO Structural Power in the CSR Context

Although the importance of CSR has been well recognized (D’Arcy et al., 2020), there is substantial uncertainty and debate regarding how to best integrate CSR objectives into a firm’s day-to-day operations (Siltaloppi et al., 2021). Since IT is used in nearly all aspects of a business, we assert that structurally empowered CIOs can be particularly important in integrating and aligning IT strategies with CSR objectives. Such integration and alignment ensure that technological initiatives transcend operational improvements, helping to embed ethical practices and broader societal and environmental considerations into the organization’s core values, norms, and culture. By leveraging their IT expertise and strategic decisionmaking authority, CIOs can effectively overcome potential information processing challenges and bottlenecks, thereby enhancing the configuration and utilization of scarce organizational resources. The strategic use, allocation, and rebundling of resources, amplified by CIO structural power, align firms closely with the organization’s CSR objectives, consequently improving CSP.

According to resource management theory (Sirmon et al., 2007), organizations can gain a competitive edge and improve performance by not merely possessing scarce resources but by effectively structuring, bundling, and leveraging them to meet strategic objectives. As shown in Table 2, the CSR literature suggests that certain organizational resources, such as financial and human resources, are important drivers of CSP (e.g., Clarkson et al., 2011) and CSR-related outcomes (e.g., Freitas et al., 2020; Rothenberg et al., 2017). Indeed, financial resources can provide the necessary capital for organizations to invest in CSR initiatives, such as implementing advanced IT systems for environmental monitoring or developing data-driven sustainability programs. Similarly, productive employees can contribute their skills, knowledge, and efforts to execute CSR strategies effectively within an organization, such as utilizing IT tools to optimize resource usage or leveraging data analytics to enhance social impact reporting.

However, while financial resources can fund CSR initiatives and productive employees can fuel these initiatives, the mere availability of these resources may not be sufficient to ensure desirable CSR outcomes. As Sirmon et al. (2007, 2008) argue, managers play a critical role in realizing the potential value of resources through effective resource management and orchestration. In this context, CIOs with sufficient structural power could act as integrators, facilitators, and key resource orchestrators that coalesce dispersed organizational resources to enhance the organization’s CSP.

## 2.2.1 CIO Structural Power Amplifies the Financial Resource Availability-CSP Relationship

Financial resource availability and CSP: Prior research has found that firms with more abundant financial resources, as indicated by higher return on assets, possess greater organizational slack (Roca‐Puig et al., 2019)— resources that can be allocated to CSR initiatives without jeopardizing core business operations (McGuire et al., 1988; Sun et al., 2021). Indeed, prior studies have found that there is a positive relationship between the availability of financial resources and CSP (Clarkson et al., 2011; Sun & Gunia, 2018). Profitability is not only a measure of self-generated funding but also “represent[s] a tangible indicator of the availability of resources to potentially fund social investments” (Julian & Oforidankwa, 2013, p. 1321). Essentially, financial resources enable firms to invest in and expand their CSR efforts. However, according to resource management theory (Sirmon et al., 2007), the mere possession of financial resources is insufficient for achieving superior outcomes; rather, the strategic structuring, bundling, and leveraging of these financial resources are critical for achieving positive outcomes. In other words, the potential of financial resources to drive CSP depends on how effectively they are managed and orchestrated, which requires active managerial intervention. This is where the role of CIOs with structural power becomes crucial.

The moderating effect of CIO structural power: In the current digital era, where IT underpins nearly all business operations, the strategic deployment of financial resources toward successful CSR outcomes is inevitably influenced by CIOs. While financial resource availability is crucial, the extent to which these resources translate into better CSP can be significantly influenced by the CIO’s structural power. Specifically, we expect CIO structural power to amplify the positive relationship between financial resource availability and CSP for the following reasons.

For a given level of financial resource availability, CIOs with greater structural power can effectively structure, bundle, and leverage financial resources to align IT investments with CSR initiatives and objectives. This is because CIO structural power, which reflects the CIO’s strategic decision-making authority, autonomy, and influence within an organization (Preston et al., 2008), allows the CIO to formally shape IT strategies that optimize the use of financial resources to support the firm’s broader business objectives, including CSR. As a result, with a set level of financial resources, a CIO with substantial structural power can prioritize and secure essential budget allocations for IT initiatives (e.g., sustainable IT operations, ethical data management practices, and transparency-enhancing IT systems) that directly improve CSP, ensuring that financial resources are strategically utilized to achieve both operational efficiencies and social impact.

Furthermore, the resource complementarity perspective posits that “resource complementarities ... can produce super-additive value for firms” (Marvel et al., 2019, p. 1098). This aligns with Sirmon et al.’s (2007) resource management theory, which emphasizes the critical role of managers in structuring, bundling, and deploying resources to maximize their potential and achieve superior organizational performance. For a given level of financial resources, a CIO with substantial structural power can better leverage a firm’s existing IT capabilities as a strategic, complementary asset to improve the effectiveness of those financial resources in strengthening CSP. Considering that IT capabilities represent an important source of competitive advantage (Bharadwaj, 2000), a powerful CIO can orchestrate the efficient allocation of financial resources to IT initiatives that integrate CSR into the business strategy, while also repurposing existing IT infrastructure to complement the allocated financial resources and thereby support CSR initiatives without requiring additional investment. For these reasons, we argue that CIO structural power amplifies the positive relationship between financial resource availability and CSP by ensuring that those resources are bundled and deployed more effectively and strategically to achieve greater overall CSR success.

Based on the above arguments, we expect that:

H2: CIO structural power amplifies the direct positive relationship between financial resource availability and CSP.

## 2.2.2 CIO Structural Power Amplifies the Employee Productivity-CSP Relationship

Employee productivity and CSP: Human capital has long been recognized as another key organizational resource that can provide a basis for competitive advantage and drive organizational performance (Becker & Gerhart, 1996; Tafti et al., 2022). Productive employees typically utilize resources more efficiently (Miller, 2023), which can reduce unnecessary waste and environmental impacts, leading to more sustainable and cost-effective CSR practices. Higher employee productivity, as measured by the ratio between revenues and the number of employees, indicates that either employees are contributing more effectively to a company’s revenue stream or that the company is optimizing its workforce. Regardless of the route, this increased efficiency generates organizational slack (Roca‐Puig et al., 2019), which provides more latitude to the company and frees up its resources that can then be redirected toward CSR initiatives, leading to strengthened CSP.

In the management literature, productive employees are found to be more engaged and committed within an organization (Harter et al., 2002), making them more inclined to participate in and meaningfully contribute to CSR initiatives (e.g., volunteering, participating in sustainability programs, or contributing ideas for social projects). However, resource management theory (Sirmon et al., 2007) emphasizes that the mere possession of high-quality resources (in this case, human capital) is insufficient to achieve superior performance; their potential must be realized through the effective structuring, bundling, and leveraging of these resources by strategic leadership, such as that provided by CIOs with substantial structural power.

The moderating effect of CIO structural power: CIO structural power can amplify the positive relationship between employee productivity and CSP in ways discussed as follows. For a set level of employee productivity, a CIO with greater structural power can foster cross-functional collaboration and coordination (Feng et al., 2021; Smaltz et al., 2006) to better integrate IT systems that support an organization’s CSR initiatives, effectively bundling human and technological resources to ensure that the efforts of employees are effectively aligned with the organization’s social and environmental goals. This alignment is in line with Sirmon et al.’s (2007) core premise that resources must be effectively managed and bundled to enhance organizational outcomes and also underscores that the strategic use of IT systems, directed by a structurally powerful CIO, can amplify the positive impact of employee productivity on CSP.

Holding employee productivity constant and leveraging their role as information stewards (Smaltz et al., 2006), powerful CIOs can facilitate more efficient communication, coordination, and execution of CSR initiatives by, for example, implementing digital collaboration platforms or CSR portals that effectively disseminate CSR information across an organization. This reflects the CIO’s strategic decision-making authority (derived from structural power) in deploying IT resources to complement and enhance human resources, consistent with the resource complementarity and bundling perspective discussed in the previous section (Marvel et al., 2019; Sirmon et al., 2007, 2008). Indeed, the increased visibility and information dissemination (enabled by a CIO with greater structural power) not only foster a sense of accomplishment among productive employees but also encourage their further participation.

As an integrator (Smaltz et al., 2006), a powerful CIO can harness technology to facilitate easier participation in CSR initiatives by productive employees, developing user-friendly IT platforms that seamlessly integrate CSR activities into their daily work. This strategic management and orchestration of technological and human resources not only motivates productive employees but also provides them with the necessary digital tools to efficiently channel their skills and energy towards improving CSP, without disrupting their core responsibilities. This is particularly impactful for productive employees, who are often recognized by practitioners as possessing advanced skills, creative thinking, and problem-solving abilities (CrossTie Solutions, 2022), making them well-suited to identify, plan, and implement impactful CSR initiatives. By effectively managing and orchestrating these high-quality human resources in conjunction with IT resources, a CIO with structural power can create unique, firm-specific resource configurations for CSR initiatives that are difficult for competitors to replicate. In this way, the CIO’s structural power significantly enhances the positive effect of employee productivity on the organization’s CSR outcomes.

Based on the above arguments, we hypothesize that:

H3: CIO structural power amplifies the direct positive relationship between employee productivity and CSP.

## 3.2 Variable Measurement

## 3.2.1 Independent Variable: CIO Structural Power

CIO structural power in the TMT is challenging for researchers to observe. Existing studies typically use surveys to assess cross-sectional variations in CIO structural power. However, since it is usually infeasible to repeatedly survey a publicly traded firm, such an approach does not capture longitudinal variations within a given company. We drew on Feng et al. (2021) by using objective power indicants to infer CIO structural power. Similar power indicants have been used to study CMO structural power (Feng et al., 2015) and CEO structural power (Kolev et al., 2017). These prior studies have demonstrated that executive structural power is multidimensional. To account for this multidimensionality, we measured CIO structural power via the following dimensions: CIO presence in the TMT, CIO compensation relative to peers, CIO pay rank in the TMT hierarchy, and CIO tenure in a top-paid position. Following Feng et al. (2021), we constructed metrics for each of these dimensions as follows.

CIO presence in the TMT is the first component of CIO structural power. Since a firm’s TMT is responsible for daily decision-making and the allocation of organizational assets (Finkelstein & Hambrick, 1990), CIO presence indicates that a firm views IT as being important (Liu & Preston, 2021) and thus desires to allocate power to an IT-related executive(s) who can share IT- and IS-related knowledge with the TMT (Chatterjee et al., 2001). Therefore, we used the proportion of a firm’s named executive officers with ITrelated job titles as the first indicator of CIO structural power. Specifically, we first text-mined the titles of all executives in the Execucomp database using a combination of the following IT-related keywords: “cio,” “cto,” “technology,” “tech,” “information,” “info,” “system,” “software,” “it,” and “is.” Next, two coders manually examined the titles identified in the first step to further rule out non-CIOs and resolve any discrepancies to reach consensus (intercoder agreement in the first step was more than 90%). Finally, we divided the total number of IT-related executive(s) by the reported TMT size to account for the relative representation of CIOs within the TMT. In our sample, 267 of 1,110 firms had a CIO present at least once during the sampling period.

The second component of CIO structural power is the total compensation offered to CIO(s) relative to other TMT members (including the CEO). Executive compensation relative to others in the TMT can indicate an executive’s relative power and centrality to the organization (Chang et al., 2010). In practice, compensation committees adjust pay differentials to better motivate employees and to signal the structure of the corporate hierarchy (Carpenter & Wade, 2002; Main et al., 1993). Therefore, we used the total compensation offered to CIO(s) as a fraction of the total compensation of the whole TMT as the second indicator of CIO structural power.

The third component of CIO structural power is the hierarchical position of the CIO. Based on the preceding arguments and the view that relative pay provides the most direct way of assessing the hierarchical position of each member of the TMT (Brass & Burkhardt, 1993), (TMT size) − (CIO pay rank) we used the following metric, (TMT size)− 1 to capture the hierarchical position of the CIO. CIO pay rank is calculated within the TMT. This metric accounts for different sizes of a TMT so that, regardless of the size of the TMT, the measure will reside on the interval [0, 1]. Furthermore, the metric is reverse coded so that larger values are associated with a higher CIO rank and thus more CIO structural power. Execucomp’s EXECRANKANN variable provides the pay rank of top executives based on total salary and bonuses.

The fourth component of CIO structural power is the CIO’s tenure. Longer tenure in a position typically suggests that an executive has greater power (Kolev et al., 2017; Krause et al., 2015). To measure tenure, we followed the extant literature (Carter et al., 2019; Erkens, 2011; Feng et al., 2021) by assuming that the CIO’s tenure begins from the first year the executive appears within a firm in the Execucomp database.

The above indicants of CIO structural power are highly correlated with each other (intervariable correlations range from 0.597 to 0.898 using the maximum sample size), which can lead to a multicollinearity issue. To address this concern, we constructed a composite measure by employing a principal component factor analysis. The factor analysis shows that all indicants load onto a single factor, accounting for 79.49% of the total variance with the following factor loadings: 0.9378, 0.9372, 0.8741, and 0.8110. After rotating the factor loadings, we obtained the Bartlett factor score and then normalized this score to [0, 10] to arrive at our CIO structural power metric.

## 3.2.2 Outcome Variable: CSP

We used the Kinder, Lydenberg, and Domini (KLD) database to construct our measure of CSP. We adopted the KLD database for three reasons. First, KLD provides comprehensive coverage of different underlying CSR dimensions (Kang et al., 2016), and prior research suggests that KLD ratings have “substantial and discernible validity with especially strong internal discriminant validity” (Kim et al., 2012, p. 767). Second, many researchers consider the KLD database to be the de facto standard for measuring CSP (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2012; Mattingly & Berman, 2006), and KLD has been used extensively by prior studies across multiple business fields, including

IS, management, accounting, marketing, and business ethics (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2020; David et al., 2007; Kang et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2012; Sun & Govind, 2022). Lastly, a recent IS study of CSP and data breach risk indicated that “there are few sources of independent evaluations of social activity, but KLD (now known as MSCI) has been shown to have several advantages over others” (D’Arcy et al., 2020, p. 1209).

Following prior research (D’Arcy et al., 2020; Kang et al., 2016), we considered the seven major CSP dimensions employed by KLD—including community, corporate governance, diversity, employee relations, environment, human rights, and product—and used the summated index of these dimensions as our measure of CSP. The data vendor of KLD evaluates its universe of firms on a yearly basis. Specifically,

the evaluations are performed each year at the same time in order to reduce the likelihood of seasonal effects. KLD uses its research staff to evaluate the companies on objective rules, and any disagreements between staff are resolved by a third party. The objective assessments are based on information from public disclosures, company and government documents, financial statements, academic publications, database searches, press releases, and interviews with corporate managers, regulators, and activist groups. Finally, the ratings are sent to each firm to provide managers with the opportunity to discuss discrepancies … In terms of scoring, each year KLD assigns each firm a one if a particular CSP strength or concern exists and zero otherwise. (D’Arcy et al., 2020, p. 1209)

See Online Appendix B of D’Arcy et al. (2020) for a detailed description of these seven CSP dimensions. Following the recommendation of Kang et al. (2016), we standardized the sum on a yearly basis using the KLD universe to arrive at our formal measure of CSP.<sup>5</sup>

## 3.2.3 Mediating Variable: Innovative Culture

Innovative culture, while not directly discernible to external observers, can be inferred through various indicators such as corporate websites (Guiso et al., 2015) or questionnaires administered to executives (Graham et al., 2022). Our study adopted a measure recently developed by Li et al. (2021), which employs a machine learning approach. This measure, derived from over 62,000 corporate earnings call transcripts, identifies five dimensions of corporate culture: innovation, integrity, quality, respect, and teamwork.

Each public company in their sample is assigned a score in each dimension to measure the corporate cultural value of the corresponding dimension. We therefore adopted the scores for the innovation dimension as an objective measure of a company’s innovative culture. A higher score indicates a stronger innovative culture. Though the measure by Li et al. (2021) is not without limitations, it represents a time-varying measure that they validated against established indicators and alternative measures. The paper’s citation count, exceeding 650 on Google Scholar as of January 2, 2025, further underscores the potential usefulness and impact of the measure. By using this measure, we maintain consistency with the extant literature.

## 3.2.4 Other Independent Variables of Interest: Financial Resource Availability and Employee Productivity

Financial resource availability: Following the extant research, we used profitability as our measure of financial resource availability since “profitability and profit are direct measures of self-generated funding, a major supply of pecuniary resources … and also represent a tangible indicator of the availability of resources to potentially fund social investments” (Julian & Ofori‐dankwa, 2013, p. 1321). Profitability was calculated using the ratio of earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA in Compustat) to total assets (Cheng et al., 2022; Ferreira et al., 2018).

Employee productivity: Consistent with prior research in the business and IS literature (Chang & Gurbaxani, 2012; Pathak et al., 2014; Rust & Huang, 2012; Song & Zahedi, 2006), we measured employee productivity using total sales divided by the total number of employees.

## 3.2.5 Control Variables

We incorporated additional variables to control for TMT, board, firm, industry, and time heterogeneities. Specifically, we included variables that reflect TMT and board heterogeneities, namely board compensation concentration,<sup>6</sup> female CEO, CEO duality, TMT gender concentration, CEO tenure, and reported TMT size. Consistent with Jo and Harjoto (2012), who highlighted the importance of corporate governance in shaping CSR outcomes, we controlled for TMT and board characteristics that are related to a company’s corporate governance and equality (e.g., board compensation concentration, female CEO, and TMT gender concentration). Further, these characteristics may partially reflect a company’s existing CSR practices (e.g., Macaulay et al., 2018; Wang & Coffey, 1992). We included CEO tenure and CEO duality as two proxies for CEO power (Kolev et al., 2017), which could influence strategic decisions and investment outlays. With boards playing a central role in CEO nomination and dismissal, a longer CEO tenure is not only indicative of the board’s acceptance of firm performance under a CEO’s leadership but also allows CEOs to improve their influence and stature in the firm. The internal networks and relationships built over time by CEOs allow them to wield and leverage structural bases of power to garner support and facilitate the implementation of the IS agenda in connection with the strategic agenda. Similarly, CEO duality, i.e., a CEO also serving as a chairman of the board, is an additional indicator of CEO influence on both the strategic and the governance aspects of the firm. Considered together, both tenure and duality are indications of strong CEO power in the upper echelons and the firm. By explicitly controlling for the above TMT and board characteristics, we attempted to reduce potential confounds and better isolate the effect of CIO structural power in the CSR context.

We also included two control variables at the firm level, namely, prior CSR performance and IT capability. Specifically, prior CSR performance was captured by an indicator variable (termed Top CSR performer) that is equal to 1 if a company’s unstandardized CSP belongs to the top tertile group within a given year in our sample, and 0 otherwise. This inclusion not only assisted in controlling for the company’s prior CSR performance but also captured the influence of upper echelons characteristics on the firm’s CSR outcomes (since this variable provides a proxy reflecting the strategic decisions and leadership impact of upper echelons members on CSR initiatives and practices). Additionally, this approach avoided the direct inclusion of the lagged dependent variable as a covariate, thereby mitigating the potential introduction of biases into our empirical analysis (Greene, 2012; Nickell, 1981). Regarding IT capability, we included an indicator variable (termed IT innovator) that is equal to 1 if a company is included on the InformationWeek 500 list, and 0 otherwise. It reflects a company’s IT capability (Bharadwaj, 2000) and reveals the company’s commitment to leveraging IT for operations and competitive advantage. Given that the InformationWeek survey requires firms to have at least \$250 million in sales to be considered for inclusion, we restricted our sample to firm-year observations with at least \$250 million in annual sales.

In addition, we included several other variables that reflect firm-level heterogeneities, namely knowledge capital, book-to-market ratio, business scope, intangible assets, number of employees (log-transformed), and financial leverage. At the industry level, we controlled for industry turbulence, industry IT intensity, and industry concentration. Lastly, we incorporated industry fixed effects and an indicator variable for years of economic contraction (1 for the contraction years 2008 and 2009, and 0 otherwise) to reduce the impact of industry-level and time unobservables.

## 4.2 Endogeneity of CIO Structural Power

While we included an extensive set of controls in our empirical model to control for TMT/board, firm, industry, and time heterogeneities, the problem of omitted-variable bias may still exist in our empirical specification. For instance, the amount of structural power endowed to a CIO could be correlated with a company’s management practice, which is omitted from the list of control variables and is unobservable to researchers. Omitting relevant variables may lead to a biased estimate of our variables of interest, leading to an endogeneity concern (due to omitted-variable bias). To alleviate such an endogeneity concern, we adopted the control function approach to “solv[e] the problem of endogenous explanatory variables (EEVs) in linear and nonlinear models” (Wooldridge, 2015, p. 420). According to Wooldridge (2015) and Lamont and Sieiro (2020), the control function approach is particularly useful when the research focus is on identifying the interaction effects between the endogenous variable and other variables (i.e., profitability and employee productivity in our case).

The first step of the control function approach was to estimate an auxiliary regression of the endogenous variable against all the control variables plus an instrumental variable and then obtain the estimated residuals from the auxiliary regression model. We leveraged geographic variation of CIO structural power by adopting the yearly average of CIO structural power within the same US Census region (four census regions in total) as our instrumental variable.<sup>8</sup> Due to organizational isomorphism and imitation (Mizruchi & Fein, 1999; Son & Benbasat, 2007; Wiedeck & Engelen, 2018), a public company is likely to endow its CIO with a similar level of structural power as other companies within the same geographic region. Therefore, a company’s CIO structural power is expected to correlate with the yearly average of CIO structural power within the same census region where the company is headquartered. Of note, information regarding a company’s headquarters state was obtained from Compustat and cross-verified using the business address listed in the company’s annual reports<sup>9</sup> and the datasets of headquarters states used by Jennings et al. (2017) and Gao et al. (2021).

As shown in Appendix Table A2, we found empirical support that our instrumental variable is significantly and positively related to CIO structural power. However, it is unlikely that a single company could significantly influence the decisions of other companies in a way that would substantially change the annual average value of CIO structural power within the same geographic region, given that each region encompasses a diverse array of firms from different industry sectors. For these reasons, we argue that our instrumental variable likely satisfies both the relevance and exogeneity requirements. We recognize potential limitations related to the exclusion restriction, as regional factors such as local institutional environments or industry clusters could potentially influence both CIO structural power and CSR outcomes. However, the broad geographic coverage of our sample and the inclusion of industry controls helped to mitigate these concerns. As indicated in the previous subsection, after obtaining the estimated residuals from the auxiliary regression, we plugged this additional variable (termed control function residuals) into Equations (1)-(3) to alleviate endogeneity concerns about omitted-variable bias.

## 4.4 Robustness Checks

We conducted a series of robustness checks to examine the robustness of our main findings; our findings remain qualitatively unchanged in each of the following tests. For the results reported in Table 6, we adopted alternative measures for some of the key variables of interest. Specifically, for Columns 1 (without innovative culture) and 2 (with innovative culture), instead of using the standardized values of the sum of the CSR dimensions, we replaced the dependent variable with the original values of the sum of all seven CSR dimensions. For Columns 3 and 4, we used the average of the standardized values of the sum of all seven CSR dimensions from t to t+1 as the dependent variable. Next, we used three power indicators (excluding CIO tenure, which was included in our original construction) in a new factor analysis to construct the CIO structural power variable. We report the results in Columns 5-7. Lastly, for Columns 8-10, we used industry median-adjusted employee productivity instead of the original values. By adjusting for annual industry medians, we accounted for sector-specific differences and compared employee productivity in a more homogeneous context.<sup>11</sup>

For the results reported in Table 7, we adopted different model specifications. For Column 1, we revised Equation (2) by measuring the dependent variable (innovative culture) at t+1 (instead of t). Subsequently, we replaced innovative culture at t with innovative culture at t+1 in Equation (3). As shown in Columns 1 and 2, our proposed mechanism (i.e., CIO structural power → Innovative culture → CSP) remains valid. For Columns 3-5, we incorporated industry-by-year fixed effects to further control for industry booms and busts. For Column 6, we revised Equation (3) to include the additional four subdimensions of corporate culture (i.e., teamwork culture, integrity culture, respectful culture, and quality culture), as detailed in Li et al. (2021).

Unlike innovative culture, the coefficients for all four other cultural subdimensions are statistically nonsignificant. Lastly, we note that some companies include a chief sustainability officer (CSO) in their TMTs, whose responsibility is to ensure that the company follows a sustainable growth strategy while being socially responsible (Fu et al., 2020). Although the prevalence of CSO is very low (a CSO was only present in approximately 0.65% of all firm-year observations in our sample), we expanded our model by incorporating CSO structural power as an additional control variable (where CSO structural power is constructed similarly to how we constructed CIO structural power). We found that the inclusion of CSO structural power did not alter our main findings and that CSO structural power is not significantly associated with CSP (p > 0.10). Given that CSO presence is extremely low, we caution that our results should be interpreted as only tentative evidence that the CSO’s structural power has low effectiveness.

Nonlinear effect of CIO structural power: Instead of using a linear model, we considered the quadratic and cubic terms of CIO structural power. In Columns 1-3 of Table 8, the quadratic term of CIO structural power is negative and statistically significant. However, calculations based on the coefficients and a simple slope plot (which is available from the authors upon request) indicate that the inflection point of the nonlinear relationship occurs when CIO structural power exceeds its maximum possible value, namely 10. The mean and the standard deviation of CIO structural power are 0.398 and 1.107, indicating that the inflection point is well over the third standard deviation of the CIO structural power. Thus, these results indicate that CIO structural power is estimated to have an overall positive effect over the entire relevant range of the structural power measure. Further, Columns 4-6 of Table 8 show that the cubic term of CIO structural power is statistically nonsignificant. Overall, the potential nonlinearity of CIO structural power does not appear to have affected our results qualitatively.

## 5 Discussion and Conclusion

Extending past research on CIO structural power and complementing the extant literature on CSR, this study demonstrates the multifaceted roles of CIO structural power in connection with a public company’s social performance. Our theoretical lenses are based on the CIO structural power framework from the IS literature (Feng et al., 2021; Preston et al., 2008) and resource management theory from the management literature (Sirmon et al., 2007, 2008). We find that CIO structural power is positively associated with CSP through improved innovative culture and that CIO structural power amplifies the effectiveness of organizational resource bases in driving CSP. These findings have the following theoretical and practical implications.

## 5.1 Theoretical Implications

This study has several theoretical implications. First, we extend the concept of structural power (Finkelstein, 1992) beyond its traditional focus on economic outcomes, demonstrating its dual roles in shaping organizational outcomes in general and CSP in particular. Although CIO structural power has been examined in several prior studies (see Table 1 for details), the non-economic role of CIO structural power remains understudied. Researchers have emphasized the importance of linking a company’s CSR initiatives to its leadership team, as this connection reveals the core decision-makers behind the company’s social involvement and performance (Manner, 2010; Sun & Govind, 2022; Tang et al., 2015). Building on Preston et al. (2008), we demonstrate that structural power equips CIOs with the strategic decision-making authority necessary to drive important non-economic organizational outcomes. As one of the first studies in the IS literature to identify the CIO as a key TMT member whose structural power is particularly crucial for advancing CSP, we highlight the dual roles of CIO structural power in this research: first, as a key driver of corporate innovative culture, fostering creativity and adaptability to help organizations address complex CSR challenges, thereby enhancing subsequent CSP, and second, as a strategic enabler that amplifies the positive effects of organizational resources, such as financial resource availability and employee productivity, on CSP. Overall, our findings broaden the theoretical framework for understanding CSP and demonstrate that the multifaceted influence of CIOs extends beyond traditional IT or operational outcomes to include vital social and environmental responsibilities.<sup>12</sup>

Second, our findings regarding the amplifying role of CIO structural power in driving the relationships between organizational resource bases and CSP underscore the importance of integrating resource management theory (Sirmon et al., 2007) and the structural power perspective (Finkelstein, 1992) to more comprehensively understand the effectiveness of an important TMT member. Managers play a critical role in orchestrating and managing resources to maximize their potential and enhance organizational performance (Sirmon et al., 2011). By demonstrating how CIO structural power moderates the impact of financial resources and employee productivity on CSP, we provide a nuanced theoretical contribution to advance both the resource management theory and the CIO power literature. These moderating effects provide a clearer understanding of how organizational resources are transformed into CSP outcomes through the strategic orchestration enabled by the structural power of a key TMT member. We also expand the CIO literature by introducing a more nuanced conceptual understanding of CIOs’ diverse roles in organizations that have varying degrees of financial resource availability and human capital.

Our study also contributes to the microfoundations perspective in CSR research (Aguinis & Glavas, 2012) by identifying innovative culture as a mediating mechanism between CIO structural power and CSP. Our study demonstrates how an individual-level factor (i.e., CIO structural power) shapes an organizational-level construct (innovative culture), which subsequently influences CSR outcomes. This multilevel integration offers a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying process of driving CSP improvement.

In addition, although innovative culture is already recognized as an important organizational resource for maintaining competitive advantage (Wei et al., 2013), our finding that CIO structural power enhances innovative culture contributes to the resource-based literature by demonstrating how senior executives can cultivate critical, intangible organizational resources. The current study expands our understanding of the mechanisms through which empowered CIOs can create sustained competitive advantage, not just through direct management of IT resources but by shaping the innovation aspect of the overall corporate culture. Our findings also align with Sirmon et al.’s (2007) extension of the classic resource-based view (Barney, 1991) by explicitly emphasizing the role of managers in orchestrating resources to create value and achieve competitive advantage—a dimension that the classic resource-based view does not fully address. Overall, these findings link the strategic role of CIOs to broader organizational success (and non-economic outcomes), further underscoring the far-reaching impact of IT leadership beyond traditional technological boundaries and highlighting key boundary conditions for the effectiveness of CIO structural power.

## 5.2 Managerial and Practical Implications

Managerially, as firms attempt to enhance their CSP, we show that the CIO could be a key player who has been overlooked by the board in the CSR context. Only recently have practitioners started to advocate for the involvement of the CIO in CSR initiatives (CIO & Leader, 2013; Nardecchia, 2021; Trueman, 2019). Attention to CIOs has substantially increased due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which has led to the heightened importance of digital transformation (CIO.com, 2022). Using an empirical investigation, we confirm the benefits of endowing CIOs with sufficient power in the CSR context. While some IT professionals recognize the underlying value of CIOs to a modern organization, many outsiders still have doubts about their value. For instance, Business Chief (2020) reports that “there has been a lot of speculation along the theme that CIO no longer means Chief Information Officer, but instead stands for Career Is Over.” Even some insiders have become less confident about the future of CIOs. Belinda Finch of Three UK (a major UK telecommunications and internet service provider) stated that “there will be a time when the CIO is no longer needed.” (Forsdick, 2022)

In contrast to the above pessimistic views, this research highlights that the value of CIOs goes beyond economic gains for modern organizations. When endowed with sufficient structural power, CIOs are instrumental for a public company’s CSP. Our empirical findings shed light on an important mechanism demonstrating how the structural power of CIOs can lead to improved CSP by influencing or reshaping the company’s innovative culture. As more and more organizations are seeking to understand drivers of enhanced CSP (Brown & Forster, 2013; O’Riordan & Fairbrass, 2014), this research helps fill a crucial gap in understanding the role of IT leadership, specifically that of CIOs, in driving CSP. This study therefore provides valuable practical insights for public companies seeking to enhance their CSP through strategic leadership and cultural transformation. Further, our results suggest that when scarce organizational resources (especially the availability of financial resources and productive employees) are geared towards CSR activities, CIOs with greater structural power can strengthen the efficacy of these resource bases in driving a much better CSP.

We hope the current research can help alleviate concerns about whether to endow CIOs with sufficient power. Combining our findings with recent research that showcases the impact of CIO structural power on corporate financial performance (e.g., Feng et al., 2021), we suggest that CIOs should be regarded as major team members of the TMT instead of peripheral players with marginal CSR value. Our empirical research can solidify the ongoing discussions regarding the multiple benefits of having CIOs in place. For public companies without a formal CIO, we believe they should actively consider appointing one (and accord them with sufficient structural power); for companies with an existing CIO of low structural power, the board of directors should reconsider the TMT’s power allocation, especially if the board wants to achieve significant improvements in both non-financial and financial performance. To increase CIO structural power, the board could increase the compensation of its CIO (in absolute terms and relative to other firms’ CIOs and other members of its TMT) and prioritize retaining the current CIO so that frequent turnover of this position does not occur. Furthermore, should it become necessary to downsize the TMT, boards should be careful to keep the CIO within the upper echelon. Also, as executives leading other functional divisions are added to the TMT, boards should consider incorporating executives with primarily IT-related responsibilities into the TMT.

## 5.3 Limitations and Future Research Directions

Our paper has several limitations to acknowledge. First, similar to prior studies using the KLD database, we relied on CSR indicators to understand the determinants of CSP. Using this approach involved an implicit assumption that the degree of CSR was uniform across all the CSR dimensions tracked by KLD. In reality, even under the same CSR category, firms within the same industry may devote different levels of effort to develop their own CSR programs. Furthermore, the impact of CIO structural power may vary across different types of CSR initiatives. In additional analysis related to our focal study, we took an initial step in examining potential disparate effects across CSR dimensions by distinguishing between CSR strengths and CSR weaknesses. Our results suggest that empowered CIOs may play a role in both enlarging CSR proficiencies and mitigating CSR concerns. However, the mechanism may differ substantially across the two settings. For instance, our empirical findings suggest that innovative culture is not a potential mediator when it comes to CSR weaknesses, nor does CIO structural power moderate how organizational resources are associated with CSR weaknesses. Thus, other mechanisms (apart from innovative culture) may be at play in how CIO structural power reduces CSR weaknesses. Similarly, while the connecting bridges forged by a CIO within and outside the firm are crucial for building upon CSR performance strengths, simply conveying and integrating information may not suffice to boost the effectiveness of organizational resources in tackling significant CSR weaknesses, such as when a firm does not have a directive to create a labor- and environmentally friendly workplace.

Second, our empirical results are based entirely on data from publicly listed firms. Therefore, it is unclear whether the current results can be generalized to private companies, many of which are also very concerned about CSR. Third, our measure of CIO title was dependent upon the computer-aided text analysis of executive titles. While using the executive title to identify functional leaders is a common practice in TMT research, we cannot rule out the possibility that other functional leaders without IT-related titles may also actively contribute to a firm’s development of IT-CSR strategy. Therefore, future research could leverage survey research or in-depth interviews to further understand how IT decision-making is conducted within the TMT. Fourth, other variables might mediate the relationship between CIO structural power and CSP. Therefore, future studies should consider collecting additional data to uncover these underlying mechanisms—for instance, researchers could leverage primary data (e.g., data collected from survey studies) to understand what specific measures a powerful CIO can take internally to help a company achieve a better CSP. Fifth, it is important to note that many other TMT factors outside of CIO structural power may influence CSP. For instance, our empirical results show that TMT gende concentration is negatively associated with CSP (see Table 5), suggesting that gender diversity in TMT could be an important consideration in driving CSP. Future research should identify additional factors that influence CSP and assess the relative effectiveness of the various contributors to CSP. Sixth, the lack of information on corporate green IT investments or green IT capabilities prevented us from fully exploring the direct technological mechanisms through which CIO structural power may influence CSP. While our use of KLD ratings aligns with prior literature, future research could validate our findings using alternative metrics for environmental performance and sustainability. Future research should aim to collect and analyze such data to provide a more precise understanding of the various pathways from CIO structural power to improved CSP outcomes.<sup>13</sup> Lastly, we acknowledge that our research is correlational, although we attempted to mitigate the endogeneity concern of CIO structural power. Thus, causal inferences should not be made. Future research should explore potential exogenous shocks that might vary CIO structural power to establish causality among the proposed relationships.

## About the Authors

His research has been published in or accepted by the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and Journal of Retailing, among other outlets. He is the recipient of the 2022 and 2020 Outstanding Junior Researcher Awards from the School of Business Administration at the University of Mississippi, the sole runner-up for the 2017 Service Science Best Article Award from INFORMS, the All-University Doctoral Prize from Syracuse University, and the Dr. Torpey Teaching Award from the Whitman School of Management at Syracuse University.

Pankaj C. Patel holds the Frank J. and Jane E. Ryan Endowed Chair in Strategy and Innovation and is a professor of management at Villanova University. He received his PhD from the University of Louisville. His research interests are the intersection of innovation and governance.

Scott Fay is a professor of marketing at the Whitman School of Management at Syracuse University. Professor Fay received his PhD in economics from the University of Michigan. He previously taught at the University of Florida. Professor Fay’s primary research approach is to develop parsimonious analytical models to gain fundamental insights about important substantive issues faced by firms and consumers. He is particularly interested in examining how firms can optimally harness the power of new technologies. He has published 30+ papers in outlets that include Marketing Science, Management Science, the American Economic Review, Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Journal of Retailing He is on the editorial board of Marketing Science, Journal of Retailing, and Journal of Interactive Marketing. He was named an Outstanding Reviewer for the Journal of Production & Operations Management and has received both the Distinguished Service Award and the Meritorious Service Award from Management Science multiple times.

Huigang Liang holds the FedEx Chair of Excellence and is a professor of information systems at the University of Memphis. His research focuses on sociobehavioral, managerial, and strategic IT issues at both individual and organizational levels in a variety of contexts, including cybersecurity, healthcare IT, online platforms, and artificial intelligence. His work has appeared in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Journal of the Association for Information Systems, among other outlets. He is currently an associate editor for Information Systems Research and a senior editor for Journal of the Association for Information Systems.
