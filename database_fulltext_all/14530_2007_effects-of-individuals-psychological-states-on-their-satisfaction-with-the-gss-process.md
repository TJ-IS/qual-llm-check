# Effects of individuals’ psychological states on their satisfaction with the GSS process

Mark Srite <sup>a,1,\*</sup>, John E. Galvin <sup>b,1</sup>, Manju K. Ahuja <sup>c,1</sup>, Elena Karahanna <sup>d,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Management Information Systems, Sheldon B. Lubar School of Business, University of Wisconsin-Milwaukeee, Milwaukee, WI 53201, United States

<sup>b</sup> Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, 801 West Michigan Street, Indianapolis, IN 46202-5151, United States <sup>c</sup> Computer and Information Systems, College of Business, University of Louisville, Louisville, KY 40292, United States <sup>d</sup> MIS Department, Terry College of Business, The University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, United States Received in revised form 1 November 2006; accepted 22 April 2007 Available online 23 July 2007

## Abstract

The use of group support systems (GSS) to improve the outcomes of, and satisfaction with, meetings is increasing. Our empirical study, drawing on the theory of job design, was initiated to investigate the relative contribution of both individual and group characteristics on GSS participants’ satisfaction with the process. Our field study found that when employees used a GSS to brainstorm on an issue of concern, two individual characteristics (locus of control and personal innovativeness) and one group characteristic (group cohesion) had significant impact on user satisfaction with the meeting process. Locus of control, however, was found to have an impact in the opposite direction to our assumptions. Computer anxiety, an individual characteristic, was not found to be significant.

Keywords: Group support systems (GSS); Process satisfaction; Locus of control; Personal innovativeness; Computer anxiety; Group cohesion; Individual characteristics; Group characteristics

Finding a way to enhance the productivity of meetings and increase participants’ satisfaction is important. One approach has been to use GSS and the satisfaction of its members is considered an important indicator of group effectiveness. Unfortunately, past research has been inconsistent in its findings. Meta-analyses of McLeod [22] and Benbasat and Lim [3] have suggested that prior studies did not capture the true complexity of GSS use.

GSS studies on satisfaction with the process and its outcomes have not shown consistent results [2]. Based on Hackman and Oldham’s theory of job design [13], it seems possible that the effects of task activity on satisfaction are mediated by the psychological states of the participants. Though sometimes termed GDSS or CSCW, we use the term GSS here to imply any interactive system that supports meeting processes of groups and aids in electronic brainstorming and voting.

We decided to extend GSS research by examining the effect of individual and group level characteristics on user satisfaction, defined as ‘‘the degree to which group members are happy with the way they arrived at their group decision’’ [29].

## 1. Relevant literature

Research has shown that groups using a GSS are more productive and their members are more satisfied with the process than groups not using one [26]. For example, GSS groups have been found to generate a larger quantity of higher quality ideas [23]. GSS help eliminate information biases of face-to-face groups and participants waste less time discussing information known to all. Indeed, in a meta-analysis, McLeod concluded that the system enhanced task focus, participation equality, task performance, consensus, and satisfaction with outcomes. Specifically, brainstorming using GSS relied on these advantages to improve access to information for all participants and enhanced the power and speed with which the information was acquired, processed, and used.

A review of GSS research suggests that satisfaction has been widely used as an outcome variable, though not always with consistent results. Dennis et al. [7] reviewed 35 experiments and field studies and concluded that the inconsistent findings were probably due to non-uniform situations. Member satisfaction was chosen as the outcome variable in 19 of the studies and 11 reported that GSS use increased member satisfaction, 5 reported a decrease, and 3 reported no change.

Fjermestad and Hiltz [11] examined 54 GSS case and field studies. Although a formal meta-analysis was not made, they provided a summary of what was studied, the methodologies used, and findings. They reported that 13 studies examined outcome satisfaction, with 11 showing that GSS improved outcome satisfaction while 2 reported no differences. They also noted that process satisfaction was the outcome measure in 22 studies and that 20 found that the GSS procedure increased process satisfaction; the 2 other studies found no difference in process satisfaction due to GSS.

A review of their ‘‘process satisfaction’’ outcome articles showed the following factors to have been considered:

Dennis and Gallupe [6] examined GSS research literature and concluded that field studies generally found positive outcomes of GSS use. Similarly, Whitaker [31] suggested that laboratory studies provide mixed results, because they make an assumption of rational information processing by participants wishing to optimize outcomes [21]. Dennis and Gallupe called for GSS research to focus more on real people in real settings.

DeSanctis and Poole [8] suggested that if people are directed to use a system, their morale and productivity would suffer when they were not satisfied with it. They also said that acceptance of, and satisfaction with, GSS was determined by the characteristics of the user, the group, the task, and the system itself. Hackman and Oldham included this as a part of their theory, shown in Fig. 1, suggesting that job characteristics influences an individual’s psychological states and thus influences personal outcomes [14]. Three psychological states influenced the outcome of high internal work motivation.

## 2. Research model

While satisfaction with the GSS process has been studied previously, the focus has usually been on the work situation (facilitation, brainstorming, decision time, etc.) rather than the psychological states of the individual. In our research we kept the work situation constant and examined the effects of individual psychological states on user satisfaction with the GSS process.

Our model focused on two areas: (1) individual and (2) group characteristics that affect the GSS process. In our work, two personality variables (locus of control and personal innovativeness), and one attitudinal variable (computer anxiety) were included as the individual characteristics (cognitive and affective structures developed by individuals to facilitate adjustments to events, people, and situations [12]) of interest. The group characteristic was group cohesion.

We posited that individual characteristics (locus of control, personal innovativeness, and computer anxiety) affect process satisfaction with GSS meetings. We also assumed that group cohesion influenced overall satisfaction with GSS meetings. The model is shown in Fig. 2.

We selected individual characteristics based on their potential importance to the GSS task. In the job characteristics model, the critical psychological states are influenced by core job characteristics. In fact, experience of critical psychological states can derive from individual differences.

The first individual difference variable selected was locus of control—the degree to which people feel that control of their own successes or failures in a specific situation [17]. This model suggests that individuals are affected by their autonomy (the way they perceive their freedom, independence, and discretion to accomplish a work task) and that this then influences their satisfaction with the process [18].

Fig. 1. Hackman and Oldham’s job design/satisfaction model.

The second individual variable chosen was personal innovativeness—a user’s willingness to try a new IT, or a measure of his or her receptivity to change. Since the GSS represented a relatively new and varied experience, it enhanced the skill variety aspect of the task (the extent to which the task uses the skills and talents of the individual). It follows that innovative individuals who are receptive and seek such experiences are likely to find the task more meaningful.

Therefore, the two individual differences focused on two of the three critical psychological states. Our study did not allow for variation in the knowledge of the outcome of the work since all individuals received the same feedback at the same time.

Three other variables were included in the job design/job satisfaction model: knowledge and skill, context satisfaction, and growth need strength. While we included constructs to capture the effects of the first two, the last was not included, since the task was a stand-alone activity, i.e. not likely to be repeated with the same set of individuals. To measure the ‘‘knowledge and skill’’ construct, we included a third individual characteristic specific to a GSS environment: computer anxiety, because it i represented a key skill. Group cohesion was used to measure the ‘‘satisfaction with the work context’’ construct.

## 2.1. Locus of control

Individuals with a strong internal locus of control tend to dominate a situation. Such individuals feel more responsible for the outcome of a GSS task. However, individuals with a strong external locus of control feel that they are victims of events and are content to let others dominate them.

Locus of control’s relationship to job satisfaction has been shown to be positive [9] and it is reasonable to suggest that this would affect the individual’s satisfaction with the GSS process.

Since the use of a GSS limits the opportunity for social exchanges, we proposed:

Hypothesis 1. The level of an individual’s internal locus of control is positively correlated with his or her satisfaction with the GSS process.

## 2.2. Personal innovativeness

Personal innovativeness (PI) is defined as willingness to try a new method. It has been suggested that this influences the acceptance of IT [1] ([1], p. 206). Research has found that innovative individuals have more positive perceptions to the advantage of a specific innovation [24]. For most GSS participants, its use might be their first GSS encounter or represent an infrequent activity. This suggests that individuals who have a higher degree of PI should view the use of a GSS as a positive activity, since it involves a willingness to try new things. This leads to:

Hypothesis 2. The level of an individual’s personal innovativeness is positively correlated with his or her satisfaction with the GSS process.

## 2.3. Computer anxiety

Since GSS necessitate computer use, we posit that individuals who experience computer anxiety are likely to view GSS as less useful than traditional meetings. This resulted in:

Hypothesis 3. The level of an individual’s computer anxiety is negatively correlated with his or her satisfaction with the GSS process.

## 3. Group cohesion

The group characteristic of interest here is cohesion. From a GSS perspective this has seldom been studied. Yet cohesiveness is the resulting psychological state from feedback to the individual ([27], Chapter 7). Cohesion was incorporated in Hackman and Oldham’s model through their construct of ‘‘satisfaction with the work context’’ (satisfaction with pay, job security, Fellow coworkers, and supervisors). In the context of performing a brainstorming task using GSS, the context is that of a group. Thus salient aspects of ‘‘work context satisfaction’’ are those that focus on characteristics of the group context.

We adopted the definition of cohesion from Chin, Salisbury and Gopal [5]: ‘‘perceived cohesion encompasses an individual’s sense of belonging to a particular group and his or her feeling of morale associated with membership in the group.’’ There have been numerous studies linking group cohesion with individual outcomes related to their group activities (e.g., [32]).

In general, the more cohesive the group, the more free the members will feel in the meetings [10]. Members will contribute and feel confident that their contribution will be valued. They will feel less afraid, anxious, or insecure. Harrison [15] stated that groups with low levels of cohesion cooperate less and are ineffective. We proposed:

Hypothesis 4. The level of an individual’s group cohesion is positively correlated with his or her satisfaction with the GSS process.

## 4.1. Research design

A field study was conducted at a large US state university using 46 employees. Personnel from a wide range of departments in the University met in ad hoc groups of 6–12 members to brainstorm on the characteristics of an effective supervisor. This specific issue was selected because an employee satisfaction survey indicated that ‘‘management skill’’ was seen as an important success factor. Rather than use a traditional face-to-face forum to generate ideas, a GSS setting was chosen for these meetings. Participation was by invitation and attendance was not mandated. Thus, the participants were there by choice and they had an interest in the subject and wanted to contribute.

The context of the research was held constant, i.e. the task (brainstorming) and the system (GSS) used by all participants in our experiments were the same. To provide for synergy among participants, the groups were divided into two major categories based on academic or non-academic status and then were further subdivided by organizational level.

This resulted in groups that consisted of one of the following:

(a) all senior administrative managers,

(b) all supervisors, or

(c) all non-managerial staff ranging in size from 6 to 12 participants.

The senior administrative managers consisted of either directors or associate directors in Finance and Administration, who were solicited by the Vice President of Human Resources. The supervisory personnel (e.g., supervisor, senior engineer, assistant director, associate director) were solicited from a list of participants of a Basic Supervisory Practical Training program that is required of all supervisors. They then identified one or more of their employees to participate in the non-managerial staff meetings (e.g., Administrative Assistant, Program Assistant, Training Specialist).

The unequal group size had the potential to bias the results of our study. To control for this, we included group size as a variable in our research model. As we had expected, its effect was non-significant (standardized b = 0.158, significance = 0.16), and we subsequently dropped it from our analysis.

Each meeting began by our explaining that the purpose of the meeting was to identify characteristics of effective supervisors and that this would be done using GSS software to brainstorm the issue. Ideas were anonymously contributed and displayed on a public screen. To reduce the number of ideas to a manageable figure each group member selected his or her top-two choices from the total list of ideas. This resulted in a reduced set (typically 6–10), consisting of any idea selected by at least one member. Finally, each member ranked the reduced list to generate an ordered list of the group’s ideas. Subsequently, the top three ideas were used to generate more specific items.

Data on the constructs of the study were collected using questionnaires completed at the end of the meetings. Appendix I shows the questionnaire items used to measure the constructs of the study. The final results (i.e. top three ideas and related items) were consolidated to form the basis of a supervisor training and evaluation course.

## 4.2. Operationalization of research variables

All research variables were measured using multiitem scales. Scales previously validated, used, and subsequently published by researchers were incorporated to measure all constructs except process satisfaction. Scales for locus of control were adapted from Rotter [25] though, consistent with more current research [28], an abbreviated measure was adopted. Scales for computer anxiety were based on the Computer Anxiety Rating Scale [16] and group cohesion items were adapted from Chin, Salisbury, and Gopal. Since a review of research revealed no scales for GSS process satisfaction, two items were constructed drawing upon empirical evidence of outcomes.

Sample characteristics are shown in Table 2 and descriptive statistics for the research constructs are presented in Table 3. In general, the sample included individuals who had reasonable levels of experience with computing technology though none of the participants had any prior experience with GSS tools. Nearly two-third of the sample was female, which was typical for non-academic employees at the University.

## 4.3. The measurement model

PLS (partial least squares) was used to assess the psychometric properties of the scales. All constructs had reflective indicators, except for process satisfaction, which was modeled as a formative construct of two distinct aspects of satisfaction: with the meeting process and with the individual’s level of participation. Psychometric properties of the scales were assessed in terms of item loadings, discriminant validity, and internal consistency. An initial confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) in PLS showed that some items did not load well on their corresponding constructs. These items were subsequently dropped from the analysis and the CFA was re-run, resulting in Table 4, which show that all items, except one in personal innovativeness (PI3), loaded well (>0.70) on their corresponding factors. The loading for PI3 was 0.65 and a decision was made to retain it. Since process satisfaction was a formative construct, the discussion on loadings and internal consistency is not applicable to this construct. As Table 5 shows, all constructs exhibit more than adequate internal consistency as evidenced by the composite reliability scores [30].

Evidence for discriminant validity is provided when, (a) indicators load more strongly on their corresponding construct than on other constructs in the model and (b) the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) is larger than the inter-construct correlations [4]. All indicators loaded more highly on their own construct than on others. In addition, a comparison of the square root of AVE with inter-construct correlations showed that all constructs shared more variance with their indicators than with other constructs. These results pointed to the discriminant validity of the constructs, and, in conjunction with the loadings and composite reliability scores, indicated acceptable psychometric properties.

## 5. Results

PLS was also used to assess the structural model. The path coefficients and explained variance using a bootstrapping procedure are shown in Fig. 3. As can be seen, both the individual and group level characteristics contributed to process satisfaction. Specifically, group cohesion (path coefficient = 0.68), locus of control (path coefficient = -0.26), and personal innovativeness (path coefficient = 0.19) explained 59% of the variance in process satisfaction. This provided support for Hypotheses 2 (personal innovativeness: significant at 0.1) and 4 (cohesion: significant at 0.01). Even though the path between locus of control and satisfaction was significant at 0.05, the relationship is in the opposite direction than hypothesized: individuals with an external locus of control are likely to be more satisfied with the GSS process. Thus, Hypothesis 1 was not supported. Finally, the relationship between computer anxiety and process satisfaction was nonsignificant (T-statistic = -0.57). This may be due to the fact that the majority of the study participants scored low on computer anxiety (mean = 1.89) and were not apprehensive about using the tool.

## 6. Discussion

Results of model testing provided empirical support for the relationships posited in the model, explaining about 60% of the variance in process satisfaction with GSS meetings. Both individual and group characteristics contributed significantly to the explanation of process satisfaction. Though only a first step, the results indicated that Hackman and Oldham’s job design theory could be a useful arrangement for evaluating GSS outcomes. Our research suggested that both the structure of the work and individual and group characteristics influenced psychological states of the individual and that this in turn influenced the outcomes of motivation and satisfaction. We thus contributed by including the psychological states of the individual and extending the investigation of satisfaction with the GSS process. By keeping the work situation constant, we were able to examine the effects of individuals psychological states on their satisfaction with the GSS process.

The results for locus of control were in the opposite direction to that we expected: individuals with high levels of external locus of control were more satisfied than those with high levels of internal locus of control. Hypothesis 2 was supported as predicted. Personal innovativeness had a significant influence on GSS process satisfaction. Hypothesis 3 was not supported: computer anxiety did not significantly affect the process satisfaction. This may be an artifact of our sample including users of personal computers with an average computer experience of 10.8 years.

Hypothesis 4 was supported; our findings were consistent with prior research [20].

Our results provide preliminary evidence that including group- and individual-level variables provides more explanatory power than using either one. Furthermore, examining interaction of individual and group characteristics may have provided a richer explanation of GSS outcomes.

## 6.1. Limitations and implications

Our study introduced realism in its sample and context. However, this may have been a source of limitations. Unlike many GSS studies, our field study involved an organization using GSS to brainstorm an issue of real concern. Participation in the meetings was seen as part of the employees’ job. Thus, our study participants were not randomly selected and this may have introduced bias into our results. However, this is a tradeoff that field studies must often make. Also, sample size is a problem if the effects may only be found in large samples. We believe that the sample size was not an issue in our experiment. As reported, the measurement model exhibited acceptable results. Finally, process satisfaction was measured using two items. Although these were important aspects, additional items focusing on relational and procedural facets of the activities and member contributions could have had an effect.

Managers may use the findings of this study to enhance satisfaction with GSS use in their organization and thus improve the quality of their outcomes. It is not sufficient to pay attention to either individual or group characteristics in isolation but important that both determinants of process satisfaction are considered.

Another factor that managers may be able to influence is that of group cohesion. Past research has shown that it is an important for successful meetings [19]. One element of success is the presence of a facilitator. As has often been said: managers should consider the use of a trained facilitator as a necessary part of the GSS process.

## 7. Conclusion

Literature has suggested that North American industrial organizations have focused on individual characteristics as determinants of effectiveness and other outcomes. Japanese organizations have been known to emphasize group cohesion over individualized rewards. Even though the relative importance of these factors may be cultural, both factors are likely to be important. Our research has shown that both areas – individual and group – need to be considered when looking at satisfaction with use of a GSS.
