# Self-efficacy, overconfidence, and the negative effect on subsequent performance: A field study

Trevor T. Moores \*, Jerry Cha-Jan Chang <sup>1</sup>

Department of Management Information Systems, University of Nevada Las Vegas, 4505 S Maryland Parkway, Box 6034, Las Vegas, NV 89154-6034, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 14 March 2008 Received in revised form 11 September 2008 Accepted 29 November 2008 Available online 15 January 2009

Keywords: Field study Metacognition Overconfidence Performance PLS Self-efficacy

## A B S T R A C T

Self-efficacy has been used to predict the level of performance or usage of IT. The psychological literature has suggested, however, that rather than promoting behavior, it can lead to overconfidence and reduce performance over time. We investigated this claim by studying the relationship between self-efficacy and performance in a field study. Overconfidence was measured metacognitively as the difference between a person’s expected performance and his or her actual performance. Using PLS and a sample of 108 students in an systems analysis and design course, we found that for the sample as a whole, selfefficacy was positively and significantly related to performance, and that performance was positively and significantly related to subsequent self-efficacy. When levels of over- and under-confidence were taken into account, however, the relationships changed. In particular, overconfidence leads to a significant negative relationship between self-efficacy and subsequent performance. Implications for user training included the need to use performance feedback in order to allow for a recalibration of selfefficacy towards a more accurate self-assessment of ability.

## 1. Introduction

Self-efficacy is considered to be a triadic relationship between three factors: cognitive (personal goals, self-evaluation of performance, and quality of analytical thinking), environmental (level of challenge and circumstances under which the act takes place), and behavioral (choices that are executed) [1].

Actual behavior (accomplishment) provides a feedback loop, with future behavior affected by subsequent outcome expectations and self-efficacy. The stronger the perceived self-efficacy, the higher the challenge people set for themselves and the better a person’s ability to cope with obstacles. By raising one’s selfefficacy, the amount of effort and time one is willing to devote to the task also increases, leading to higher performance.

Given that IT has no value unless it is used, self-efficacy has been adopted within the literature as a task-specific construct to predict its level of performance or usage [18], as a mediator in technology acceptance, and as the product of IS training [19]. It would appear that we need to enhance the self-efficacy during training.

The problem, however, is that there is evidence to suggest that one can become overconfident when a person’s belief about their expected level of performance exceeds their actual performance. In particular, it was suggested that self-efficacy is only satisfaction in one’s level of performance, and complacency may result, leading to a negative relationship between self-efficacy and performance over time [25].

Overconfidence can affect a software engineer’s ability to properly estimate the cost of a software development project [13]. Initial success can also lead financial analysts to become overly confident in their ability to predict the market, leading to lower subsequent performance [11].

Bandura observed a similar effect [2], where students playing a management simulation game retained a high level of selfefficacy in the face of declining performance. This effect was explained in terms of ‘‘complacent self-assurance.’’ But in spite of this, the problem of overconfidence was dismissed as being of no consequence.

However, if self-efficacy is meant to be a good predictor of IT usage or competence, a negative relationship would have a significant impact on the utility of self-efficacy in IT training programs. Therefore, we decided to investigate whether there was any evidence that contradicted the expected relationship.

## 2. Background

Judgment of self-efficacy is made under conditions of uncertainty, because it is typically measured before a task is performed. First impression judgment of self-efficacy is particularly important because it affects the activities that a person decides to pursue, the effort devoted to the activity, and subsequent self-efficacy judgment. Typically, there is a bias towards overly positive selfevaluation because of a tendency to seek confirming rather than non-proving evidence, leading people to overestimate their ability to perform a task.

Other studies have also found that positive information about expected performance (i.e., high performance anchors) increased self-efficacy but not performance. Mildly negative performance anchors had been found to lead to increased effort, attention to strategy, and performance by increasing the challenge of the task, although the effect of positive and negative feedback appeared only to be significant when the task was complex [28]. This suggested that, to promote performance in complex tasks, selfefficacy must be held in check.

## 2.1. The effect of overconfidence

Vancouver and his colleagues [26,27] suggested that high selfefficacy may reduce motivation and have a negative effect on subsequent performance. The positive relationship usually found between self-efficacy and performance is then due to a strong positive relationship between prior performance and subsequent self-efficacy, and a weak negative relationship between selfefficacy and subsequent performance.

They argued that this effect is consistent with Perceptual Control Theory [20], which suggested that human behavior was driven by the perceived difference, or error, between the perceived current external state and an inner desired state. People act on their environment in order to bring their perceived state closer to the desired one. Thus, high self-efficacy may lead to a premature belief that the goal state has been reached, leading to decreased effort and performance.

These hypotheses were tested in a series of experiments using the Mastermind strategy game as an example of complex analytic thinking. The game requires the player to determine the correct arrangement of four colored pegs. In the first experiment, selfefficacy was measured as the perceived likelihood (on a scale of 1– 10) of finding a solution by a certain round. Performance was the number of rounds the player actually took, while the goal was the number of rounds to find a solution in the next game.

As expected, correlations between self-efficacy, performance, and goal was positive and significant. When the analysis was changed to a within-persons (repeated measures) design across individual games, however, self-efficacy and goal for the next game was positively related to prior performance, but negatively related to actual performance in the next game.

When self-efficacy was manipulated by changing the solution to match the players’ attempt on the third attempt, there was no significant difference in the number of rounds required to find a solution compared to a control group, but for the game after the manipulated trial, self-efficacy was higher for the experimental group. For each point increase in self-efficacy, however, performance decreased.

## 2.2. The counter-argument

The experiments conducted by Vancouver et al. were criticized by Bandura at a number of different levels (see [3]). Philosophically, the negative error-reducing mechanism of PCT was seen as fundamentally opposed to SCT’s supposition that people are aspiring, proactive organisms.

Empirically, the claim that self-efficacy had an invariant or debilitating effect on performance was dismissed as being simply false; Bandura argued that under normal conditions past performance was the best predictor of future performance but under challenging conditions prior performance had no predictive value. Any correlation between prior and subsequent behavior simply reflected the degree of commonality of their determinants.

Methodologically, the value of using the Mastermind game was questioned, being seen as too simple to tell anything meaningful about how people behaved in the real world. The fact that the game was one of chance also created problems, since self-efficacy was being related to ability to guess rather than ability to acquire skills to do better.

Furthermore, the use of a within-person design to uncover the negative relationship was criticized, because there was no inherent superiority between intra-personal and inter-personal designs. Rather, they suggest that intra-person designs had the weakness that when one or more traits was stable over time, there may be insufficient variance to detect effects and the contribution of a determinant became underrated.

In response to these criticisms, however, a recent study analyzed the relationship between self-efficacy and performance across a series of four in-class tests [21]. Using a sample of students taking a statistics course, performance was found to be positively and significantly related to subsequent self-efficacy, while selfefficacy was negatively, although not significantly, related to subsequent performance.

## 3. Research model

While it is hard to imagine how individuals could be motivated to tackle more and more complex tasks during their lifetime without some sense of ability-to-do-so, this goal-attainment spiral cannot continue indefinitely. Regardless of the features that might contribute to task failure, they must affect the user’s selfassessment, and that must have an ongoing impact on both beliefs in efficacy and subsequent performance.

Therefore, we set out to investigate the relationship between prior judgment of self-efficacy and performance at time (t1) with subsequent judgment and performance at time (t2). Given the importance of initial first impression as a determinant of tasks chosen by an individual, we made a distinction between initial judgment of self-efficacy and the ongoing relationship between prior and subsequent self-efficacy and performance. We hypothesized that judgment of self-efficacy at time (t1) would have a significant impact on all subsequent constructs. The research model, therefore, was as shown in Fig. 1, where SE1 is a measure of a person’s initial judgment of self-efficacy at time (t1), denoted by PERF1, and SE2 is the level of self-efficacy prior to subsequent performance at time (t2), denoted by PERF2. The two measures of performance allowed us to determine whether the complacency due to overconfidence was a product of initial or prior judgment of self-efficacy. We also examined whether the explicit feedback of PERF1 had an impact on subsequent self-efficacy (SE2) and performance (PERF2).

Following Social Cognitive Theory, we begin by hypothesizing that self-efficacy measured just before a task would be an accurate predictor of actual performance. Computer self-efficacy has been significantly related to the level of usage of IT [17], technology acceptance [9,16], and between self-efficacy and skill acquisition [7,24]. We expected that the higher a person’s prior self-efficacy, the better one’s subsequent performance. This relationship should hold at all times (t1 and t2). Therefore:

H1a and 1b. Prior self-efficacy positively affects subsequent performance.

However, overconfidence during preparation for a task has been posited as reducing motivation to continue working on it. Thus prior self-efficacy could negatively affect subsequent performance. Then either H1a or H1b would be negative, because enhancing selfefficacy as part of an IT-training program may lead to lower use or performance because of a mistaken belief in one’s expertise. Trainees may stop paying attention to the trainer because they believe they have mastered all the skills.

On the other hand, Perceptual Control Theory would suggest that withdrawal of effort occurs because the individual felt that the difference between the current and goal states had been eliminated. This initial judgment of self-efficacy may be formed by experience with similar types of system, or from a person’s belief in his or her ability to acquire new skills.

Early judgments of skill, however, have often been found to be negatively related to later performance. This suggests that a negative relationship may exist between initial judgment of self-efficacy and later performance. Further evidence of this fall-off in competence has been seen in post-adoption IT usage, where IT use declines, or users restrict themselves to a small subset of features out of habit and do not seek to expand their repertoire of skills [12]. Therefore, we posited:

H2. Initial judgments of self-efficacy negatively affect later performance.

It is known, however, that people revise their self-efficacy as training progresses, with good performance leading to higher selfefficacy, etc. This would suggest that any relationship between preand post-test self-efficacy would be a function of performance, not prior self-efficacy; a test given during training is meant to act as an objective confirmation (or rejection) of a person’s belief in his or her ability, with self-efficacy being adjusted due to this feedback.

On the other hand, given the propensity of people to seek confirming evidence to support their positive view of themselves, self-efficacy may be a more stable construct and less susceptible to revision because of good or bad performance. If a test result is in agreement with a person’s expectation, self-efficacy is likely to increase, because evidence has been provided of the accuracy of their judgment.

If the test result shows they are not as good as expected, the trainee will attempt to resolve the discrepancy, either by revising self-efficacy downwards or by rejecting the results of the test, believing that the questions were not a true test of ability. Thus the test-taker may blame the trainer for inadequate instruction. This would suggest that a person will maintain a level of self-efficacy, regardless of the result. Therefore:

## H3. Prior self-efficacy positively affects subsequent self-efficacy.

A similar relationship between prior and later performance may also occur; however, Bandura suggests that such a relationship is simply a reflection of the degree of commonality of their determinants, unless the task is challenging. For others, prior performance accounts for any contribution made by self-efficacy [10]. In performing any task, it is difficult to imagine that one’s intellectual and motivational abilities could not result in a significant relationship between prior and later performance. One would expect that people who perform well in a task would also perform well in a later one. Therefore:

## H4. Prior performance positively affects subsequent performance.

The greater one’s performance, the higher one’s level of satisfaction. Thus levels of satisfaction have also been found to be important in understanding how IT users’ beliefs about a system change over time. A negative relationship between personal outcome expectations and usage is explained in terms of an unrealistic expectation of the benefits of the technology, leading to dissatisfaction and lower levels of usage [6]. Satisfaction, prior use, and perceived usefulness have also been found to be important factors in understanding the ongoing intention to use an IT system [4,15].

The extent to which performance leads to an adjustment in selfefficacy, however, may depend on the extent to which an individual attributes the cause of any discrepancy to internal (ability, traits) or external (situational) factors. This effect is consistent with Attribution Theory, and has been cited as an important element in understanding the reciprocal relationship between performance and computer self-efficacy. In short, if prior performance is seen as satisfactory, one is likely to raise selfefficacy in anticipation of greater levels of subsequent performance. Therefore:

## H5. Prior performance positively affects subsequent self-efficacy.

We further extended our study by examining the effect of overconfidence on the relationships in our research model. We define overconfidence in terms of a difference between the level of expected and actual performance. Knowing what one knows is metacognition; this involves self-monitoring and internal control of the cognitive processes. Metacognitive self-assessments have been related to judgment of learning (JOL) [14], the time one allocates to study [22], and one’s ability to solve problems or acquire new skills [8].

By asking a subject to predict his or her score in an upcoming test, we can assess the extent to which he or she feels they have acquired the knowledge and skills to perform well. The difference will define the confidence mode. Overconfidence suggests that one’s actual performance is at a lower level than expected. Underconfidence has actual performance exceeding expected performance. Our last hypothesis, therefore, was:

H6. Being over- or under-confident significantly affects the relationship between prior and later self-efficacy and performance.

## 4. Method

In order to examine the proposed model and hypotheses, we replaced the within-person analysis with a between-person structural equation model, thus replacing the guessing game with a field study in which the task involved acquiring knowledge, and performance was assessed using a standardized test.

Taking a test is a real-world example of a complex, decisionmaking task, involving time management and the use of proper revision techniques. The explicit feedback provided by the test result is expected to affect one’s later self-efficacy. We assume the goal is the same across students, and that is to achieve the highest grade possible.

The sample was a set of undergraduate MIS students taking a core IS Analysis and Design course. The task consisted of taking a test on the student’s declarative and procedural knowledge of data flow diagrams (DFDs) and entity-relationship diagrams (ERDs). Both techniques were considered essential skills for our graduates.

Two tests (PERF1 and PERF2) were given approximately 4 weeks apart; each accounted for 10% of a student’s overall course grade. The tests consist of multiple-choice questions and two modeling questions to assess students’ declarative and procedural knowledge of the topics. The number of multiple-choice questions answered correctly and the points assigned by the instructor on the modeling questions were used as the performance scores.

The marks for the multiple choice questions were assessed against an answer key. The assessment of the modeling questions was made with reference to a model answer as guide. Marks were deducted for errors, such as incompleteness or symbols used incorrectly. To increase confidence in the accuracy of the scores, a sample of 65 student answers were marked by two other instructors familiar with the area. A two-way mixed effect ANOVA (consistency type) suggested an intraclass correlation of 0.77 for DFDs and 0.85 for ERDs (both having p = 0.000). The marks given by the primary instructor were thus consistent with those given by the two other instructors, and could therefore be judged fair and accurate.

Given that the test assessed declarative as well as procedural knowledge, self-efficacy items were developed for both types of knowledge. Five items in decreasing order of difficulty from ‘‘no help at all’’ to ‘‘with help from the instructor’’ for declarative and procedural aspects of DFD and ERD were developed using ‘‘yes/no’’ and a 10 point confidence scale to capture magnitude and strength.

We developed metacognitive items to differentiate overconfident from under-confident respondents. We developed four items that asked the student to predict their exam performance in terms of a numeric score (0–100%) for declarative and procedural aspects of the DFDs and ERDs. The items are given in Appendix A.

The DDFD and DERD items captured declarative self-efficacy (S) or metacognition (M), respectively. Declarative knowledge involved factual knowledge about DFDs or ERDs, such as the meaning of an arrow in a DFD, or a rectangle in an ERD. The PDFD and PERD items captured procedural self-efficacy or metacognition. Procedural knowledge involved the ability to take a short description of a problem and develop an appropriate DFD or ERD. The performance items related to the score achieved on the test.

Students were asked if they wished to participate in the experiment on the first day of class, and encouraged by earning two extra credit points if all required activities were completed. The first instrument containing the self-efficacy (SE1) and metacognitive items was given to the students on week 4 of the semester, after completing an introductory series of lectures on ERDs and DFDs. The first test (PERF1) was given in week 5. Further instruction and hands-on exercises on developing DFDs and ERDs continued. The same instrument was then given to the students on week 7, with the second test (PERF2) given on week 8. A total of 108 usable responses were collected across three classes.

## 5. Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Partial Least Squares (PLS-Graph 3.0 Build 1126). PLS is used to analyze relationships in structural models. The technique is fairly robust with small samples and does not require normal distribution of the variables. Kolmogorov-Smirnov’s test of normality indicated that none of the items were normally distributed $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . PLS also provided a between-subjects analysis.

We defined each of the measures as reflective rather than formative because the items were designed to reflect the underlying constructs. Therefore, pathways from each latent variable (SE1, SE2, PERF1, and PERF2) led outward to each of the variables. To meet the normal criteria for acceptance, factor loadings should be at least 0.6 and preferably greater than 0.7, while the standardized betas (path coefficients) should be at least 0.2 and preferably greater than 0.3.

## 6.2. Overconfident versus under-confident

We defined over- and under-confidence in terms of a metacognitive assessment of one’s likely performance on PERF1, one week before taking the test. Factor analysis of the four metacognition items indicated that all four items loaded onto one factor with loadings of 0.92 and above and explained 87.5% of the variance. Reliability (Cronbach’s a) of the four items was 0.95. Therefore, we derived the metacognition score by averaging the four items. Given that the metacognitive items loaded as one construct, we also derived an overall test score by averaging the percentage scores of each test component.

The level of over- and under-confidence was therefore calculated by subtracting the overall test score, PERF1, from the metacognition score at time (t1). Respondents with a positive difference were considered overconfident and those with a negative score under-confident. Three respondents were found to be within 1% of their actual score and were excluded from the analysis in order to sharpen the boundary between the two samples without jeopardizing the overall sample size. This resulted in over- and under-confident groups of 52 and 53 respondents, respectively.

Using one-way ANOVA, we tested the differences in the two self-efficacy and performance measures between the groups. The self-efficacy scores were derived by averaging all self-efficacy items at time (t1) or (t2) for each respondent. Fig. 3 shows the result of the PLS analyses for the two groups. The major difference between them was the relationship between SE1 to PERF2 and PERF1 to SE2. The overconfident group showed a strong, significant negative relationship from SE1 to PERF2 but weak and insignificant relationship from PERF1 to SE2. Although the path coefficient from SE2 to PERF2 was high (0.44), it was not significant. Therefore, for the overconfident group, all hypotheses were supported except H1b and H5. For the under-confident group, all paths were significant except for SE1 to PERF2, which was consistent with the full sample results. Combinations of the results provided support for H6.

## 7. Discussion

The model for the full sample (n = 108) suggested that first impressions of self-efficacy, SE1, positively and significantly impacted subsequent performance (PERF1) and subsequent selfefficacy (SE2). The relationship also held at time (t2) between SE2 and PERF2. These results provided support for the contention that self-efficacy and performance are positively related. While there is also a significant relationship between prior performance and selfefficacy, this did not usurp the role of self-efficacy in promoting higher levels of performance.

Although the relationship between SE1 and PERF2 was negative, it was insignificant. This result is similar to that found by others. For the sample as a whole, therefore, there did not seem to be any evidence to support the claim that self-efficacy just before a task can have a debilitating effect on performance.

The model for the overconfident group (n = 52) showed that initial self-efficacy, SE1, dominated all subsequent relationships. In particular, a significant negative relationship was found between SE1 and later performance (PERF2). This relationship was strong enough to remove the positive relationship between PERF1 and SE2, and between SE2 and PERF2. It would appear that overconfidence, measured in terms of overestimating one’s initial performance, promotes an unwavering belief in one’s ability. Indeed, the path coefficient between SE1 and SE2 was the strongest in the model, and suggested a failure to incorporate the results of the feedback provided by PERF1. Subsequent self-efficacy (SE2) no longer played a role, and performance was based on one’s prior performance.

The model for the under-confident group (n = 53) supported this interpretation. Although there was still a significant relationship between prior and subsequent self-efficacy, the path coefficient was significantly lower than that for the overconfident group. Instead, the feedback provided by PERF1 was incorporated into subsequent self-efficacy, and so self-efficacy at time (t2) continued to play a role in promoting levels of performance. These results suggested that a significant negative relationship between self-efficacy and performance could exist, but it was not a preparatory effect.

Although the self-efficacy of both groups increased signifi cantly over time, being under-confident reduced self-efficacy but increased performance. These results were consistent with the suggestion that increasing the challenge of the task had a positive effect on effort and attention to strategy, especially when the task was complex. When developing skills in analysis and design, self-efficacy should be held in check by emphasizing the challenges involved and in understanding and creating DFDs and ERDs.

However, a single measure of self-efficacy may be unreliable because there is no way to identify users who are overconfident and likely to show lower levels of performance over time. A measure of performance is needed to make a distinction between these users. In particular, one’s initial level of metacognition must be measured and compared with actual performance.

For those that are deemed to be under-confident, a follow-up assessment of self-efficacy is likely to provide an accurate prediction of ongoing performance. Further training would only be needed when the follow-up assessment identified a need. For the overconfident group, however, a follow-up assessment of self efficacy would provide little information. Our study suggested that performance would be significantly lower than for the underconfident group and a follow-up assessment could recommend a second round of training to instill a more realistic appreciation of user ability.

## 8. Limitations

Measures of self-efficacy should be taken just prior to performing the task. In our study, however, the measure was taken a few days before taking the test. The students’ level of selfefficacy, therefore, should include an estimate of how much they believed in their test preparation.

The alternative would have been to ask students to complete the self-efficacy instrument a few minutes before taking the test, but this would be likely to interfere with the students’ preparation and would probably have led to a low response rate or low quality data. Some temporal separation between data collection and task was necessary, and consistent with other studies.

By necessity, the test at time (t2) was more difficult than the test at time (t1). This could have led to deflated performance scores for PERF2, and the potential to reduce any relationship between PERF2 and prior constructs. This problem was due to our field study approach in which we tried to study the effects of selfefficacy on performance in an ongoing, live environment. Because of the short in time between the two tests (4 weeks), using the same test could have resulted in inflated scores that would not be representative of an improvement in ability.

Furthermore, we assumed that goal effects and outcome expectations were constant across students: we assumed that all students tried to complete the course with the highest grade possible. However, some studies suggested that self-efficacy was not a significant predictor of task performance when personal goals were included in the analysis [23].

We also assume that each student would recognize the complexity of the task involved and also the difficulty of doing well. Self-efficacy has a greater effect on performance when task complexity is high [5]. Given that our sample involved students, it could be argued that taking a test is a common task and therefore not perceived as being complex. On the other hand, even if a student believed they were good at taking tests, it did not follow that they would also be confident in achieving a high score in our test.

## 9. Conclusions

Conducting a field study, we found that one’s first impression of self-efficacy was positively related to performance at time, and weakly and negatively related to later performance. When levels of over- and under-confidence was taken into account, however, there was a strong negative relationship between self-efficacy at the start and later performance by those who were overconfident. For the under-confident group, there was a positive relationship between self-efficacy and performance.

We also found that high self-efficacy could have a debilitating effect on performance, depending on the extent to which subjects were initially over- or under-confident, and the extent to which they adjusted their self-efficacy based on explicit performance feedback. Our study also highlighted the importance of providing timely feedback on actual performance to recalibrate the predictability of the relationship between self-efficacy and performance.
