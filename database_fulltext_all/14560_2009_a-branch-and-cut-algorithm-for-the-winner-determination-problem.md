## a b s t r a c t

The Winner Determination Problem is the problem of maximizing the bene<sup>fi</sup>t when bids can be made on a group of items. In this paper, we consider the set packing formulation of the problem, study its polyhedral structure and then propose a new and tighter formulation. We also present new valid inequalities which are generated by exploiting combinatorial auctions peculiarities. Finally, we implement a branch-and-cut algorithm which shows its ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in a big number of instances.

## 1. Introduction

In the past, auctions were a tool for selling single items, such as paintings or antiques, while nowadays auctions permit a wide variety of exchanges. In recent years, the use of auctions for conducting sales has been increasing as a consequence of new demands and technological advances. In particular, the Internet has promoted the use of auctions, supplying the consumers with a number of speci<sup>fi</sup>c web sites for virtual auctions, see [1,15,23]. Associated with these new possibilities, the need to improve the performance of the decision making systems used by the organizers of auctions has become evident, and consequently the study of the mathematical aspects of these systems has begun.

Auctions where bidders express their interest in lots (bundles) of different items are called combinatorial auctions. In combinatorial auctions, the valuation given by a bidder to a set of items may differ from the sum of valuations of individuals items, i.e., combinations of items could be more or less valuable than single items. The problem of deciding which set of items (if any) is sold to each bidder in such a way that the organizer's pro<sup>fi</sup>t is maximized, is the so-called Winner Determination Problem, WDP. It is assumed here that there is only one unit of each item to be sold or, in other words, that all the items are different.

The best known examples of combinatorial auctions are sales of the rights to the use of radio spectrum by the US Federal Communications Commission (http://wireless.fcc.gov/auctions) and daily electricity supply auctions, see [10]. Also combinatorial auctions have been successfully used for assigning bus routes (http://www.t<sup>fl</sup>. gov.uk), publicity time slots [14] and adjacent pieces of real estate [16]. In [25], the reader can <sup>fi</sup>nd an interesting overview on combinatorial auctions in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of transportation services. Also when assigning public goods, combinatorial auctions can be a useful tool, as shown in [4], where a double auction mechanism for allocating resources to public goods (in presence of complementarity) is developed. Double auction problems and multi-round combinatorial auctions can be consulted in [26,3] respectively.

The mathematical study of WDP as a combinatorial optimization problem starts in [21] and, since then, the <sup>fi</sup>eld of combinatorial auctions has become a lively interdisciplinary research area. Up till now, most papers by operations researchers on combinatorial auctions focus on developing heuristics, studying the complexity of the problem and applying some integer programming techniques. Exact methods for the WDP have been proposed in [2,21], but these methods do not seem to perform better than the implementation of the natural formulation of the problem on an integer programming commercial solver. Also fast algorithms have been developed for solving WDP among which we draw attention to (i) the one referred to as CAMBO, presented in [11], which asks bidders to express preferences between bids and (ii) CABOB, presented in [24], a branching algorithm which branches on bids instead of on variables. For a survey of the state of the art and the application <sup>fi</sup>elds, we refer the reader to [12,20].

In this paper, we present several properties of the usual integer programming formulation for the WDP and then we use some of these properties to deduce new constraints that improve the linear programming relaxation. We develop a polyhedral study of the problem which some of the published algorithms could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from, and present the results of our computational experiment, which show that all of these analysis reduce the computational effort for obtaining optimal solutions.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we review the formulation of the Winner Determination Problem. This formulation will be preprocessed according to the results obtained in Section 3. In Section 4 we present several properties of the optimal solutions to the problem and in Sections 5 and 6 we study two families of facets associated to the formulation, namely cliques and lifted odd holes. In Section 7 we present computational results obtained with the branch-and-cut algorithm which includes preprocessing and addition of several families of inequalities. Section 8 sums up some concluding remarks.

## 2. Integer programming formulation

Let $J { = } \{ 1 , { \ldots } , m \}$ be a set of bidders and let $I { = } \{ 1 , { \ldots } , n \}$ be a set of distinct items (goods). Each bidder announces the (positive) amounts he is willing to pay for certain subsets of objects (bundles, bids). For simplicity we work with a single set $S { = } \{ s _ { 1 } , { \ldots } , s _ { q } \}$ , which contains all the subsets of objects demanded by at least one of the bidders. For each bid $s { \in } S$ we call $b _ { j s }$ the amount that bidder $j \in J$ has announced he is willing to pay for s (which will be zero if he did not offer anything for $s ) .$ . Then, the Winner Determination Problem (WDP) is to decide which bids are assigned and to whom so that the pro<sup>fi</sup>t obtained by the manager of the auction is maximized. In fact, the solution to WDP is usually called the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient solution because it determines the allocation of items while, in practice, a bidder does not necessarily pay what is bid (see [7] for the economic aspects of bidder's payment).

Since there is only one unit of each product available, it is obvious that bids which share a product cannot be simultaneously assigned. Less obvious (see [12]), another kind of constraint to be added to the problem is that each bidder will receive at most one bid. Otherwise, inconsistencies could appear when the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of a union of bids is less than the sum of the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts of these bids. For instance, consider an auction with two objects, a and b, and suppose that bidder 1 offers 105 for the set {a, b} and 100 for each of the sets {a} and {b}. If both sets containing a single item are assigned to bidder 1, with an expected bene<sup>fi</sup>t of 200, he could argue that his offer for the set {a, b} was 105.

In [21], the WDP was formulated as an integer programming problem, by means of binary variables $y _ { j s }$ taking value one when bid s∈S is assigned to bidder $j \in J ,$ as follows.

## 3. Preprocessing

In this section we give several ways to reduce the size of an instance of (WDP) before solving it. We consider three kinds of preprocessing. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one makes use of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the objective function in order to <sup>fi</sup>x variables to zero. The second one reduces an equivalence class of Def. 6 to a single bundle. And the third one compares an upper bound, obtained under the condition that a given bundle is assigned, with a lower bound on v(WDP) to discard this bundle.

## 3.1. Fixing variables to zero

If one bundle is a subset of another bundle, the big one will be assigned to a bidder only if he offers more for it than for the small one. ## 4. Useful inequalities

Next, we give and prove two properties of optimal solutions to (WDP), which are given in terms of useful inequalities. Useful inequalities can be added to the formulation since they cut off a part of P(G) where there are not optimal (integer) solutions to (WDP) but also a set of fractional solutions, where the optimum of the linear relaxation could be reached.

$$

\begin{array}{l l} \left(\mathrm{WDP} ^ {*}\right) \max \sum_ {j \in J} \sum_ {s \in S} b _ {j s} y _ {j s} \\ \text {   s.t.   } \sum_ {j \in J} \sum_ {s \in S: s \ni i} y _ {j s} + \sum_ {j \in J} \sum_ {s \in W (i)} y _ {j s} \leq 1 & \forall i \in I \backslash I ^ {*} \\ \sum_ {s \in S} y _ {j s} \leq 1 & \forall j \in J \\ y _ {j s} \in \{0, 1 \} & \forall j \in J, s \in S. \end{array}
$$

## 6. Lifted odd hole inequalities

Another family of facet-de<sup>fi</sup>ning graphs that can be used to strengthen the formulation are odd holes. We present two properties of the odd holes in GWDP and take advantage of these properties to design a speci<sup>fi</sup>c separation procedure for lifted odd holes.

## 6.1. Properties and lifting of odd holes in GWDP

Proposition 6. For any odd hole in GWDP:

(1) It has 0, 1 or 2 nodes per column and 0, 1 or 2 nodes per row of the diagram.

(2) If there are two nodes in the same column, $i . e . , y _ { j _ { 1 } s }$ and $y _ { j _ { 2 } s } j _ { 1 } ,$ $j _ { 2 } { \in } J , j _ { 1 } { \neq } j _ { 2 } , s { \in } S ,$ then it contains 2 nodes at rows j and $j _ { 2 } , y _ { j _ { 1 } s _ { 1 } }$ and $y _ { j _ { 2 } s _ { 2 } } ,$ such that $s _ { 1 } \cap s _ { 2 } = s _ { 1 } \cap s = s _ { 2 } \cap s = \emptyset .$

Proof.

(1) If a row or column had three nodes, the graph they induce is complete which contradicts that it is part of a hole.

(2) If the node adjacent to $y _ { j _ { 1 } s }$ in the odd hole is not in the same row, the graph induced by it and $y _ { j _ { 1 } s } , y _ { j _ { 2 } s }$ is complete which cannot happen. $\mathrm { I f } s _ { 1 } \cap s _ { 2 } \neq \emptyset \mathrm { o r } s _ { 1 } \cap s \neq \emptyset \mathrm { o r } \dot { s } \cap s _ { 2 } \neq \emptyset ,$ , the graph induced by $y _ { j _ { 1 } s _ { 1 } } ,$ $y _ { j _ { 2 } s _ { 2 } } , y _ { j _ { 1 } s }$ and $y _ { j _ { 2 } s }$ <sup>; ;</sup>cannot be a subgraph of a hole. □

A direct consequence of Prop. 6 is that holes of length <sup>fi</sup>ve can adopt the four different shapes illustrated in Fig. 5.

Odd holes can be separated in polynomial time with the method given in [8], see also [9] and [17]. Nevertheless, the exact separation of odd holes provides us with valid inequalities which are not facets, since facets are obtained applying iteratively the lifting procedure given in Prop. 1 to the odd hole inequalities. Then, separating odd holes exactly could result on inequalities that, after be lifted, are not so deep as other lifted odd holes, or even the process could <sup>fi</sup>nish without <sup>fi</sup>nding any violated odd hole, when there exist violated lifted odd holes. The result can be an inef<sup>fi</sup>cient approach, as noted in [13,17] and other papers in the literature where heuristic separation of (lifted) odd holes has been used to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently solve set packing problems.

The following results shows how a family of odd holes can be immediately lifted to produce tighter inequalities.

Proposition 7. Let

$$
\left\{y _ {j _ {k} s _ {k}}, k = 1, \dots , p \right\}\tag{8}
$$

be a set of nodes inducing an odd hole in GWDP. $I f j _ { k }$ are different for all $k { = } 1 , . . . , p$ , then

$$

$$

is a valid inequality for (WDP).

Proof. Since rows and columns of GWDP are sorted arbitrarily we can assume, without loss of generality, that the set (8) is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as

$$
\left\{y _ {j s _ {j}}, j = 1, \dots , p \right\}.\tag{10}
$$

By hypothesis, this set de<sup>fi</sup>nes an odd hole, thus giving a facet

$$
\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} y _ {j s _ {j}} \leq \frac {p - 1}{2}\tag{11}
$$

associated with the polyhedron of the induced subgraph.

Consider any node in the <sup>fi</sup>rst column of GWDP other than $y _ { 1 s _ { 1 } } ,$ say $y _ { 1 s _ { r } } .$ It is linked to some nodes in the set $( 1 0 ) \colon y _ { 1 s _ { 1 } } , y _ { 2 s _ { 2 } } , y _ { p s _ { p } }$ and, ${ \mathrm { i f } } r { \le } p ,$ to $y _ { r s _ { r } } .$ Therefore, the optimization problem of Prop. 1 is solved on a subgraph given either by one or two paths with $p { - } 3$ or $p { - } 4$ nodes in total. In any case, the optimal value will be $\frac { p - 1 } { 2 } - 1$ and the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the lifted variable $y _ { 1 s , }$ will be 1.

If we consider now a node in the <sup>fi</sup>rst column of GWDP other than $y _ { 1 s _ { 1 } }$ and $y _ { 1 s , }$ or a node in the second column other than $y _ { 2 s _ { 2 } }$ since it is linked to $y _ { 1 s , }$ , the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the lifted variable will be again 1. Any other node in the <sup>fi</sup>rst two columns will be lifted with coef<sup>fi</sup>cient 1.

Now, consider a node in the third column, $y _ { 3 s _ { r } }$ , other than $y _ { 3 s _ { 3 } }$ . It is linked to $y _ { 3 s _ { 3 } } , y _ { 4 s _ { 4 } } ,$ all the nodes in the second column and, if t≤p, to $y _ { t s _ { t } } .$ . Going on with this process, all the nodes in the <sup>fi</sup>rst p columns will be lifted with coef<sup>fi</sup>cient 1, leading to inequality (8), which is a facet of the subgraph induced by the p <sup>fi</sup>rst columns of GWDP. □

From the valid inequality (8), facets of the polyhedron associated with formulation (WDP) could be obtained by lifting the remaining nodes of GWDP. Nevertheless, carrying out this procedure implies solving an NP-hard problem (in general) for every lifted variable.

## 7. Computational experiment

## 7.1. The data

A computational experiment was carried out in order to check the performance of our developments in terms of time and duality gap reductions with respect to the original formulation by using a commercial IP solver. We evaluated several families of instances, some of which follow from a generator for combinatorial auctions and other are newly proposed here. Throughout all the testing, we used CPLEX version 9.1 and we coded in C, on a Sun Java Workstation W2100z with 2 Opteron dual processors 2.6 GHz and 4 GB of RAM. We always used CPLEX's default settings. Wide-ranging computational studies reported in the literature for solving the combinatorial auction problem are those in [2] and in [24]. They used the solver CPLEX 8 and CPLEX 6.5 respectively.

The aforementioned generator is called CATS 2.1 (Combinatorial Auction Test Suite), was introduced in [16], and may by found at http:// www.cs.ubc.ca/\~kevinlb/CATS. CATS instances generator needs three parameters to run (q, n and the auction distribution) and gives the set of bids in which a set of bidders is interested and their related bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Different auction distributions that CATS 2.1 generator offers are the following.

FR (fixed random). The number of items for each bid is uniformly distributed on $\{ 1 , . . . , n \}$ and $b _ { j s } { \sim } U ( 0 , 1 0 0 0 )$ for all $j \in J , s \in S$

LR (linear random). The number of items is uniformly distributed on $\{ 1 , . . . , n \}$ and $b _ { j s } { \sim } U \left( 0 , 1 0 0 0 | s | \right)$ for all $j \in J , s \in S$

D (decay). The number of items is generated starting with 1 and repeated incrementing the size of the bid until a random number on (0,1) exceeds α=0.55. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are $b _ { j s } { \sim } U \left( 0 , 1 0 0 0 | s | \right)$ for all $j \in J , s \in S .$

B (binomial). Requests $g$ items with probability $0 . 2 ^ { g } 0 . 8 ^ { m - g } { \binom { m } { g } }$ and do $b _ { j s } { \sim } U \left( 0 \right)$ , 1000|s|) for all $j \in J , s \in S .$

P (paths). The goods at auction are the edges of a graph, and bids request a set of edges that form a path between two nodes.

R (regions). Nodes of a graph represent the goods at auction and bids request a set of adjacent goods.

A (arbitrary). Nodes of a graph represent the goods at auction and bids request a set of goods sharing an arbitrary relationship

FR, LR, D and B are known as CATS legacy distributions and tend to generate bids easy to describe mathematically while P, R and A are known as CATS realistic distributions and were developed to represent more economically realistic scenarios. In fact, there are some other CATS distributions, either legacy or realistic. The ones we have chosen are those for which the average LP gap of some size is greater than 1%.

In case of legacy distributions the output <sup>fi</sup>le gives the set of bids a single bidder does. In order to have instances with multiple bidders, we have generated bene<sup>fi</sup>ts according with the corresponding rule for all the bidders. In all legacy cases, the random technique is used to determine which items should de<sup>fi</sup>ne a bid.

Instances from realistic distributions provide a set of bids assigned to a set of bidders verifying that the price of a bid s∈S is constant for any interested bidder, i.e., $b _ { j s } = B _ { s } \forall j \in J .$ Since in the WDP different bidders could offer different prices for the same bid, we have adapted distributions P, R and A by doing $b _ { i s } { = } \tau { \times } B _ { s }$ where τ∼U(1, 1.5).

In general, our data instances are larger than those usually considered in the literature. Comparing with [2,24], we deal with 9000 variables for distribution FR while they deal with 2000, with 30000 for LR and D (1750 and 800, respectively). Moreover, usually auctions with $q / n = 1 0$ are chosen in the literature as illustrative instances, whereas we solve instances with $q / n \in \{ 0 . 1 , 0 . 5 , 0 . 6 , 1 , 2 , 1 0 \}$ Our computational study showed that, frequently, instances with small values of this ratio have larger gaps. In particular, the ratio q/n=10 uses to lead to problems with small gaps, i.e., easy problems.

## 8. Conclusion

We have studied the Winner Determination Problem from a polyhedral point of view, deriving several families of facets and other inequalities that can be added to the formulations, as well as several preprocessing techniques. These results have been embedded into a branch-and-cut algorithm for optimally solving the problem, and extensive computational results show that the running time and duality gap of dif<sup>fi</sup>cult instances can be considerably reduced using this approach.
