# A comparison of the usability of performing ad hoc querying on dimensionally modeled data versus operationally modeled data

Dušan Vujošević <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Ivana Kovačević <sup>a</sup>, Milija Suknović <sup>a</sup>, Nenad Lalić <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Belgrade, Faculty of Organizational Sciences, Ul. Jove Ilića 154, 11000 Belgrade, Serbia

<sup>b</sup> University of East Sarajevo, Faculty of Education, Ul. Svetog Save 24, 76300 Bijeljina, Bosnia and Herzegovina

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 21 April 2010 Received in revised form 26 December 2011 Accepted 9 May 2012 Available online 23 May 2012

Keywords: Dimensionally modeled data Data models Business intelligence Usability Learnability

## a b s t r a c t

To study the usability of dimensionally modeled data (DMD), we conducted an experiment involving 113 participants to analyze their performance in ad hoc querying tasks. The results show that DMD's usability is high according to both objective and subjective measurements. Usability is dependent upon the segment of data model used and, in particular, on the type of task being performed, with the highest results for aggregations and holistic view creation. Interestingly, dimensionally modeled data seem to be distinguished by learnability. The study's results partially agree with the results obtained in usability studies of relational data models and similar data structures.

## 1. Introduction

Dimensional modeling is a data modeling technique used to design business applications that enable intensive analytical querying through the higher performance of the queries and the high usability of the data being analyzed. The analytical querying that bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from dimensional modeling is business data querying, which frequently uses actions such as summing, calculating averages or displaying trends. Claims of the high performability of analytical queries performed on dimensionally modeled data (DMD) are under constant scrutiny and generally receive positive evaluations. In contrast, claims of the high usability of DMD remain, with rare exceptions, unchecked, as though practitioners and theoreticians assume they are self-evident.

Data model usability is the extent to which a data model can be used with effectiveness, ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and satisfaction, facilitating the exchange of critical information [7] and data structure comprehension in tasks with entity-based representations [15]. To separate the data model's usability from the software's usability, some tests can be conducted twice: once using pencil and paper and again using the software prototype [53]. The study that we present here is exploratory because it aims to determine the research design and methods for quantifying DMD usability and gain insight into this usability. The novelty of our study is in the fact that we analyze DMD usability from the point of view of business users, i.e., nontechnical users, using a software prototype. The other important novelty of our study is that we separately examine various aspects of usability, such as accuracy, completeness, time, holistic view, subjective perception, memorization and typical errors. We also examine the impact that the type of task and the main characteristics of dimensional modeling have on some of these usability aspects. In addition, we draw parallels with similar usability studies and incorporate some of the insights we obtain into the design of our study.

Our study focuses on the usability of the DMD from the point of view of business users. The tools and techniques that these users employ range from very structured tools, such as canned and parameterized reports, to completely unstructured data mining techniques, such as neural networks. Ad hoc querying and OLAP techniques, such as slicing and dicing, occupy the middle ground.

The ad hoc querying technique incorporates basic principles of direct object manipulation [25], a paradigm that facilitates the understanding and use of computer interfaces. Ad hoc querying users have to navigate through tables and columns, but they do not need to be aware of the syntax details of the underlying query languages. This makes ad hoc querying convenient for use in our usability study. Namely, the controlled test has shown that even for query languages claiming to be simple, a reasonably timed tutelage proved insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient for the user to acquire a familiarity with the language [4,25]. Ad hoc querying tools belong to a group of dynamic query interfaces, which make querying faster, easier, more pleasant and less error-prone than other querying interfaces [1,54,59].

Structured business intelligence tools, such as reports, dashboards and scorecards, present reconstituted data at the interface level. As a consequence, business users only need to understand the structure of the data models that form the foundation of the reports and other tools they use and are not required to have insight into a wider set of row data, which may or may not be dimensionally modeled.

However, unstructured techniques offer users insight into the way data is modeled in the underlying data repository. The OLAP sliceand-dice technique could be appropriate for measuring some aspects of DMD's usability, especially when it comes to the understandability of the main concepts of dimensional modeling, but, due to its inherently dimensional paradigm, the OLAP slice-and dice-technique does not leave enough room for interpreting the quantitative measurements of the usability of DMD compared with non-DMD.

Other unstructured techniques, such as data mining and neural networking, also expose underlying data models to their users, but they are too complex for the business community to use without extensive tutelage. Only ad hoc querying makes the usability comparison possible because it exposes DMD directly and openly to non-IT professionals, while it can also expose non-DMD to them. Therefore, ad hoc querying appears to be the most suitable tool for researching the usability of DMD and differences in user performance with data models.

A comparison of the usability of DMD with the usability of non-DMD provides us with a context for a broader interpretation of DMD usability; it could also prove useful in situations in which DMD are being introduced, particularly if other data are already being used. When DMD become available to business users, the same data usually exist in one or more data sources. Most of these data can be referred to as operationally modeled data (OMD). DMD can be interpreted as a restructured copy of OMD, usually gained from OMD using techniques known as information integration, extraction, transformation and loading.

Therefore, one can anticipate questions such as “Is there a usability bene<sup>fi</sup>t from investing in DMD?” and “Should we expose our business users to both types of data and teach them when to use each type?” We can also imagine business users succumbing to an analysis of OMD in the absence of other means of <sup>fi</sup>nding the answers they need. Thus, studying OMD usability is not only useful hypothetically; it can also support real, albeit infrequent, business data usage.

Dimensional modeling is regularly presented in contrast to operational modeling [11,34,35], also referred to as transactional modeling. Following the supplementary role and existential dependence of dimensional modeling upon operational modeling, one has the impression that the former technique acts as a kind of “younger sibling” to the latter. However, dimensional modeling may actually have predated normalized modeling in the late 1960s [35].

The two terms used in this paper, DMD and OMD, were selected from several alternatives. One option was to use the term Entity-Relationship Modeled Data instead of OMD. The division of these two techniques would not be correct in such a case because the dimensional modeling technique is based on the entity-relationship technique as well, meaning that it is a subset of a set of modeling techniques that use entity-relationship diagrams in presenting data models.

The other alternative was to use the terms Denormalized Data and Normalized Data. However, this systematization would emphasize only one of the differences between the two ways of organizing data and omit other differences. These differences, which we wanted to examine, would also remain neglected had we used the term Star Schema Modeled Data, which describes a data model diagram that may be relevant to IT professionals but is not relevant to, or even in the purview of, the business users whose performance this study aims to measure. Similarly, we did not wish to focus the study on the Dimensional Model and its counterpart, the Operational Model, because we planned to focus on the usability of the data modeled to conform to one or the other model.

Finally, we considered using the terms Data Warehouse and Operational Database or Transactional Database. This systematization would be too broad, keeping in mind that this study aims to describe how a data model impacts the access to data that is presented directly to users. Additionally, these terms would not be entirely correct because some data warehouses are not dimensionally modeled and furthermore, operational databases could be viewed through the lens of a dimensional model in some cases.

## 2. Related studies

Analyses of the usability of dimensional models have the greatest relevance to our work. These earlier studies are predominantly concerned with usability from the point of view of application developers, likely because most business users never observed data models directly, while developers, on the other hand, work with them constantly and have discovered over time that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of data models can be far reaching, impacting the overall design of a software system [25].

Other usability studies with <sup>fi</sup>ndings relevant to our study focus on the usability of data structures with characteristics similar to the analogous characteristics of relational data models. Such data structures include hypertext and menus whose usability has been researched heavily (e.g., in [20,22,27,28,39,42,61]). The related work also considers the types of business user tasks, task solving approaches and their corresponding usability implications (e.g., in [6,12,16,29,41,48]). As we will describe later, <sup>fi</sup>ndings from previous studies help us by pointing out factors that produce usability differences, but they could also be misleading by indicating data types with a higher level of certain aspects of usability.

## 2.1. Dimensional model usability

The worldwide acceptance of dimensional modeling suggests that this technique ful<sup>fi</sup>lls its basic presumptions, including that of the high usability of DMD. Practitioners and the academic community generally prize DMD for its usability. However, controversies do occasionally arise. For example, some experts argue that there is no significant bene<sup>fi</sup>t of dimensional modeling in terms of higher data usability or other similar bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [26]. When pure ad hoc querying is needed, it is recommended that data be organized in a normalized model, although it has been suggested that DMD are appropriate for aggregates and summations because they are inherently dimensional in structure. Skeptics minimize the role of dimensional modeling in the development of analytical applications but see a place for it in the end-user interface [30,31]. Finally, there is a dominant pro-DMD usability view, which is generally not skeptical of dimensional modeling and, therefore, does not doubt the allegedly high usability of DMD, to the extent that its proponents may not see the necessity of looking more closely at the claim [32,34,35]. According to the proponents of this approach, dimensional models are characterized by their simplicity, intuitiveness, understandability and navigability, whereas data in normalized tables, although pure and with minimal redundancy, present a challenge for analysts to navigate [2].

Previous studies of dimensional model usability compared diagrammatic representations of dimensional models and relational models. Their usability was examined in terms of understandability and their ability to be remembered. Experiments measured the accuracy and patterns of users' recall of the data model diagrams [11,17] or the success of diagram modi<sup>fi</sup>cation tasks [49]. Dimensional model usability has also been studied in the context of the effectiveness of the dimensional design patterns used in the design of dimensional models [32].

One study [11] compared star schema and entity-relationship diagrams to determine how they impact recall accuracy. One of four diagrams was randomly assigned to the 250 participants: a simple or complex star schema diagram or a simple or complex entityrelationship diagram. Participants were given 45 or 90 seconds to look at the diagram and 3 or 4 minutes, respectively, to reproduce it on a separate sheet of paper without looking at the diagram. Researchers counted the number of correct entities and relationships recalled by the participants. The results of this study strongly suggest that star schema diagrams are easier to comprehend than entityrelationship diagrams. The entity recall order supported the idea that users intuitively distinguished between fact tables and dimension tables, whereas no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant pattern was found in the recall order of entity-relationship diagrams.

In a similar study [49], 95 subjects were asked to modify either a star schema diagram or an entity-relationship diagram according to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>ctitious scenario. The results showed that inexperienced subjects performed better with the star schema diagrams in terms of both time and score, which implied that the star schema is more easily understood by inexperienced users, who comprise the likely target audience of a data warehouse. However, the low signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the results indicates that more data need to be collected.

An experiment with 21 participants [32] found that designing dimensional models is faster and more accurate when supported by dimensional design patterns, a technique that includes the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the use of commonly known and recognized mental models in the dimensional model design process to facilitate the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of commonly used dimensions. Roughly stated, it could be assumed that dimensional design patterns are an extrapolation along an axis connecting entity-relationship models without a speci<sup>fi</sup>c pattern, star schema models with a simple pattern and dimensional design patterns with a rich pattern. According to this view, one can expect to encounter the same usability trends with the <sup>fi</sup>rst two models.

## 2.2. Hypertext topology usability

Hypertext is an approach to information management in which data are stored in a network of nodes connected by (hyper) links [50]. Hypertext is broadly associated with web sur<sup>fi</sup>ng, but it can be found elsewhere. Depending upon the strictness of the de<sup>fi</sup>nition, hypertext can include many data structures and the term itself is sometimes used metaphorically to indicate some form of linkage between database items [48]. In many of its aspects, hypertext schema layouts evoke relational diagrams. A node of hypertext is analogous to a relation table found in relational databases. A node contains information and hyperlinks that allow interconnection with information from other nodes, whereas a relational table has data columns and key columns that allow users to place that table's data and data from other relational tables in the same context. Hence, a similar nature of usability could be expected between these two data structures; because the usability of hypertext has been widely investigated due to the omnipresence of this data structure, we can pro<sup>fi</sup>t from the investigation's results to predict and explain the usability of the relational data models that we study.

The common presence of hypertext does not mean that this data structure is absolutely user friendly. The main usability problems of hypertext include disorientation, digression and cognitive overhead. The structure of hypertext is also rather unpredictable. Hypertexts do not have a customary structure, such as books or lists and they can be much more disorienting and overwhelming than linear forms of information presentation [3,10,21,39]. Studies have consistently found that one of the most common complaints associated with hypertexts is that the user becomes disoriented within them and tends to digress from the optimal problem-solving path [1,5,38,39]. The excessive burden on users' hypertext reading and navigating processes may also be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as cognitive overhead [48]. Heavy mental loads can result from having to make decisions concerning what links to follow or abandon given the large variety of choices available, or from trying to determine whether information is important or not, or from trying to maintain one's place in the forest of information trees [22]. Users exhibit a cognitive tendency toward viewing link navigation as illogical and thus toward distrusting links [24].

Most hypertext usability studies try to <sup>fi</sup>nd differences in hypertext usability within the context of different hypertext topologies and/or different user activities. The two main hypertext topologies are a strict hierarchical topology, in which nodes are grouped in a hierarchical arrangement, allowing movement up or down by only one level at a time and network topology, in which any node can be connected to a number of other nodes, regardless of their semantic distance from each other. Other topologies exist [3,42], such as the frequently investigated mixed hierarchical topology, which is a combination of the other two. The DMD structure is similar to the shallow hierarchy of the two levels, while the OMD structure is similar to the network. If we focus on table hierarchies, OMD structure is similar to the mixed hierarchy with varying depth.

Studies have shown [38,39,42] that participants using network topology performed worse than participants using hierarchical topology. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly slower completion times and greater user disorientation were recorded when using the network topology, apparently because the network topology tended to overwhelm and disorient the users more than the strict or mixed hierarchical topologies.

In studies of the usability of hierarchical topologies, it has been found that a moderate level of depth and breadth is superior to both a fairly deep hierarchy and a broad hierarchy [40], while depth has more usability signi<sup>fi</sup>cance than breadth because users favor more shallow structures over deeper structures [33,52,56,60].

The optimal shape of a link hierarchy is not the same in all situations and it depends on a previous knowledge of hypertext and accumulated user habits [28]. As users gain experience, the breadth and depth trade-off for processing time becomes insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant [44]. When it comes to more complex tasks, such as exploratory tasks, studies that considered the effects of different tasks on performance showed that exploratory tasks were best supported by a network, or mixed hierarchical structure, whereas searching tasks were best supported by a strict hierarchical structure [46,51].

## 2.3. Menu usability

User menus are structures that are similar to hypertexts and relational data tables. As with hypertext, user menu structures can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed within basic topologies. It is often assumed that user menus have a strict hierarchical topology, but they can also have network or mixed hierarchical topologies. The similarity of the topologies between hypertext and user menus results in a similarity in usability implications. However, although they are sometimes subtle, differences in these data structures do exist. Menu items usually appear on a screen in an organized manner, often at the same time as other items of the same level, similar to data from relational table columns displayed in a spreadsheet. Hypertext links, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, seldom appear with links from other levels and are often isolated from those at the same level. Furthermore, hypertext is generally associated with web content, whereas user menus are associated with software applications. Similar to hypertext links, but different from items in relational columns, menu items generally have a conveying purpose, i.e., they serve as a tool for users rather than as a goal, such as values in a relational data table. Keeping these similarities and differences in mind, we will look at usability research that focused speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on menus.

User performance decreases as the depth of a menu structure increases, both in terms of access time and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [36,42,61]. Thus, it is widely accepted that one large menu is more time-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than several small submenus supporting the same choices. This remains true even if we overlook the time overhead of moving between submenus—the greater effectiveness of one large menu over several small menus originates primarily in the cognitive processes that follow a user's selection of menu items. It has been suggested [52] that deeper menus lead to decreased accuracy due to three types of forgetfulness: (1) users simply forget the target word because they have to concentrate on possible paths they need to take to locate the actual target, (2) they may forget the pathway to the target and thus lose direction within the structure, or (3) they may forget the association between a descriptor item at the parent level and the target at a deeper level. An analogy can be made between large menus and denormalized tables.

Similar to <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding mixed hierarchical hypertext usability, mixed hierarchy menu networks — analogous to OMD — offer users full control over navigation <sup>fl</sup>ow but have the disadvantage of complexity, while their navigation may be daunting for an inexperienced user [22]. Several menu studies [9,19,20] demonstrate that the factors impacting usability can change over time. Changes in menu design may improve the usability of a menu for <sup>fi</sup>rst-time users [19,20] while decreasing it for long-term users.

There is a distinction between menu types that closely resemble those of DMD and OMD, i.e., a distinction between simultaneous and sequential menus (Fig. 1). Sequential menus (a), analogous to OMD (b) and also referred to as hierarchical menus, present the user with choices that must be made in some predetermined order; the impact of a given choice is constrained by the accumulated total of all previous choices. In contrast, simultaneous menus (c), analogous to DMD (d), are represented by the presence of multiple active menus on a screen at the same time. The usability of sequential menus is characterized by a certain rigidity, which produces disorientation and dif<sup>fi</sup>culties with tasks that are not simple, particularly when explorations and comparisons of the results from multiple selections are required [27]. An analogous rigidity can be observed for OMD, although the rigidity of OMD can be mitigated using joins performed by an ad hoc querying tool. The usability of simultaneous menus is characterized by a certain complexity because more than one menu from multiple hierarchy levels can be displayed on the screen at the same time. However, simultaneous menus are more <sup>fl</sup>exible because they provide users with a broader context for choosing menu items and gaining direct access to them. If sequential menu hierarchies can be converted into simultaneous menu presentations, this strategy is a good option if complex or exploratory tasks are anticipated [8,27]. These results are af<sup>fi</sup>rmed by a similar study, which focused on multipane interfaces [8].

As shown in a study of expendable indexes [61], the usability advantages of large amounts of data presented at the same time in an interface begin to decrease after a certain point, possibly due to physical screen limitations and the fact that a data surplus causes disorientation. Similarly, denormalized tables could appear to be overwhelmed with data.

## 2.4. Typologies of business user tasks

As we have observed, studies concerning the usage of different data structures reveal a number of common tasks performed by business users. One can <sup>fi</sup>nd the same or similar task classi<sup>fi</sup>cations when looking at the use of ad hoc querying and other business intelligence tools. Data structure usability depends on the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty or type of task, which could also be true for DMD and OMD.

The term “dif<sup>fi</sup>culty” describes the degree of cognitive load required to complete a given task [13,23,45], which makes the concept of dif<sup>fi</sup>culty a perceptual counterpart to cognitive complexity [47]. Software that intends to support complex tasks should reduce the ambiguity and uncertainty by providing further de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of relevant problems and additional information [12], which reminds us of the additional columns and tables added to DMD.

The classi<sup>fi</sup>cations of business task types follow differences in the activities needed to complete tasks and differences in the form of the tasks' results. For example, one can distinguish between tasks with search targets established in advance and tasks with search targets speci<sup>fi</sup>ed interactively as part of the search activity itself [48], or one can distinguish between searching tasks, exploratory tasks and browsing activities [51]. Another classi<sup>fi</sup>cation considers clearly or ambiguously speci<sup>fi</sup>ed tasks [55]. It may be surprising to learn that counterintuitively, but consistent with theory, users who perceive a task to be highly ambiguous may approach the problem more systematically and actually perform better [58]. Perceived ambiguity favors a systematic approach to problem solving and thus shows improved performance [18]. In our case, by making DMD unambiguous, we may actually dissuade users from taking a systematic approach, lowering their performance.

A study comparing differently structured hypertext topologies found that when searching for speci<sup>fi</sup>c items, the search time was similar across all structures, but for implicit targets, the different hypertext structure shapes had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on participants' search time and performance [6]. Accordingly, one can expect to see a greater performance difference between DMD and ODM for implicit tasks, i.e., for exploratory and browsing tasks, which are inherently analytical.

It can be noted that the mental process of task solving is a complex cognitive activity that does not exactly follow the same steps as automatic data processing through computer algorithms [16,29,41]. This means that the high performability of DMD with some operations performed directly by prede<sup>fi</sup>ned SQL may not be valid for operations that business users perform using an ad hoc query interface.

## 3. Research model

The determining factor in our exploratory study is the data modeling type. The outcomes of our research are the most interesting usability aspects of ad hoc querying that could depend on data modeling types, which we formulated as hypotheses stated in alternative forms. Following the dominant <sup>fi</sup>ndings from previous usability studies of dimensional models [11,17,32,49], we expect the effect of the data types to favor the greater usability of DMD. Why should we expect to see a similar usability performance pattern in ad hoc querying as in previous studies of model diagrams? Working with a model diagram may seem easier than working with raw data; therefore, the two kinds of activities may not appear to be comparable. However, it has been demonstrated [43] that graphic displays revealing relationships between objects are neither more accessible nor more comprehensible or memorable than textual presentations.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst usability aspects that we include in our research model are Task Result Accuracy and Completion Time. While these two usability aspects are prevalent in various usability studies, the new and unique contribution of our model is that we measure these aspects in the context of business users performing ad hoc querying, as opposed to activities performed by database designers and modelers. We formulate our hypotheses as follows:

Fig. 1. Analogy between the menu types and tables from the DMD and OMD.

H1. Business users performing ad hoc querying with DMD obtain higher task result accuracy than business users performing ad hoc querying with OMD.

H2. Business users performing ad hoc querying with DMD complete tasks faster than business users performing ad hoc querying with OMD.

Another novelty of our model is the introduction of dimensional modeling innovations into the focus of our study. Namely, not all segments of data are transformed to the same extent during the extraction, transformation and loading processes. Apart from tablenaming conventions and a general decrease in the number of tables, most dimensional modeling innovations affect only subsets of DMD. This allows us to study the separate usability impacts of particular innovations through the following hypotheses:

The presence of dimensional model innovations in the data subset used by business users to perform a particular ad hoc querying task improves the task result accuracy (H3a) and completion time (H3b) of business users performing ad hoc querying with DMD compared to business users performing ad hoc querying with OMD.

To emphasize the comprehensive approach of our model, we also study how the type of ad hoc querying task being performed impacts data usability. It is usually assumed that DMD are introduced to support analytical tasks. We explore the following hypothesis:

The higher task result accuracy (H4a) and the shorter completion time (H4b) of business users performing ad hoc querying with DMD compared to business users performing ad hoc querying with OMD are likely to appear in analytical tasks.

The next speci<sup>fi</sup>c unique contribution of our research model is its forward progression from standard usability aspects of accuracy and time to a Holistic View, a more complex aspect of usability that specifically applies to the analytical use of data. Our introduction of the Holistic View follows the opinion that the measurement of performance in terms of speed and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in answering questions is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient to determine effectiveness in more complex learning and problemsolving situations [14]. The ad hoc querying technique empowers business users to obtain a full picture of their business through multiple successive queries, which may be more or less interrelated. We expect DMD to be more motivational and supportive of the creation of a complete insight, as stated in the following hypothesis:

H5. Business users performing ad hoc querying with DMD develop a better holistic view of the phenomena described by the data than business users performing ad hoc querying with OMD.

Many usability studies have reported that in certain cases, data users feel disorientation or discover that navigation through some data structures is daunting [22,38,39,42,61]. In our model, this perception of disorientation is expected to vary between DMD and OMD, together with the other business users’ impressions that we introduce, such as the ease of tasks or work enjoyment, which belong to the area of Subjective Perception:

H6. Business users performing ad hoc querying with DMD develop a more positive subjective perception of the queried data than business users performing ad hoc querying with OMD (Fig. 2).

## 4. Methodology and experiment

The experiment was organized as a voluntary activity for undergraduate university students with no prior knowledge of data modeling or business intelligence software. Students received points for their participation that they could also gain through other activities. A total of 113 students of information technologies and business administration agreed to take part in the experiment. Many of the participants are expected to become business users following their studies.

The participants were required to solve 36 different tasks using an application especially developed for this experiment, inspired by business intelligence applications commonly found on the market and described in the literature [34,35]. The tasks were ordered by degree of dif<sup>fi</sup>culty, from those we expected to be easy to more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult tasks. The application interface was split vertically into two panes. The left pane displayed a list of tables with data (a) (Fig. 3). By clicking on a table name with the mouse, the user could see the names of that table's columns (b). By clicking on the name of a column, the participant could see all of the data from the column in the right interface panel through a browse query or SELECT DISTINCT query, which suppressed duplicate rows in the answer. By clicking on more than one column, the participant was able to view existing combinations of values from the columns not only for columns belonging to the same table but also from those belonging to different tables (c). In these cases, the application took care of table joins.

Namely, the application generated a select statement in SQL based on the selected columns. A select operation was then executed on the tables. If the additive values were among the selected values, their sums were also returned to help the users work faster. The participants were able to <sup>fi</sup>nd the answers to all of the tasks by looking at the selection set, i.e., the records returned by a selection operation they performed when clicking on the column names (also called a hit set [25]).

The participants were taught how to use the application. They were told that the experiment compared different types of software rather than their personal achievements and that the study was intended for academic and not commercial purposes. The participants were also instructed how to document the time following each task and the researchers used this information to calculate how much time each participant spent on a particular task. The participants were allowed to leave tasks that they found too complicated unsolved. For 31 of the 36 tasks, the participants’ results were compared against the correct results and each person was given zero points for an incorrect or nonexistent answer or two points for a correct result. One point was given for incomplete correct results; therefore, the accuracy measurement included completeness as a measure of information quality [57].

The application contained data representing a <sup>fi</sup>ctional sample of 80 point-of-sale records and their contextual descriptive tables. All data were created to resemble real point-of-sale records, but no actual or recognizable product, store, company or person's name was used to prevent possibly biased associations that could impact the way that the participants solved tasks. The participants were randomly assigned either DMD or OMD so that 56 participants were assigned DMD, while the other 57 were assigned OMD. Both sets of data conveyed the same information. Every participant was assigned the same tasks so that their results could be the same and their results were evaluated against a single set of correct answers. An independent sample t-test with an α level of .05 was used to reveal a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between the group mean values of accuracy, time and the other variables discussed.

The DMD was split between eight relational tables, one of which was a fact table, while seven were dimension tables (see Fig. 4 for the dimensional model diagram; note that the participants were not presented with this or any other diagram). Out of the seven dimension tables, two did not have corresponding tables in OMD (Date Dimension and Time of Day Dimension), two dimensions corresponded each to one OMD table (Promotion Dimension vs. Promotional Offers and Supplier Dimension vs. Supplier), one dimension corresponded to two OMD tables (Payment Dimension vs. Method of Payment and Payment Program) and two dimensions each corresponded to <sup>fi</sup>ve OMD tables (Product Dimension vs. Product, Price List, Shelving, Agreement Product, Supply Agreement; and Store Dimension vs. Retail Store, Store Responsible, Town Responsible, District Responsible, Region Responsible). Overall, OMD was dispersed into sixteen relational tables (Fig. 5).

In OMD, the responsible employees were listed in four hierarchically interrelated tables and the table from the lowest hierarchical level had a relationship with the table Store. In DMD, all of the responsible employees were listed in a single table titled Store Dimension. An alternative to this hierarchy listing could have been to present the data in a hierarchy of geographical locations, but then the participants’ previous knowledge of geography could have produced a performance bias.

Apart from denormalization, another important innovation of dimensional modeling is the date dimension. In our experiment, Date Dimension consisted of nine columns: Date (using the format 28/12/11), Full Date, Day of the Week, Month Name, Year, Weekday Indicator, Selling Season, Week Number in Calendar Year and Promotion Event. The Date Dimension corresponded to a single Date column in OMD, which used the format 19:53:28:12:11. Obviously, the date and time formatting in OMD could have been different. DMD also had a Time of Day Dimension with columns for Time of Day, Hour, Shift, Segment of Day and Morning or Evening.

According to the dimensional modeling approach, in addition to the Date Dimension and Time of Day Dimension, extra columns can be introduced to other dimensions, which is another example of data restructuring. Our DMD contained the columns Town and Street and Number for values that were embedded in the Address column in the OMD, where they were separated by a comma. The decomposition of embedded attributes is not necessarily a DMD vs. OMD issue, but both literature [34] and practice suggest that it is an almost inevitable step in dimensional modeling and is, therefore, a legitimate focus for our comparison. Another result of the data restructuring was that the participants using OMD had to look for the prices at which a product was sold using the Price Lists table, which included price list title, period of validity, product name and unit price, while the participants using DMD had to search though a much wider POS Transaction Fact table to <sup>fi</sup>nd the unit price and place it in the context of product and dates. The data restructuring also introduced four new columns with calculated values such as taxes and pro<sup>fi</sup>t within the DMD table POS Transaction Fact.

## 5. Results

The results of our experiment fully support hypotheses H1, H5 and H6, while hypotheses H2, H3a, H4a and H4b are supported with some exceptions and H3b remains unsupported. The usability of DMD is almost always higher than or equal to the usability of OMD. Before we discuss our more general conclusions, we will describe the impact of moderating factors on particular tasks.

## 5.1. Usability of the innovations of dimensionally modeled data

Some of the main characteristics of dimensionally modeled data impacted all of the experimental tasks, while other characteristics only in<sup>fl</sup>uenced task subsets. The participants using OMD were inevitably presented with twice as many tables as the DMD participants. One group was shown tables called dimensions and the other group was not. The impact of these differences on user performance can be anticipated in each and every task result. Additionally, in a majority of the tasks, while one group searched for data in Invoice and Invoice Item tables, the other group looked at a single table to obtain the same data. Other characteristics of the models did not necessarily impact every task in the experiment, simply because tables with those characteristics were not present in every task. Bearing in mind that no task can be representative of a single characteristic independent of other characteristics, we can still analyze the results of tasks using tables with denormalization, Date Dimension, Time of Day Dimension and data restructuring.

## 5.1.1. The impacts of denormalization on usability

The <sup>fi</sup>rst of seven tasks that involved employees responsible for a particular sale, either in task conditions or in task solutions, was an assignment (Nr 6 in Table 1) that asked the participants for the name of the employee in charge of a purchase described through its date and product names. The participants using DMD had more correct results for this task; however, there was no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant difference between the two groups in the time spent solving the task.

The correct answer for two tasks (Nr 12 and Nr 13) that asked participants to list the individuals in charge of a store involved listing four people whose <sup>fi</sup>rst and last names were given in the data. For both tasks, the accuracy of the results corresponded to the types of data the participants used, with those using DMD exhibiting signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly more correct answers. Although these tasks clearly demanded that the participants list all responsible employees (in plural), an astonishing number of participants entered the name of only one responsible employee, usually the person at the lowest hierarchy level. Here, too, we found a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between

DMD and OMD users, with more single-name results from those using OMD (t=2.917, pb.004). Several participants named more than one but fewer than four names of responsible employees, but the differences exhibited in such results were not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. For the <sup>fi</sup>rst of these two tasks, the time it took participants to solve the assignment differed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly between the DMD and OMD user groups, with the participants using DMD spending more time to solve it, while there was no such difference for the time spent on the second task.

These tasks were followed by three additional tasks (Nr 14, Nr 15 and Nr 16), in which the participants were asked how many items were sold in each store for which a responsible employee was provided. To solve this type of task, the participant had to search for a given name in the hierarchy of responsible employees and select the column Sold Quantity from the table containing that information. It is interesting that participants using DMD spent signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly less time solving the <sup>fi</sup>rst of these three tasks than the participants using OMD. No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences were present in the time spent solving the other two tasks. Again, the accuracy of the results was significantly different between groups and the participants using DMD had more correct answers.

Another task (Nr 20) asked the participants to determine the amount of items sold for each of the <sup>fi</sup>ve responsible employees who were listed. There was no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in accuracy between the two groups for this task for reasons that may not be related to the way the hierarchy is represented but rather because of the complexity of the result, as will be discussed later in this paper following an analysis of the impact of the task type on performance. However, the group using OMD spent signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more time solving this task than the group using DMD.

## 5.1.2. Usability impact of introducing the Data Dimension and the Time of Day Dimension

The date played a role in seven of the tasks. For three of these tasks (Nr 1, Nr 2 and Nr 23) where the answer was the date, the date with the time of day, or the date with the name of a product, there were differences in the accuracy of the results between the two groups of participants in favor of DMD, whereas there were no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences for the temporal variable.

In three tasks (Nr 6, Nr 7 and Nr 8), the date was part of the constraint de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the task—the participants had to locate the responsible employee for a district and the store where a particular product was sold on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c date or list the supplier or producer companies whose products were sold on speci<sup>fi</sup>c dates. All three tasks revealed fewer errors for participants using DMD, while one of them showed a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in the completion time between the groups, with OMD participants spending signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly less time on the task. Finally, the task (Nr 28) that dealt with the time trend of product prices did not produce signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in usability aspects, likely due to additional data restructuring.

Two tasks (Nr 29 and Nr 31) addressed the days of the week, which were included in the Date Dimension column. Because OMD participants did not have weekday names available in their data, the text of the task included information regarding the day of the week for a particular date and the participants could use this to determine the days of the week for each of the 18 sequential dates in the data sample. Although this may not seem complicated at <sup>fi</sup>rst glance, it appears that it was much simpler to extract weekday names from the data sample because the participants using DMD performed better both on the task that asked for the days of the week when the merchandise was sold within the two store departments and on the task that asked whether more merchandise was sold on Fridays than on an average weekend day. The DMD participants spent significantly less time on the <sup>fi</sup>rst of these tasks than the other group.

There were three tasks (Nr 2, Nr 11 and Nr 30) that dealt with the time of day. The <sup>fi</sup>rst of these tasks asked for the date and the period of the day when a sale was made using a speci<sup>fi</sup>c payment method and program within a given store. This time, the group using OMD was offered no additional information and had to conclude on their own that the period of day was evening based on the information 19:53:28:12:11. As previously stated in our analysis of tasks dealing with dates, the DMD participants performed better. The same group of participants also had more correct answers for tasks that asked for a list of products that sold best in the morning and the number of invoice items made in the morning. Compared to the previous discrepancies, these were the easiest to predict because it is understandable that OMD participants could not all have the same de<sup>fi</sup>nition of morning as the dimensional data modelers.

Here, it must be noted that although better, the precision of the DMD participants in the date and period of day tasks is still not very high (with an average accuracy of 55% for DMD and 18% for OMD in all of these tasks). This suggests that they would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a data model tutorial.

## 5.1.3. Impacts of data restructuring on usability

One of the participant tasks (Nr 9) was to list the towns in which certain types of stores were located. The correct answer consisted of <sup>fi</sup>ve town names, which the DMD participants were more successful in producing. For this task, the participants in both groups were tempted to use the name of the store rather than <sup>fi</sup>nd the name of the town. Indeed, three stores had the name of the town in which they were located as their actual name, but two other stores bore the name of a well-known city quarter and a name that concatenated a town's name and a number. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more OMD participants included these two store names among the names of the towns in their responses (t=4.99, pb.001).

Another task (Nr 4) required participants to <sup>fi</sup>nd the address of a store whose type and approximate opening year were provided. Uniquely in the context of our experiment, the performance of this task suggested a greater usability for OMD, without important differences in accuracy but with less time spent on the task by the OMD participants. We conclude that the restructuring of the Address column increased the performance of tasks using its substrings, as shown in task Nr 9, but the price paid was the decreased performance of tasks using the entire string. However, this price is not very high because Town is a useful analytical classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and Address is not. Dimension tables should include as many meaningful text-like descriptions as possible, so the Address column could have existed in our DMD. This suggests that data restructuring should be designed cautiously.

Investigating the effects of further restructuring, we come across a task (Nr 28) that asked the participants for the prices of a particular product and whether this product became more or less expensive during the period analyzed. It appears that OMD was again more helpful in this case, although no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant correlation with accuracy or speed was found in the group of participants. However, this task came close to breaking the monotony of the superior accuracy exhibited by the participants using DMD.

Another task (Nr 24) required its participants to calculate how much direct pro<sup>fi</sup>t was lost after a 10% discount was applied to some products. Although we expected the DMD participants to produce better results, this task was so complex that none of the participants were able to solve it correctly. However, two similar tasks (Nr 21 and Nr 22) that consisted of a simpler summation of either the amount of tax in the context of a promotion or the pro<sup>fi</sup>t in the context of a supplier showed the strikingly greater usability of the precalculated columns in DMD.

## 5.2. Usability in the context of task type

Some task types always favored better results with DMD, whereas other task types produced better results with DMD only in the presence of dimensional modeling improvements. One task type did not produce a difference in accuracy in most cases. The impact of the task type is stronger on Task Result Accuracy then on Completion Time; only one task type (aggregation) notably impacted Completion Time.

Among the six tasks that asked for values in one or two <sup>fi</sup>elds, the accuracy of three tasks was correlated with the type of data that the participant was using, in which each of these three tasks dealt with an invention of dimensional modeling. One task (Nr 4) revealed that the DMD participants did not pro<sup>fi</sup>t from address <sup>fi</sup>eld restructuring and required even more time, whereas the other two tasks (Nr 3 and Nr 5), which asked for the supply price of a given product and for a product paid in cash within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c store, had no dimensional modeling innovations from which to bene<sup>fi</sup>t and revealed no differences in the performance of the two groups.

Among the ten tasks that asked for a list of values, seven tasks pro-<sup>fi</sup>ting from denormalization or from Date Dimension or Time of Day Dimension produced differences in the performance of the groups, whereas the other three tasks (Nr 10, Nr 26 and Nr 27), which did not involve particular DMD innovations, produced no differences. Two of these three tasks (Nr 26 and Nr 27) tested the performance in successive tasks, where the solution of the second task was a subset of the solution of the <sup>fi</sup>rst task, but no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in accuracy or time were found between the two groups.

A completely different performance pattern could be noted for tasks in which the solution was an aggregated numeric value, where accuracy in nine out of ten tasks was greater for participants using DMD, although three of the nine tasks (Nr 17, Nr 18 and Nr 19) did not bene<sup>fi</sup>t directly from any of the innovations of dimensional modeling. This difference in performance patterns, where aggregation activities are more sensitive to the data type than list forming or searching for a simple value, agrees with the customary classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of aggregation as a more analytical activity, while the other two activities are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as transactional activities. The only task with no performance differences between the two groups was the task (Nr 24) that asked for the amount of pro<sup>fi</sup>t lost due to a reduction in the price. While the other tasks asked for simple sums of one column's values, this task required several sums and subtractions to be performed and, apparently, a basic knowledge of microeconomics.

In the <sup>fi</sup>nal two tasks, the computer screens were turned off and the participants were asked to list all of the column names (Nr 35) and the values of the product names (Nr 36) that they remembered from the data. No statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences were detected between the two groups of participants.

The more complex tasks in our experiment included <sup>fi</sup>rm goaloriented tasks, for which the solutions required writing small reports and more loosely goal-oriented tasks, with the expectation of written essays. The solutions of <sup>fi</sup>ve <sup>fi</sup>rm goal-oriented tasks combined values from several columns; in some cases, they included the conclusions expected from the participants. Only two of these tasks re<sup>fl</sup>ected differences in the accuracy of the two groups of participants, possibly because one of them dealt with days of the week, while the other worked with the time of day. The other three showed no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences, although one task, which asked for prices and the trend of price changes, was supposed to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from restructuring, while the task that focused on income from products sold by <sup>fi</sup>ve responsible employees was supposed to pro<sup>fi</sup>t from denormalization. The solution of a third task was in the form of values from within four categories, i.e., stores, store departments and the supplier contact person's name and address. The participants’ results remained incomplete in most cases.

## 5.3. Impact of data types on the holistic view

Three loosely goal-oriented tasks were conceived in our experiment to measure the performance of data browsing to extract a holistic view of the phenomenon being analyzed. We asked the participants to write short textual essays: the <sup>fi</sup>rst to report on sales in a particular town (Nr 32), the second to write letters to two sales representatives representing business partners whose merchandise was being sold (Nr 33) and the third to write an analysis comparing the sales before and after the holidays (Nr 34). These three tasks could be categorized as analytical, complex and without a <sup>fi</sup>rm singular goal or predictable processing approach, which establishes a difference between these three tasks and the previous tasks. The time allocated for the <sup>fi</sup>nal task was limited to 10 minutes, which did not appear to affect performance; the same performance pattern was found for this task as for the other two tasks, on which the participants spent an average of 10 to 13 minutes.

The researchers examined the texts and counted <sup>fi</sup>ve different measures for each task, for which they later calculated averages. All <sup>fi</sup>ve measures revealed the advantages of using DMD. The facts listed in the report included all data from the sample, the numbers calculated and the conclusions made. The aggregations included all of the numbers that did not exist in the data sample. The conclusions made were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by researchers based on the number of times the participants wrote statements such as “…sales were greater than…”, “…the greatest amount was gained with line…” or “TV advertising had no effects”. The categories were counted according to the number of table columns that were used to write a report. The broadness of context was the number of categories in the sentence in the essay, which contained the greatest number of categories (Table 2). The same pattern of difference was noted when comparing each task on its own, except the <sup>fi</sup>rst task (Nr 32), in which the number of facts showed no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between the two groups. For two of the tasks that measured time, there were no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the time spent writing the texts, although to a small extent, the OMD users spent less time completing the tasks. The analysis of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of determination shows that the moderately higher relative amounts of variance of the construct referred to as Holistic View can be explained by Categories used (89.2%), Broadness of context (66.3%) and Facts included (60.8%), rather than by Aggregations made (25.1%) or Conclusions made (15.8%).

## 5.4. Overall accuracy and time performance

The accuracy of the participants’ results speaks clearly in favor of the greater usability of DMD (Table 1), with 22 out of 31 tasks solved more accurately. As further proof of the trend toward higher accuracy, there is a discrepancy among the groups with the number of answer boxes left blank, meaning that the participant did not complete the task, with 2.11 empty boxes out of 34 (6.2%) for the participants that used DMD and 5.65 empty boxes (16.6%) for the participants using OMD (t=4.711, pb.001). Contrary to the expectations derived from previous studies [11], the tendency to add unnecessary details to the results was not correlated with the participant groups; task results with this type of detail remained infrequent.

Analyzing the time spent solving tasks is more complex. Of the 33 tasks that measured time, only ten produced statistically relevant correlations between the groups of participants and the time spent solving a task. For the <sup>fi</sup>rst three tasks, the participants using DMD needed more time to solve the tasks, while for the following seven tasks, the participants using OMD required more time. This could cautiously be interpreted as evidence of a kind of learning process in which the participants using DMD were stimulated by the data to invest more time in understanding it at the beginning, resulting in better ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency later on. Therefore, we can hypothesize that one type of data is better characterized by learnability than the other. Learnability is the usability aspect that describes the ability of a software product to enable the user to learn how to use it.

The total time spent solving all 33 tasks reveals a correlation with the data used by the participants (t=2.242, pb.027). The DMD participants spent an average of 94 minutes solving tasks, whereas the OMD participants spent 104 minutes or 10.2% more time.

## 5.5. Subjective data perception

After the participants completed their tasks, they were asked to express the extent to which they agreed with eight statements, ranking their responses on a scale of 1 (“I disagree completely”) to 5 (“I agree completely”). Statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the participants’ responses between the groups were detected in half of the statements: “Tasks were simple to solve”, “The data used was clearly structured”, “I used the data structure easily” and “While searching for data, I often did not understand where I was” (Table 3). We can conclude that the participants using DMD had a more positive subjective perception of ad hoc querying tasks than the participants using OMD.

The Cronbach's alpha coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for the internal consistency of our eight-item questionnaire measuring subjective perception is α=0.779, which is a satisfying reliability level. It allows us to sum the scores of the items into a Subjective Perception Index, while noting whether a particular statement has a negative character, in which case the score has to be subtracted. However, the two components of the questionnaire extracted through the principal component analysis do not allow a meaningful interpretation. The subjective perception construct is correlated with the overall accuracy (r=.283, pb.01), the total time $( r = - . 3 7 7 , p < . 0 1 )$ and, with the highest intensity of the three, the overall ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ratio of the overall accuracy / total time (r=.417, pb.01). Although they are not very strong, these correlations provide another validation of the construct.

The participants were also given the opportunity to provide comments and suggestions for the researchers, which were checked to determine whether comments existed and whether the assessments were positive or negative. Some comments contained both positive and negative assessments. The levels of these three values were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different among the groups using differently modeled data.

## 5.6. The analysis of the research model

The research model of our study was tested with the use of the SEM technique. The generalized least squares method was applied to these estimations because a certain degree of correlation between the constructs measuring objective usability existed. The obtained model is shown graphically (Fig. 6), where the results show the estimate of the correlation between the two main usability factors, standardized regression weights and the proportion of variance for each observed variable explained by the factor.

The obtained SEM model is clear and easily interpretable. It is based on the entire sample of the participants (N=113). ## 6. Conclusion

The experiment revealed a nearly consistent difference in usability between the two types of data, i.e., in almost all of the compared contexts, dimensionally modeled data proved to be equally or more usable than operationally modeled data. The participants in the experiment that used dimensionally modeled data had better results for the following: accuracy in the majority of tasks with prede<sup>fi</sup>ned answers, quality of written essays, time spent performing tasks, trend in time spent performing tasks and subjective evaluation of the data. There were no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between the groups in data memorization, accuracy in performing simple tasks not dealing with innovations of dimensional modeling and accuracy in some tasks with complex results. The participants using operationally modeled data were more prone to commit certain types of errors. The innovations of dimensionally modeled data that were tested in this experiment proved to have certain usability advantages, with the exception of some examples of data restructuring, which did not necessarily improve usability (Table 4).

The most interesting and somewhat unexpected contribution of this study was the hypothesis regarding the learnability of DMD. Another important contribution of this study regards the overall usability of DMD—for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, the high usability of DMD was formally con<sup>fi</sup>rmed within the context of business user activities. Its advantages are presented in such important areas as supporting a holistic view and subjective perception, all of which speak strongly in favor of introducing DMD to business users instead of OMD. The structure of DMD's usability suggests that analysts particularly bene<sup>fi</sup>t from DMD in the cases in which their analysis relies on aggregates and

## 6.1. Limitations and discussion

The decisions made by the researchers when modeling the two experimental data samples certainly had an impact on the participants' performance, but it seems unlikely that any signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different decisions—for example, concerning formatting the date value in OMD—would produce an essentially different pattern of usability indicators. If the participants were required to use a complete data set instead of a sample, their cognitive task processing would remain similar to that observed in the experimental tasks, except that they would be more interested in higher logical levels of some hierarchies, such as product lines or most sold products, rather than products.

It should be noted that our OMD was a “light” version compared with the many possible real-life scenarios, in which we are likely to <sup>fi</sup>nd outdated columns and tables, tables without referential integrity, tables with more complex levels of abstraction, data split among several differently structured source databases, data that is illegible without application decoding and so on. Such usability disadvantages often exist in OMD and potentially eliminate OMD in the race against DMD even before the competition started, so they were beyond the scope of our study but should not be neglected when making reallife decisions.

As always in this kind of study, we need to ask ourselves whether the incentives could have changed the results [11]. Apart from motivation, stress is an important factor affecting performance. For example, a menu study [56] found that when users were stressed, they made 96 percent more errors and took 16 percent longer. The stressor in that study was the simple instruction that the participants work quickly, while the control group was given suggested instructions to avoid rushing. For participants who were kindly asked to be careful and to do their best, with no requirement to achieve a minimum score and without time limits (except some participants' possible desire to <sup>fi</sup>nish their tasks and leave the lab as early as possible), our experiment presented what we believe to be an average level of motivation and stress, similar to any realistic scenario in which decision support systems and ad hoc querying use is not mandatory and motivation and stress depend on an individual's personality and perception of the situation.

## 6.2. Further research

While using OMD is generally mandatory in organizations, using DMD is often discretionary, especially when it comes to unstructured usage, such as ad hoc querying. The nature of this unstructured usage is such that it is often unclear at the start whether it will be useful, which can decrease the readiness to perform dif<sup>fi</sup>cult tasks [37]. This emphasizes the need for further research to improve the database and data warehouse usability for nontechnical users.

The measurement instruments presented in this study should be treated as a <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt in the further development of more appropriate and accurate scales. Tasks Nr 1 to Nr 31, which were used to measure accuracy and time, could be replaced by other sets of tasks. Further efforts could be undertaken concerning the precision and completeness of the tasks' classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. More care could be taken to equally distribute the different task types throughout the different subsets of data.

The main question left unanswered by our study is why tasks with results in the form of small reports did not bene<sup>fi</sup>t from DMD. This issue seems important because such tasks can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as analytical. To investigate this more closely, an experiment could focus solely on this question. When comparing future research with our study's results, it would be particularly interesting to see whether the usability parameters reveal similar trends that suggest a learning process.

Surrogate keys, junk dimensions, outrigger dimensions, bridge tables, aggregated dimensions or snow<sup>fl</sup>ake schema could also be attractive topics for future experiments. Some of these principles, innovations, or modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of dimensional modeling are likely to diminish the usability of DMD, but for the most part, these elements can remain hidden from business users who could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the advantages they provide without seeing them. It could also be interesting to analyze how business users cope with different approaches developed to handle slowly changing dimensions. The results of such studies could help business intelligence designers, who may wonder what to present to end users. In addition, dimensional data samples for further research could be constructed to contain two or more star schemas with conformed dimensions. It would also make sense to compare usability between the simplest type of support for the relational model, which includes single table modeled data [25] and DMD or OMD for scenarios with small amounts of data.

An additional study could focus on the usability of star schemas containing a periodic snapshot table or an accumulating snapshot table. Another area that could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from comparative usability studies based on our research model is dimensionally modeled data used as a solution for particular areas: for example, within the insurance industry, in healthcare, or by capturing industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c process-oriented data, such as product return behavior. A subset of chosen measurements, for example essays and subjective perception questionnaire, might be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for some studies.
