# Conflict and identity shape shifting in an online financial community

John Campbell,\* Gordon Fletcher<sup>†</sup> & Anita Greenhill<sup>‡</sup>

\*Faculty of Information Sciences and Engineering, University of Canberra, Canberra, Australia, email: john.campbell@canberra.edu.au, <sup>†</sup>Salford Business School, University of Salford, Salford, UK, email: G.Fletcher@salford.ac.uk, and <sup>‡</sup>Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, Manchester, UK, email: A.Greenhill@manchester.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper challenges traditional explorations of online communities that have relied upon assumptions of trust and social cohesion. In the analysis presented here, conflict becomes more than just dysfunctional communication and provides an alternative set of unifying principles and rationales for understanding social interaction and identity shape shifting within an online community. A model is advanced that describes the systematic techniques of hostility and aggression in technologically enabled communities that take the form of contemporary tribalism. It is argued that this tribe-like conflict embodies important rituals essential for maintaining and defining the contradictory social roles sometimes found in online environments. This research offers a critical interpretive perspective that focuses on the link between identity shape shifting behaviours and the power relations within an online financial community. The analysis reveals how conflict between positions of power can help to align the values and ideals of an online community. With this study we seek to motivate a re-examination of the design and governance of online communities.

Keywords: online communities, character theory, conflict, identity shape shifting finance

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Online communities of practice can be difficult to reconcile into traditional organizational or social structures. However, they are still defined, organized and maintained by power relations, and the historical and cultural contexts that these power relations embody (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Critical information systems literature mostly focuses on the impact of technology innovation within formal organizational and social contexts (see Forester, 1992; Mumby, 1988; Myers & Young, 1997; Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997 and Stahl, 2003). Consequently, little is known from a critical perspective about alternative information systems contexts such as those that are found in online communities. This paper redresses this gap in the literature by undertaking an analysis of the power relations in an online community of stock marke investors.

There is a long history in many countries of people from all social levels trading on stock markets – even Karl Marx was said to have done so (Wheen, 2000). Despite their popular appeal, stock markets are historically the domain of the rich and powerful who have the means to invest and the ability to acquire the research resources necessary for successful investment. Internet-based financial communities are changing these traditional relations by providing small investors access to information, and the knowledge and opinion of like-minded traders. For many participants, these online environments instil a strong sense of camaraderie and facilitate a deep experience of community (Blanchard & Markus, 2004). Unfortunately, they are also susceptible to the manipulative and exploitative behaviours of dishonest participants who are able to use multiple messages and/or identities for personal gain (Campbell, 2001). This manipulative behaviour is not only damaging to the members of the online community, but can also have implications for wider society through increased stock price volatility and the creation of false market signals (Chenoweth, 2006; West, 2007).

In this paper, a critical interpretation of the power roles observed within an online financia community is achieved using a theoretical model of contemporary tribalism. A conflict-based character theory is developed, which challenges earlier research that relied on constructions of trust to explain observed behaviour in online groups (Handy, 1995; Jarvenpaa et al., 1998; Ridings et al., 2002; Oxendine et al., 2003; Li et al., 2004). In contrast, it is argued that conflict also embodies important social practices essential for defining and maintaining the contradictory social roles frequently found in online environments. Most importantly, this interpretation provides a cogent way of understanding the deceptive and manipulative behaviours readily observable in some online financial communities; particularly the phenomena of multiple identities and identity shape shifting (Wood & Smith, 2001; Cybenko et al., 2002).

## 2 TRUST AND CONFLICT IN VIRTUAL COMMUNITIES

As a means of interpretation, trust is a problematic and disputed territory that is grounded in a range of culturally and historically specific assumptions regarding human association and organization (Shapiro, 1987; Muyskens, 1998; Tweney, 1998; Fletcher et al., 2006). However, in the study of online communities, trust is the framework most commonly used to examine the social interactions that unify and structure a community (see, for example; Geyskens et al., 1998; Grey & Garsten, 2001; Mayer et al., 1995; Knights, 2001; McKnigh et al., 1998; Ridings et al., 2002 and Stewart, 1999) The emphasis on trust draws upon prior research into team performance which identified affect-based trust (emotional attachment) and cognition-based trust (rational assessment) as important for the effectiveness of faceto-face teams (McAllister, 1995; Cohen & Bailey, 1997). In work team environments, conflict is usually undesired and must be managed or resolved (Montoya-Weiss et al., 2001; Rutter, 2001). Conflict is seen to be particularly difficult to manage in technologically enabled environments as groups lack the social control normally available to face-to-face teams (Jarv enpaa et al., 1998). In contrast, we argue that the relationships that are defined through conflict reveal an alternative set of unifying principles and rationales for communities, includ ing those that are technologically enabled. We draw upon anthropological positions about tribal role-taking, politics and reference group activity to explore conflict and identity shape shifting within a virtual community.

Addressing technologically enabled groups as tribal groups presents a range of research possibilities and understandings. While the concept of a tribe is a contentious and muchdebated construct within Anthropology, it is a well-documented and analysed level of organi zation and community (Murdock, 1967). The notion of tribe must be used cautiously however as it can be regarded as an ethnocentric imposition enforced upon observed traditional organizational structures (Papstein, 1989). Nevertheless, the concept of tribe can provide a useful interpretation for certain social structures as long as the term is used in a neutral way and not in such a manner as to marginalize, dominate or disadvantage that group of people to which the term refers (Lewis, 1998; Suzuki, 2004).

While prior studies of communication in virtual communities have viewed conflict as a dysfunctional force requiring management (Montoya-Weiss et al., 2001; Hinds & Bailey, 2003; Hinds & Mortensen, 2005), this study views conflict as an integral element of the social inter-relations found within an online community, thereby echoing anthropological observations of social conflict (Beattie, 1964). This conflict-oriented analysis provides a lens for interpreting online financial communities that recognizes the existence and persistence of discord and dissonance. This interpretation highlights the simultaneous pressures for both collaboration and competition within a group and acknowledges that these two divergent social forces are necessary but incongruent and are therefore not always reconcilable.

## 3 TRIBALISM AND VIRTUAL COMMUNITIES: A CRITICAL METHOD

This study draws on critical interpretivism to understand interactions within virtual communities and draws on empirical material from an ethnographic examination of a technologically enabled online community that is defined by participation and presence in an online finance forum (Doolin & McLeod, 2005). The overarching objective of most forum participants is to profit from trading stock market securities. As a group, the forum’s purpose and utility is paradoxical. Community members are at times allies, attempting to collaboratively generate investment strategies and uncover information that might assist them in their mutual aim of achieving monetary gain from trading or investing. However, these same allies are at othe times adversaries who take opposing positions in specific market transactions.

We argue that this community is maintained by a cycle of conflict conducted by and directed towards specific tribe-like roles that are adopted by certain group members – what we label in this paper as the Big Man, the Sorcerer and the Trickster. Consequently, the interchanges between these key roles in the community are examined to obtain a broad understanding of these roles and the relationships they maintain with others in the community. While the examples offered here centre upon individuals, this is not the most significant level of organization for the community. Cataloguing and detailing individual actions and antagonisms revea the composition, dynamics and solidarity of the community. However, it is the continuous cycle of conflict among individuals that provides central communal focal points and offers mechanisms that maintain the community. The dynamism and continuous evolution of the community is revealed through the changing relationships between key members of the group and the fact that their roles are achieved and maintained only with the continuing support of the community (Firth, 1958). As a consequence, individuals who are assigned particular roles in this paper’s coarse taxonomy may not occupy the same role at a future date. The emphasis here is upon the roles of the community and on how the roles identified are themselves a persistent and necessary aspect of the community.

Our research method is broadly ethnographic in style and draws upon the research foundations presented and articulated in anthropology and utilizes a critical ethnographic approach for data collection (Avison & Myers, 1995; Myers & Young, 1997; Myers, 1997). A key difference in our approach from other studies of online communities is our attempt to analyse and discuss our textual evidence – the transcripts of the discussions in the forum – in a manner that moves away from textual analysis and simple concordance. We are attempting to provide a cogent interpretation of the complex interlinking of conversations that bind sometimes seemingly disparate and disconnected thoughts and actions into what can be described as community. Consequently, we focus attention upon the key actions and activities of participants in the broadest sense both between and within conversation threads. In the following section, we use anthropological tribe theories to provide a rich description of online community life. Later in this paper we examine a specific example of conflict to highlight how the concepts of tribal roles can be used to better understand social interaction and shape shifting behaviour in an online environment.

## 4 A CRITICAL THEORY OF TRIBALISM IN VIRTUAL COMMUNITIES

While the examples that are presented later in this paper are drawn from the interactions within a contemporary online community, a significant body of research exists in social anthropology that also concerns itself with the conflictual inter-relationships found in traditional communities (for example, Chagnon, 1983 and Dentan, 1979). One of the most concentrated collection of this form of work concerns the organization of Papua New Guinea’s Highland tribes. Within these ethnographies, conflict is a regular and commonly described feature of life. The volume of work in this area (for example, see Benedict, 1935, Burridge, 1965; Fried, 1975; Glasse, 1965; Keesing, 1935; Malinowski, 1979 and Salisbury, 1965) and the range and scope of these anthropological examinations enables a broad taxonomy of community roles to be extrapolated and represented at a conceptual level. This taxonomy is of direct utility to this paper in that it enables a structured investigation of the different participants of an online forum. In this taxonomy we focus on what, in the context of the data gathered to date, are arguably the three most significant roles, that of Big Man, Sorcerer and Trickster.

These three roles of key significance form the trinary of interlinked association illustrated in Figure 1. This formation and reformulation of individual identity reflects the theoretical struc turalism of Levi Strauss (Carroll, 1981). Our model also portrays an interaction element that impacts on the status of the actors within the community. This status loss/gain relationship reflects the theory of limited good (Foster, 1965). The theory of limited good posits that attitudes and behaviours are fashioned by the view that many of the desired things in life such as wealth, honour, respect and status, power and influence all only exist in finite and limited quantities. As illustrated in Figure 1, status is there to be divided and redivided, but not to be augmented.

Most significantly, the model for the key roles identified within the online financial community provides a lens for understanding the inter-relationship and fluidity of the roles held by individuals. The cycle of experience is not, however, universally experienced by all members of a forum and only identifies those participants who achieve these specific roles. The holders of each role are defined, at least in part, by their interaction with the other holders of key community roles. While individuals may not directly identify with the terms we use here, their actions generally indicate an awareness of those with similar roles as well as those who have different roles of their own. The trinary of key roles also represents potential directions for the change in status available to these participants. While a role shift can move in any direction between these three positions, there is a tendency over time for individuals to move away from the Trickster role towards the more mature roles of Sorcerer or Big Man. The individual key roles and their dynamics are discussed in further detail in the following sections.

## 4.1 The Big Man

One of the best known tribal figures is that of the Big Man. While the historical context of this role identification is predominantly masculine, our use of the nomenclature ‘Big Man’ is gende neutral and assigned to those who act out that particular role within the community irrespective of their known or presumed gender identity. The significant fact about these individuals is that leadership is achieved through status and wealth and not, in most causes, directly linked to family lineage or customary assignment (Meggitt, 1965; Weiner, 1989). Big Men do not enjoy the privilege of permanency in their role. The position requires maintenance and is dependent upon the continued support of the community. This is garnered in a variety of ways depending upon circumstance but generally sought through gifts (Firth, 1958). The obligation of receiving gifts cements the continuing support of others within the community (Leach, 1976; Weiner, 1989; Smart, 1993).

A community does not generally have a single Big Man but rather many Big Men (Salisbury, 1965). The relationship between these men can fluctuate through the entire range of human emotions. However, the relationship of Big Men tends towards a median of amicability. One o the skills of being a Big Man is the ability to expertly execute diplomacy as much as the ability to fight. In this variety of ways, Big Men are pivotal figures in the community. They offer a form of order and stability for the remainder of the group by absorbing many conflictual situations personally (Breton, 1999). The requirement to achieve the role itself adds a social dynamic, as younger men are eager to gain the status of Big Man (Godelier & Strathern, 1991).

The Big Man is readily seen within the online community where this leadership role is ascribed and maintained outside the confines of the forum’s formal rules of operation. Thus, Big Men provide a mediating and managerial role that is not related to the mechanica moderation of a forum but to the social organization and maintenance of the community. Big Men, as befits their role, also distribute gifts in the form of information about particular securities and trading strategies. The Big Man in this situation is under no obligation to provide these or any other gifts but apparently does so to ensure the longevity and status of his position in the community (Godelier & Scott, 2003).

The Big Men ensure the stability of the community by confining conflict to other Big Men or other big names within the community. This conflict can produce beneficial side effects by providing information gifts to the loyal but largely silent supporters. Not surprisingly, the role of Big Man is a temporal one. There are others waiting to assume the role at any sign of weakness, or when the gifts of information imparted by the Big Man are shown to no longer warrant the loyalty of supporters.

The Big Men are not the only form of role holders on this stage. While they may be the most respected and sought out individuals in the community, their position is also pre-requisite on the presence of others who assume different and sometimes antagonistic roles.

## 4.2 The Sorcerer

Another key figure also found in the literature on tribalism in Papua New Guinea and elsewhere is the Sorcerer (Grimble, 1954; Firth, 1958; Evans-Pritchard, 1976; Weiner, 1989). Because there is such importance placed on the obligations that receiving gifts from a Big Man implies, a lack of reciprocity has the potential to disrupt the order and stability of the community. Sorcerers, while participating in the community, also set themselves apart from that community and do not necessarily conform to community norms (Stewart & Strathern, 1999). The Sorcerer manipulates people within the community by precipitating and perpetuating moral conflict. Their actions are not happenstance or spontaneous, but are pre-meditated with clearly thought out purpose.

The Sorcerer does not offer support to, or recognition of, any of the Big Men unless there is an immediate personal benefit in doing so. In the context of an online financial community, the

Sorcerer as the disrupter may assume the role of the ‘ramper’, the person who will offer information to the community in order to benefit their own position. This is done with little or no regard for the detrimental effects that this may have on other individuals within the community. Precipitating this conflict provides the Sorcerer with benefits that override any concern for the stability of the whole community. Identifying the multi-faceted, tactical and self-motivated actions of the Sorcerer for what they are requires skill and experience, and is generally outside the abilities of many within the community except for other Sorcerers and the Big Men (Stewart & Strathern, 1999).

## 4.3 The Trickster

In contrast to the previous roles, the Trickster is generally a comical yet complex figure that is found in most of the world’s culture (Radin, 1956). While Tricksters may sometimes be mistaken for a Sorcerer or even a Big Man, their antics are usually nothing more than an annoyance. However, Dibbell (1998) makes references to the role of a Trickster character in his account of the LambdaMOO rape incident. This often cited example of cyberculture also diminishes the degree to which the Trickster can be dismissed as a mostly harmless role in online communities. The destructive potential of Tricksters is partially confirmed in the context of the online financial community where the Trickster’s actions can have the potential to become a costly annoyance. In general, they do not consider the consequences of their actions as the ‘trick’ is performed for the sake of the trick itself. If a Trickster participates in a stock ramping activity, it is not necessarily to benefit their own position as they may not even hold the security. Their actions are more closely akin to that of an experiment, proof perhaps for nobody other than the Trickster themselves that they can manipulate or influence other people. The Trickster’s own inexperience and naiveté may also partly explain their actions. By performing various tricks they become more knowledgeable of the motivations and sentiments that shape the community. This acquisition of knowledge may eventually enable a Trickster to assume a Sorcerer or Big Man role.

In the background to these three archetypes are the vast majority who do not participate in the conflict of the online financial community. While they are broad targets for the activities of the Sorcerers and Tricksters, they do not occupy individual roles of importance. They offer general tacit support to particular Big Men and accept gifts of information in exchange. This support is revealed in the maintenance of stability within the community and the continuity of the dominant framework of roles and relationships.

## 5 TRIBALISM, CONFLICT AND IDENTITY SHAPE SHIFTING

While traditional societies rely on metaphysical explanations of identity shape shifting (Lindenbaum, 2002), the opportunity for individuals to experiment with different identities or different roles is very real within technologically enabled environments. Many online com munities rely upon the simple but naïve assumption that one physical user will equate wit one single forum identity. Within online environments, identity shape shifting can be accomplished simply by assuming a new nickname. For more sophisticated shape shifters, this change of identity is reinforced by shifting stated loyalties, changing support for a particular stock or security, using different grammatical styles, or even changing the accuracy of the spelling in postings. Identity shape shifting is a repeated focus for discussion in the online financial community that we studied just as it is an often-used source of story in traditiona cultures (Silverman, 2001).

While identity shape shifting may not have significant consequences in some online communities, in others it can be the prelude to the manipulation, exploitation and social control of community members. It is important for community members to be able to identify and protect themselves from the possibly nefarious intentions of a shape shifter’s actions. At the very least, it is important to identify the veracity of the claims and the level of threat that identity shape shifters bring to the community. The difficulty of detecting a shape shifter can also be accentuated in situations where the pseudonym being used is not a new one, but one purposely chosen for its similarity to the pseudonym of a targeted member (examples we have observed include situations where Calrisian shadowed Calrissian, BuddFox and BudFox, etc.). Individuals seeking more significant roles within the community may also experiment with different identities in order to better understand and test the community’s tolerance and knowledge. Confrontation and conflict play important roles in helping community members protect themselves from identity shape shifters as conflict can facilitate the conditions necessary for the unmasking of perpetrators as we shall see in the following section.

## 5.1 An example of tribalism, conflict and identity shape shifting

The data presented here comes from a popular Australian online financial community that has been in existence since 1995. Membership includes amateur investors, day traders and professional brokers all of whom, despite their diversity of expertise, share a common interest in stock market investment. The first author has long-term experience in observing this online financial community and his expertise and sensitivity to the research context enables an informed interpretation of the community in a way that seeks to understand the sentiments and experiences of the group at a local level (Atkinson, 1990; Myers, 1999).

The following examples centre on an episode of conflict between a Trickster whom we have called Crashing, and an established Big Man whom we have called Minot. In addition to the Trickster and Big Man roles, the following extracts also demonstrate how those that occupy the Sorcerer role seek to advantage their own position without conforming to community norms. Together, these examples provide an exemplary interpretation of how the three character types interact in the community. ## 6 CONCLUSIONS

In all research, the theoretical position adopted affects what is seen, and what is seen as being important. Our analysis provides only one interpretation of the operation of an online financia community and there are likely to be many other equally valid interpretations. Consequently, the findings presented here should not to be taken as proof of a theory of contemporary tribalism in online communities. On the contrary, the relevance of the theoretical perspective used in our analysis emerged from the pattern of behaviours observed in an online financia community over an extensive period of time. Consequently, the tribal perspective itself is no the main focus of this research – it is the social roles and power relationships that exist in the online community and their link with identity shape shifting that are pivotal. The tribe-oriented theoretical position is useful only to the extent that it has facilitated critical reflection on the power relations and experienced life within the online financial community. The value of the research is not just in revealing the power conflict, but in how conflict enriches the community in terms of knowledge about itself and in providing new and useful trading information. In this way the community is as much nurtured by the conflict as it is controlled by its own historica and cultural context.

This study has provided an example of how information systems research insights can be gained from anthropological interpretations regarding communities and affiliated groups and, in so doing, exposed how conflict defines roles, attitudes and behaviours within an online community. The tribal framework helps us, in part, to make sense of the historical and cultura context of the online financial community by providing a critical interpretation of the self-interest and power relations that are sometimes manifest in shape shifting and role conflict. As a result, the theoretical approach has provided a rich description and deeper understanding of the social dynamic within the community.

Our analysis focuses on a specific event involving conflict between role-holders in an online financial community. This conflict is typical in the forum and the examples presented here exemplify the role of conflict as a social force and highlights the support these activities bring to those involved in the cycle of acquiring and losing status. These skirmishes assist with the establishment of a social order for the group and are specific indications of who holds status positions within the community. This interpretation also provides a means for understanding why and in what context participants in online communities might adopt multiple pseudonyms. Even if conflict has a negative outcome for particular individuals, the dynamic nature, continuing interest and ongoing interaction between other group members ensures the overall continuity of the community. The issue of trust is not a significant factor in this community as true identities are rarely revealed and the assessment of an individual’s standing within the community is largely based on the value of the information gifts that they supply. Consequentially, individuals who regularly post information about trading opportunities, who participate in conflict exchanges and who convince other community members of their status are likely to be more highly regarded.

Our findings have important implications for the design of both the forum software and the governance mechanisms required to support an online financial community. These artefacts must support a balance between the types of conflict that are destructive and those that are essential for the maintenance and well-being of an online community (Campbell et al., 2007) The impartial enforcement of a ‘terms of use’ policy is the commonly used means for moderating conflict and other undesirable behaviours in online environments. However, our analysis shows that conflictual power relations play a critical role in aligning the values and ideals of the community through social stability and order. Consequently, future research is required into the design of software and community governance mechanisms that recognize these attributes and support greater scope for self-regulation.

The significance of our findings is also found in the opportunities that the tribe-oriented approach offers for future research into the study of the social engagement of online commu nities. In recognizing that conflict is an inevitable, irreducible and a core component of social interaction, the scope for further research of online communities is greatly expanded. While the role focus in this research has been somewhat coarse, the opportunity remains for more refined and detailed research into the cultural practices and roles within online communities. Our findings also have important implications for the community members. While information technology can extend traditional community boundaries, it is important for participants not to be taken in completely by illusions of camaraderie and solidarity. While there is great value in many of the symbiotic relationships that emerge from within these online environments, all members (particularly the less experienced) need to reflect on their own place among the different social roles within the community.
