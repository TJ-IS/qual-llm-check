# Modeling Supply-Side Dynamics of IT Components, Products, and Infrastructure: An Empirical Analysis Using Vector Autoregression

Gediminas Adomavicius

Department of Information and Decision Sciences, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, Minnesota 55455, gedas@umn.edu

Jesse Bockstedt

Information Systems and Operations Management, School of Management, George Mason University, Fairfax, Virginia 22030, jbockste@gmu.edu

Alok Gupta Department of Information and Decision Sciences, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, Minnesota 55455, alok@umn.edu

rior IS research on technological change has focused primarily on organizational information systems and technology innovation; however, there is a growing need to understand the dynamics of supply-side forces in the introduction of new technologies. In this paper we investigate how the interdependencies among information technology components, products, and infrastructure affect the release of new technologies. Going beyond the ad hoc heuristic approaches applied in previous studies, we empirically validate the existence of several patterns of supply-side technology relationships in the context of wireless networking. We use vector autoregression (VAR) to model the comovements of new component, product, and infrastructure introductions and provide evidence of strong Granger-causal interdependencies. We also demonstrate that substantial improvements in forecasting can be gained by incorporating these cross-level effects into models of technological change. This paper provides some of the first research that empirically demonstrates these cross-level effects and also provides an exposition of VAR methodology for both analysis and forecasting in IS research.

Key words: information systems and technology trends; supply-side forces; technological change; technology ecosystems; technology forecasting; time series analysis; vector autoregression

## 1. Introduction

Information systems and technology (IS&T) have become critically interwoven with the operations, communication, and organization of modern firms and society. The rate of change in the core technology of IS&T (e.g., software and hardware) is astounding and often overwhelming for potential adopters. Making sense of the IS&T landscape is a difficult process for firms because of the large number of available technologies and the complex interrelationships among them (Adomavicius et al. 2008). Surprisingly, very little IS research has explored the nature of change in IS&T at the base technological level, i.e., individual technologies and functions. Most IS research on technological change has focused on organizational innovation, in other words, the diffusion and adoption of new IS&T by organizations and the resulting innovations in processes, operations, and organizational structure. Swanson’s Tri-Core model (1994), which is focused almost exclusively on IS innovation in organizations,<sup>1</sup> has guided much of the IS innovation research over the past two decades. (See also Fichman and Kemerer 1999, Lyytinen and Rose 2003 for comprehensive reviews of related research.)

Some researchers have recognized the need to incorporate the analysis of base-level information systems and technology into organizational innovation research. In particular, Grover et al. (1997) suggest that Swanson’s model (1994) can be extended to include innovations at the base technology level. Swanson and Ramiller (1997) recognize core technology capabilities in their organizing vision of IS innovation, and

Lyytinen and Rose (2003) incorporate base information technologies as a construct (along with system development and service innovations) in their threeset model of IT innovations. However, these studies do not look specifically at the dynamics of change in IS&T at the base technology level or at the interactions among technological artifacts; instead, the focus remains primarily organizational.

Understanding how IS&T changes at the base technology level is necessary for several reasons. First, the motivations for research in organizational IS, i.e., the strong dependency of organizations on technology, also apply to research on developments in base IS&T. This dependency requires organizations to understand the nature of technological change and, more specifically, the supply-side dynamics of new technology introductions in order to make more informed decisions about technology selection and adoption. Additionally, from an adopting organization’s perspective, the IS&T landscape can be daunting, with an overwhelming number of technology options to choose from and a myriad of complex relationships among these options. Organizations regularly update the software and hardware that support enterprise networks and systems as well as meet the computing needs of individual employees. For example, in the context of wireless networking technologies, organizations must choose vendors and appropriate models of wireless routers, compatible laptops with the correct chipsets, adapter cards for PCs, and other peripherals that align with the organization’s IT architecture, budget, and possibly its long-term IT strategy. Knowledge about the landscape of these base-level technologies, including trends and future projections, can help organizations more effectively make these decisions. As can be expected, practitioners are seeking new frameworks, tools, and methodologies to improve their understanding of the dynamics of change in IS&T (Adomavicius et al. 2008). Furthermore, a better understanding of these dynamics can help technology producers improve designs and better time technology introductions. Finally, from an IS researcher’s perspective, insights on the dynamics of change at the base technology level may help improve forecasts of trends in information technology development and diffusion and also help in the identification of new applications for IS&T in organizations.

The goal of this paper is to empirically demonstrate the existence of cross-level supply-side interdependencies of technology introductions in the component, product, and infrastructure (CPI) classes of IS&T (Adomavicius et al. 2008) and also show that, by considering these interactions, analysis and forecasts of technological change can be improved. Going beyond the ad hoc heuristic approaches typically used in similar endeavors (e.g., Adomavicius et al. 2008,

Funk 2007), we utilize vector autoregression (VAR) to identify significant Granger-causal relationships (Granger 1969) and produce useful forecasts of technology introductions. Our analysis is performed in the context of wireless networking technologies, where we use a data set of more than 3,500 Wi-Fi technologies introduced over an eight-year period. This paper presents three key contributions to the IS research literature. First, the results of the research provide a significant extension to the initial research on the CPI model presented in Adomavicius et al. (2008) by being the first paper to demonstrate a practical estimation approach for identifying and quantifying cross-level supply-side effects in the introduction of new information technologies. This is an interesting and significant result in an underexplored area of research on the dynamics of technological change. Second, using out-of-sample data, we provide evidence that incorporating these cross-level effects can substantially increase the performance of technology forecasts. Third, we provide one of the first formal expositions of the VAR methodology in IS research, a technique that is well established and often used in macroeconomics and public policy research but not typically utilized in the IS research domain. In summary, this work promotes the importance of taking a systems view of technological change and demonstrates the gains in understanding and forecasting achievable by utilizing the VAR approach in concert with the CPI model.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides a review of relevant literature and the research framework that serves as the basis for our empirical analysis. Section 3 discusses our research methodology, including the data and research context, an overview of the VAR methodology, and the specification of our empirical model. Section 4 presents the results of our empirical analysis, which identifies and validates the cross-level effects defined by the CPI model. We also demonstrate empirically that incorporating these effects leads to significant improvements in forecasts of supply-side technology forces. Section 5 discusses the implications of this research for theory and practice. Section 6 concludes the paper with a discussion of limitations and possible extensions.

## 2. Conceptual Foundations

To develop our empirical research framework, we draw on prior research on the nature of technological change. We first discuss the advantages of utilizing a supply-side perspective in the analysis of technological change, which have been introduced in prior literature. We then discuss theory on the structure of technology and how these concepts apply specifically to IS&T. Finally, we discuss theory on the cross-level interactions in IS&T and how these concepts shape our empirical analysis.

2.1. Supply-Side Forces in Technology Change As noted previously, IS research on diffusion of innovations has largely ignored supply-side forces in favor of demand-side organizational adoption issues. Moreover, a major critique of the extant research on technological change and diffusion theory in general is that there has been a lack of work that considers the importance of supply-side factors (e.g., Brown 1981, Attewell 1992). Critics argue that the institutions that supply and actively market new technologies significantly affect the spread and adoption of these technologies. Furthermore, technological change and innovation is largely driven by developments in the technical knowledge base, which is primarily the result of the supplyside efforts of institutions that develop and produce new technologies, such as research centers, corporations, and universities (e.g., Tornatzky 1983, Tornatzky and Fleischer 1990). In fact, in many information technology contexts, ongoing research and development leads to new technologies for which consumers only discover uses once the technologies are introduced. For example, many consumers utilize only a fraction of the technological capabilities embedded in standard personal computer, yet companies like Intel continue to develop and improve computer components largely as a function of supply-side efforts.

A few scholars have considered the role of supplyside forces in the organizational adoption of information technologies. Early on, Zmud (1984) recognized the need to model both “need-pull” and “technologypush” forces in the adoption of modern software practices. The “technology-push” forces referred to new technology introductions (supply-side) that provided new usage opportunities for organizations. Gatignon and Robertson (1989) argued that supply-side incentives played a key role in the adoption of laptops by sales organizations. Currie and Seltsikas (2001) recognized the supply-side effort of application service providers as a driver of innovation and adoption of IT-outsourcing services. An underlying theme of these few examples is the general recognition that technology producers put significant effort into the development of new technologies, which acts as a strong supply-side force driving technological change and adoption. However, there is a general lack of IS research on the structure and dynamics of technological change from this supply-side perspective.

Many scholars have argued on either side of the philosophical debate about whether demand-pull or supply-push forces fundamentally drive technological change (e.g., Adner and Levinthal 2001, Sahal 1985), and many have also argued that it is a combination of supply- and demand-side forces that is responsible (e.g., Arthur 2009, Nelson 1995). Considering this debate, we recognize that, regardless of one’s philosophical view on the genesis of technological innovation, the instantiation and execution of these innovations into devices and technologies are largely supply-side endeavors (e.g., Arthur 2009, Dosi 1982, Nelson 1995). We believe that adopting a supplyside view is an appropriate perspective for analyzing technological change and, therefore, we focus our research on measuring the cross-level effects in the introductions of new technologies. Furthermore, although technology adoption can certainly be an indicator of technological change, adoption data do not capture the impact of supply-side forces from unsuccessful technologies on further technological development. For example, the Apple Newton, released in 1993, is considered to be one of Apple Computer’s biggest product flops; however, it is also considered to be a very early predecessor of the iPhone and laid the technology groundwork for most personal digital assistant (PDA) designs. In many cases, through supply-side activities technology firms create new markets and demand, where none previously existed, for innovative and disruptive products (e.g., Christensen et al. 2004).

## 2.2. IS&T Structure: Components, Products, and Infrastructure

Technology has been conceptualized as a complex system going back many decades (Simon 1962, 1969; Alexander 1964). Modern information technologies are no different; they consist of many interacting and interdependent parts that operate in conjunction to form systems. Several scholars have conceptualized technology as being made up of systems of components (e.g., see Langlois and Robertson 1992, Tushman and Rosenkopf 1992, Rosenkopf and Nerkar 1999, Arthur 2009). In these systems, linkages and interfaces govern interrelationships and technologies can best be developed as modules that can be easily combined and altered to create a variety of solutions (Baldwin and Clark 2000, Arthur 2009). Despite the inherent system structure of technology, empirical research on technological change has largely ignored this fact (Tushman and Murmann 1998).

Beyond recognizing the systemic nature of technology, few scholars have attempted to develop a theory of technology structure. Recently, Arthur (2009) proposed the following principles of technology structure. First, technologies are combined from component parts or assemblies, which themselves are technologies. Second, technologies are always organized around a central concept or governing principle. Arthur recognized that, to understand technology, one must understand the concept it is addressing and its assembly as a system of components. Furthermore, technology designs typically employ a main assembly, which addresses the core usage context, and subassemblies, which address the supporting needs of the technology. For example, a personal computer has a core computing usage context and contains a main assembly to achieve this task (e.g., CPU, RAM chips, hard drive) and additional subassemblies to support the main assembly and additional functions (e.g., a power supply, cooling fan, I/O ports). Technological change can be achieved through the development of new designs (i.e., configurations) of components, assemblies, and systems of assemblies as well as through developments of radically new technologies.

The ideas of Arthur (2009) echo the concepts in the CPI model of information technology presented by Adomavicius et al. (2008). They argued that basic technologies within IS&T systems can be classified into three specific technological classes depending on the usage context (i.e., governing principle): components, products, and infrastructure. Like Arthur’s definition, components are technological subunits, designed to perform a specific function, that are combined together to form a technological solution in a specific usage context, such as hard disk drives, 802.11 chipsets, or video cards combining to provide computing capabilities. A new development or change in a component technology would occur as existing subunits are improved or refined or as new components are introduced. Products are technologies that comprise combinations of components and embody a design or specific information about how components are combined; they provide a solution to a “user”<sup>2</sup> in a given usage context, such as cellular phones as a solution for mobile communication. Like Arthur’s concept of innovation in assembled technologies, innovations or developments in products occur when new combinations of components are developed. For example, standard electrical components can be combined in different ways to create circuits that perform very different functions. Infrastructure technologies support and extend the system-of-use of product technologies in a usage context. For example, in the context of personal computing, a printer and a wireless router extend the capabilities of a laptop computer. So if we considered the usage context of the product as being composed of a system of technologies, infrastructure would be the devices that extend the capabilities and peripherals of the product technologies.

The CPI model is one way to conceptualize the structure of technology that is particularly well suited for information systems and computing technologies. We choose to adopt this model for our analysis because it has been shown to be a useful way to reduce the complexity of the technology landscape by providing a realistic classification mechanism for information technologies (Adomavicius et al. 2008). We can use the CPI model to focus on the interactions among classes of IS&T rather than the potentially huge number of interactions among specific individual technologies. Additionally, the CPI conceptual model captures the essentials of the systemic nature of information systems and computing technology, it has been qualitatively shown to be an effective representation from a practitioner’s perspective (Adomavicius et al. 2008), and it aligns well with other conceptualizations of technology structure.

## 2.3. Cross-Level Effects in Components, Products, and Infrastructure

Interactions and interdependencies among the parts of complex technology systems are a primary factor in determining the trajectory of technological development (Rosenberg 1969, David 1975, Hughes 1983). It has been established in prior literature that technologies within a system influence one another with respect to development and change. For example, Henderson and Clark (1990) introduced the notion of architectural innovation, which involves changing the linkage mechanisms among technological subsystems to complement generational innovation of the subsystems themselves. Baldwin and Clark (2000) discuss how changes at the component level drive technological change as new modules are invented, existing ones are reconfigured, and new combinations of modules are developed.

The theory of cross-level co-evolution (Campbell 1990) provides an interesting theoretical lens for analyzing technological change in information technology and systems. Specifically, cross-level co-evolution refers to the impact of new technological developments at one level of the technology hierarchy on the other levels (Campbell 1990, Rosenkopf and Nerkar 1999). Research on the interactions among modules, subsystems, and classes of technologies is becoming increasingly more important as the reach of information technology grows within society and the complexity of information systems increases. A few scholars have attempted to analyze these interactions using various methodological approaches. For example, Ethiraj and Levinthal (2004) use a simulation approach to explore the dynamics of technological change in modular systems; Langlois and Robertson (1992) use case studies on the PC and stereo industries to theorize about the benefits of modular systems in terms of new technology development; Jenkins and Floyd (2001) use qualitative case studies to explore the co-evolution of Formula 1 racing technology across firms as a result of component knowledge transfer; and Funk (2007) performs an industry-level analysis to identify the impact of product design hierarchies on technology developments in the music industry. Forman (2005) provides one of the few empirical papers that accounts for interactions among multiple classes of technologies in his analysis of investment in and adoption of advanced Internet applications. However, in general, there has been a lack of rigorous empirical research on the cross-level interactions affecting change in information systems and technology. Lyytinen and Rose (2003) note this research gap, stating that existing theory on technological change in IT must be expanded to understand the changing relationships among different technologies over time.

There is also a significant amount of research in marketing and economics on the role of network externalities in the co-evolution of high-technology products. Research in this area provides another perspective on technological co-evolution that must be recognized, and we provide a brief overview of some key contributions. Katz and Shapiro (1994) provide one of the first thorough accounts of system-based development and competition, in which complementary products and systems of related technologies interact with one another through network effects. The most commonly researched area in this context is the role of indirect network effects because of compatibility in software and hardware products (e.g., see Bayus 1987, Bucklin and Sengupta 1993). Recently, Nair et al. (2004) demonstrated that strong indirect network effects arise in the production and demand in high technology markets because of strong interdependencies between compatible hardware and software. Stremersch et al. (2007) perform an extensive empirical analysis of individual product level data to establish the role of indirect network effects among high-technology software and hardware with respect to product growth.

A key reason for our use of the CPI model is its inclusion of a set of possible interactions among the classes of IS&T (see Figure 1). Each arrow in Figure 1 represents a potential interaction or interdependency that could impact the development and change of information systems and technology. The framework contains within-level interactions, such as infrastructure technologies driving subsequent developments in future infrastructure technologies. More interesting, however, are the cross-level interdependencies, which include component-product, product-infrastructure, and infrastructure-component relationships. Each of these relationships represents a potential cross-level influence a technology in one class may have on technologies in a related class. Conceptually, the ideas of cross-level interactions among technologies are starting to take hold in the research community, but the empirical evidence is lacking (Murmann and Frenken 2006), with the exception of Forman (2005), discussed previously. Most of the work in this area has adopted qualitative approaches, industry-level analysis, or ad hoc empirical methodologies.

Practitioners and researchers would benefit from understanding these cross-level effects in the changes of information technology for several reasons. Let us consider the IT context of wireless networking. Firms that produce wireless technologies, organizations that adopt wireless technologies, and end users who utilize wireless technologies all make decisions based on their perceptions of future trends in this industry. For example, an organization purchasing wirelessenabled laptop computers would want to time its purchase decision optimally so as to get the most value out of the set of wireless technologies available on the market, considering anticipated future wireless technology capabilities. Similarly, a technology producer would benefit by understanding the complex supply-side relationships among its devices (say, for example, wireless USB adapters) and compatible wireless devices from other producers, such as wireless routers and networking software installed on laptop computers.

The systemic nature of information technology and the potential interactions among component, product, and infrastructure classes can significantly impact the development and introduction of new technologies in this context. New technologies released in one class can impact the development of technologies in the other classes. For example, a new low-power wireless chipset with a small footprint is a component that could provide opportunities for new handheld devices (products) to be introduced with new capabilities that incorporate this component in their design. This would be an example of interaction among supply-side forces of component and product technologies, specifically in the direction of a new component impacting the development of a new product design. Consider a second example, a new method of wireless data routing could be introduced as part of new networking infrastructure technologies, which in turn would create an opportunity for the design of new products that operate on this infrastructure. In this case, an interaction between infrastructure and product supply-side forces would exist. The CPI framework shown in Figure 1 defines all possible interactions among and within the three technology classes; however, the existence of these potential interactions is likely to be context dependent. Any firm or consumer operating within the wireless networking ecosystem would benefit from knowing which of these dynamics operates in this technological context and how the strength, direction, and timing of these effects can be used to better understand and predict technological change.

The initial work on the CPI model (Adomavicius et al. 2008) has identified the total set of possible cross-level interactions; however, the presence of these relationships will certainly vary based on the technological context or domain. We would expect that subsets of these relationships may or may not exist depending on the specific conditions in the context under study. The heuristic methodological approaches applied in prior research have not focused on providing statistically sound estimations of the existence, magnitude, and impact of these interrelationships. In this paper, we adopt wireless networking technology as our research context; in this context, there are specific idiosyncrasies that may dictate the existence of the interactions proposed in the CPI model. For example, wireless networking requires interoperable capabilities among components and infrastructure for a viable communications network. This might suggest strong forward interdependencies between component and infrastructure technologies in this context. Furthermore, wireless networking technologies have tended to be released in generational waves (e.g., 802.11b, 802.11g) with backward compatibility. These characteristics may reduce the need for strong dependencies from products on components because older products are capable of operating on newer networks that use both new components and infrastructure. Our goal in this research is to identify, using robust empirical methods, which of these cross-level interactions exist among supply-side forces and demonstrate that the CPI model provides useful mechanisms for understanding and forecasting technological change. To achieve this goal we employ the VAR technique, which provides effective facilities for analyzing interdependent relationships and for performing accurate forecasts, as we will show in the following sections.

Additionally, VAR provides a statistically rigorous yet atheoretical approach that is especially well suited for developing and testing theory (Stock and Watson 2001), thus allowing us to empirically estimate and analyze the relationships suggested by the conceptual CPI model in prior research.

## 3. Research Methodology

## 3.1. Data and Context

Wireless networking technologies, like many information systems and technologies, operate by design as a system and follow a standard protocol (i.e., IEEE 802.11) with well-defined components, products, and infrastructure. Wireless networking technologies are also widely utilized by both organizations and individuals, which reinforces the relevance of our chosen research context. Furthermore, wireless networking technologies have evolved quickly over the past decade, providing a unique opportunity to observe and analyze substantial technological change over a relatively short time horizon.

An additional benefit of studying wireless networking technologies is that there is an industry organization, the Wi-Fi Alliance (Wi-Fi.org), responsible for certifying new technology introductions. The Wi-Fi Alliance is the primary certifying organization, and any information technology that has wireless networking capabilities can be certified. Certifications are awarded by the alliance prior to commercial release based on the results of interoperability tests, and technologies that are successfully certified are allowed to be labeled with the official certification logo. Based on a review of the database of certifications awarded by the alliance, we have concluded that an overwhelming majority of companies participate in this process (more than 300 including all major producers, e.g., Dell, HP, Apple, Cisco, Linksys) and that the standard certification practice is to certify a series of new technologies (e.g., a chipset series or laptop series) rather than each individual instantiation of a newly released wireless technology. Therefore, the data on certifications by the Wi-Fi Alliance should provide a good proxy of supply-side forces in the introduction of new wireless technologies because it captures an overwhelming majority of the market, and certifications of technology series provide a more realistic representation of new technological developments than do certifications of each individual model or device introduced. Examples of technologies that are certified include networking equipment; consumer electronics (e.g., cameras, mp3 players, and video game consoles); storage devices and servers; adapter cards, chipsets; computers and PDAs; printers; and mobile phones. Our data set contains information on every certification awarded during the eight-year period between early 2000 and early 2008, which includes more than 3,500 new wireless networking technologies released during that time period.

We coded the Wi-Fi Alliance certifications into the component, product, and infrastructure technology classes based on preassigned technology categories determined by the Wi-Fi Alliance, which directly translate into these classes. For example, wireless chipsets, internal and external adapter cards, and USB adapters were coded as component technologies because each clearly acts as a component by providing wireless capabilities for another device. Access points, Ethernet bridges, and broadband gateways were coded as infrastructure because these technologies either form or extend the network infrastructure necessary for wireless communications. Consumer electronics and personal computing devices with wireless capabilities were coded as products; this included PDAs, laptops and PCs, printers, mobile phones, and mp3 players. In the case of products, the certifications represent new product designs that incorporate wireless networking capabilities, but the certificates do not cover any third-party wireless components included in the product. For example, a certification of a Dell laptop is a certification of the Wi-Fi client software and hardware configuration, or design, specific to the laptop that works with a third-party wireless chipset, not to the wireless chipset component itself. Therefore, product certifications represent the development of technology by the product producer and are not double-counts of the certifications of third-party components they may contain. A complete mapping of the Wi-Fi Alliance categories to the component, product, and infrastructure technology classes is provided in Table A1 in the appendix (available at http://dx.doi.org/10.1287.isre.1120.0418) and a plot of the raw counts is shown in Figure 2.

Using monthly counts of certifications (releases) for each class, we obtained three time series. With data from March 2000 through March 2008, we have a total of 97 monthly observations in the form of time series $\mathbf { C o m p } = \{ c o m p _ { 1 } , ~ c o m p _ { 2 } , \ldots , c o m p _ { 9 7 } \} ,$ Prod $= \{ p r o d _ { 1 } , \ p r o d _ { 2 } , \cdot \cdot \cdot , p r o d _ { 9 7 } \} ,$ and $\mathbf { I n f r a } = \{ i n f r a _ { 1 } ,$ $i n f r a _ { 2 } , \ldots , i n f r a _ { 9 7 } \}$ . ## 3.2. The General VAR4p5 Model

Identifying significant interdependencies among technology classes would provide support for the existence of cross-level effects and provide insights into the nature of change in information technologies. Additionally, understanding the supply-side dynamics among the classes of IS&T can help firms better forecast supply-side forces in technological change. To look for these interdependencies, we used a VAR empirical model for analyzing the comovements of the component, product, and infrastructure time series. VAR is an empirical framework proposed by Sims (1980, p. 14) to address the “too many incredible restrictions” imposed by traditional simultaneous equation models for analyzing time series data. Sims (1980) argued that in most economic systems all variables will affect all other variables, and the VAR framework was proposed to model these endogenous relationships. Specifically, a VAR model is an n-equation, n-variable linear model where each variable is regressed on its own lagged values and the past values of the other $n - 1$ variables (Stock and Watson 2001). The VAR framework provides a systematic approach for analyzing the dynamics of multiple time series, and the corresponding statistics are relatively easy to interpret (Enders 1995).

Over the past few decades, VAR has become a widely used and important technique in the applied econometrician’s toolkit (Enders 1995, Kennedy 2003). VAR provides a coherent and credible technique for data description, forecasting, inference, and policy analysis (Sims 1980, Stock and Watson 2001). VAR is commonly used in macroeconomic studies $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ McNees 1990, Sims 1992), studies of markets $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ Booth et al. 1997, Campbell and Shiller 1988), and political science studies (e.g., Freeman et al. 1989, Enders and Sandler 1991, Sprecher and DeRouen 2002); however, it is not often applied in the IS research context. Backus (1986) argues that VAR is very useful for testing models with undefined or hard to define restrictions because it provides fairly unrestricted approximations to reduced forms of general models without assuming a priori that any variables are exogenous. Similarly, Pagan (1987, 1995) views the VAR technique as a major methodological approach to econometrics. See also McNees (1986) for a comparison of VAR to other traditional econometric techniques.

The vector autoregression is ideally suited for the analysis of cross-level interdependencies among information technologies for several reasons. First, there are obvious endogenous relationships among the development of IS&T components, products, and infrastructure. The nature of interoperability, compatibility, and versioning in the IT industry all imply strong interdependencies between the development and release of new technologies in the three IS&T classes, which is exactly what we hope to identify empirically. Second, although there is some prior research contending that cross-level interactions are present in technological evolution, there have been no rigorous empirical studies to support these theories (Murmann and Frenken 2006). Additionally, there is a lack of a priori assumptions and restrictions about the form of models to analyze these relationships; VAR provides a useful approach for modeling these types of systems and for identifying relationships without imposing unnecessary or unfounded model restrictions. Third, a goal of this research is to provide formal estimation and forecasting models for the CPI theory of development and change in IS&T, and Backus (1986) argues that VAR provides a sound foundation for building theoretically motivated models.

The general standard reduced-form VAR(p) model with lag order $p$ (see Enders 1995, Greene 2003) is

$$
\begin{array}{c} \mathbf {Y} _ {t} = \mathbf {c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c c} \mathbf {A} _ {1} \mathbf {Y} _ {t - 1} + \mathbf {A} _ {2} \mathbf {Y} _ {t - 2} + \dots + \mathbf {A} _ {p} \mathbf {Y} _ {t - p} + \varepsilon_ {t}, \\ t = 1, \ldots , T, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $\mathbf { Y } _ { t } = ( y _ { 1 t } , y _ { 2 t } , \ldots , y _ { n t } ) ^ { \prime }$ denotes an $( n \times 1 )$ vector of time series variables, c is an $( n \times 1 )$ vector of constant terms, $\mathbf { A } _ { i }$ are $( n \times n )$ coefficient matrices, and $\varepsilon _ { t }$ is an $( n \times 1 )$ vector of disturbances that have zero mean and are serially uncorrelated. It should be noted that the predictor variables in the right-hand side of each equation in the VAR are the same: lagged values of each time series variable. The equations of a full (unrestricted) VAR(p) model are estimated separately by least squares (Lütkepohl 1993).

## 3.3. Model Specification and Estimation

Selection of a vector autoregressive model involves three basic decisions: (1) variable selection, (2) lag length selection, and (3) model form selection. Because we are specifically interested in the interrelationships between IT components, products, and infrastructure, we naturally chose these three variables $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the corresponding time series) to make up the VAR system. To correct for distributional issues of these variables, we use the natural log of the number of technologies released in each class each month as the unit of analysis. Because a few months in our data set contain zero releases, the raw count data were adjusted by adding 0.5 before taking the natural log. Therefore, the actual time series used in the analysis were $\{ \ln C o m p _ { t } \} \ = \ \{ \ln ( c o m p _ { 1 } + 0 . 5 ) , ~ \ln ( c o m p _ { 2 } \ + ^ { . } 0 . 5 ) , . . . \} ,$ $[ \ln P r o d _ { t } \dot { ] } = \{ \ln ( p r o d _ { 1 } + 0 . 5 ) , ~ \ln ( p r o d _ { 2 } + \bar { 0 } . 5 ) , . . . \} ,$ , and 8ln $I n f r a _ { t } \rangle = \{ \ln ( i n f r a _ { 1 } + 0 . 5 ) , \ln ( i n f r a _ { 2 } + 0 . 5 ) , . . . \} $ , where $c o m p _ { i } , p r o d _ { i } ,$ and $i n f r a _ { i }$ refer to the number of component, product, and infrastructure technologies, respectively, released in month i. As mentioned earlier, the last nine months of the observed data were reserved as an out-of-sample set and, therefore, each series has T = 88 monthly observations.

The common approach for determining the appropriate lag length p of a VAR model is to choose the order such that a measure of forecast precision is minimized (Lütkepohl 1993, Enders 1995). In other words, the optimal lag length maximizes the fit between the observed time series and the estimated predicted process. Forecast mean squared error (MSE) is a standard measure used in lag order selection, and three commonly used statistics for measuring the forecast MSE of a VAR model are the Aikaike information criterion (AIC), the Hannan-Quinn criterion (HQC), and the final prediction error (FPE). In the specification of our VAR model with the three series 8ln Comp 9, 8ln $P r o d _ { t } \} ,$ and 8ln Infra 9, the AIC, HQC, and FPE all agreed that the optimal lag length for the given data set was p = 6 (i.e., 6 months). For detailed discussion of these statistics, see Lütkepohl (1993, §4.3).

Determining the model form entails testing cointegration and stationarity of the VAR system and variables. If either of these are present, a more sophisticated multiple time series model should be used, such as a vector error correction model (VECM) in the case of cointegration. Using the Engle-Granger (1987) procedure, we checked for cointegration for lag orders up to 10 and found no significant cointegration present in the data.<sup>3</sup> This suggests that the standard VAR model is appropriate, and it is not necessary to specify a VECM.

The basic VAR(p) model assumes a stationary (and stable) time series process. If the process is not stationary, de-trending of the data can be performed in several ways, such as differencing the data or including a deterministic trend variable in the VAR model (Lütkepohl 1993, Enders 1995). Because we are interested in the cross-level effects in IS&T, we decided to preserve the magnitude of releases in our model and, therefore, incorporated a deterministic time trend variable in the model instead of differencing the data.<sup>4</sup> In addition, to control for any possible seasonality effects in the time series, we included 11 seasonal dummy variables (a standard approach for monthly data).<sup>5</sup>

The estimates of VAR regression coefficients typically are not as informative as analyzing relationships among variables because of the complicated dynamics inherent in VAR models (Lütkepohl 1993, Enders 1995). For this reason, VAR analysis does not typically discuss magnitudes of coefficients and focuses instead on Granger-causality tests, impulse response functions, and forecast error variance decompositions (Stock and Watson 2001). Therefore, we report the general estimation results in Table 1 (the estimated model coefficients can be found in the appendix, Tables A4–A6) and then provide detailed analysis using the aforementioned techniques in the next section. However, it should be noted that the R<sup>2</sup> values and regression F -statistic suggest reasonable fit of the model, and the Durbin-Watson (D-W) statistic provides initial evidence of a lack of autocorrelation.<sup>6</sup>

Another assumption of the general VAR(p5 model is the whiteness of the residuals of the estimated model (Lütkepohl 1993, Enders 1995). Diagnostic tests showed that autocorrelation, nonnormality, and heteroskadacsticity were not issues with the estimated VAR model. ## 4. Empirical Analysis and Forecasting with the VAR Model

In this section, we first discuss the analysis of our estimated VAR system and demonstrate the significant interrelationships among introductions of new IS&T components, products, and infrastructure. Next, we demonstrate the use of the estimated VAR model for forecasting future technology releases. We conclude by discussing and addressing two potential issues with the interpretation of our results.

## 4.1. Empirical Results

Standard practice for VAR analysis is to report Granger causality test results, impulse response function (IRF) analysis, and forecast error variance decompositions (FEVD) (Lütkepohl 1993, Enders 1995, Stock and Watson 2001). IRF and FEVD together are often called innovation accounting (Enders 1995) because they provide forecast analysis of the effects of shocks to the innovations $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ error terms) in the VAR equations.

4.1.1. Granger Causality Analysis. Granger causality test statistics help determine whether the lagged values of one variable help predict values of another variable in the VAR system (Granger 1969). Specifically, in a VAR(p) system, if a time series (e.g., ln $C o m p _ { t } )$ does not improve the forecasting performance of ln $I n f r a _ { t } ,$ then ln $C o m p _ { t }$ does not Grangercause ln $I n f r a _ { t }$ (Enders 1995). Granger causality is typically tested using Wald tests of the null hypothesis that all coefficients of the corresponding lags are equal to zero (Enders 1995). Typical Granger causality tests are pairwise, looking at the influence of a onevariable series on another one-variable series. However, Lütkepohl (1993) also defines a test in which a K-dimensional VAR process $\mathbf { Y } _ { t }$ is split into an M - dimensional process $\mathbf { Z } _ { t }$ and a (K-M)-dimensional process $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { x } } _ { t } ,$ and Granger causality tests between $\mathbf { Z } _ { t }$ and $\mathbf { X } _ { t }$ are performed. This is only different from standard pairwise tests when $K > 2$ and allows the testing of Granger-causal relationships among subsets of series in the VAR process.

Table 2 provides the p-values of all possible pairwise and subset Granger causality tests for the estimated VAR model and shows that there are several significant Granger-causal relationships in the estimated VAR model. Most noticeable of the pairwise tests are the significant Granger-causal relationships between the ln Comp and the ln $I n f r a _ { { _ t } }$ series. Specifically, ln Comp Granger-causes ln $I n f r a _ { t } ,$ and ln Infra Granger-causes ln Comp . This type of twoway Granger causality provides strong evidence of a feedback relationship between the releases of component and infrastructure technologies in the wireless networking ecosystem. It is also apparent that both ln Comp and ln Infra are significantly influenced by their own past values. Additionally, the ln $P r o d _ { t }$ series Granger-causes the ln $I n f r a _ { t } ,$ highlighting an additional interrelationship among classes of information technology. Interestingly, the ln $P r o d _ { t }$ series seems to develop independently of both the ln Comp and ln Infra series as well as its own past values. The subset Granger causality tests provide further evidence of both the tight interrelationship between ln Comp and ln Infra and also the apparent independence of ln Prod .

4.1.2. Impulse Response Functions. Impulse response functions plot the response of current and future values of the variables in the VAR model to a one-unit increase in the current value of one of the VAR error terms (Stock and Watson 2001, Enders 1995). This assumes that the error returns to zero in subsequent periods and that all of the other errors are zero. IRFs are an alternative means of measuring the influence of one variable in the VAR system on another variable. Figure 4 provides the nine possible impulse response functions for the estimated VAR. Each plot in Figure 4 can be interpreted as showing the corresponding response (i.e., increase or decrease in magnitude) of a variable over time given a one-unit increase of a “shocked” variable at time zero.

Similarly, the magnitude of the effect of ln Infra on ln Comp in the third time period is 0.2, implying a one unit increase in ln Infra at time zero results in a 0.2 unit increase in ln Comp in the third month. It is very important to note here, however, that the magnitudes of these relationships are context specific. In another setting, or using data from another technology ecosystem, we may observe a different set of significant relationships among components, products, and infrastructure with different magnitudes of effect. The present research is intended to first demonstrate the application of the CPI model and the VAR methodology for analyzing interdependencies among supply-side relationships in information technologies, where we use Wi-Fi technology as a case for the proof of concept.

4.1.3. Forecast Error Variance Decomposition. Forecast error variance decomposition (FEVD) analysis provides the proportion of movements in a time series because of shocks of both the time series variable and other variables (Enders 1995). More specifically, the FEVD tells us the percentage of the variance of the error made in forecasting a variable because of specific shocks of all the variables in the system at a specified time horizon (Stock and Watson 2001). In other words, the FEVD is like a partial R<sup>2</sup> for the forecast error by forecast horizon. Whereas Granger causality tests and impulse response functions give us a sense of the significance of interactions among variables in the VAR system, FEVD provides some sense of the magnitude of effect by specifying the percentage of forecast error variance accounted for by each variable in the system.

Figure 5 provides a graphical representation of the FEVD, where each graph depicts the proportions of forecast error variance, up to 12 periods (one year) ahead, accounted for by shocks in each of the variables (Lütkepohl 1993). As would be expected, each variable accounts for the largest proportion of its own forecast error variance. Approximately 20% of the ln Comp error variance is accounted for by shocks in ln Prod and ln Infra, with the large majority from ln Infra. Approximately 40% of the error variance in ln Infra is accounted for by the other two variables, about 20% each. About 25% of error variance ln Prod is accounted for by ln Comp and ln Infra.

4.1.4. Interpretation and Analysis of Granger Causality, IRF, and FEVD Results. A few key interpretations can be formulated from results presented above. First, there appears to be a strong relationship between introductions of new component and infrastructure technologies. The significant Granger-causal relationships in both directions between components and infrastructure suggest this. Considering the characteristics of the wireless networking context, one potential explanation of why we would expect to see this result is that, by design, wireless networks require compatible components and infrastructure. Referring to the IRF plots (Figure 4) for ln Comp → ln Infra, it does appear that all significant shocks are in the positive direction, implying the number of component releases is related to the number of infrastructure releases down the road. The dynamics in the opposite direction are a bit more complicated. The number of infrastructure technologies appears to have both negative and positive relationship with the number of components depending on the lag length and forecast horizon. However, when multiple lags are taken into consideration, the analysis highlights the complexities of the interrelationship between infrastructure and components. This might suggest that the direction of impact that infrastructure has on components varies within the optimal lag window chosen for the entire VAR model. Furthermore, these results suggest an interesting theory that the timing of infrastructure releases in the wireless networking context can significantly impact the supply-side forces for wireless networking components. Considering the inherent generational nature of the 802.11 standard, it might be reasonable to expect that technology producers time supply-side decisions about technology releases based on their predictions about new 802.11 generations. This may explain the time-varying effects witnessed.

Products appear to have a significant positive impact on the number of infrastructure technologies released, but the relationship is not reciprocal. This suggests that as more products are released, correspondingly more infrastructure technologies are developed to support these products. For example, as more electronic devices, such as iPhones, gained Wi-Fi capabilities, new routers and access points were introduced that handle higher load capacity and offer better encryption and multimedia capabilities. However, our analysis did not show any significant Grangercausal relationships between components and products or a significant influence of infrastructure on products.

An intuitive theory can be inferred from these results because in this context of wireless networking technologies, many of the components are add-on type technologies or adapters that upgrade the capabilities of older product technologies. Additionally, the 802.11 standards for wireless networking are backwards compatible, meaning that older devices will work with newer standards. Therefore, it could be that these adapters act as a bridge for older products (e.g., Dai and Kauffman 2002) and reduce the need for new products with wireless capabilities beyond those provided by these adapters or the third-party wireless components used in product designs. Furthermore, most product technologies can be utilized in many contexts outside of wireless networking. In other words, wireless networking is only one of many features for most of these products (e.g., mobile phones, laptops, or video game systems). Thus, supply-side introductions of new product technologies might be driven more by influences from technology classes in other industries than by the influences among wireless components and infrastructure. In this data, unfortunately, we are not able to explicitly account for cross-industry effects, a limitation we discuss in more detail in §6.

We should also note here, however, that the lack of significant relationships with relation to product technologies, especially the lack of product-to-product interactions, may also be because of issues with our empirical model and data classification. Intuitively, one would expect that growth in the number of new components and infrastructure would correspond to growth in new product technologies. For example, as network infrastructure improves, we might expect new product devices to be introduced that can take advantage of higher access speeds and bandwidth. The number of product certifications awarded by the Wi-Fi Alliance is much lower than component and infrastructure certifications, especially in the first half of the time series. This is largely because the Wi-Fi

Alliance tends to award certifications at the product series level rather than for each individual model (e.g., one certification may be awarded for the entire Dell Inspiron 13<sup>00</sup> laptop series because they all use the same Wi-Fi client and component configuration). This may result in an undercounting of the real product technology introductions and, thus, impact the magnitude of the relationships identified in our empirical analysis. The goal of our empirical analysis is to measure the dynamics, and more importantly the directionality, of impacts, which is captured by the VAR model. Therefore, although an undercounting of actual product releases may be an issue with the available data that may impact interpretation of the magnitude of the observed effects, it is reasonable to trust the directionality and significance of the relationships identified. We discuss this further in §4.3, where we present some modifications to our VAR estimation to address these issues.

In general, the results of our VAR analysis demonstrate that significant cross-level effects are present in the wireless networking context. These results provide strong evidence that the interactions defined in the CPI framework provide a useful mechanism for analysis of change in Wi-Fi technologies. However, it should be noted that the results presented here are specific to the wireless networking context and not necessarily generalizable to other information technology domains. In an alternative context (e.g., mobile telephony), we might expect to find a different set of significant Granger-causal relationships that are specific to that given ecosystem. The CPI framework outlines all of the potential interrelationships among components, products, and infrastructure, and the VAR methodology is a useful technique for uncovering the specific relationships at work in a specific context.

## 4.2. Forecasting Analysis with Out-of-Sample Data

Forecasting is one of the primary objectives of multiple time series analysis (Lütkepohl 1993), and our VAR model can provide useful forecasts of component, product, and infrastructure technology introductions. Forecasts produced from a VAR model are based on conditional expectations assuming independent white noise and are made starting from period t through the forecast horizon of h steps. Therefore, a forecast consists of a point estimate along with a confidence interval based on forecast errors. Forecast errors have zero mean and, thus, the forecasts are unbiased (Lütkepohl 1993). Point estimates are determined based on the estimated VAR model, including the corresponding values of the deterministic variables (i.e., seasonal dummies and deterministic trend) and 95% confidence intervals of the point estimate that incorporate the forecast error.

Figure 6 presents plots of the actual ln Comp, ln Infra, and ln Prod time series and the corresponding predicted time series for each variable based on the estimated VAR model. Recall that the last nine monthly observations were omitted from the sample used to estimate the VAR model, specifically left for an out-of-sample test of the forecast accuracy. Following the recommendations of Lütkepohl (1993), we compared the predicted values for the final nine months versus the actual values and observed that the 95% confidence intervals for each point estimate encapsulate the actual values in all cases with the exception of one data point in the ln Comp series (which is a statistical outlier). Additionally, a visual inspection of the forecasted series in comparison to the actual data reveals a close tracking and consistent directional movement for most points in the out-ofsample region.

Second, based on the Granger causality analysis presented previously, we know that infrastructure is significantly impacted by both components and products. Therefore, including them in the infrastructure forecast may account for significant improvements to the model and, thus, forecasting accuracy. Although products were not found to have any significant Granger-causal influences, it had significant room to improve, in terms of forecast accuracy.

It should also be noted that although explanatory models (i.e., standard regression models) improve in performance (e.g., as measured by $R ^ { 2 }$ or similar metrics) with the inclusion of additional parameters, it is common in predictive modeling for simpler models with fewer parameters to outperform more complex models in terms of forecast accuracy (Enders 1995). In other words, for a predictive model the inclusion of more parameters can actually hinder the predictive performance. Coefficient uncertainty increases as models become more complex and this error is extrapolated into the forecast predictions (Enders 1995, Kennedy 2003).<sup>8</sup> Therefore, considering that the VAR model is a substantially more complex model than is the individual ARMA models in terms of the number of parameter estimates, it can be strongly concluded that incorporating the interrelationships among component, product, and infrastructure supply-side forces provides an improvement in forecasting technology introductions.

The accuracy of the forecasts produced by the estimated VAR model suggests two important conclusions for the present analysis. First, the CPI framework of cross-level interactions is a viable conceptual model for performing technology forecasts, and it provides substantial advantages over observing introductions of component, product, and infrastructure technologies separately. Second, the specified model for the VAR analysis is both a reasonably accurate as well as informative representation of the underlying data generation process for the supplyside forces governing the release of new IS&T components, products, and infrastructure in the wireless networking context.

## 4.3. Addressing Potential VAR Model Issues

It is necessary to discuss and address two potential issues that may impact the interpretation of our empirical results. The first is the potential impact of multicollinearity, which is a common problem with vector autoregressions. The second lies in the classification of data in the CPI framework.

4.3.1. Dealing with Multicollinearity. Multicollinearity is a common problem in vector autoregressions because of the inherent endogeneity of the technique and the use of lagged variables in the model (Litterman 1979, Gilbert 1995, Schlegel 1985). The biggest concern with multicollinearity in vector autoregression models is that it can significantly impact the accuracy of forecasts and potentially mask Granger-causal relationships because of correlations among independent variables. Multicollinearity is a data issue, and a rigorous test for multicollinearity is to compute the condition index (or condition number) of the data matrix. The condition number is the square root of the ratio of the largest and smallest eigenvalues of the square matrix X’X (Belsley et al. 2004, Kennedy 2003), where X is the data matrix of independent variables under question in our estimation (in this case ln Comp lags 1–6, ln Prod lags 1–6, and ln Infra lags 1–6). Because each equation of the VAR estimation utilizes the same data matrix, we only need to calculate the condition index once for the full model. A condition index greater than 30 indicates strong collinearity (Kennedy 2003), and the condition index for our data matrix is 29.5.

Kennedy (2003) suggests two options for dealing with multicollinearity. First, you can do nothing and accept the potential limitations that come with collinear data; this is often the approach taken with VAR estimation as evidenced by the significant lack of multicollinearity discussion both in the classical VAR references and in papers using VAR techniques. Alternatively, one can modify the estimated model to reduce collinearity among data variables by adding more data; including new variables; or, in the case of vector autoregressions, using subset restrictions on the model parameters. Subset restrictions are applied prior to estimation to determine which VAR parameters should be included and excluded from the model by either maximizing some information criterion (such as AIC, HQC, or FPE, as discussed previously) or by using a Bayesian approach that incorporates some prior distribution information (Lütkepohl 1993). The result is a restricted model in which the data matrix is not necessarily identical for each equation in the VAR. Using AIC and HQC, we determined and estimated a restricted VAR model, and the condition indices for the resulting data matrices of each equation were well below the collinearity threshold: 9.5 for the products equation, 12.4 for the components equation, and 11.65 for the infrastructure equation. To see if our results changed substantially, Granger causality and forecasts were calculated for the restricted model and are presented in Tables 4 and 5.

4.3.2. Data Classification. A second potential issue that may impact the interpretation of our empirical results relates to the classification of our data set into the CPI framework. The issue comes from the application of the notions of components and products to the data of wireless networking certifications. Infrastructure technologies are clearly delineated from other technologies in the wireless networking context. However, the distinction among product and component certifications is not as clear. Furthermore, the number of product certifications is small relative to components and infrastructure, especially in the first half of the time series. The Wi-Fi Alliance categorizes the technology releases into the categories outlined in Table A1 in the appendix, and we assigned these categories to components, products, and infrastructure based on the description provided by the Wi-Fi Alliance.

Accurate classification of technologies into the component, product, and infrastructure classes will be context specific, and there are some potential issues specific to the wireless networking context that may be impacting our results. First, wireless networking was introduced primarily as an add-on technology, and only after some initial adoption did laptops and PCs start incorporating wireless chipsets internally. This may account for the relatively low number of product introductions in the first half of the time series, but note that products make up a much larger proportion of all certifications in the second half of the series (see Figure 2). A second potential contextspecific issue is that the certification process used by the Wi-Fi Alliance may be dampening the true number of products released. Certification of a product technology by the Wi-Fi Alliance is typically done at the series level, where multiple product models in the same series would share a certification. In these cases, it is arguable whether the total number of new products with wireless capabilities should be counted as individual models or at a higher product class level. Third, a lower number of product certifications may stem from the fact that some products may use “plugand-play” type wireless components, where no additional product innovation is needed to enable wireless capabilities in the product. Because the Wi-Fi Alliance product certification is not a certification of the wireless component contained in a product, but instead a certification of client software and additional hardware in the product that works with the wireless component, it might not be necessary for all new products with built-in Wi-Fi components to pursue additional certifications beyond those awarded to the components. Therefore, it is possible that some overlap exists between the product and component constructs with respect to the certifications used to measure wireless networking technologies. Many of the products may contain wireless networking components, and many components are sold as stand alone add-ons for existing products; it is not explicitly clear what aspect of each technology is being certified or to which construct each technology should belong.

To explore this issue, we modified our technology classification scheme by combining the components and products into a single construct (i.e., a series that counts the sum of the previous two series) and reestimating our model as a two-variable vector autoregression (i.e, ln Prod Comp, ln Infra) with six lags per variable, seasonal dummy variables, a time trend variable, and subset restrictions to remove collinearity. The model specification was determined in the same fashion as our original full model (see the appendix for details). As above, we are most interested in evaluating the impact this rethinking of the model has on the core empirical results of this research: Granger causality relationships and technology forecasting. Tables 6 and 7 present the results of these two key measures.

The results presented in Tables 6 and 7 show that significant Granger-causal relationships exist between the combined component/product class and the infrastructure class of wireless networking technologies. Furthermore, the forecast performance is slightly improved over the previous CPI classification and is substantially better than the baseline performance of forecasts that used each series independently. These results suggest that in the context of wireless networking technologies, combining the previously coded components and products into one class is a viable way to represent cross-level interactions. The addition of products to the component class does not significantly change the relationships between components and infrastructure that were observed in our original, three-variable model. However, we should note here that an important implication of this analysis is that the significant Granger-causal relationship among products and infrastructure would be missed in this new classification. These relationships highlight a different interaction present in the wireless networking context that cannot be explained by the relationship between components and infrastructure. Including products in the model provides additional insights on how the nature of add-on adapters and backwards compatibility might impact cross-level supply-side interactions (discussed in §4.1.4), which provides a good reasoning for following the original CPI framework.

## 5. Discussion

We have shown, through detailed empirical analysis, that it is possible to identify and analyze crosslevel interactions among supply-side introductions of new wireless networking technologies. These results capture the idiosyncrasies of the wireless networking context and yet are intuitive to understand and interpret as well. In addition, the results provide an exposition of how the CPI framework and VAR methodology can be applied to formally and practically analyze supply-side dynamics of technological change in general.

## 5.1. Implications for Practice

The results of this research provide insights into the nature of supply-side dynamics and technology introductions in the IS&T landscape. This paper outlines an empirical framework on which analytical tools for technology forecasting and analysis can be built. Practitioners are in need of new tools and frameworks for reducing the complexity of the IS&T landscape to aid in decision making for technology investment and product development (Adomavicius et al. 2008). The research presented in this paper has demonstrated a general result that it is possible to identify strength, direction, and timing of interactions among the component, product, and infrastructure classes of information technology. These types of results provide information for improving technology forecasts and planning.

Generally, practitioners in the information technology industry can benefit from this type of research in several ways. From a technology adopter’s perspective, improved forecasts, and more generally an improved understanding of the dynamics of IT component, product, and infrastructure development, will help firms make more informed and confident technology adoption decisions. From a technology producer’s standpoint, firms that produce technology components, products, and/or infrastructure can use the CPI model with the VAR empirical methodology to analyze their respective IS&T landscape to improve new product introductions and product development initiatives. Suppliers and innovators can better choose when to release new technologies and where to invest in new developments. In general, tools that help practitioners understand technological change and the complexities of the technological environment will improve IS&T-related decision making and management.

With respect to the particular technology context analyzed in this paper (i.e., Wi-Fi technologies), there are several specific insights that can be gained from a practitioner’s perspective. Considering our additional analysis to control for multicollinearity and classification issues, we believe the interactions observed are both intuitive to understand and representative of the supply-side dynamics at work in the wireless networking context. Four specific characteristics of the wireless networking context provide useful theoretical explanations for the effects we observe in our analysis. First, wireless networking technologies are backwards compatible, which might explain the lack of cross-level influence between components and products as well as between infrastructure and products. Additionally, because many of the wireless components act as add-ons or adapters, they may provide a “bridge” between older product generations and newer generations, thus limiting the corresponding cross-level effects. Second, because by design both components and infrastructure are necessary for the creation of working wireless networks, it seems reasonable to expect the strong interactions observed among these classes. Third, wireless networking technologies have been released in generations (e.g., 802.11 b, g, n), and this might serve as an explanation for the time-varying effects across classes. Additionally, this may explain some of the negative effects witnessed between infrastructure and components because technology producers may limit technology releases when a new generation is planned. Fourth, many of the products in our data set have a primary usage function in another context (e.g., mobile telephony, video gaming), suggesting that external factors from other industries could be driving developments in products more so than the interactions from the wireless networking industry (i.e., cross-industry effects). This might be an additional explanation for the lack of significant observed effects on products. We believe that including these points about the characteristics of the specific research context under study improves the interpretation of our results. We also note that a major goal of this research was to demonstrate the usefulness of the CPI framework and VAR methodology for the empirical identification of cross-level interdependencies, and we expect that analysis of different technology contexts would result in the discovery of different patterns of cross-level interactions.

## 5.2. Implications for Theory and Research

There are several key contributions from this research. First and foremost, this paper provides strong empirical evidence to support the validity and usefulness of the CPI conceptual model. By utilizing the VAR methodology to analyze cross-level effects in the CPI framework, we go well beyond the qualitative and ad hoc heuristic-based techniques used in prior research for evaluating technological change. VAR provides a statistically rigorous yet atheoretical approach that is well suited for theory development and testing (Stock and Watson 2001); this allowed us to provide formal empirical estimation and forecasting capabilities for the theoretical CPI model incorporating the concept of cross-level interactions. Using Granger causality tests, impulse response function estimations, and forecast error variance decompositions we were also able to delve deeper into the interrelationships involved in technological change. Additionally, we demonstrated a substantial improvement in forecasting of technology releases by incorporating the dynamics of cross-level relationships. Prior research has primarily focused on technological change from the point of view of a singular technology. This work promotes the importance of taking a systems view of technological change, and it demonstrates the gains in forecasting and understanding that can be achieved by utilizing the CPI model.

Although VAR is a commonly used technique in economics and other social sciences, to the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first papers in the IS discipline to utilize the VAR methodology. In this paper we demonstrate how the VAR methodology allows a researcher to test the dynamics of temporal relationships among variables without strong theoretical restrictions typically used in simultaneous equation econometric methods. In addition, VAR provides a multiple time series framework for effective forecasting. This freedom provides the researcher with a statistically rigorous and effective framework for the development and testing of theory (Stock and Watson 2001, Backus 1986). We believe there are several areas in which the VAR methodology may prove useful in the IS discipline. For example, VAR is often used in the analysis of monetary and government policy and could similarly be applied to the analysis of IT governance, spending, and pricing policies. VAR has also been used to evaluate the efficacy of anti-terrorism policies and, thus, could be applied in the information security context to analyze the relationships among attacks and security policy variables. Furthermore, with the growing use of data from multiple online sources (i.e., blogs, social networks, etc.), VAR may prove a useful methodology for analyzing interdependent relationships among trends or themes in the information created and consumed online. In general, any IS context in which the researcher wishes to capture the interdependencies and evolution of multiple time series (e.g., prices, purchases, reviews, system failures, etc.) would be appropriate for the application of VAR. We hope that more IS researchers will add VAR and other time series econometric approaches to their analytical tool kit.

Finally, this research examines how technological developments in one technology class impact development in another class, which is an interesting and underexplored aspect of research on technological change. The complexities of modern information systems and technologies will only increase as they become more interconnected and interdependent. More research focused on the dynamics of crosslevel effects in the development of basic technological artifacts will only help improve our understanding of the role of technology in organizations. This paper demonstrates some of the first empirical research addressing this issue and provides a platform on which further research can be built.

## 6. Limitations and Extensions

There are several limitations of the research presented in this paper that must be identified. First, the reader must be careful not to interpret causal inferences from the results of our empirical analysis. Although we found evidence of significant Grangercausal relationships among several variables in the model, Granger causality does not imply true causality. Granger causality implies that prior values of one variable provide information that helps improve predictions of later values of another variable. Even if both variables are driven by a third process not captured in the lag-structure of the VAR, a significant Granger-causal relationship between the two variables could still be witnessed.

A second potential limitation of this study relates to our data. The data we use in our model represents certifications of new wireless network technology introductions, which do not include performance information or information on actual adoption and usage patterns by organizations and consumers. Therefore, the interpretation of the results of this paper is limited to the supply-side dynamics of new technology introduction in the wireless networking context. It is also possible that there were several other wireless technology developments during the time frame of our sample that never became certified, either because of the failure of a technology or because the producer opted out of the certification process. The classification of products, components, and infrastructure is context specific and, in the case of this research, dependent on the certification process implemented by the Wi-Fi Alliance. As noted previously, there may be an undercounting of new product technologies because of the Wi-Fi Alliance certification process, which could impact the magnitude of the measured product numbers; however, we do note that the underlying dynamics and directionality of the relationships among products, components, and infrastructure should be captured by the VAR estimation. In particular, in our restricted VAR model that controlled for some specific data-related issues (such as multicollinearity), we found a significant ln Prod → ln Prod relationship as well as an ln Prod → ln Infra relationship. In summary, we believe context specific issues with technology classification should be expected, as witnessed with products in our analysis. We also note here that it is very difficult to obtain comprehensive data on every technology introduction across an entire industry. The Wi-Fi Alliance is the only certifying body for interoperability for wireless networking technologies, which leads us to believe that the certifications data used in this study serve as a reasonable proxy for supply-side technology introductions.

A potential extension of this research is to explore potential cross-industry technology interactions, for example, relationships between software and hardware industries, wireless networking and PC industries, or consumer electronics and telecommunication industries. We acknowledge that our data set is limited in its reach across industries; however, wireless networking has become a ubiquitous feature in most hardware devices and, therefore, some cross-industry relationships likely are embedded in our analysis, although not explicitly accounted for (i.e., our data set includes certifications of $\mathrm { P C } s ,$ mobile phones, video game consoles, and home audio equipment; however, we do not explicitly analyze cross-industry relationships). We hope to pursue this interesting area of research in our future work and continue to develop a deeper understanding of the general information technology ecosystem.

A second potential extension of this research is a detailed exploration of the role of standards in the supply-side dynamics of technology introductions. The empirical analysis presented in this paper has exhibited the usefulness of the CPI model and VAR methodology to identify the existence of cross-level interdependencies among technology classes. In this paper we focused on the wireless domain, and our analysis suggested that the 802.11 standard and the backwards compatibility among its versions may play a role in these interdependencies. This is certainly worth further exploration; however, a detailed analysis of the role of standards in the evolution of the wireless networking ecosystem would require the identification of all standards that could potentially influence wireless technologies (e.g., security and encryption, multimedia, data protocols, computing, etc.). Although such an exploration is outside the scope of the present research, this constitutes an interesting direction for future work.

We would also like to comment on the generalizability of the specific results obtained from the wireless networking technology case analysis. We note that the specific relationships observed in this case might not necessarily be observed in the analysis of any other IS&T context. Instead, we have used the wireless networking technology context to demonstrate the validity of the CPI model and demonstrate its application with VAR for technological forecasting. We would expect different CPI relationships to be present (or not present) in other contexts, depending on the idiosyncrasies of the specific technological domain. This paper shows how applying VAR to the CPI model can uncover and statistically validate specific interrelationships in a given technological context.

Finally, it should also be noted that although VAR analysis has become a widely used and generally accepted econometric technique, it is not without its criticisms (see Kennedy 2003, pp. 330–331, for a brief overview of both benefits and criticisms). Cooley and LeRoy (1985) provide an often-cited critique of VAR, and Runkle (1987) discusses some of the controversy over using VAR. Specifically, the key criticisms are that (1) policy evaluation is not usefully conducted with VAR because of the lack of interpretability of coefficients and the abstract nature of innovation accounting and (2) VAR results are often not robust in the number of variables, lags, and data frequency (Kennedy 2003). We recognize this debate and the potential issues raised by VAR opponents.

We note, however, that our analysis has used VAR for general analysis and basic forecasting, both of which appear to be widely acceptable applications of the methodology. Furthermore, multicollinearity is often an issue with VAR estimations because by design VARs incorporate endogenous relationships among all variables in the model (e.g., Litterman 1979), but after controlling for this potential issue we saw practically no change in our results.
