# Searching for management information systems: coherence and change in the discipline

Tor J Larsen\* & Linda Levine<sup>†1</sup>

\*Department of Leadership and Organizational Management, Norwegian School of Management, Nydalsveien 37, 0442 Oslo, Norway, email: tor.j.larsen@bi.no, and <sup>†</sup>Software Engineering Institute, Carnegie Mellon University, 4500 Fifth Avenue, Pittsburgh, PA 15213- 3890, USA, email: ll@sei.cmu.edu

Abstract. The now familiar and longstanding discussion on the status of the field of management information systems (MIS) consists of at least two themes – the lack of coherence in MIS and the question of rigour vs. relevance (academic vs. practical concerns). The research questions we pose here ask: what themes or ideas represent the centre of MIS or its zones of coherence – or is diversity and fragmentation the rule? and will the centre or zones change over time? Within MIS research, is there evidence of theory building that contributes to a cumulative research tradition? Using a co-word analysis approach – to analyse the patterns in discourse by measuring the association strengths of terms representative of relevant publications – the researchers found 62 specific centres of coherence. The data documented a high degree of change in centres of coherence over time. Evidence of theory building was extremely weak. A cumulative research tradition remains elusive. MIS centres of coherence change over time – we think, partly in response to practical pressures. We suggest that MIS opens a richer and more difficult debate on its theory, practice, and identity as a discipline in the 21st century university.

Keywords: co-word analysis, management information systems, meta-analysis, paradigm, theory building

## INTRODUCTION

No end appears to be in sight for the now familiar and longstanding discussions on the status of the scientific field of management information systems (MIS)<sup>2</sup> – its identity and its value, with respect to its role as a field, within the university, and in relation to industry practice (e.g. Weber, 1987; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Alavi & Carlson, 1992; Benbasat & Weber, 1996; Robey, 1996; Briggs et al., 1999–2000; Westfall, 1999; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Paul, 2002; Avison, 2003; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Weber, 2003; Carr, 2003; Ives et al., 2004; Lyytinen & King, 2004). This ongoing debate and relentless self-examination appears to be made up of at least two themes. The first focuses on coherence in MIS and in framing questions such as the following: Does MIS have a core and overarching theory? A cumulative tradition? Are other disciplines referencing MIS (e.g. Davis, 2000; Walstrom & Hardgrave, 2001; Baskerville & Myers, 2002; Vessey et al., 2002; Pfeffers & Ya, 2003)? The second theme revolves around the question of rigour vs. relevance, which is also occasionally expressed as a debate between academic and practical concerns – where a further refinement focuses on degrees of purity in the use of research methods (e.g. Robey & Markus, 1998; Benbasat & Zmud, 1999; Nurminen & Eriksson, 1999; Goles & Hirscheim, 2000; Hirschheim & Klein, 2003).

While such concerns are valid, we suggest that the MIS identity crisis is not a unique or isolated phenomenon but part of more widespread anxiety about the nature and role of university education and disciplinary knowledge (e.g. Corman & Poole, 2000; Scandura & Williams, 2000; Mohrman et al., 2001; Rynes et al., 2001; Frickel & Gross, 2005). Pressures continue to be exerted from at least three significant sources: cross-disciplinary activity, university-industry centres for applied research, and programme development in distance education. Crossdisciplinary programmes and centres have tantalized us with opportunities for integration but, in reality, too often served as a quick fixes or patches to tired curricula. Industry centres in universities provide a transfusion of funding for research and offer up real-world problems for students to work on but simultaneously raise questions about how universities can retain objective and unbiased research agendas using this model. Distance education has effectively removed physical barriers but simultaneously dislodged the primacy of the physical place of learning, raising unprecedented questions about where and how learning occurs.

To date, throughout these transformations, a vision for university education beyond a catalogue mentality focused on the ‘content’ of disciplines has eluded us. These issues, felt to a greater and lesser degree across many departments and disciplines, have resulted in uncertainty across the curriculum. In MIS, these tensions are further exacerbated by continuous and rapid technology change and by a widely perceived gulf between academia and practice.

Like their business counterparts, universities are struggling to define 21st century practice. The university environment, perhaps more than any other institution in a knowledge-based economy, is obliged to seek and construct alternatives to mechanistic industrial models that appear to be breaking down. DeGeus, (1997) writes: ‘The need for control is so fundamental that it has dominated management literature for 100 years. Books abound on financial control systems and on organizational theory for effective managerial control . . .’ [But] ‘to manage a corporation effectively, we must learn how to treat institutions as living ecosystems – set up with the recognition that they will live or die according to the same natural laws that govern human growth and development’ (pp. 141, 128).

Could it be, then, that some aspects of the fragmentation that we perceive in MIS, or that we experience as a lack of coherence, are functions of a larger loss of order and unity, emanating from ageing, brittle models of academic institutions? These eroding forms govern our current understanding of disciplines and the university itself – the house of learning for bodies of knowledge. Reinventing the university and disciplinary knowledge challenges us to look at organizations as ecosystems, rather than as edifices. By doing so, we open the door to seeing the university institution, not as a massive filing cabinet or catalogue of content, but through alternative metaphors for networks and systems of systems. Our research takes a step towards realizing this perspective: (1) acknowledging the exhaustion of current institutional models (2) cautioning against a melodrama that ‘the sky is falling’ in MIS, and (3) urging more empirical investigation of disciplines as adaptive systems.

In MIS, we believe that when researchers and practitioners talk about ‘rigour vs. relevance’ they are actually comparing the use of research methods and theory for academic goals with useful contributions to practice. However, in applied research, practice is not divorced from theory, but a real-world test function for theory. Consistency is necessary for both theory and practice; if each individual circumstance is treated as a situational, there is no mechanism for making comparisons and learning. Ideally, research results contribute to theory and practice by indicating where the theory is adequate or inadequate and where practice is more or less successful. This means that the separation often made between theory and practice is problematic – it is simplistic and reductive. It follows that a dichotomy between rigour (theory and methods) and relevance (practice) is equally problematic.<sup>3</sup> Consistency in the selection and use of methods is a prerequisite for replication studies, syntheses and meta-analyses. However, an exclusive focus on methods may neglect implications for practice. All of these considerations lead us to believe that evidence of theory building is an appropriate lens for analysing evidence of coherence and a cumulative research tradition within MIS.

Ongoing discussion on the discipline of MIS has focused on the nature of its core and sub areas. One approach distinguishes between the internalist view (Westin et al., 1994) and the externalist view (Whitley, 1984). The internalist view builds on Kuhn’s (1970) notion of a dom inant paradigm in the tradition of normal science, which is interspersed with periods of revolution. This dominant paradigm takes the form of an overarching theory, which is subscribed to by a research community. The externalist view treats a discipline as a ‘complex network of interacting researchers whose ideas may stem from a number of disciplines who therefore form an intellectual community’ (Vessey et al., 2002, p. 132). Banville & Landry (1989) and Landry & Banville (1992) characterize this network as a ‘fragmented adhocracy’. Another example of the externalist view is expressed through Porra’s (1999) notion of ‘colonial systems’. Vessey et al. (2002) demonstrate that there is considerable diversity in MIS research in the topics addressed, research approaches employed, reference disciplines used, and levels of analysis. Their findings dispute the internalist view.

Benbasat & Zmud (2003) argue that a definition of the information system (IS) artefact can serve as the platform for defining appropriate MIS research. Ives et al. (2004) present a strong counter argument. They advocate the field of MIS research is best seen as a ‘colonial system’ where colonies have strong inner ties but loose outer connections. They assert that the glue in MIS is a common interest in information technology (IT) and IS. Similarly, King (1993) describes the discipline of MIS as driven by ‘. . . a shared interest in a phenomenal event – the rise and consequences of radical improvement in information technology’. . . . and any, ‘attempt to build a longstanding academic field on a phenomenon, especially a revolutionary phenomenon, will fail’ (p. 293). As Fitzgerald (2003) aptly observes: ‘In IS, we stand with our backs to the technology, the computer, the machine or whatever, and look outward towards the world at large’ (p. 227).

We have come to appreciate Winnie the Pooh’s dilemma – he has to choose between two desirable options. We agree with our colleagues in MIS who argue that ‘letting a thousand flowers bloom’ is a necessary prerequisite for developing theories and expanding our knowledge. The continuous surge of new technologies and fads also contributes to this abundance. Fitzgerald describes a common ground which is laudable but has yet to be realized. The current state of a thousand flowers does not absolve us of the need to understand the composition of MIS and its areas of work. Vessey et al. (2002) address this by considering the role of diversity in MIS. We extend the inquiry to investigate the centre of MIS or its zones of coherence as they change over time.

To sum up, our study takes a step towards looking at MIS research content in its associations – it represents a forward-pointed investigation based on looking backwards at previously published research. We frame two key questions:

First, what comprises the centre of MIS or its zones of coherence<sup>4</sup> – or is diversity and fragmentation the rule? And, will the centre or zones change over time? Second, within MIS research, is there evidence of theory building that contributes to a cumulative research tradition?

The article proceeds to cover Background and propositions, Method, Analysis, Discussion and Conclusions.

## BACKGROUND AND PROPOSITIONS

Our starting point for considering the centre of MIS or its zones of coherence builds on the discussion of MIS as a field in its own right. Kuhn (1970) argues that a scientific field is distinct (from other sciences) when a high level of consensus about a paradigm, shared theoretical structures, and commonly applied methodological approaches can be found. The terminology associated with scientific fields includes: area, community, discipline and field. Field is the term we use most frequently in this article.

A paradigm can be more precisely described as having four basic components: (1) an exemplar, or piece of work that stands as a model for those who work within the paradigm; (2) an image of the subject matter; (3) theories; (4) methods and instruments (Ritzer, 1975). Pfeffer (1993) suggests that the level of paradigm development in a field affects 14 outcomes including social ties, department issues, faculty and publication. Although these prerequisites have not been systematically addressed, researchers concur that a common set of theories derived from a paradigmatic platform does not exist in MIS (Benbasat & Weber, 1996; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Weber, 2003).

We infer that MIS is in a pre-paradigm position. Additionally, the concept of paradigm as Kuhn (1970) defines it builds on research in the physical sciences. Such a definition may not serve as well in the social sciences where pluralistic models are more appropriate as the basis for understanding and analysis (Banville & Landry, 1989). More recently, Frickel & Gross (2005) focus on scientific/intellectual movements (SIM). They describe key characteristics of a SIM and three are of special note. First, a number of SIMs may exist under a larger umbrella, such as a field. Second, SIMs are inherently political. And third, the intellectual aim and scope of a SIM may urge the revival of past ideas to counter what is perceived as pernicious current tendencies. Given that MIS is a social science, multiple theories, approaches and SIMs compete for attention and status in MIS (Weber, 1987; Benbasat & Weber, 1996; Robey, 1996; Davis, 2000; Avison et al., 2001). Our main contention is therefore that diversity and fragmentation in MIS research results in many foci (centres of coherence) that change over time.

Consequently, in MIS, one would not expect to find a single set of ideas reflecting theoretical unity. More likely, publications within MIS would cover a range of themes and theories (Alavi & Carlson, 1992; Vessey et al., 2002). Indeed, Baskerville & Myers (2002) declare (Table. 1, p. 4) that research contributions in MIS include five bodies of knowledge somewhat unique to MIS, and within each of these, multiple concepts, theories, processes and applications have been developed. Although the field is not unified, it contains active subdisciplines (Benbasat & Weber, 1996; Robey, 1996; Baskerville & Myers, 2002; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Weber, 2003). These publications refer to a ‘range of themes and theories’, ‘bodies of knowledge’ and ‘active subdisciplines’. They indicate that one expects to find areas of activity – each exhibiting some degree of internal consistency and difference relative to others. We agree with this view; however, we cannot say reliably what constitutes and defines an area and its borders. Common sense is not always common and is worthy of empirical investigation. For example, do the notions of themes, concepts, theories and subdisciplines serve as delineators? Due to this uncertainty, in this paper, we speak of ‘centres’ or ‘zones of coherence’ to denote the concept of foci in MIS. Our first proposition reads:

## P1.1: Within MIS, many centres of coherence exist

Many scholars recognize MIS as a field in its own right even though a common overarching theory<sup>5</sup> does not exist. Members of a scientific community do share the use of common vocabulary. We anticipate that generic terms such as information, IS, IT and information management serve as such linguistic glue. Methods terminology and issues are also likely to be a common concern.

P1.2: Methods issues and generic terms for the field of MIS will form the strongest (but not most substantial) centre of coherence

Could it be that practice often overshadows theory? IT developments strongly influence MIS. Therefore, state-of-the-art IS (e.g. ERP, EDI and EIS) and IT (e.g. the Internet and the Unified Modelling Language) may form the basis for centres of coherence. Also, centres of coherence representing subdisciplines (e.g. strategy, systems development and business process reengineering) may appear more distinctly than centres representing theories (e.g. behavioural theory, cognitive psychology and organizational communication). This leads us to the following:

P1.3: Centres of coherence representing IS/IT and subdisciplines will appear more distinctly than centres of coherence representing theories

We expect a high degree of volatility with respect to centres of coherence. Because the field of MIS is constantly changing its focus, we expect that change is more likely than constancy. Ives et al. (2004) corroborates this, emphasizing the ‘importance of evolutionary models as lenses through which we view the history of our own field’ (p. 119).

P1.4: Centres of coherence will exhibit a high degree of change over time

We have asserted that diversity and fragmentation in MIS results in many foci that change over time. This suggests that consistent efforts to build theory will be the exception. Typically, an article will make a limited attempt to position itself with respect to previous published research, e.g. survey of related literature. More extensively, theory building includes: replications of studies, extension of an existing model or theory, testing of theory in different settings, further development of existing theory (more radical than incremental), refutation of existing theory and proposal of new theories.

Our final proposition reads:

P2: Theory building is the exception rather than the rule in MIS research.

## METHOD

The discussion on Methods is divided into four parts. First, we introduce the topics of citation and classification methods to help the reader understand our chosen method – co-word analysis – in a larger context. Second, we discuss journal selection and data preparation. Third, we focus on the anatomy of leximaps, which are the resulting output of performing the co-word analysis. Leximaps are graphical representations of terms and their associations in a network. Finally, we explain our procedure for analysis.

## Citation and classification studies

Citation and classification studies constitute the major approaches to investigating patterns in research (Vessey et al., 2002). In this article, we are concerned exclusively with classification. For example, one approach to performing an in-depth analysis of research studies within an area, such as group decision support systems (GDSS), might employ meta-analysis techniques. Meta-analysis uses the common variables and relationships in empirical data to discern general and overarching patterns across different studies. Another approach consists of analysis of topic or subject matter, which takes a broader perspective. This process involves selecting a topic and reviewing many journals and conference proceedings in order to find evidence of patterns, trends, similarities and differences. The articles considered in a topical analysis may be either quantitative or qualitative and need not adhere to any common method. Rather than predetermining topics of interest, our research objective focused on identifying centres of coherence in MIS, building from the ground up. We were looking for a method that would allow us to achieve this objective in a data-driven manner.

We selected co-word analysis as our research method. At the time, the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) at Carnegie Mellon® University was supporting research and development on processing very large corpora using co-word analysis and information retrieval. We were able to benefit by having access to the developers and the technical environment. This approach had been used to characterize patterns in the discipline of software engineering and its evolution as a body of knowledge (Coulter & Monarch, 1998). However, much of the previous research applied this technique to data files consisting of keywords and titles of publications. Subsequently, Monarch (2000) addressed differences between information science and IS using a richer data set, comprised of article abstracts. Because one of the present authors had contributed to data gathering and preparation for that investigation, we had access to the data necessary to perform a rich semantic analysis of themes in MIS research.

Monarch (2000) surveys the history of research on co-word analysis (Callon et al., 1986; 1991; Turner et al., 1988; Courtial & Law, 1989; Whittaker, 1989; Law & Whittaker, 1992; Courtial, 1994). He describes the technique as revealing ‘patterns and trends in technical discourse by measuring the association strengths of terms representative of relevant publications . . . A main tenet of co-word analysis is that the identified patterns of representative term associations are maps of the conceptual structure or knowledge network of a technical field and that a series of such maps produces a fairly detailed representation of the subject matter of a discipline’ . . . ‘Co-word analysis enables the structuring of data at various levels of analysis: (1) as networks of links and nodes (nodes hold terms); links connect nodes, thereby forming networks; (2) as distributions of interacting networks; and (3) as transformation of networks over time periods’ (Monarch, 2000, pp. 5–6, at http://www.slis.ualberta.ca/cais2000/monarch.htm).

A note about the distinction between co-word analysis and co-citation analysis is in order. Whereas co-citation analysis maps ‘the structure of a research field through pairs of documents that are jointly cited . . . coword analysis deals directly with sets of terms shared by documents . . . and maps the pertinent literature directly from the interactions of key terms instead of from the interactions of citations’ (Monarch, 2000, pp. 5–6; italics ours; Small, 1973). Co-word analysis shows links on the basis of content.

## Journal selection and data preparation

Practical circumstances dictated our focus on the time period of 1990–2000, namely: our interest in characterizing the current state and recent past in the discipline of MIS, our performing this data collection in 2001, and finally insufficient availability of data prior to 1990. We selected representative journals to ensure solid coverage of MIS. However, it was also essential that article abstracts were available through an online database. This placed constraints on the data available for analysis, as several journals we would have liked to include were not available in this form at the time of data preparation, e.g. well-established US and European journals such as Information Systems Research (ISR) and Information Systems Journal (ISJ). In an exploratory study such as this, we also felt the need to begin with a limited data set deemed central to the field. Some 300 journals in IT have been identified and examining all of them is clearly unrealistic. Moreover, the ‘top’ journals are the ones most likely to focus on central tendencies (rather than subspecialties) and most likely to insist on a theory orientation. We have selected a subset of journals where we are most likely to find theory – and if it is not in evidence here, it is unlikely to be found elsewhere.

Representative journals for the years 1990–2000 were identified iteratively based on published expert opinion, their inclusion in the Institute for Scientific Information’s (ISI) most cited journal list and their co-word similarity to journals most commonly acknowledged as representative of MIS.

Five journals were initially selected for analysis: Information and Management, Information Systems, International Journal of Information Management, Journal of Management Information Systems and Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ). These were selected based on the discussion of Ellis et al. (1999), their high citation rank according to ISI, and their accessibility. In a preliminary round of co-word analyses of these journals, one, Information Systems, was found to be much more technically oriented with respect to databases and much different in subject matter from the others in this group. The final rounds of analysis on which the results of the present study are based were not applied to this journal. The total number of articles analysed for MIS was 1325. As we were particularly interested in changes through time, we created two data subsets, for analysis of the time periods 1990–1994 and 1995–2000.

We needed comparable data sets with respect to numbers of records (abstracts) and approximate time periods. In order to compensate for the fact that more articles were produced in the early part of the decade, we divided the data set into slightly uneven time periods so that there were roughly an equal number of records in each subset. Minimum sample size requirements for the co-word analysis did not permit creating more than these two subsets.

Data preparation involved deleting generic terms of a non-MIS nature (e.g. terms such as ‘article’ and ‘background’) and creating master text files for each time period. Once the data were prepared, we were able to use the co-word analysis tool to generate leximaps. These maps were then manually formatted – involving limited intervention with the output – to assure that all nodes and associations were clearly readable.

## Understanding leximaps

The subsets of 1990–1994 and 1995–2000 consist of about 25 pages respectively. For each subset, the pages are numbered LM1 (leximap 1), LM2, and so on. Each page contains the graphical display of a set of terms and relationships among terms.

The strength of the relationship among terms determines the leximap numbering. The first page (LM1) contains the centre(s) of coherence with the highest calculated coupling and cohesion among terms. As the strength of the relationship among terms decreases, centres of coherence follow-on subsequent pages – the last page containing terms with the weakest relationships. For example, in Figure 1 below, in the first leximap (LM1) for the period 1995–2000, there are two centres of coherence consisting of ‘generic MIS terms’ and ‘EDI’. As can be seen in Figure 1, a leximap consists of terms (nodes) and lines. Each of these is discussed below.

The term ‘EDI’, as a member of a centre of coherence, is surrounded by a thick border. A term can belong to more than one centre of coherence (e.g. ‘benefit’). A term that is related to other terms within a centre of coherence is surrounded by a thin border (e.g. ‘information system’). Note that a term surrounded by a thin border will also appear on one other leximap in the series where it is a member of a centre of coherence.

A line denotes the strength of the relationship between terms. Within a centre of coherence (where each term is surrounded by a thick border), a thick line between two terms represents a strong relationship. This means that the two terms are frequently found together in article abstracts. A stippled line represents a weaker relationship. In summary, a centre of coherence is defined by two representations: (1) terms surrounded by a thick border, and (2) terms connected by a thick or stippled line. A label for a centre of coherence must be inferred and assigned based on its member terms. In Figure 1 we would label the centre to the left ‘EDI’ and the centre to the right ‘generic MIS’.

A thin line denotes a weak relationship between a term that is a member of a centre of coherence with a term that is not a member of that centre. For example, at the bottom of LM1 (Figure 1), the term ‘organization’ is connected to the term ‘information system’. This means that ‘information system’ has a weak relationship with ‘organization’. Within the 25 leximaps for the subset of 1995–2000, EDI and generic MIS, as centres of coherence, exhibit the strongest relationships among terms (because they appear on the first leximap – LM1).

## Procedure for analysis

We performed a preliminary review of the maps with our SEI colleagues who advised on the co-word analysis technique. Our colleagues offered heuristics for analysing output and encouraged us to generate informal preliminary hypotheses and then to examine the leximaps to see if these could be confirmed. The generation of the leximaps is mathematical, with the co-word algorithm using multiple passes through the data to produce pair-wise connections of terms, which represent a term network. However, interpretation of the leximaps is clearly more inductive.

## Monarch (2000) observes:

Leximaps can be interpreted and primary focus points can be identified as intersections of important themes. This is not a precise activity. In some cases, leximaps have a single, highly connected pair of terms; in others, there are five or more terms exhibiting high connectivity, though only two or three stand out as primary. In the former cases, the theme can be a word or a short phrase. In the latter cases, several words and phrases are needed. In most cases, the main themes of leximaps can be identified using words and phrases contained in the map. Generally, these are term(s) from node(s) with the most links (p.10).

Next, the authors conducted a series of working sessions, walking through the leximaps – considering the software package’s calculations and the placement of centres of coherence into a sequence (e.g. LM1, LM2, . . . LM25). To a large degree, increased familiarity and understanding of the maps influenced our approach and interpretive analysis. Typically, a working session would result in identifying unclear issues, suggesting a definition, and specifying an approach or need for further analysis. We would then independently generate notes and document directions for the next step to be taken. In the following session, we would review the notes and directions and, if in doubt, the process would be repeated. Microsoft NetMeeting was used as the vehicle for document sharing.

The reporting of our analysis is atypical relative to straightforward empirical research. In order to consider the propositions we have set out, it is necessary for the analysis to include reflections on the results. Additionally, because our method evolved in an interpretive manner, it is critical to give the reader basic insight into our problem solving activity.

## ANALYSIS

Our analysis comprises a two-part investigation. Part one of the analysis addresses the following questions: what comprises the centre of MIS or its zones of coherence – or is diversity and fragmentation the rule? And, will MIS centre or zones change over time? Part two of the analysis addresses the following concern: within MIS research, is there evidence of theory building that contributes to a cumulative research tradition?

## Part 1: centres of coherence

Our first proposition is that many centres of coherence will emerge for each of our two subsets. We found 49 centres for 1990–1994 and 49 centres for 1995–2000. Not all centres of coherence are distinct and some are overlapping. In particular, generic terms such as ‘information’ or ‘system’ may be connected to many other key terms. Although it is difficult to be precise about the exact number of unique centres of coherence, we conclude that most centres carry meaning. The inference drawn is that, within MIS, many clusters of research activity exist. Proposition P1.1 is supported.

Proposition P1.2 suggests that methods issues and generic terms for the field of MIS will comprise the most cohesive centre of coherence. We have already presented LM1 for the period 1995–2000 in Figure 1. For comparison, LM1 (the leximap with the strongest coupling and cohesion) for the period 1990–1994 is presented in Figure 2 below.

When comparing Figure 1 with Figure 2, we see that generic terms exist as a centre of coherence in each. As predicted in P1.2, a centre of coherence representing generic MIS terms is found on leximap 1 (LM1) in both time periods. We observe that many terms are common to both time periods (e.g. ‘system’, ‘management’ and ‘information’) but some new terms appear or are more prominent in LM1 for 1995–2000 (e.g. ‘user’, ‘result’, ‘model’, ‘process’, ‘technology’ and ‘research’). The generic vocabulary in 1995–2000 is richer than in 1990– 1994. We do not find a centre of coherence representing ‘methods’ issues in either of these two maps. To summarize, P1.2 is supported with respect to generic terms but not with respect to method issues. However, centres representing methods are found on leximaps LM5 and LM25 for the period 1990–1994 and on leximaps LM3 and LM19 for the period 1995–2000. In all of the four maps, the centres of coherence for methods include traditional terms such as: ‘reliability’, ‘validity’, ‘measurement’ and ‘instrument’.

To better understand the dynamics of change, we developed a framework to describe patterns of change in centres of coherence through time. We needed to account for a wide range of patterns:

• Stasis: no change in form – centres of coherence that largely stay the same;

• Evolution: emergent concepts gaining maturity, with complex associations and interrelationships forming – evolving centres of coherence;

• Revolution: new concepts emerging in a robust form, with rich connections – new centres of coherence;

• Devolution: decline of a concept, previously richly connected – centres of coherence with less content or being split into smaller and disjointed centres;

• Termination: disappearance of a concept.

## Comparison 1: strategic and competitive advantage

We identified centres of coherence that were represented in both sets of maps (1990–1994 and 1995–2000), assuming that we would be able to trace theory building or the lack thereof in the two time periods. For our analysis, we selected the two terms ‘strategic’ and ‘competitive advantage’, hypothesizing that authors writing on these two issues would be thinking ‘strategically’ and attending to the patterns of the past as well as projections for the future. Thus, they would be cognizant of previous theoretical contributions, making this a good candidate area to display evidence of theory building.

Theory building, by our working definition, involved a publication going beyond the expected review of related literature to include one of the following: meta-analysis, summary of previous or related research efforts and expression of a cumulative research objective – in other words, an objective which explicitly acknowledges the value and use of earlier findings.

The leximap application allows access to the data (article titles, abstracts and keywords) through investigation of the ‘line’ connecting any two terms. Figure 3 consists of LM7 for 1990– 1994, where the two selected terms are found. (For the period of 1995–2000, the two terms are found on LM2, which is not included here.)

The lines connecting ‘strategic’ and ‘competitive advantage’ pointed us to 47 articles across both data sets. The analysis consisted of examining the article title and abstract for each, checking for evidence of theory building. We were unable to find any theory building or continuities in the research on strategic and competitive advantage. Rather, the contributions were ‘all over the place’ – consisting of isolated, discrete investigations with few cumulative, theoretical connections to previously published articles.

We were concerned with the lack of evidence for a cumulative research tradition. We wondered about a real incompatibility between tradition and innovation – between a desire for coherence in the discipline and the pressure that compels each researcher-author to make original and innovative contributions. There is little reward for advancing what someone else has created. There is much reward for creating something new, even if it is inherently difficult or impossible to advance.

## Comparison 2: decision support systems and decision

Perhaps what we saw with ‘strategic’ and ‘competitive advantage’ was an anomaly and greater continuity might be detected elsewhere? We decided to turn to a well-established topic to see if we might find signs of a cumulative research tradition there. We selected the terms ‘DSS’ (decision support systems) and ‘decision’ – as represented on both sets of leximaps.

We were able to discern traces of a cumulative research tradition, although this was more evident within 1990–1994 than within 1995–2000. Across the 31 articles in 1990–1994 (the line connecting ‘DSS’ and ‘decision’) we saw a wide range of issues tackled, with both conceptual and specific (national, local) perspectives represented. Four of the 31, or 13%, displayed evidence of theory building – where the contributions explicitly acknowledged the use of previous results to extend ideas further through discussion of theory and/or testing. One meta-analysis was identified.

For the corresponding map on ‘DSS’ and ‘decision’ in 1995–2000, we found fewer publications – only nine articles. A healthy spread of issues and perspectives was represented but there was no sign of theory building either within this set or across 1990–1994 and 1995–2000. In 1995–2000, we were unable to discern any empirical attempts to validate a previously existing model or to extend existing theory. There was actually a 75% drop in publications between the two time periods.

We saw something of a mystery: while DSS seemed to be waning in the 1995–2000 period, the term ‘decision’ was more richly connected. In addition, we noticed new activity in the area of GDSS. We speculated that more evidence might be found if we acknowledged that DSS in 1995–2000 may have evolved with terminology split in two directions. In order to see the bigger picture of the evolution of DSS in 1995–2000, we needed to look at an additional map, factoring in the terms ‘GDSS’ and ‘group’, as discussed below.

In the 1990–1994 subset, we found 16 articles, covering a wide range of issues. Once again, the contributions appeared discrete, sharing only generic terms and a common topic. We were unable to discern signs of theory building or a cumulative research tradition. In the 1995–2000 subset, we found seven articles, including one example of a review on the future direction of GDSS. No meta-analyses were present (we return to the topic of meta analysis in the Discussion section.) Across the two time periods, there was a 50% decline (15 articles as compared with seven).

## DISCUSSION

The researchers found 62 specific centres of coherence. Most of these centres were classified as IS/IT, or subdisciplines. Few centres of coherence exhibited a clear focus on theory (see Table 1). We also found that over time a high degree of change in centres of coherence occurs (see Table 2). Our detailed analysis on theory building found diversity to be the dominant pattern (see Table 3). What explains the diversity that we have seen? We return to the longstanding debate on the status of MIS – and its identity and its value, with respect to its role as a field, within the university, and in relation to industry practice.

Practical concerns may be driving researchers to address ‘hot’ issues, current themes, or new IS/IT. Host organizations may require researchers to address their pressing problems – or else organizations may elect not to expend time and money supporting research. Staying current may be the dominant mindset for academics and practitioners. On the other hand, theory building requires a long-term perspective and commitment to embark on the process of formulation, test and refinement of established ideas.

Thus, it is no wonder that coherent and cumulative theory development is a rare phenomenon. We cannot say that these practical concerns, alone, drive research in MIS.

However, we do believe that the discipline of MIS is being eclipsed by practical concerns including new tools and technology. And yet, ironically, this is the area we rarely look to for future guidance: instead, we privilege theory development over these more mundane or distracting concerns. Perhaps we have been looking for a future in all the wrong places? A number of researchers are attempting to point a way. For example Porra (1999) uses colonial systems to demonstrate diversity in MIS; and Hirschheim and Klein (2003) discuss an information systems development (ISD) body of knowledge, distinguishing between the fours types of technical, ethical, applicative and theoretical knowledge. Like these researchers, we are convinced that it is time to intensely scrutinize the present false and reductive relationship between theory and practice.

Our research results may be subject to bias on four levels, relating to interpretation, journal selection, data and focus. First, our analysis is interpretive. Second, the journals in our sample target the general area of MIS and therefore include articles over a wide range of topics. This may contribute to the diversity that we have seen. Third, the data for our investigation consist of article titles and abstracts. Clearly, analysis of full article texts might have yielded different results. As we have already observed, we were unable to include a number of well-established US and European journals in our sample, due to constraints on data preparation. Most of these materials are now available online and would be excellent for use in future studies.

Finally, a selection of different journals – with more specific foci – might have also yielded different results. For example, we saw limited evidence of theory building in the area of DSS. More evidence might have come into play had the journals of Decision Sciences and Decision Support Systems been part of the sample. In fact, our searches for meta-analyses in MIS revealed approximately 20 entries, running from 1989 to 2004 (see Appendix A). Of these, seven pertain to DSS, GSS and GDSS. Nonetheless, the strength of our chosen method rests with (1) the selection of journals that are central to the field of MIS and (2) that co-word analysis does not impose preconceived beliefs about centres of coherence but builds on the data.

What future work might expand our understanding of this research inquiry? Many questions remain to be answered relating to: new methods for analysing the content of research activity, such as co-word analysis; definitions of the discipline of MIS; searches for a core set of properties within MIS; and evolutionary patterns with respect to the development of the field. Follow-on research might exploit and extend the use of co-word analysis. This could include analyses of full texts and other sources (e.g. conference proceedings) and analyses of other journals over a longer period of time. In addition, researchers might investigate alternative analytical approaches and tools that could be used to empirically test the content of the field over time, e.g. Van de Ven & Poole’s (1995) notion of four basic theories for explaining processes of change: life cycle, teleology, dialectics and evolution.

There is also work for us to build on. For example, ongoing attempts have been made to define the discipline of MIS. Recently, Lee (1999) wrote:

The four interrelated dimensions of MIS are as follows: (1) MIS involves not just information technology, but also its instantiation; (2) MIS involves, as reactive and inextricable elements, both an information system and its organizational context; (3) MIS involves information technology as a form of intellectual technology; and (4) MIS involves the activities of a profession or corporate function which are integral to the essence of what ‘MIS’ is (p. 9).

Lee defines four dimensions of MIS. He does not offer any explicit comments on the role of theory (with respect to these dimensions) and is obliged to describe the discipline at a high level and in generic terms. Does coherence in the discipline of MIS require definitions that more explicitly address the roles of theory and practice in the creation of a cumulative research tradition? Answering this question opens the door to the challenging topic of disciplinary paradigms and bodies of knowledge.

Earlier, we discussed the concept of paradigm (Kuhn, 1970; Ritzer, 1975; Pfeffer, 1993) and suggested that MIS is in a pre-paradigm position (Benbasat & Weber, 1996; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Weber, 2003). Additionally, the concept of the paradigm may not serve as well in social sciences (Banville & Landry, 1989) where pluralistic models are common. Does this mean that MIS will never have a dominant paradigm, or is it merely too soon? Will the recent attempts to define and circumscribe the IT artefact and establish a core set of properties comprise the closest approximation to overarching theory (Larsen, 1999; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Whinston & Geng, 2004)? Or will ‘the source of the field’s creativity, vitality and long-term survival’ rest with ‘fresh perspectives, discipline newcomers, boundary spanners and topical outliers’ (Ives et al., 2004, p. 108)?

Lyytinen and King (2004) consider the evolution of disciplines such as Biology and Physics, including their derivative branches in the 18th, 19th and 20th centuries, and warn that a strong core theory will inhibit growth of MIS. They embrace three alternative drivers: (1) the salience of the issues studied, (2) the production of strong results and (3) the maintenance of disciplinary plasticity. Similarly, Cheon, Grover & Sabherwal (1993) state that ‘maturity in IS research will be signalled by increasing diversity’ (p. 132; see also Alavi & Carlson, 1992). We have already expressed reservations about such polarizing positions and suggest it is more likely that the discipline will experience periods of expansion (diverse new ideas) and contraction (consolidation and agreement on key concepts) through time, in a more episodic fashion. MIS, like sociology, may also be better viewed as a multiple-paradigm science (Ritzer, 1975).

Ritzer distinguishes between different social paradigms relating to facts, definition and behaviour. These serve as lenses on aspects of the discipline of sociology, each with complementary theories. It is noteworthy that Ritzer shifted his emphasis from paradigms to the subject of metatheorizing (Ritzer, 1991); and more recently, he has focused on ‘McDonaldization’ as a global industry trend towards standardization, routinization and deskilling of jobs (Ritzer, 2000, 2002). Ritzer’s changing focus – from theory to practice – may parallel an inevitable shift from MIS theory to the IS artefact and practice-based research.

Future MIS research might build theory ‘bottom up’, pursuing patterned phenomena to understand practical instances of IS and IT, including their categorization and definition. Theory is more likely to emerge under such circumstances and in sub- and specialized areas of the discipline.

As we have already suggested, MIS is not alone in re-examining its identity and value as a field, within the university, and in relation to industry practice. In addition to fractures in the discipline of sociology, similar concerns have been expressed by researchers in Organizational Communication (Corman & Poole, 2000; as noted in Ashcraft, 2001), Organization Science (Rynes et al., 2001) and Information Science (Monarch, 2000; Ellis et al., 1999). We observe that other signs of disciplines under stress – competing for funding and recognition in the university environment – translate into a plethora of applied R&D institutes, including interdisciplinary centres of an overlapping nature.

Our findings about coherence, change and the role of theory in MIS – and the struggle to define MIS – may reflect a larger crisis relating to disciplinary knowledge in the university institution and the potential failing of outdated forms. Reinventing the university and disciplinary knowledge challenges us to look at organizations as systems, rather than as edifices or monuments. This opens the door to seeing the university institution as a network or a system of systems.

## CONCLUSIONS

No end appears to be in sight for the now familiar and longstanding discussions on the status of MIS. This debate touches upon the identity and value of MIS as a field within the university and in relation to industry practice. Two themes dominate the discussion – the question of rigour vs. (practical) relevance and the lack of coherence in MIS. Could it be, we reiterate, that some aspects of the fragmentation that we perceive, or that we experience as a lack of coherence, are functions of a larger loss of order and unity, emanating from ageing, brittle models of academic institutions? Is the field of MIS in need of alternative metaphors for its identity? These are daunting and serious questions – and clearly the answers are beyond the scope of the present investigation. However, our debates will remain fruitless and frustrating until we begin to frame such forward-looking questions and to expand the nature of our inquiry.

As a step towards realizing this vision, the present investigation examines research contributions in MIS in light of a cumulative research tradition. We ask: what comprises the centre of MIS or its zones of coherence – or is diversity and fragmentation the rule? Will MIS’ centre or zones change over time?

Using a co-word analysis of titles and abstracts in four recognized MIS journals for the two periods of 1990–1994 and 1995–2000, we found 49 centres of coherence in each time period. While the centres of coherence representing generic MIS terms demonstrated the greatest cohesion in each time period, other broad areas such as IS/IT and subdisciplines were identified. Across the time periods, we found a limited number of centres of coherence directed at methods issues (10) and specific theory issues (four) out of a total of 98 centres.

The analysis of change in centres of coherence over the two time periods follows a dynamic pattern. Out of 33 changes in centres of coherence, nine that were present in 1990–1994 did not exist in 1995–2000, and seven were new in 1995–2000. Seven centres remained largely the same over the two periods. In summary, MIS research is heavily influenced by practical concerns, fads, trends and the desire to stay current.

Our analyses of theory and the evolution of a cumulative research tradition over the two time periods focused on (1) strategy and competitive advantage, and (2) decision, DSS, GSS, GDSS and group. We found limited evidence of theory building. Generally, the patterns revealed diversity, made evident through the many stand-alone contributions.

Ironically, ambiguity, fragmentation and change represent the most dominant patterns. A cumulative research tradition remains elusive. MIS centres of coherence change over time – we think, partly in response to practical pressures. We suggest that MIS opens a richer and more difficult debate on its theory, practice and identity as a discipline in the 21st century university.
