# The defensive use of IT in a newly vulnerable market: The New York Stock Exchange, 1980–2007

## a b s t r a c t

New technologies enable entrants to create business models that threaten incumbents in a range of industries. This paper extends the framework of newly vulnerable markets to explore the dynamics of competition between entrants and an incumbent through a study of the New York Stock Exchange. For over 200 years, the NYSE has operated a physical market for trading securities. Beginning in the 1960s, it employed information technology to process increasing trading volumes and to disseminate data on stock prices and volumes. The Exchange invested heavily in IT for its trading floor, and defended it against electronic markets enabled by new technologies. In 2005 various pressures forced the NYSE into a merger with Archipelago, a leading electronic exchange. The NYSE’s latest market system, Hybrid, launched in 2006, is the first at the NYSE that enables investors to bypass the trading floor completely. Despite the new technology, its share of trading volumes fell. This paper documents four eras of IT management at the exchange, presenting a detailed 27- year history. The evidence shows that while IT investments helped the Exchange defend floor trading and its market share for a number of years, it finally had to adopt the technology of all-electronic exchanges.

## 1. Introduction

The development of a new, technologically-enabled business model can make an existing market newly vulnerable to competition. Clemons et al. (1996) present a framework for analyzing these newly vulnerable markets that arise under the following conditions:

\- Contestable – a market in which new entrants can offer similar or superior services to the dominant, incumbent firm in the marketplace using innovative, new technology, especially information and communications technologies.

\- The incumbent’s profitable customers subsidize unprofitable ones – an opportunity exists for a new entrant to take advantage of average cost pricing, and target profitable customers in a market, leaving the incumbent with less profitable customers or those who require more complex products and services.

\- Fixed investment and barriers to exit – earlier capacity investments, and exit barriers for incumbent firms can make it difficult or impossible for them to withdraw from unprofitable or more costly-to-serve market segments.

Past studies have used the framework of newly vulnerable markets to examine the impact of information and communications technologies on online music and online news (Clemons et al., 2003), on electronic travel distribution (Granados et al., 2008) and on credit card issuance (Clemons and Thatcher, 2008).<sup>1</sup> In past research, new entrants were able to capture market share relatively quickly as incumbents failed to respond fast enough or appropriately to the new threat (Tushman and Anderson, 1986; Henderson and Clark, 1990; Chandy and Tellis, 2000).

The purpose of this paper is to extend the newly vulnerable markets framework with two propositions that address the dynamics of competition:

1. The speed with which the incumbent loses market share is inversely related to the extent that it applies information and communications technologies to defend its market position. In other words, the more IT is used in supporting an incumbent’s newly vulnerable market position, the slower the entrants’ inroads.

2. There may be a delay, but at some point the entrants’ new business model and innovative use of technology will force the incumbent to radically modify its model, or to adopt the entrants’ model in order to survive.

In this paper we examine how the New York Stock Exchange utilized information technology to defend its newly vulnerable business model of a physical floor for trading stock and an intermediated, ‘‘specialist” system against new entrants providing fully-electronic markets. The NYSE was able to defend this model for a number of years until a precipitous drop in market share and a change in management forced it to totally revise its business model. The case supports an extended, dynamic theory of newly vulnerable markets in which IT investments by the incumbent can slow the inroads new entrants make.

## 2. Background – IT and the NYSE

From the use of electromechanical ticker in the late 1800s to disseminate prices, to brokers’ phone lines to market floors, the securities industry has been quick to use IT. The continuing growth of trading volumes and additional listings in the 1970s led the NYSE to consider its alternatives for expanding its trading capacity. Rather than undertake costly expansion of its physical floor, the NYSE chose to invest in IT to increase the capacity of the market. With no increase in floor space or headcount, the exchange’s average daily trading volumes grew from 45 million shares in 1980 to 157 million in 1990, with further growth to 1.0 billion in 2000.

In 2007, the NYSE completed its \$20 billion ‘‘merger of equals” with Euronext, the operator of the Paris, Amsterdam, and Brussels stock exchanges and the LIFFE futures market, was completed in 2007. NYSE Euronext had revenue of \$4.2 billion in 2007, up from the \$2.4 billion in 2006.<sup>2</sup> It placed 542nd in the Fortune 1000 ranking of US firms by 2007 sales revenue.<sup>3</sup>

At year-end 2007, the NYSE-listed securities from 2384 US company issuers, up from 2313 in 2006. The market value of its US listings totaled \$15.7 trillion at year-end 2007, up from \$15.4 trillion a year earlier<sup>4</sup> (see Fig. 1). In comparison, the market capitalizations of domestic listings for the next largest markets, the Tokyo Stock Exchange and Nasdaq Stock Market, were \$4.3 trillion and \$4.0 trillion respectively at year-end 2007. The NYSE’s average daily trading volume in 2007 was 2.1 billion shares with a value of \$119.2 billion, compared to \$61.0 billion for Nasdaq and \$40.7 billion for the London Stock Exchange, its two closest exchange rivals in trading volume (www.world-exchanges.org).

Although an NYSE-listed stock may trade on other markets, until 2003 the NYSE market held a dominant position in trading, controlling 80% or more of total US trading volume in its listed stocks in the period 1982–2003. As Table 1 illustrates, in recent years the NYSE floor’s market share has dropped to less than 50%, while Nasdaq has expanded its market share in the trading of NYSE-listed stocks, reaching 37% in 2007.

Prior to its initial public offering in March 2006, the Exchange was controlled by its 330 member firms which are the broker–dealer and specialist firms that own the NYSE’s 1366 ‘‘seats.” Access to the floor and participation in the NYSE market requires a seat to be owned or leased. A seat provided trading privileges and access to the trading floor, an iconic 37,000 square foot space on Wall Street that was built in 1903. On the floor in 2003 there were more than 3000 traders, 307 of whom were specialists working for one of seven specialist firms that use their own capital to trade and maintain a ‘‘fair and orderly” market.<sup>5</sup>

The NYSE’s auction market structure ensures fair and competitive prices by matching the most aggressively priced orders together. Every NYSE stock is assigned to a single specialist, who works from one of the NYSE’s 20 posts. A specialist is usually responsible for 5–10 individual stocks. The rest of the NYSE’s floor population consists of brokers, handling orders for clients on a commission basis, or clerks or exchange officials. Between 1990 and 2005, specialist participation ranged from a low of 17.2% of total trading volume (1993, 1994, and 1995) to its historic high in 2001 of 30.2%. In the remaining 69.8% of trading volume in 2001, the specialist oversaw the direct matching of investor orders.

The rise in specialist participation up to 2001 was attributed to the reduction in the pricing increment from eighths to sixteenths of a dollar in early 1997, and then to pennies in January 2001. The NYSE market has ‘‘public order priority,” which means specialists only trade for their own account at a price once all limit orders at that price are executed. In 2001, specialists traded more when they could fill orders by pricing 1 cent better than the best limit order rather than taking the previously required risk of improving by 12.5 cents.

Since 2005, various forces have caused specialists’ participation in total NYSE trading to fall. It declined from 17.6% in 2005 to 14.6% in 2006, then dropped to 7.8% in 2007. The growing role of algorithmic trading is credited with dropping specialists’ participation (Hendershott et al., 2008). As trading software places aggressive limit orders in NYSE stocks, the specialist role in supplying liquidity profitably was effectively crowded out by computer-based traders away from the exchange floor.

## 2.1. Contestability – new entrants, new markets

The NYSE faces two forms of competition: competition for order flow and competition for listings.<sup>6</sup> Order flow competition comes from established markets and new entrants. Through a provision known as unlisted trading privileges (UTP), provided in the 1934 Securities Exchange Act, securities listed on any national securities exchange may be traded by other such exchanges.<sup>7</sup>

Through UTP, much of the trading in NYSE-listed shares takes place away from the NYSE. In addition, NYSE listings can trade in the “third market" in which a broker routes an order to a market-making firm, which is registered with the National Association of Securities Dealers (NASD).

Since the late 1980s, a growing number of new entrants have competed for NYSE trading volume. Technology makes it easy to set up an all-electronic market (Scott and Barrett, 2005); the entrant does not need a physical floor or in person transactions (Barrett and Scott, 2004). It is necessary to achieve critical mass in any market, but the demands of institutional investors and algorithmic traders favor an online market. These traders need to execute immediately at the current price before the market moves to execute their strategies. Specifically, the NYSE has faced increasing competition from electronic trading systems (see Table 2). These include ECNs (Electronic Communication Networks) such as Instinet and Alternative Trading Systems (ATSs) such as the now defunct-Optimark, and the Chicago Match at the Chicago Stock Exchange.

Initially, ECNs were more successful at capturing Nasdaq trading volume. The seven ECNs’ share of Nasdaq share volume rose from 24% in 2000 to 53% in 2003. In 2003, less than 10% of trading of NYSE-listed shares was estimated to have gone through ECNs.<sup>8</sup> In 2007, however, NASD reported trading of NYSE-listed stocks was 35.1%, up from 25.8% in 2006 and 17.8% in 2005.<sup>9</sup> Online markets were structured to attract low-risk, simple orders that were cost-sensitive. The NYSE floor retained more complex orders that were less cost-sensitive, but it lost volume to the ECNs who captured increasing numbers of simple trades.

While there was little erosion of the NYSE’s position until 2005, the experience of stock exchanges in Europe demonstrates the intense competitive pressures on the industry. According to the Wall Street Journal<sup>10</sup>:

‘‘Just before the Euro was launched in 1999, there were still 32 stock exchanges dotted around Europe. Since then, three exchange operators have come to dominate the business. And soon it may be two, Deutsche Börse AG. . ..has held informal talks to purchase the London Stock Exchange, which operates Europe’s largest exchange. . .Behind this action and other consolidation that has swept Europe in the past few years is the same factor that has driven U.S. exchanges to consider mergers: fierce competition to offer the lowest cost of trading.’’

In addition to competition for trading order flow, the NYSE also faces competition for exchange listings from a variety of sources. The other markets for companies to list their stock are the Nasdaq Stock Market and the American Stock Exchange, or overseas markets such as the London Stock Exchange’s AIM (Alternative Investment Market), which had 34 US companies listed in early 2007. The listing requirements for AIM, Nasdaq and Amex are less onerous in terms of firm size and market capitalization.<sup>11</sup>

Compared to secondary market trading, the NYSE’s and other exchanges’ listing businesses have been less disrupted by IT.

## 2.2. Four eras of investment in NYSE trading technology

Based on our discussions with the NYSE’s former Chief Information Officer (CIO) and his colleagues about strategy and IT investments, and reviews of documents dating back over 20 years, we identified the following objectives for IT at the NYSE up to 2005:

1. Enhance and extend strategic resources by providing sufficient capacity for processing trade transactions.

2. Enable efficient trade execution.

3. Provide a high quality market for securities.

4. Reduce labor expenses and demands for costly physical space.

5. Compete effectively with new types of electronic markets, and preempt other entrants.

Based on the levels of IT spending and the functionality added, we identified four periods in the exchange’s IT strategy: the first prior to 1982, and the second from 1982 to 1995, the third from 1995 to 2005, and the fourth since 2005 (see Table 3). During the 1982–1995 period the objective was to provide sufficient processing capacity to function properly with volumes three times those of an average day. This capacity “multiplier" was later increased to five times daily volumes. The major IT projects at this time were to integrate existing systems and develop better capabilities focused on the NYSE’s ‘‘point-of-sale” (POS), which is how the Exchange refers to its specialists’ posts. The IT projects at the NYSE in this period were not competitively motivated, and the few electronic rivals to the NYSE were not seen as serious threats.

Beginning in 1995, the NYSE invested more heavily in IT as trading volumes accelerated and competition from the Nasdaq Stock Market became more heated. The NYSE’s share of market value of all US listings fell to 68% in 1999 from 87% in 1990.

ECNs such as Instinet and Island were attracting trading away from the Nasdaq market, which operated with dealers posting bid and ask quotes and trading over the telephone. In addition, several European exchanges including London (1986), Paris (1991), and Stockholm (1991) had closed their market floors and were becoming fully-electronic exchanges.

From 1995 until its 2005 Archipelago merger, the NYSE’s IT strategy had been to use technology aggressively on the floor to meet new, vigorous competition from a number of sources as well as satisfy demands for trading capacity. IT goals included shorter turnaround times for orders being reported back as completed trades, converting the market price increment from eighths of a dollar (12.5 cents) to sixteenths (June 24, 1997), and then to decimal pricing (January 29, 2001), and achieving greater levels of market transparency for participants off the floor. The Direct+ system (2000), for instance, was a response to the threat from ECNs. Automatic execution however was initially restricted to orders of up to 1099 shares and subject to a 30-second waiting rule for sending a follow-on orders for the same trader. The system bypassed the floor broker and the specialist, and worked like an ECN for users of the system; it handled about 11% of NYSE trades in 2005.

In the late 1990s, algorithmic trading and statistical arbitrage strategies were being developing as a way to lower transactions costs and capture short-term profit opportunities. Although the NYSE took steps to provide faster execution of orders, these measures did not fully meet the demands of algorithmic traders and those with rapid trading strategies. The NYSE’s specialist floor market was considered too slow for such statistical and software-driven trading, and the restrictions on Direct+ discouraged electronic trading strategies. The growing competitive threats – Optimark, Liquidnet, and Posit – and leadership changes at the NYSE ended its strategy of using IT to maintain the primacy of the floor market.

The fourth era arrived with John Thain, a new CEO of the Exchange, announcing the merger with Archipelago, operator of the ArcaEx ECN, on April 20, 2005. The merger agreement meant Arca shareholders would own 30% of the new entity’s equity and NYSE seatholders would have a 70% stake. Through the merger, the NYSE had acquired the IT capability its rivals were using with rising success. After the merger, Arca continued to operate independently of the NYSE floor. Fig. 5 shows that NYSE Arca contributed a rising share of total volume.

While the merger signaled a change, the NYSE remained committed to floor trading and investing in systems to combine the best of floor and electronic trading. In 2006, Direct+ was further enhanced to become the NYSE Hybrid, a market system that allows for completely electronic trading or for floor specialist intervention according to the options selected by the customer.

## 3. IT functionality and exchange capacity – analysis

Over the four eras described, the NYSE invested heavily in technology to insure that its floor-based market functioned reliably. Capacity planning at the NYSE has been based on a model that translates trading volumes per day into a number of messages per second required to send the orders, cancellations, quotes, and trade reports associated with that volume of share trading. The Exchange has followed a policy to have messaging capacity that meets the following three objectives:

1. Capacity for twice the highest 5 min peak in messages per second.

2. Capacity for 2.5 times the average high peak over a 10 day period.

3. Capacity to process five times the average daily volume.

From October 1987 to October 26, 1998, when a ‘‘systems communication problem” halted NYSE trading between 1:16 pm and 2:15 pm, there were no IT-related disruptions to trading.<sup>12</sup> In 2004, the NYSE was able to process 6000 orders, quotes, cancels and other messages per second, up 20-fold from its 285 messages per second (MPS) capacity in 1993. In October 2005, the NYSE experienced a record five-minute peak of 14,100 MPS, 14 times greater than the 998 MPS record peak of 2001. These capacity expansion policies required regular investment to upgrade its network hardware.<sup>13</sup>

## 3.1. IT Investment results

What has the impact been of the NYSE’s investment in IT? As a useful simplification, we consider that the NYSE uses a variety of inputs to produce ‘‘trades”, its primary output, and a major source of fees and revenues. Trades are ‘‘produced” through a combination of inputs, and we use various metrics of the NYSE output in the discussion that follows. Table 4 describes the variables in the analysis. The descriptions of NYSE systems come from NYSE publications, its annual Fact Book, and our interviews.

The NYSE and the Securities Industry Automation Corporation (SIAC) provided data on nominal IT investment at the NYSE from 1982 to 2002, which we adjusted for inflation using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) as a deflator. SIAC develops and operates the various systems used at the NYSE including trading and regulatory surveillance systems. Since this paper focuses on the extent to which IT enables trades on the floor, we collected only the IT investment data associated with developing and operating floor support systems (e.g., SuperDOT, Common Message Switch, Display Book), and the communications infrastructure. IT investment for developing market surveillance systems was excluded because such systems are not directly related to NYSE capacity or order handling productivity. Trades executed (nearly 7 millions per day in 2006) have increased more rapidly than IT investment reflecting price-performance improvements in IT equipment. Automating previously manual tasks and reducing the need for paper-based clerical processes, these systems led to a reduction in the number of NYSE floor employees.<sup>14</sup> As Fig. 4 indicates, IT staff full-time equivalents (FTEs) have increased while the number of NYSE floor employees has steadily declined reflecting a shift of labor from floor clerical personnel to floor technology support workers.

IT is largely responsible for these results – greater trade processing capacity and reduced NYSE floor staff. Investments in message processing and systems like SuperDOT, Broker Booth Support System, and Display Book facilitate the substitution of faster electronic workflows for physical movement of floor brokers, and more error-prone voice and written order and trade recording. Faster processing speeds improves market quality by making traders more confident in placing larger and more aggressive orders, knowing they will trade quickly or can be swiftly cancelled (Hendershott et al., 2008). Costly exceptions processing has fallen. The percentage of uncompared trades (when the buyer and seller ‘‘sides” of a trade do not match on price, quantity, and stock symbol) has dropped considerably over the years, to less than 0.2% daily, on eight times more trading volume.<sup>15</sup>

## 3.2. Market quality performance

As the NYSE reduced clerical labor and used IT to defend its competitive position, how was market quality affected? Improving the floor’s market quality with IT is one way the NYSE could have prevented further inroads from its rivals. The Exchange developed two indicators, price continuity and quotation spread, to measure the quality of markets and began publishing them in 1982. Price continuity denotes the size of the price variation from one trade to the next in the same stock (NYSE FactBook). The figures in our data correspond to the percentage of all transactions that occurred with no change or a minimum of 1/8th point (\$0.125) change, which is the threshold in the Exchange’s market quality statistics.<sup>16</sup> The bid-ask or quotation spread is a key measures of market quality, and is the difference between what a buver is willing to bid for a stock and the seller’s asking price. The NYSE data indicate the percentage of bid and ask spreads that were <sup>1</sup> point (\$0.25) or less, which was the breakpoint used by the Exchange for collecting data. For example, 85 means that the quotation spread was <sup>1</sup> point or less in 85% of exchange-published quotes. The higher the percentage is, the more liquid the market.

The use of Prais–Winsten regressions corrects for the autoregressive errors in our time series data (Prais and Winsten, 1954). ## 4. Management and market changes

For many years, IT initiatives enabled the floor’s capacity to keep up with demand, and forestalled inroads of electronic markets. The NYSE was managing on its own terms – retaining and defending its floor trading-based business model. The exchange’s stable competitive performance in attracting trading volumes and listings in the presence of a growing number of rival alternative trading platforms reflected a strong market position though 2003. However, regulatory changes beginning in 1997 led to greater choices for market participants interested in the fastest possible trade executions. The regulatory changes included:

\- 1997 – Nasdaq Order Handling Rules. Limit orders held by Market Makers had to be reflected in their bid and offer quotes, and the best prices on ECNs had to be integrated into the Nasdaq quote montage. Investors gradually became accustomed to greater speed and efficiency in Nasdaq trading, which exposed the NYSE floor to less favorable comparisons over time

\- 2000 – Elimination of NYSE Rule 390. NYSE member firms were now allowed to use ECNs and other off-exchange systems as long as their prices were as good as the best quotes in the market.

\- 2001 – The pricing increment for US stock markets shrank from eighths and sixteenths of a dollar to one penny, allowing spreads to narrow, and reducing the benefits of potential price improvements on the NYSE floor.

\- 2004 – Publication of the Regulation NMS proposal to overhaul the structure of the national markets. After implementation in 2007, the new ‘‘trade-through” rule meant any ECN with a better price in an NYSE stock would have to receive trade orders.

Fig. 5 shows an alarming loss of market share in the trading of NYSE-listed securities beginning in 2004. Why did NYSE market participants increasingly want their trades executed without human intervention? Although market liquidity and order flow are ‘‘sticky” due to the participation externality in trading, fully-electronic markets were proving faster and more capable to what was now a critical mass of users.

## 4.1. New management and the first steps toward fully-electronic trading

In late 2003, the large compensation package of the NYSE CEO and Chairman, Richard Grasso became public and eventually led to his resignation. During his eight years as CEO and Chairman, the NYSE performed well and added 1300 new listings. Grasso was a staunch defender of the floor market and the specialist system.

‘‘Many Wall Streeters believed that the Big Board needed to modernize – and even embrace automation. But Mr. Grasso and the specialists argued that their system of human judgment was superior because it helped investors get the best prices, damped volatility in the market and brought together buyers and sellers who might otherwise not find each other. Even as he propped up the roles of his human traders behind the scenes, Mr. Grasso took pains to trumpet how much technology was installed at the Big Board. He liked to boast that the NYSE had spent \$2 billion on technology over the last decade. Flat screens displayed buy and sell orders on an array of stocks. One instrument showed a three-dimensional simulation of the activity on the exchange. The new technology sped orders to the floor. But once they arrived there, orders were executed the old-fashioned way: A specialist oversaw an auction in which floor brokers could step in and participate. The NYSE claims much of the public prefers it this way It did put in place an automated system three years ago that permitted small investors to bypass specialists. But a Big Board spokesman said few investors actually use it.<sup>18</sup>’’

At the time of its 2005 merger with Archipelago, a company that went public in 2004, NYSE seats were exchanged for shares, and the NYSE effectively became a public company. The Exchange justified the combination by pointing out that some customers want greater speed and choice in order execution, and that in particular institutional investors want to purchase stocks anonymously, something hard to do with the physical floor business model. With ArcaEx, the NYSE could offer a fully electronic trading alternative, and the change to a shareholder ownership structure removed seat-member, not-forprofit governance of the NYSE, which was seen by many as an obstacle to change. While specialists and brokers benefited financially from the physical market, member control of the NYSE prevented it from innovating in ways that would conflict with firms with floor operations.

## 5. The results

The exchange fully embraced electronic market technology with the launch of its NYSE Hybrid trading system in 2006 Has this alternative trading process allowed it to maintain regain market share with a dual physical and electronic structure? Data on a sample of 20 stocks at the NYSE that were early hybrid stocks over a four month period from October 2006 to January 2007 showed the NYSE market share in the trading of the 20 sample stocks remained flat at about 63%.<sup>19</sup> However, the overall NYSE market share fell 3% during that period to 66%, a trend of a 1% per month decline. The volume of shares traded for the 20 stocks in our sample rose 37% during the period compared to a 10% increase for the total NYSE share volume. From this sample, it appears that the hybrid market may have stimulated additional trading volume but it is not reversing the downward trend in the NYSE’s market share.

Hendershott and Moulton (2008) examined the impact of the Hybrid trading system and its predecessors, and found that floor trading activity began ‘‘to noticeably decline in 2002 . . . [and] the substitution of electronic trading for floor trading continues and grows from 2002 onwards.” The evidence indicates the NYSE’s legacy floor market has been displaced by electronic trading, with the NYSE holding a decreasing percentage of all trading despite their 2006 introduction of Hybrid and all-electronic trading. A September 2007 article summarized the effects on the NYSE floor:

‘‘The ‘‘crowd’’ — the brokers and clerks on the floor — has dwindled to about 1700 from a high of more than 3000. Before the exchange became a public company in 2005, its members controlled 1366 seats, or licenses to trade. Now, about 800 brokers pay the annual \$50,000 fee for a license. . . .But even on its busiest days, the trading floor is a sideshow compared with the centerring attraction it was several years ago. Most of the action in shares listed on the New York Stock Exchange happens electronically, between the computers of traders at brokerage houses, mutual fund companies and home offices in spare bedrooms.’’<sup>20</sup>

## 5.1. Two propositions to extend the newly vulnerable markets theory

The purpose of this paper is to provide evidence on the dynamics of newly vulnerable markets and the effects of defensive IT strategies when a market is under threat from new business models. We do not generalize to the relatively small number of other stock exchanges, but we believe that an in-depth case study of one, unique organization, the New York Stock Exchange, exhibits how an organization used IT to defend its business model over a long period of time in which it was ‘‘vulnerable” to new IT-enabled entrants. The NYSE was able to use information and communications technologies to maintain its market share for almost twenty years, supporting our first extension of the newly vulnerable markets framework: the speed with which the incumbent loses market share is inversely proportional to the extent that it applies ICT to defend its market position. In other words, incumbents in a newly vulnerable market with little or no IT capabilities will see their positions erode more rapidly than those that can use IT defensively to prop up a threatened business model, as the NYSE did.

A second proposition is supported by our analysis of the history of the NYSE, particularly its change of ownership structure, merger with Archipelago and the Hybrid system. In newly vulnerable markets, after some time, the defensive use of IT will not be sufficient to offset the entrant’s superior business model. At this point the incumbent will be forced to radically modify its own model, or to adopt the entrant’s new model in order to survive. In the case of the NYSE, this radical change occurred when new management took over, the Exchange developed its Hybrid system, and merged with Archipelago.

## 6. Conclusions

How should incumbent firms react when their markets become threatened by entrants with IT-intensive business models? The extant theory, the newly vulnerable markets framework, establishes three conditions that enable entrants to make rapid inroads against incumbents – contestability, cross-subsidies, and high fixed investments. The theory predicts that established players will be unable to sustain their market position under these conditions when entrants apply innovative IT and new business models. We have added two propositions concerning how swiftly the entrant overtakes the established player. The practical implications are that incumbents can defend a business model for a sustained period of time, as the NYSE did. Furthermore, incumbents can affect the timing of the radical changeover through their use of IT in supporting the established business model.

As an incumbent, the NYSE successfully used IT for many years, quite possibly for too long, to defend its floor market. The Exchange invested about \$2 billion after 1990 to provide systems that helped to sustain trading on a physical floor with the specialist system. The analysis shows that the NYSE’s investments in information technology provided it with the ability to maintain its physical trading market with adequate reliable capacity, and keep its share of trading volume at over 80% for over twenty years despite aggressive competitors.

The Exchange benefited from this investment through the added capacity it created to process trades, and through systems that supported the individual trader roles at the heart of its physical market. Around 2005, however, it was clear that a transformation in trading had occurred, and the NYSE needed to develop a new strategy to survive as a trading venue. While difficult to prove, it appears the NYSE’s competitive position in trading would be better if it had responded and invested more aggressively in transformational IT prior to 2005.

The NYSE case demonstrates the dual role of information technology in newly vulnerable markets. In one role the technology enables new entrants to attack the legacy business models of dominant players. IT’s other role is to provide the capabilities for incumbents to defend their business models if they choose to do so. An incumbent might opt for this path because it believes that the new entrant’s model will fail, or because the incumbent feels its model is superior. Eventually one technology and one model will emerge to become dominant. The challenge for managers is to know when to use IT and other resources to defend their business model, and when to recognize it is time to change that model in response to a major technological transformation in their industry. For the strategic manager, IT can be applied to sustain an established – but newly vulnerable market – even while entrants apply IT to overtake the incumbent firm.
