# Health information privacy concerns, antecedents, and information disclosure intention in online health communities

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Dual calculus Health status Personal health information Privacy calculus Privacy concerns Self-disclosure

## A B S T R A C T

This study explores the antecedents and consequences of health information privacy concerns in online health communities by integrating the dual calculus and protection motivation theories. On the basis of survey data from 337 users, health information privacy concerns, together with informational and emotional support, signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence personal health information (PHI) disclosure intention. Privacy concerns are negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by two coping appraisals (i.e., response e<sup>fi</sup>cacy and self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy) and positively a<sup>f</sup>ected by two threat appraisals (i.e., perceived vulnerability and perceived severity). The perceived health status di<sup>f</sup>erentially moderates the e<sup>f</sup>ects of privacy concerns and informational support on the PHI disclosure intention.

## 1. Introduction

The Internet has become a useful channel for seeking and sharing health-related information in the past decade [1]. The Pew Research Center reported that 59% of American adults have searched for online health information, whereas 35% of American adults have searched for medical solutions online for themselves or someone else who might have a disease [2]. Online health communities (OHCs), which have been accelerated by advancements in Internet technology, have recently emerged as an alternative platform for people searching for health information and self-healthcare management [3]. An OHC refers to a series of virtual discussion groups consisting of members who share collective interests in health topics [4]. The advantages of OHCs include 24-h availability of information and support from people beyond the restrictions of geographic proximity, cost savings, lack of embarrassment, and access to more diverse support networks and information [4,5].

The OHC existence and development are largely driven by in formation sharing. However, once individuals opt to disclose their personal health information (PHI), they can face the risk of privacy invasions [6]. For example, people’s PHI can be used for marketing purposes without authorization. People are also largely concerned about employers and insurance companies that can obtain access to their PHI, which can lead to discrimination. Disclosure of sensitive PHI (e.g., genetics, mental health, domestic violence, reproductive care, substance abuse, and sexually transmitted diseases), can result in social stigma. An individual’s choice of information disclosure can cause trouble to his or her family in several cases. For example, a physical examination report can leak information about genetic disorders that run in families. The Harris Interactive poll reported that many American adults have serious anxieties on how their health information are obtained and utilized [7]. Health information privacy concerns arise when individuals are required to provide su<sup>fi</sup>cient PHI to obtain the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of utilizing OHCs. These concerns can even cause individuals to refuse providing information to OHCs and decline healthcare services from OHCs. Therefore, investigating the underlying drivers of OHC users’ information disclosure intentions and privacy concerns is becoming more important.

However, prior studies on the information disclosure behavior in OHCs exhibit three gaps. First, although an impressive body of previous studies has examined information privacy and information disclosure behavior in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts [8–10], the health information privacy issues in the OHC <sup>fi</sup>eld are rarely examined. The information privacy dilemmas in OHCs seem to be similar to those in other e-commerce websites, social networks, or virtual communities in appearance, but it has its uniqueness. Health information disclosed in OHCs is more sensitive than other information forms, such as demographic pro<sup>fi</sup>les, lifestyle interests, or purchase history information. Rohm and Milne suggested that individuals are more likely to be concerned with PHI use, such as medical history or medical records [11]. Considerable sensitive information can increase the risk beliefs and lower the intentions to disclose [12]. Unlike other online communities, people bene<sup>fi</sup>t from participating in OHCs by obtaining social support rather than monetary rewards or enjoyment [13–15]. Although many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information disclosure have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in other communities, these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts may not be the main drivers to self-disclosure in OHCs. Therefore, a further examination of the potential risks (i.e., privacy concerns), bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and their relationships with information disclosure in the OHC context is required.

Second, despite a large body of studies on the relationship between privacy concerns and information disclosure intentions [16] and a handful of studies that have proposed the antecedents of privacy concerns [6,8], few studies have integrated information disclosure, privacy concerns, and their antecedents. Phelps et al. argued that the causes of privacy concerns must be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed to investigate privacy issues [17]. Privacy concerns can be major obstacles for information disclosure. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of releasing information to others can supplant an individual’s privacy concerns to some degree. Identifying the antecedents of privacy concerns also helps to lower the anxiety of people and improve information disclosure intentions. Incorporating the antecedents of privacy concerns and building an integrative model to explain individuals’ information disclosure intentions are essential to obtain a more comprehensive insight into information disclosure in OHCs.

Third, prior research suggests that several personal characteristics (i.e., previous privacy invasion) and situational risk factors (i.e., information type, intended purpose, and requesting stakeholder) can moderate the relationships between the information disclosure intention and its antecedents [18,19]. However, no prior study directly in cluded personal health factors as moderating variables. Considering that health information disclosure is a health behavior, we propose that several health factors can have moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects on the relationships of privacy concerns and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts with intentions to disclose information. The perceived health status, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects an individual’s overall health, is a commonly studied health factor [20]. Previous research reports a direct or indirect e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived health status on information disclosure intentions [6,18]. Nevertheless, no prior work has explored the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived health status on information privacy research. Therefore, how the perceived health status strengthens or suppresses the e<sup>f</sup>ects of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and health information privacy concerns on information disclosure intentions must be investigated.

The current study attempts to build a framework that integrates the dual calculus theory and protection motivation theory (PMT) to examine the aforementioned issues. The integrated framework can provide a holistic view of individuals PHI disclosure intentions in the OHC context. We analyze information privacy concerns and their ante cedents and consequences (i.e., PHI disclosure intention) simultaneously by using the dual calculus theory. The dual calculus theory assumes that the intention to disclose information is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by two interrelated trade-o<sup>f</sup>s: privacy calculus and risk calculus [21]. In the privacy calculus theory, the intentions of individuals to disclose PHI are based on the trade-o<sup>f</sup> between the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and perceived risks (e.g., privacy concerns) of information disclosure in OHCs. In the risk calculus model, privacy concerns are regarded as the net risks, which are based on threat and coping appraisals, perceived by an in dividual. Privacy concerns will increase when privacy threats outweigh the coping ability to protect privacy. The risk calculus model, however, does not specify the variables for threat and coping appraisals [21]. In contrast, the PMT identi<sup>fi</sup>es the speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables for threat and coping appraisals and is widely used to examine individuals’ fear appeal on their attitudes and behaviors [38]. Therefore, we adopt four speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables (i.e., perceived vulnerability, severity, self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and response e<sup>fi</sup>cacy) from the PMT and investigate their e<sup>f</sup>ects on privacy concerns using the risk calculus model. We integrate these two theories in a complementary manner to assess the privacy concerns and PHI disclosure intention of OHC users.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Information disclosure and privacy concerns

Information disclosure refers to “the personal information individuals intentionally and voluntarily reveal about themselves to others in an interpersonal relationship” [22,p. 166]. Thus, information disclosure has an immense impact on di<sup>f</sup>erent online environments, which involve personal information sharing such as e-commerce, social network services, and location-based services [8,10,23,24]. Among these online environments, OHC is a community-driven service under Web 2.0 and allows people to satisfy their health-related information requirements. OHCs bene<sup>fi</sup>t users by providing them support when they reveal their PHI [6]. OHC users can disclose their PHI in exchange fo health advice for their health risk evaluation, disease prevention, or treatment suggestions from doctors [6,25]. They can also share their medical histories, experiences, and feelings with patients who have experienced similar health problems [1].

Nevertheless, users are hesitant to reveal their PHI because their personal information is private. Privacy concerns related to disclosing personal information are considered the central issue in information privacy literature [16,21]. Privacy concern is the “individual’s subjective views of fairness within the context of information privacy” [26,p. 337]. OHC users, who are concerned about their privacy, are afraid of exceeding the baseline of consumers’ individual rights, committing privacy infringement because of the collection and other applications of providers, and threatening the con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality of their individual information. Given these issues, the development of health information services has been hindered by the struggle between users attention to privacy and their active disclosure of health information.

## 2.2. Dual calculus perspectives

Previous studies were conducted from di<sup>f</sup>erent perspectives to investigate the nature of privacy concerns and information disclosure behavior. Prior research adopts di<sup>f</sup>erent theories, such as the theory of reasoned action, privacy calculus theory, and social contract theory [6,8,12,27]. Among them, the privacy calculus theory is widely adopted to investigate consumer privacy concerns because it integrates both the risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of privacy concerns [28,29]. This privacy calculus model assumes that individuals must perform a risk-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis before they can decide whether to disclose their personal information [30]. Individuals are often willing to disclose information if the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are more considerable than the perceived risks that result from such disclosure [28].

Despite the empirical support for the privacy calculus model in previous studies (e.g., Ref. [24,29]), several limitations must still be addressed. First, although this model has highlighted the critical e<sup>f</sup>ects of expected risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts on information disclosure, they can di<sup>f</sup>er in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts [23,31]. The current study further tailors privacy calculus to the OHC context and reexamines the speci<sup>fi</sup>c bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risks of disclosing PHI. Second, the privacy calculus model only examines the relationship between privacy concerns and information disclosing intentions but neglects the antecedents of privacy concerns. Several previous studies recommend that researchers must explore the relationship between privacy concerns and their antecedents [16,21,32]. Although previous studies have recognized several antecedents [33–35], how they a<sup>f</sup>ect privacy concerns remain unknown [32].

Li suggested a dual calculus model to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap, with the assumption that privacy calculus, which is a<sup>f</sup>ected by risk calculus, signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uences an individual’s privacy decision [21]. The privacy calculus model posits that a person’s intention to disclose PHI is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the balance between the information disclosure bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and potential privacy concerns. In contrast, risk calculus depends on the balance between coping e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and expected risks. The expected risks increase privacy concerns, whereas the coping mechanisms (e.g., self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy) lower privacy concerns in risk calculus [21]. However, this dual calculus model has not been applied in empirical research on information disclosure in OHCs. Therefore, the present study attempts to investigate the aforementioned issues by applying the dual calculu framework.

## 2.3. Privacy calculus

The concept of privacy calculus has received much attention from scholars. Laufer and Wolfe contended that a calculus of behavior, in which an individual performs a risk-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis among situational constraints, is an important antecedent of personal information disclosure [30]. The privacy calculus theory postulates that individuals behavioral intentions and follow-up actions are positively a<sup>f</sup>ected by the potential gain of disclosure and negatively a<sup>f</sup>ected by their expected loss from a potential privacy violation [36]. This theory conforms to the expectancy theory, which states that people engage in a behavior that minimizes negative results and maximizes positive results. Personal information will be disclosed if the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of disclosure are considerably higher than the perceived risks [28].

The privacy calculus theory is now widely applied to investigate the intentions to disclose information and behavior in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts (e.g., electronic commerce, online social networks, and location-based services) [8–10,23,31]. However, limited research focuses on online health information disclosure. In fact, OHC users also perform a costbene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis to decide on whether to disclose health information. Therefore, the privacy calculus model is also suitable for the analysis of information disclosure in the OHC context.

The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information disclosure can vary in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts. Previous studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information disclosure that users value the most, including <sup>fi</sup>nancial rewards, social rewards (e.g., socialization, connectedness, and relationship building), perceived usefulness, personalization, self-representation, and enjoyment [23,31]. The current study focuses on information disclosure in a special online community (i.e., an OHC), which has rarely been in vestigated in prior studies. An OHC provides opportunities for individuals who have similar health interests to obtain informational and emotional support [3,14]. Many OHC users disclose their information to obtain social support in the form of informational and emotional support [13,14] and not for other bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information disclosure in other contexts (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nancial rewards, personalization, and enjoyment). Nevertheless, social support is rarely examined as a bene<sup>fi</sup>t of information disclosure in previous research. Therefore, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in the information privacy calculus of OHC members require further investigation.

## 2.4. Risk calculus and the PMT

Although many studies focus on the outcomes of privacy concerns, limited research has systematically examined the antecedents of privacy concerns [16]. Risk calculus, which measures an individual’s perceived net risks (i.e., privacy concerns) on the basis of threat and coping appraisals, was introduced to build a holistic view of privacy concerns [21]. Net risk is low when a person responds to a perceived threat that can be conquered; otherwise, net risk is high [21]. A higher level of privacy protecting ability consequently helps to lower privacy concerns [21].

Previous studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several threat appraisal variables (e.g., previous privacy invasion, perceived vulnerability, social awareness, and perceived severity) and coping appraisal variables (e.g., privacy self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, perceived control, perceived usefulness, and monetary rewards) [21,33,35,37]. However, the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the same antecedent on privacy concerns are contradictory according to other studies. For example, Mohamed and Ahmad determined that self-e<sup>fi</sup>- cacy positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences privacy concerns in the context of social networking sites [35], whereas Akhter determined that self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences privacy concerns in the context of online transactions [37]. Therefore, further empirical studies are required to examine the relationship between these antecedents and privacy concerns in the OHC context. Moreover, prior studies on the relationships between privacy concerns and their antecedents were examined in a disjointed manner [16]. The antecedents were not developed in a theoretical framework but were examined randomly [32]. The lack of an e<sup>f</sup>ective organization for these antecedents increases the di<sup>fi</sup>culty in forming a uniform perspective to analyze their impact on privacy concerns [32]. Therefore, an integrated framework must be developed to analyze the relationship between privacy concerns and their antecedents [32].

The PMT, which is based on the health belief model, was proposed to explain how fear a<sup>f</sup>ects individuals and their relevant health behavior [38]. It is one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential theories that explains the behavioral intention of an individual to exhibit protective behavior and prevent risks [39]. The PMT emphasizes the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social, environmental, and individual ability factors on individual behavior. It enables an individual to evaluate information privacy threats and his/ her ability to alleviate the said threats. The PMT illustrates two underlying determinants for privacy concerns: threat appraisal and coping appraisal. The former typically comprises perceived vulnerability and severity, whereas the latter consists of self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy and response e<sup>fi</sup>- cacy. These appraisal processes are similar to the trade-o<sup>f</sup> between threat and coping mechanisms in risk calculus. Thus, the current study applies the PMT in the analysis of risk calculus to systematically examine the antecedents of privacy concerns.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

## 3.1. Perceived benefits and PHI disclosure intention

The OHC di<sup>f</sup>ers from other online communities because of the uniqueness of its participation group, motivation, and demands [40]. One of the clear bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the OHC is social support to users [13,14]. Social support refers to the care and support from other members in a social group that can be perceived by an individual [41]. This support includes the experience of being cared for, responded to, and assisted [41]. Previous studies suggest that social support can improve the health conditions of people who have been su<sup>f</sup>ering from serious diseases [42–44].

Prior studies have classi<sup>fi</sup>ed social support into many di<sup>f</sup>erent types under di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts, but the most common types of social support are informational and emotional support [45,46]. These two types of social support are the main targets for examining social support in online communities [47]. The principal motivation of people to parti cipate in OHCs is to exchange information and share emotions [45,48]. OHCs provide a convenient information communication, allow patients to exchange information and suggestions about disease treatments, and search for other similar patients who they can share their emotions with [25,48]. Thus, our hypotheses focus on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of two types of social support (i.e., informational and emotional support) on PHI disclosure intention.

## 3.1.1. Informational support

Informational support provides suggestions, advice, knowledge, and referrals to help people solve their problems [14,47]. OHC users acquire knowledge and advice for health risk evaluation, disease prevention and diagnosis, and treatment suggestions from doctors by seeking informational support [25]. They can also obtain medical experiences and treatment history from patients who have experienced similar health problems [1]. When users acquire more health information, they can understand and improve their health conditions better [14].

People acquire social support through social interaction with others. Such interaction allows them to have mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial cooperation that requires them to exchange information fairly [49]. People who provide information will also receive informational support from others [14]. This continuing information exchange is the major process of establishing interpersonal relationships [50]. In particular, an open and sincere self-disclosure of information is a means to establish an intimate and reciprocal relationship [50]. The self-disclosure of a user’s personal health record and treatment stories can provide others with the convenience of obtaining the health information they require [51]. OHC users must disclose their health information to obtain additional in formational support and satisfy the demand for mutual bene<sup>fi</sup>t in the community. Thus,

H1. Perceived informational support is positively related to PHI disclosure intention for OHC users.

## 3.1.2. Emotional support

Emotional support is the expression of emotional concerns, including caring, understanding, empathy, trust, and love [47,52]. Although several studies have investigated the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of emotional support in o<sup>fl</sup>ine health-related support groups, people may still be unable to obtain these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts because of their physical dis abilities, time and resource limits, remote living places, embarrassment in face-to-face communication, and other factors [14,44]. With no re strictions on geographic proximity, OHCs enable more people to participate in online social interactions and obtain the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of emotional support. These bene<sup>fi</sup>ts include decreasing patients’ loneliness and depression, increasing their ability to cope with stress, and encouraging them to continue with treatment regimens [42,43]. Emotional support can provide warmth to an individual’s heart and help him/her feel better even if it does not provide direct assistance to solve his/her health problems [47,53].

Emotional support is manifested through reciprocally supportive exchanges of providing and obtaining similar informational support. Always receiving support without providing support can lead to guilt, shame, and feeling of unable to live independently. Therefore, OHC users can maintain a balance between obtaining and providing emotional support. Open and sincere disclosures of private intimate information in emotional communication, including sharing feelings and fantasies, have been seen as an important sign of a close relationship [50]. Therefore, people are usually willing to disclose their PHI in an OHC with appropriate emotional support. Thus,

H2. Perceived emotional support is positively related to PHI disclosure intention for OHC users.

## 3.2. Privacy concerns and PHI disclosure intention

Previous research had utilized privacy concerns to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the risk/ cost dimension of the privacy calculus equation [21]. It re<sup>fl</sup>ects the inherent concern of an individual regarding the potential loss of private information [26]. The privacy calculus model shows that privacy concerns negatively a<sup>f</sup>ect individuals’ willingness to disclose their private information [8]. Many studies have empirically veri<sup>fi</sup>ed this inverse relationship in the contexts of online transactions, location-based ser vices, and online social interactions (e.g., Ref. [24,31]). Technologies have enabled online health consultation and diagnosis possible in the OHC context. However, these functions are highly dependent on PHI use. Given that users are asked to provide PHI, privacy concerns often simultaneously arise regarding the improper handling of such information. OHC users are largely concerned about information disclosure that can lead to negative consequences such as stigma, prejudice, and discrimination, particularly when highly sensitive health information related to sexually transmitted diseases, physical disabilities, mental disorders, drug abuse, and even genetic traits is required [18,54]. Given that privacy concerns can a<sup>f</sup>ect health information disclosure, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3. Health information privacy concerns are negatively related to PHI disclosure intention for OHC users.

## 3.3. Threat appraisals and privacy concerns

## 3.3.1. Perceived severity

Perceived severity is the evaluation of individuals based on the severity of consequences caused by a threat [35,28]. When individuals feel threatened, they will generally adjust their behavior according to the risk level. On the one hand, when individuals perceive that a more serious consequence will be caused by the threat, they are more likely to undertake coping mechanisms [55,56]. On the other hand, in dividuals who perceive a low risk degree are less likely to obtain recommended threat responses [56,57].

Threat appraisal in OHCs is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the risk perception of losing information privacy. Although threats to personal information may not cause the same severity as threats that directly impact one’s self [58], perceived severity has a considerable impact on preventing the information security threat [59]. Perceived severity is also positively related to information privacy concerns [35]. Individuals’ expectations of possible negative events, such as Internet fraud or identity theft, positively a<sup>f</sup>ect their privacy concerns [60]. Individuals who have a higher degree of perceived severity are more likely concerned with their PHI privacy in OHCs. Therefore,

H4. Perceived severity positively a<sup>f</sup>ects health information privacy concerns for OHC users.

## 3.3.2. Perceived vulnerability

Individuals evaluate the possibility of encountering a threat in perceived vulnerability [38]. Once individuals consider themselves as more prone to an unfavorable threat, they will undertake protective action to weaken the threat [55,61]. Vulnerability describes the perceived potential negative outcomes of information disclosure [8]. An individual may have considerable information privacy concerns when he/she perceives the risk of private information loss [35].

Users’ perception of vulnerability in OHCs can increase because of their neglect of information privacy or weak protection measures provided by OHCs. The higher the perceived vulnerability, the more likely a user’s PHI is exposed to the risk of misuse and abuse. This risk can lead to discrimination and social stigma. Therefore, individuals are more likely to be concerned about their PHI when they perceive that they are vulnerable to privacy risks. Thus,

H5. Perceived vulnerability positively a<sup>f</sup>ects health information privacy concerns for OHC users.

## 3.4. Coping appraisals and privacy concerns

## 3.4.1. Response eficacy

Response e<sup>fi</sup>cacy evaluates how e<sup>f</sup>ective an adaptive response mitigates a threat [38]. Given that information privacy is considered a problem of uncertainty or risk [62], response e<sup>fi</sup>cacy is likely to play a key role in lowering this risk. People who perceive more privacy protection from a personalization provider have less system-speci<sup>fi</sup>c privacy concerns [63].

Response e<sup>fi</sup>cacy evaluates the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of privacy protection mechanisms provided by OHCs in lowering privacy invasion. Online communities can present privacy seals and privacy assurance statements to decrease the invasion of user privacy [64]. They can also provide a number of new technologies to protect online health information privacy, such as access control mechanisms, encryption methods, and anonymization methods, to hide patients' identities from their medical records [65]. Safe OHC’s privacy-enhancing responses lead to fewer concerns that individuals have about the collection of their PHI. If users feel that their PHI cannot be e<sup>f</sup>ectively protected by the OHC, they are more likely to be concerned about their information privacy. Therefore,

H6. Response e<sup>fi</sup>cacy negatively a<sup>f</sup>ects health information privacy concerns for OHC users.

## 3.4.2. Self-eficacy

Self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy is the expectancy of individuals’ capabilities to perform a recommended coping behavior [38]. Self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>u ences individuals’ intentions to take protective behavior in various contexts. It addresses the belief of individuals’ ability to control their information and is closely related to perceived ability to control privacy self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy and perceived privacy control [24,33,34].

Privacy-enhancing measures in OHCs are available to provide pro tection against privacy invasion, but users must know how to utilize them. The protection of health information also involves several arcane medical terminologies, policies, and regulations, which are relatively di<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand. Individuals with more con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their ability to manage privacy information can have fewer privacy concerns [33,37]. In contrast, OHC users with low self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy are more concerned about their privacy because they feel incompetent in deciding when and how to provide access to their PHI. Thus,

H7. Self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy negatively a<sup>f</sup>ects health information privacy concerns for OHC users.

## 3.5. Moderating efects of perceived health status

Perceived health status is the relative level of illness and wellness of individuals. Researchers determined that perceived health status signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly moderates the relationships between individuals’ health behavior intention and its antecedents [66–68]. Individuals are more likely to be risk seeking when they encounter problems that involve life–death choices rather than other life problems, such as personal money, investment, or public property [69,70]. When individuals perceive themselves as seriously ill, they can focus more on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information disclosure rather than worry about the loss of privacy. Thus, we expect that the perceived health status can moderate the relationship between informational support and PHI disclosure inten tions. Individuals who perceive their health to be “very good” requires less information and advice of diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment than those with poor health conditions. Hence, they are less likely to fully disclose information about their illnesses.

H8a. Perceived health status negatively moderates the relationships between informational support and PHI disclosure intention for OHC users, such that the better the perceived health status, the positive relationship between informational support and PHI disclosure intention will be attenuated.

OHC users can share emotional support with others who are suf fering from similar health issues [71]. Healthy users feel less depressed and lonely compared with unhealthy OHC users. They are not so eager to receive comfort, compassion, and care, which can be obtained from their close relationships with others. Therefore, healthy users can be less likely to emphasize the importance of emotional support to disclose their PHI than unhealthy users. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H8b. Perceived health status negatively moderates the relationships between emotional support and PHI disclosure intention for OHC users, such that the better the perceived health status, the positive relationship between emotional support and PHI disclosure intention will be attenuated.

OHC users must consider the risk of privacy invasion before disclosing their health information. Users with poor health are more sensitive about their PHI [6,72]. Their health information can no longer be kept secret once released [73]. The disclosure of some sensitive health information, such as mental health disorders, substance abuse, and sexually transmitted diseases, may increase the risk of judgment and stigma. Thus, unhealthy individuals are reluctant to provide their electronic health records, medical test results, and sensitive health information until their information privacy is guaranteed. Conversely, healthy users are less concerned about protecting their information privacy because they perceive their health information as less sensitive. Therefore, unhealthy users have a higher level of privacy concerns regarding the disclosure of their information than unhealthy users. Thus,

H8c. Perceived health status positively moderates the relationships between health information privacy concerns and PHI disclosure intention for OHC users, such that the better the perceived health status, the negative relationship between health information privacy concerns and PHI disclosure intention will be attenuated.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Participants and procedure

We collected data through both online and o<sup>fl</sup>ine surveys. Our questionnaire was divided into four parts. The <sup>fi</sup>rst part described the purpose of the academic research and condition of the participant’s voluntary and anonymous involvement upon survey completion. Survey information was maintained con<sup>fi</sup>dential. The second part evidently stated that only OHC users could participate. We brie<sup>fl</sup>y introduced the OHC de<sup>fi</sup>nition and provided the <sup>fi</sup>ve leading Chinese OHCs to identify actual OHC users. These OHCs are as follows: “Good Doctor” (www. haodf.com) is the most popular Chinese doctor–patient interaction community. “Baby Tree” (www.babytree.com) is a large Chinese parenting community. “39 <sup>fi</sup>tness” (jianfei.39.net) is a large Chinese weight loss community. “TMJY” (bbs.tnbz.com) is a large Chinese community for diabetics. “GDXZ” (hbvhbv.info) is a large Chinese community for patients with chronic hepatitis. Participants were asked to select the OHCs they have visited for half a year or longer so that they have extensive experience. The third part required participants to provide their individual information (e.g., education, gender, age, and income). The fourth part included questions designed to measure the nine constructs in this research.

The survey was divided into two stages. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage was a pilot study. The questionnaires were sent to 18 OHC users after completing the design. Results show suitable reliability and validity. We also requested users to provide suggestions on the questionnaire structure and literary expressions adopted. We amended the questionnaire on the basis of their feedback. The second stage was the formal survey. The theoretical population of this research is the OHC users. First, we conducted a paper survey at three hospitals and two medical colleges in China. We asked whether they have visited any of the <sup>fi</sup>ve OHCs before providing them with the questionnaire. We then sent the questionnaire when we obtained positive replies. Next, we posted on the <sup>fi</sup>ve OHCs in China. The posts included the survey purpose and a URL linked to a web-based questionnaire. We also applied the snowball technique in data collection to increase the sample number by requesting participants to share the survey link through IM services (e.g., WeChat and QQ).

## 4.2. Measurement development

The questionnaire included a set of measures. All measurement items for constructs in the research model were adapted from existing literature to <sup>fi</sup>t the OHC context. We reviewed many relevant studies in the domains of psychology, healthcare, and information systems to select proper measurement items and improve content validity. We selected a 7-point Likert scale that ranges from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” to measure all multi-item constructs, except for the perceived health status, which is measured by a single scale (i.e., would you say that your health is generally very poor, poor, fair, good, or excellent?). This single-item measure has empirically demonstrated good reproducibility, reliability, and discriminant scale performance in previous medical studies (e.g., [74]). In the measurement of self-rated general health status, a single-item measurement is a good alternative to multi-item measurement [74–76]. This measure has also been widely used by other studies in the context of health (e.g., Ref. [68,77]). Thus, the use of single-item for perceived health status is appropriate in this study. Given that the original scales were written in English, we back translated these scales into Chinese. We compared the original English version and back-translated English version of the questionnaire to improve translation accuracy. After developing the original questionnaire, we utilized an expert judgment approach to enhance the content validity of our study. We sent the questionnaire to eight experts in the domain of information systems and healthcare. We reworded several items based on these experts’ suggestions to ensure that these items were unambiguous. The <sup>fi</sup>nal measure items are listed in Table 2.

## 4.3. Control variables

We introduced age, income level, and educational level as the control variables in our model. These variables are often used as controls in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information sharing or healthcare [68,79,80]. We assumed that OHC members with di<sup>f</sup>erent ages, income levels, and educational levels exhibit various levels of PHI disclosure intention because they possess diverse backgrounds and personal requirements. Therefore, we treated these three variables as control variables.

## 4.4. Data assessment

The representativeness of our sample should be evaluated. Although we were unable to obtain the entire demographic data of Chinese OHC users for a reasonable comparison, we compared the characteristics of the respondents with the demographics of Chinese Internet users to identify di<sup>f</sup>erences, if any. We obtained Chinese Internet user data from the 2016 survey report provided by the China Internet Network Information Center (CNNIC) [81], an organization that releases authoritative reports on China's Internet development. We performed a nonparametric chi-square test on the distributions of gender, age, educational level, and income level between our sample and the statistical data from CNNIC. The test results for the demographic characteristics indicate that the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance levels of gender, age, education, and income are p = 0.257, 0.368, 0.270, and 0.138, respectively, thereby suggesting no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences between our sample and the statistical data from CNNIC. Thus, our sample is representative of Chinese Internet users.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Discussion

This study attempts to investigate the determinants that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the disclosure of personal information in the OHC context. Thus, we developed and empirically tested a model that incorporates risk and privacy calculi. We derived several key <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study.

First, this study determined that privacy concerns are negatively related to the information disclosure intention in the privacy calculus. This result is consistent with prior <sup>fi</sup>ndings in other contexts [18,24,31]. However, unlike previous studies that mainly focus on the monetary rewards [15], perceived usefulness [23], or personalization [24,29], the current study determined that informational support and emotional support were the two main perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that have a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on PHI disclosure intentions. The individuals are willing to disclose their information to obtain informational support and emotional sup port.

Second, four antecedents of privacy concerns in risk calculus were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by using the PMT: perceived vulnerability, perceived severity, self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and response e<sup>fi</sup>cacy. Understanding the antecedents of privacy concerns would be a suitable starting point to eliminate obstacles in disclosing PHI. Consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of previous studies [33–35], perceived severity and perceived vulnerability positively a<sup>f</sup>ect privacy concerns. Response e<sup>fi</sup>cacy has rarely been investigated in prior studies. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant support for response ef-<sup>fi</sup>cacy as determinants of privacy concerns was determined as hypothesized. The present study also determined that self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy negatively a<sup>f</sup>ects privacy concerns. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with that of a previous study [37] but was inconsistent with those of other prior studies [34,35]. Di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts and sample characteristics may result in inconsistency. For example, the samples of two previous studies [34,35] comprise middle school students and undergraduates. These individuals may not have a “scienti<sup>fi</sup>c” understanding of Internet use [91] and may overestimate their own self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy. In contrast, our study sample includes all age brackets. In addition, privacy protection in OHCs requires more medical knowledge and Internet skills. Users who are knowledgeable in these areas are less concerned about their personal information privacy, thereby displaying a negative relationship between self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy and privacy concerns.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

This study enriches privacy-related research with several novel insights. First, we proposed a research framework that integrates the dual calculus theory and PMT in a complementary manner to examine the privacy concerns and PHI disclosure intentions of OHC users. We conducted a survey and demonstrated that risk and privacy calculi play important roles in individuals’ decision making on health information privacy issues. Thus, the dual calculus process has been veri<sup>fi</sup>ed in the OHC context. This study attempts to empirically verify the dual calculus model. This study also shows that four protection motivations a<sup>f</sup>ect health information privacy concerns. The integrated model can facilitate the overall analysis of factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the disclosure intention of individual health information.

Second, we extended the application of privacy calculus theory in the e-health context. Previous studies on privacy calculus mainly focus on the contexts of electronic commerce, social commerce, online social networks, and location-based services [8–10,24]. In contrast, the present study focuses on the OHC context. Consistent with previous studies in other contexts, we demonstrated that an individual performs a costbene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis of their perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and privacy concerns to determine whether he/she will reveal health information. Previous studies generally consider monetary rewards or tangible income as information disclosure bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. However, individuals are unwilling to sell their health information to a website [92]. Therefore, we proposed and empirically determined that informational and emotional supports are e<sup>f</sup>ective bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in OHCs. These conclusions indicate the existence of context di<sup>f</sup>erences in the application of privacy calculus theory. Scholars must focus on these di<sup>f</sup>erences and develop unique privacy calculus models under di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts.

Third, we extended and empirically tested the Antecedents → Privacy Concerns → Outcomes (APCO) model in the e-health context. Smith et al. suggested that privacy researchers must focus more on both the antecedents and outcomes of privacy concerns, as well as be on alert for a macro model that called APCO research model [16]. They also argued that the APCO model requires some parameterization if contextual di<sup>f</sup>erences are clearly salient [16]. We proposed four antecedent variables and one outcome variable (i.e., PHI disclosure inten tion) of the privacy concerns by considering the particularities in the OHC context. This parameterization in the OHC context is the development and empirical test of the APCO model, which contributes to a clearly analysis of the causes and results of information privacy concerns, as well as the analysis of an individual’s information disclosure behavior.

Finally, we examined the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of an individual’s perceived health status on the relationships of three variables of privacy calculus with the PHI disclosure intentions. Perceived health status has been rarely examined in existing studies on information privacy. The limited empirical studies considered the direct or indirect in<sup>fl</sup>uences of perceived health status on an individual’s intention to disclose information [6,18,93]. The present study empirically supports the previous observation that the perceived health status signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly mod erates the relationships between an individual’s health behavior intention and its antecedents [66–68]. We determined that the perceived health status has a di<sup>f</sup>erentiated moderation e<sup>f</sup>ect on health behavior intentions. In particular, the result indicates a negative moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived health status on the linkage of informational support with PHI disclosure intentions and a positive moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived health status on the linkage of privacy concerns with PHI disclosure intentions.

## 6.3. Managerial implications

This study o<sup>f</sup>ers several important practical implications for both OHC administrators and users. Individuals intentions to disclose their PHI are based on the trade-o<sup>f</sup> between their privacy concerns and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information disclosure. Therefore, these individuals must properly handle the dilemma of information disclosure and information privacy. OHC administrators must increase the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information disclosure and decrease the privacy concerns of users to improve users PHI disclosure intentions. Users must understand that information disclosure can result in privacy leaks. Protecting their private health information is highly important as they obtain better health services.

Moreover, informational support and emotional support are con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed to be important for PHI disclosure intentions. OHC adminis trators must improve the information services of OHCs and increase informational support for users. For example, administrators can provide a more convenient interface and provide a “one-to-one” expert consultation by strengthening the capacity of information analysis. Both administrators and users must take measures to promote a warm community culture, which comforts and encourages individuals to discuss their sensitive health issues and receive emotional care.

This study also reveals the joint e<sup>f</sup>ect of threat appraisal and coping appraisal on privacy concerns. Administrators must actively announce the website’s privacy policy and strengthen private information pro tection. Users must strengthen their knowledge about the privacy policies of an OHC, identify the type of private health information that can be submitted to the OHC, and improve their capacity to control their private information. These approaches can decrease users’ privacy concerns and promote users’ information disclosure.

The moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived health status also indicates that individuals with di<sup>f</sup>erent health conditions have di<sup>f</sup>erent views on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risks of information disclosure. Unhealthy users can insist more on utilizing OHCs in a private manner than healthy users. The OHC administrators must design personalized health services based on these di<sup>f</sup>erent health demands and especially focus on a non-stigmatizing service design.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

This research has several limitations. First, our data relied on a self reported survey, which may potentially introduce biases. Future research can adopt measurements that are more objective or employ pairwise design to enhance the validity of the current <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Second, our data were obtained in only one country. Thus, the generality of the research results must be further examined. Given the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>u ence of culture on privacy concerns [26,94], how cultural factors in-<sup>fl</sup>uence OHC users’ privacy concerns must be explored further. For example, uncertainty avoidance can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the privacy concerns of an individual. One characteristic of the Chinese people is the relaxed requirement for accuracy [95]. Thus, their uncertainty avoidance is unclear. This attitude can result in insu<sup>fi</sup>cient sensibility to the hazards of privacy leaks and low privacy concerns. Third, only <sup>fi</sup>ve OHCs were selected for the questionnaire survey. These OHCs mainly focus on nonlife threatening diseases. Given the limitations of the health topics of these OHCs, whether the research results can be generalized to all types of OHCs is still unclear. Fourth, we relied on subjective survey data to assess information disclosure intentions. Thus, this study su<sup>f</sup>ers from the typical disadvantages of any intention versus actual behavior model. Future research can employ objective measures to assess the actual PHI disclosure behavior, such as PHI disclosure quantity, quality, and frequencies.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study raise several possible future research di rections for exploration. First, informational support and emotional support are the main bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of disclosure of PHI. However, these two support types remain ambiguous and must be further speci<sup>fi</sup>ed. For example, Lederman et al. identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three types of health information in online health forums: experiential information, scienti<sup>fi</sup>c information, and non-medical factual information [96]. Communication privacy management theory states that individuals establish di<sup>f</sup>erent boundaries based on di<sup>f</sup>erent PHI types, and then conduct di<sup>f</sup>erent behavior rules [18]. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of these types of informational support on an individual’s intention to disclose information can be inconsistent and must be explored further. Second, we investigated the moderating role of perceived health status in this research. Future research can explore additional moderators, such as the health information sensitivity, the Big Five personality traits, and trust. Third, we used a single-item scale to measure perceived health status. Future research can explore the e<sup>f</sup>ect of speci<sup>fi</sup>c health status, such as physical and psychological health status. Fourth, the online community size and structure can in <sup>fl</sup>uence the social and psychological process of community members. Therefore, future research can be extended to other OHCs with di<sup>f</sup>erent health sizes and structures.

## 7. Conclusion

This study attempted to apply the dual calculus theory and build an integrative model to explain individuals’ information disclosing intentions, which expanded our knowledge in the area of information privacy. It further identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (i.e., informational support and emotional support), which have been overlooked in other contexts and can e<sup>f</sup>ectively enhance information disclosure intentions in OHCs. This study also advances the existing literature by applying the PMT to incorporate the antecedents of privacy concerns. Several threat and coping appraisal variables were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the antecedents of privacy concerns. Results reveal how the privacy concerns can be decreased and information disclosing intentions be improved. This study also provides novel knowledge by providing empirical evidence that health status di<sup>f</sup>erentially moderates the relationship between privacy calculus and PHI disclosure intention. The e<sup>f</sup>ects of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts or risks on the PHI disclosure intentions are di<sup>f</sup>erent among individuals with di<sup>f</sup>erent health status. Thus, providing di<sup>f</sup>erent privacy services for people with di<sup>f</sup>erent health conditions is essential.
