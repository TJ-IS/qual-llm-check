# Examining the antecedents of user gratification and its effects on individuals’ social network services usage: the moderating role of habit

Chao-Min Chiu<sup>1</sup> and Hsin-Yi Huang<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Information Management, National Sun Yat-sen University, Kaohsiung City, R.O.C.; <sup>2</sup>Department of Computer Science and Information Management, Soochow University, Taipei City, R.O.C.

Correspondence: Hsin-Yi Huang, Department of Computer Science and Information Management, Soochow University, No. 56, Kueiyang St., Sec. 1, Taipei City 10048, Taiwan, R.O.C.. Tel: +88 62 2311 1531 ext. 3814; Fax: +88 62 2375 6878; E-mail: hyhuang@csim.scu.edu.tw

## Abstract

User loyalty or continued use is critical to the survival and development of any website. Focusing on the social network services (SNSs) context, this study proposes a research model for investigating individuals’ use motivations and the moderating role of habit with regard to gratification and continuance intention. This research integrates two influential media communication theories, media system dependency (MSD) and uses and gratifications, to examine SNSs-related behaviors. To comprehend online users’ motivations in depth, three motivations derived from MSD (understanding, orientation and play dependency relations) are operationalized as reflective, second-order constructs. The three motivations are theorized to affect parasocial interaction positively, and parasocial interaction is hypothesized to positively affect the gratification that individuals derive from SNSs usage. Furthermore, this study hypothesizes that gratification positively affects individuals’ continuance intention. Finally, we theorize that habit moderates the impact of gratification on continuance intention. Data collected from 657 Facebook users provide strong support for all six hypotheses. The results indicate that individuals’ motivations (i.e., the understanding, orientation and play dependency relations) positively affect parasocial interaction, which in turn has a positive effect on gratification, and subsequently continuance intention. In addition, the results show that habit has a small but negative moderating effect on the relationship between gratification and continuance intention. Implications for theory and practice are discussed, and suggestions are made for future research.

## Introduction

Social network services (SNSs) have become an international phenomenon. They provide an online service, platform or website that enables the construction and re<sup>fl</sup>ection of social networks or social relations among individuals. Facebook and Myspace are two well-known SNSs. Before being overtaken by Facebook, Myspace was the largest SNSs site. Between November and December 2011, the number of unique U.S. Myspace users fell to 24 million with a loss of over a million unique U.S. users (TechCrunch, 2012). Myspace then confronted crippling problems associated with its accelerated decline and substantial <sup>fi</sup>nancial losses. TechCrunch (2011) observed that ‘MySpace, once the king of the Internet, is in very real danger of becoming just a footnote in Internet history’. Whether Facebook will prove to be the king of the Internet or just the next Myspace is unknown. However, one thing that is certain is that users’ loyalty or continued use dominates the development and survival of an SNS. Thus, it is important for SNSs managers to understand the factors that drive user loyalty or continued use.

Many people use SNSs as a media to meet their psychological and social needs. Uses and grati<sup>fi</sup>cations (U&G) theory is a psychological communication perspective that examines how individuals use mass media to gratify their psychological and social needs. Accordingly, recent studies on SNSs use consider U&G to be a suitable approach to understanding whether people can gratify their need to connect with other people through an SNS (Johnson & Yang, 2009; Chen, 2011; Lee & Ma, 2012). Grati<sup>fi</sup>cation is the core concept of U&G theory (Katz et al, 1974). Grati<sup>fi</sup>- cation is the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of a need through an activity such as media use (Herzog, 1944). According to Bryant & Miron (2004), if an individual’s needs are ful<sup>fi</sup>lled, they are likely to repeat the experience, that is, continue to use the media. Therefore, this study adopts U&G theory as the theoretical framework for understanding the underlying psychological and social dimensions of motivations to continue SNSs use.

U&G theory emphasizes the linkage among motives, media behavior, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and repetitive behavior. Establishing or maintaining a relationship of friendship or intimacy with others (parasocial interaction) is a major activity (media behavior) on an SNS. However, prior U&G studies on social media use paid less attention to the linkage among usage motivation, parasocial interaction and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. In addition, previous studies that applied U&G to examine social media use generally followed its premise that users control their access to media content according to their goals/needs (Patwardhan & Yang, 2003). Those studies took the individuals as the focal point of analysis and thus ignored the interrelationships between individuals and the media. To <sup>fi</sup>ll the void, this study suggests integrating U&G theory with another theory that elaborates speci<sup>fi</sup>c levels of dependency relations between the individual and the medium.

Dependency relations are at the core of media system dependency (MSD) theory (Ball-Rokeach & DeFleur, 1976). At the micro (individual) level, MSD suggests three goal dimensions (understanding, orientation and play) with two sub-dimensions (personal and social), providing six levels of dependency relations between the individual and the medium. Such a dependency on the SNSs can be viewed as the usage motivation, which in turn may affect the extent of the users’ parasocial interaction. Recent U&G studies on web-based media used generally adopted motivations that re<sup>fl</sup>ect instrumental and hedonic orientations of media use, such as information seeking, entertainment, passing the time, convenience and status seeking (e.g., Luo et al, 2011; Lee & Ma, 2012). Those studies lacked a theoretical framework (i.e., MSD theory) for identifying media use motivations and rarely conceptualized motivations from the perspective of personal and social goals.

Therefore, this study intends to integrate U&G and MSD theories to obtain a better understanding of individuals’ SNSs use behavior.

Habit may be an important determinant of individuals media behavior (Rosenstein & Grant, 1997), which has been ignored in prior U&G studies. Habit is ‘learned sequences of acts that have become automatic responses to speci<sup>fi</sup>c cues, and are functional in obtaining certain goals or end states’ (Verplanken & Aarts, 1999, p. 104). Spontaneous SNSs use resulting from the automatic retrieval of the learned association (Verplanken & Orbell, 2003) implies that habit formation is involved and that the impact of cognitive-oriented variables to the subsequent behaviors decreases because the conscious evaluation of SNSs use is not necessary. Information systems (ISs) researchers also suggest that habit may interact with cognitive-oriented variables to in<sup>fl</sup>uence behavioral intention (e.g., Khalifa & Liu, 2007). Grati<sup>fi</sup>cation involves the cognitive evaluation of one’s needs ful<sup>fi</sup>llment and is an important determinant of media use. However, the moderating effect of habit on the relationship between grati<sup>fi</sup>- cation and continuance intention has been largely ignored in prior U&G studies on SNSs use. To make U&G theory more comprehensive, this study suggests adding the moderating effect of habit to the theory.

Overall, this study aims to integrate the U&G theory and the MSD theory to explore why individuals continue to use SNSs (see Figure 1). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we examine the usage motivations in terms of the multidimensional nature of individual MSD relations. Furthermore, this study examines whether habit moderates the relationship between grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention. There are four research questions: (1) Are an individual’s MSD relations multi-dimensional in nature? (2) Which dimensions of MSD relations have a direct effect on parasocial interaction? (3) To what extent does parasocial interaction matter in driving user grati<sup>fi</sup>cation? (4) Does habit reduce the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation on continuance intention?

## Theoretical background

## U&G theory

U&G theory states that individuals’ media use is motivated by a set of social or psychological needs. In ful<sup>fi</sup>lling their needs, people are active and goal oriented in selecting certain media and content for consumption (Katz et al, 1974). In other words, individuals who use the media are motivated by goals and uses that are self-de<sup>fi</sup>ned, and they actively participate in the communication process, which can improve, restrict and otherwise affect the effects of exposure. The theory posits that people expect grati<sup>fi</sup>cation as an outcome of their media use experience and are likely to visit the same media offerings again if their needs are grati<sup>fi</sup>ed. Katz et al (1974) proposed three principal objectives of U&G: (1) explaining how people use media to gratify their needs; (2) understanding the motivations of individual media behavior; and (3) identifying the consequences that follow from needs, motivations and behavior. In addition, Katz et al (1974) also provided a clear agenda for U&G theory: ‘(1) the social and psychological origins of (2) needs, which generate (3) expectations of (4) the mass media or other sources, which lead to (5) differential patterns of media exposure (or engagement in other activities), resulting in (6) need grati<sup>fi</sup>cations and (7) other consequences’ (p. 20).

In 1974, Rosengren argued that researchers should outline a paradigm for visualizing U&G graphically. Rosengren (1974) puri<sup>fi</sup>ed the fundamental components of several independent efforts to reestablish the essential tradition in the mass communications arena. He outlined a paradigm for U&G research and visualized these concepts graphically (Figure 2). Rosengren (1974) indicated that individuals are affected by the social environment and individual characteristics, generating their basic needs. To gratify their needs, individuals will perceive problems and seek solutions. These problems and solutions then act as the motivation for grati<sup>fi</sup>cation behavior derived from using the media, leading to one of two consequences: grati<sup>fi</sup>cation or non-grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. The <sup>fi</sup>nal perception (grati<sup>fi</sup>cation or non-grati<sup>fi</sup>cation) will affect the individual or society, and so the cycle will start afresh.

According to Katz et al (1974), ‘understanding the motivations of individual media behavior’ is one of the principal objectives of U&G theory. However, U&G theory lacked a precise explanation or speci<sup>fi</sup>c classi<sup>fi</sup>cation for motivation and became a theoretical knowledge gap. To <sup>fi</sup>ll that gap, this study integrates another theory into U&G theory and constructs the individual’s media use motivations from a theoretical perspective.

## MSD theory

Understanding the motivations for individual media use is one of the major purposes of this research. As the existing research focuses less on explaining why people rely on the medium to gain resources to meet their goals, this study uses MSD theory to elaborate on individual motivation with regard to medium use.

According to Ball-Rokeach (1985), MSD is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘a relationship in which the capacity of individuals to attain their goals is contingent upon the information resources of the media system’. If individuals, groups and organizations want to attain their goals, they must rely on the resources that other individuals, groups or systems control and vice versa. The media system is viewed as an IS that controls ‘dependency-engendering’ information resources. In other words, individuals who want to achieve their goals must rely on the media to access the required information resources.

MSD relations can be analyzed at the macro level (e.g., system) or the micro level (e.g., individual) (Ball-Rokeach et al, 1990). For instance, the macro level of MSD relations has been useful for examining the relationship between the media and other social systems, while the micro level of MSD relations demonstrates an individual’s personal goal-seeking in the media. This study focuses on the relationship between the media and individuals at the micro level, that is, individual MSD relations.

Individual MSD relations can be summarized according to three goals: understanding, orientation and play (Ball-Rokeach et al, 1984; Ball-Rokeach, 1985). According to DeFleur & Ball-Rokeach (1989), the goals of understanding focus on individuals’ needs to comprehend and explain the world around them and themselves within it. The goals of orientation center on the need to behave effectively and appropriately in interpersonal interactions and in the making of personal behavioral decisions. Goals of play focus on escapism and enjoyment (Ball-Rokeach et al, 1984). Each of these three goals is further conceptualized across individual and interpersonal (social) dimensions, yielding the following six types of MSD relations at the individual level: social understanding and selfunderstanding, action orientation and interaction orientation, and solitary play and social play (Ball-Rokeach et al, 1984) (see Table 1).

The motivations for media use in different contexts found in previous studies are similar to individual MSD relations. According to Grant et al (1991), people develop dependency relations with the media as a means of attaining understanding, orientation and play goals. People are motivated to understand themselves and their social environment, obtain guidelines for behavior to direct their actions effectively and interact successfully with others, and have a means through which to release tension or to escape (DeFleur & Ball-Rokeach, 1989). Prior studies (e.g., Ball-Rokeach, 1985; Grant et al, 1991) conceptualized understanding dependency relations as the need for information-seeking, orientation dependency relations as the need for interpersonal connection, and play dependency relations as the need for entertainment. Table 2 shows how individual MSD relation dimensions map onto media use motivations. Accordingly, individual MSD relations may be treated as the motivations for individuals’ media use.

Consequently, individual MSD relations can be viewed as the media use motivation and be integrated with U&G theory. In U&G theory, the root question is ‘Where do I go to gratify my needs?’ (Grant et al, 1991). In other words, U&G theory focuses on individuals who can gratify their needs through the media. Prior U&G research has typically sought to identify and separate various dimensions of motives. However, it lacks a theoretical framework for constructing the individuals’ media use motivations. MSD theory focuses on the question ‘Why do I go to this medium to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll this goal?’ (Grant et al, 1991). In other words, it can provide the theoretical basis for explaining individuals’ media use motivations, which is lacking in U&G theory. Furthermore, individual MSD relations can be operationalized as personal and social dimensions to examine his or her media use motivations. Therefore, the integration of U&G and MSD can <sup>fi</sup>ll the theoretical gap of U&G. Consequently, the current research integrates these two theories to examine individuals’ media use behaviors.

## SNSs usage

SNSs have recently become an important research area for scholars interested in the social impact of online technologies (Boyd & Ellison, 2007; Ellison et al, 2007). User-generated media such as Facebook, YouTube, Wikipedia and Flickr have revolutionized the Internet by changing individuals’ communication, entertainment and information. Compared with traditional media, usergenerated media enable audiences (individuals) to generate their own content and play active roles. Audience activity lies at the core of U&G theory (Katz et al, 1974). Unlike earlier media approaches, such as hypodermic needle theory, two-step <sup>fl</sup>ow theory and agenda setting theory, which placed the audience in a passive role, U&G clari<sup>fi</sup>es the active role of the audience in medium selection as well as in selecting the content of the messages rather than just waiting for the media or messages to reach them passively.

SNSs usage has been examined from the perspectives of technology acceptance model (TAM) (Sledgianowski & Kulviwat, 2009) and social capital theory (Ellison et al, 2007). In terms of TAM, the major components (perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness) all highlight the utility of using information technology. Nevertheless, for people who use SNSs to maintain or expand their own social relationships, the ease of use or usefulness may not be a major concern. Our research emphasizes how people can increase their interaction with ‘friends’ online and obtain grati<sup>fi</sup>cation from this interaction. In addition to

TAM, social capital theory can also be used to explain SNSs usage (e.g., Ellison et al, 2007). According to Coleman (1988), social capital theory provides a framework for understanding how social relationships can be productive resources. For example, Chiu et al (2006) indicated that social interaction ties (the structural dimension), trust, the norm of reciprocity, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (the relational dimension), shared language and a shared vision (the cognitive dimension) were positively associated with the quantity and quality of knowledge sharing in virtual communities. However, these social capital factors may not be the motivations for individuals’ SNSs usage.

Recent SNSs studies also indicate that individuals rely on SNSs for information seeking, socializing and entertainment (e.g., Kim et al, 2011; Smock et al, 2011; Lee & Ma, 2012). In other words, most people use an SNS because they want to have fun with ‘friends’ or by themselves (play dependency), acquire suggestions from ‘friends’ to address their problems (orientation dependency), or learn the latest news around themselves (understanding dependency). Therefore, compared with prior theories, the integration of U&G and MSD can provide a more comprehensive theoretical basis for examining the individual’s media usage behaviors in the SNSs context. In contrast to traditional media, user-generated media, that is, an SNS, provide an interactive platform for individuals to gratify their information-seeking, interpersonalconnection and entertainment needs (goals). In addition, the major behavior on an SNS is the establishment or maintenance of a relationship with remote people (parasocial interaction). Then, a positive experience of media behavior (parasocial interaction) may induce grati<sup>fi</sup>cation of the medium usage, which in turn results in continued use of an SNS. In addition, this study examines the interaction effect of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and habit on continuance intention. The related constructs are introduced below.

## Parasocial interaction

The concept of parasocial interaction was <sup>fi</sup>rst introduced by Horton & Wohl (1956) to describe a seeming face-toface relationship between audience and performer. The authors argued that this form of relationship was ‘intimacy at a distance’ because the media personality appeared frequently and consistently. In addition, the friendly, attractive, trusted and unchanging features of the media personality gave the media users an ‘illusion of intimacy’ (Hoerner, 1999). Media users feel an apparently intimate, face-to-face association with the performer because the process by which the human brain interprets such experiences is the same whether the individual interacts with others appearing in the media or with others directly (Kanazawa, 2002). Ballantine & Martin (2005) also proposed that a parasocial interaction can be seen as similar to an interpersonal social relationship or friendship. When parasocial relationships develop over time, this relationship will entail increased certainty and liking (Berger, 1986). Therefore, parasocial interaction could be treated as a supplement, complement or substitute for interpersonal (face-to-face) interaction (Rubin et al, 1985).

In this study, parasocial interaction refers to individuals’ behavior in establishing and maintaining a relationship of friendship or intimacy with others using the SNSs. It focuses on interpersonal interaction. On the basis of Rosengren’s (1974) paradigm for U&G (Figure 2), parasocial interaction (media behavior) may induce grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, after which individuals’ grati<sup>fi</sup>cation increases the users loyalty toward the SNSs (repetitive behavior). Previous studies examined the role of parasocial interaction in several contexts, such as television news viewing (Rubin et al, 1985), television shopping programming (Grant et al, 1991) and online communities (Ballantine & Martin, 2005). However, prior research focused less on the individual’s parasocial interaction in the SNSs context. Therefore, this study would treat parasocial interaction as the media behavior of Rosengren’s (1974) U&G framework for examining individuals’ SNSs usage behaviors.

## Grati<sup>fi</sup>cation

According to Katz et al (1974), grati<sup>fi</sup>cation is the core concept of U&G theory. Recent studies also used U&G theory to examine the individual’s behavior in the SNSs context (Baek et al, 2011; Chen, 2011; Kim et al, 2011; Smock et al, 2011; Lee & Ma, 2012). However, previous U&G studies lacked a precise operational de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ‘grati<sup>fi</sup>cation’. Grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, as derived from U&G theory, emphasizes the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of social or psychological motives by consuming the media purposefully. On the basis of U&G theory, this study proposes that grati<sup>fi</sup>cation can be seen as the state in which needs and goals have been ful<sup>fi</sup>lled through purposeful media consumption. Hence, the term ‘grati<sup>fi</sup>cation’ is conceptually distinct from satisfaction.

Grati<sup>fi</sup>cation is distinct from satisfaction and focuses on the achievement of goals by using the media. In other words, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation emphasizes the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of an individual’s personal and social goals through media communication. Prior research paid attention on satisfaction, for instance, the pleasurable state resulting from the appraisal of one’s IS usage (Bhattacherjee, 2001) and consumption experience (Oliver, 1981). However, the positive ful<sup>fi</sup>llment state of interpersonal communication through media consumption (i.e., grati<sup>fi</sup>cation) has been overlooked in prior studies, thus becoming a knowledge gap. Therefore, this study examines grati<sup>fi</sup>cation in the SNSs context.

## Habit

Triandis (1980) de<sup>fi</sup>ned habit as ‘situation-behavior sequences that are or have become automatic … the individual is usually not conscious of these sequences’ (p. 204). Verplanken & Aarts (1999) highlighted the features of a habit. First, the habit has a history of repetition. If a behavior is performed more frequently, it is more likely to become habitual. Second, it is a kind of automaticity. Automatic behavior will be triggered by speci<sup>fi</sup>c cues in the context of an activated goal. The other four characteristics of habit are uncontrollability, lack of awareness, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and identity. According to Bargh (1996), habit is an automatic process that can be executed ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently, controlled to a limited extent and performed without awareness. In addition, habit is considered a way of minimizing cognitive effort in information processing (Verville, 1988).

Habitual media use has been considered as one of the major reasons for media consumption behaviors (LaRose et al, 2003). Diddi & LaRose (2006) de<sup>fi</sup>ned a media habit as individuals falling into a pattern of repeated media behavior without active self-observation. Wu et al (2010) argued that individuals extend the duration of their experience with the media and are willing to pay more attention to the media simply because of their media habits. Lindbladh & Lyttkens (2002) argued that habitual behavior is not only effortless but also ef<sup>fi</sup>cient because cognitive processing and the deliberate control of the individual are minimal. In addition, habits may produce positive effects, such as saving time or decreasing the mental effort involved in decision making (Rosenstein & Grant, 1997).

Prior U&G research may have largely underestimated the importance of habit in determining individuals’ media behavior (Rosenstein & Grant, 1997). According to Nisbett & Wilson (1977), the problem of U&G research is that people may have less introspective access to the higher order cognitive process that affected their behaviors. In other words, individuals’ media behaviors may mostly be based on their unconscious mind, that is, habit. According to Limayem et al (2007), habit can be seen as one of the antecedents of behavior or behavioral intention. In addition, prior studies, such as Khalifa et al (2002), Khalifa & Liu (2007) and Limayem et al (2007), have indicated that habit moderates the relationship between behavior/behavioral intention and its antecedents. It is reasonable to infer that unconscious habit would have an interaction effect with other conscious predictors of behavioral intention, such as grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. Consequently, treating habit as a moderator between grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention can produce a better understanding of SNSs usage behavior.

## SNSs continuance intention

According to a report of eMarketer (2013), nearly 1.73 billion people worldwide use social networks in 2013. In other words, almost a quarter of the world’s population uses SNSs to establish or maintain their social relationships on the Internet. However, individuals may not always be loyal to the SNSs they have used. In May 2011, Facebook not only lost 7 million active users in the United States and Canada but also lost more than 100,000 in the United Kingdom, Norway and Russia (Gross, 2011). Meanwhile, ‘half of Americans think Facebook is a passing fad, according to the results of a new Associated Press-CNBC poll’ (Arbel, 2013). Therefore, individuals’ continued use of an SNS is important to the development and survival of that SNSs.

An individual’s continuance intention is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the intention to continue using an IT’ (Deng et al, 2010). Studies have also used this construct to measure individual behavior in several different <sup>fi</sup>elds, including IS usage (Bhattacherjee, 2001), online auction usage (Wang & Chiang, 2009) and SNSs usage (Sledgianowski & Kulviwat, 2009). Therefore, this study models SNSs continuance intention as the dependent variable for measuring the subjective probability that an individual will continue to use SNSs in the future.

## Hypotheses

On the basis of Rosengren’s (1974) U&G paradigm (Figure 2), this study proposes a research model for SNSs usage (Figure 1). Building on U&G theory, this study investigates the motives for grati<sup>fi</sup>cation-seeking (motives), positing that such behaviors result in different patterns of actual media consumption (media behavior). Finally, media consumption behavior is categorized as gratification or nongratification. Therefore, in this study, the six dependency relations (i.e., self and social understanding, action and interaction orientation, and solitary and social play dependency relations) together form the concept of motives, parasocial interaction represents the concept of media behavior, and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation re<sup>fl</sup>ects the concept of gratification in Rosengren’s (1974) paradigm. In addition, SNSs continuance intention (dependent variable) represents repetitive behavior and habit, which should also be considered as a moderator. Figure 1 represents the theoretical relationship among motives, media behavior, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, habit and continuance intention.

## Understanding dependency relations and parasocia interaction

Understanding dependency relations can be conceptualized in terms of the dimensions of social understanding and social self-understanding. DeFleur & Ball-Rokeach (1989) proposed that social understanding dependency relations are evident when individuals use media information resources to understand and interpret people, cultures and events of the present, past and future. In addition, they also indicated that people can depend on the media to acquire information resources for better understanding the social environment and meet their goals. According to MSD relations, self-understanding dependency relations represent the development of expanding or maintaining people’s capacity to explain their own beliefs, behavior, self-concepts or personality. People can obtain diverse and personally relevant topics from media ISs and learn to be assertive and self-con<sup>fi</sup>dent to overcome personal crises and failures and to actualize the self through work, hobbies and personal relations (Ball-Rokeach et al, 1984).

According to MSD theory, understanding dependency relations focuses on individuals’ needs to develop a better understanding of themselves and discover the latest events around them (Grant et al, 1991). Rubin et al (1985) proposed that the parasocial relationship is related to using the media instrumentally. Therefore, individuals’ instrumental media use, such as seeking information to gain insights into themselves or for understanding the news around them, may be associated with parasocial interaction. In other words, the individual’s perception of the reality of the information leads to a greater af<sup>fi</sup>nity with the information provider. They also indicated that information motivation can be used to predict parasocial interaction with the information provider. Perse (1990) also argued that parasocial interaction originated from seeking information from the media. In other words, individuals who have greater information-seeking motivations will have more parasocial interactions with their ‘friends’ on an SNS.

Understanding dependency relations positively affect parasocial interaction.

## Orientation dependency relations and parasocial interaction

In MSD theory, orientation dependencies can be formulated as ‘how to act and interact with others’. DeFleur & Ball-Rokeach (1989) proposed that action orientation dependency relations are developed to guide the individual’s own speci<sup>fi</sup>c behaviors. In addition, people usually depend on media information not only for everyday coping behaviors but also for more important life events. Interaction orientation dependency relations raise questions about what to say, what others are like and how one should behave (Ball-Rokeach et al, 1984). This category refers to obtaining guidance on dealing with personal relationships or developing effective conversational, social and interpersonal skills (Loges, 1994).

In light of MSD theory, when interpersonal interactions are limited by reality, individuals depend on the media to obtain advice about how to act and interact with others to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their interpersonal connection needs. They thus may develop parasocial relationships with advice providers. According to Rubin et al (1985), parasocial interaction is an interpersonal effect that grows out of an information orientation. They argued that individuals form interpersonal attachments with someone who provides kind, personal guidance about their concerns (Levy, 1979). Rosengren & Windahl (1972) argued that the individual’s need to seek social interaction fosters parasocial interaction. Further, Wright (1978) indicated that friendship is grounded partly in how other people help individuals to gratify their needs. Therefore, online ‘friends’ who offer suggestions to guidance-seeking individuals will be perceived more favorably.

Orientation dependency relations positively affect parasocial interaction.

## Play dependency relations and parasocial interaction

The term ‘play’ connotes escapist goals and the enjoyment of entertaining activities. The same classi<sup>fi</sup>cation as understanding and orientation dependency relations, individual vs social distinction is also made with regard to play dependency relations. According to MSD relations, solitary play dependency relations refer to when the individual’s major goal is to gain pleasure, esthetic experience, stimulation or relaxation from media content. In addition, the absence or presence of others is secondary because the major concern is the message content itself, as when an individual is watching a movie or attending a concert. Social play dependency relations are developed to facilitate social inter-communication between people. This refers to the enjoyment or escape into and through the company of others (Loges, 1994). In contrast to solitary play, the media content is less important than mutually enjoyable experiences with friends, family or others who coparticipate in the same media behavior (Ball-Rokeach et al, 1984; DeFleur & Ball-Rokeach, 1989).

On the basis of MSD theory, people devote much of their media use time to gratifying their entertainment motivations. They not only rely on the media to escape from reality and reduce their stress but also use the media as a form of recreation and to share their pleasurable experiences with their friends and family. By using the SNSs for escapism and enjoyment, such as passing the time or having fun with ‘friends’, people come to perceive their experiences as pleasurable and are more willing to increase their interaction with ‘friends’. In addition, Cole & Leets (1999) argued that parasocial interaction stems from people’s intention to attain pleasure and avoid pain. In other words, individuals who want to relax or have fun with ‘friends’ online may increase their interactions with entertainment providers. Therefore, the greater the play dependency, the more magni<sup>fi</sup>ed will be the effects of parasocial interaction. In addition, parasocial interaction can be predicted by feeling happy when consuming the medium (Perse, 1990).

Play dependency relations positively affect parasocial interaction.

## Parasocial interaction and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation

In this study, parasocial interaction is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as establishing or maintaining an individual’s relationship of friendship or intimacy with a remote user (i.e., a ‘friend’) on an SNS. It focuses on interpersonal interaction rather than on the interaction between users and the SNSs. In addition, parasocial interaction is often called ‘intimacy at a distance’ or the ‘illusion of intimacy’ (Horton & Wohl, 1956). The basic concept of parasocial interaction is that the users perceive the remote person on the media as friendly, attractive, trustworthy and unchanging (Horton & Wohl, 1956). Therefore, individuals who interact with ‘friends’ online will increase their grati<sup>fi</sup>cation through their SNSs usage experience. Levy (1979) also found that parasocial interaction was a common experience that was rooted in affective ties to media personalities. In other words, the more interactions with online ‘friends’, the greater the increase in individual grati<sup>fi</sup>cation experienced by using the SNSs.

Parasocial interaction positively affects gratification.

## Grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and SNSs continuance intention

According to Deci & Ryan (1985), if people feel happy about doing something, they are self-determined and intrinsically motivated to continue to do that thing. An affective response is associated with extreme arousal, leading to a focus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c targets. Therefore, the response can have a powerful effect on ongoing behavior (Patterson & Spreng, 1997). Thus, when people obtain a positive effect, such as grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, from online social interaction, their choice of action will be affected by these positive emotions. According to social exchange theory, the norm of reciprocity implies that members strive to balance what they contribute to the SNSs with what they receive from it. When SNSs users are grati<sup>fi</sup>ed with their online interaction experience, they are more likely to reciprocate by engaging in bene<sup>fi</sup>cial behaviors toward the virtual community, such as loyalty or continued use.

Gratification positively affects SNSs continuance intention.

## Moderating effect of habit between grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention

According to Palmgreen & Rayburn (1985), individuals are aware of the grati<sup>fi</sup>cation obtained via media behavior, whereas habit is an unconscious act (Martin, 2008). Aarts et al (1998) saw habitual behavior as an automatic behavioral response that is not preceded by a cognitive analysis process because of the learned association between human behavior and satisfactory results. Given the above, it is reasonable to theorize that habit affects the relationship between grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention.

According to Martin (2008), unconscious habit controls most human behavior. When individuals form a habit, their behavior depends mostly on the unconscious mind rather than on conscious judgment (e.g., grati<sup>fi</sup>cation). Therefore, the effect of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation on continuance intention will be decreased by habit. Conversely, if individuals have not developed strong habitual behaviors, their cognitive evaluations, such as grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, may play an important role in guiding their behaviors. Accordingly, habit attenuates the strength of the relationship between grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention. According to Jolley et al (2006), human behavior may be more easily predicted if the joint effect of the unconscious habit and conscious satisfaction are considered. While researchers have examined the interaction between habit and satisfaction in the context of online gambling (Jolley et al, 2006) and online shopping (Khalifa et al, 2002; Khalifa & Liu, 2007), they have focused on the affective response, which is derived from online gambling and shopping and has not received attention in the context of the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of personal and social goals (i.e., grati<sup>fi</sup>cation) in the SNSs context. In addition, prior research, such as Khalifa & Liu (2007) and Khalifa et al (2002), mainly examined the positive moderating role of habit between satisfaction and repurchase intention/stickiness and was less focused on whether habit would negatively moderate the relationship between grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention in the SNSs context.

SNSs usage habit negatively moderates the relationship between gratification and continuance intention toward the SNSs.

## Research methodology

Following Chin et al (1997) and Salisbury et al (2002), this research is conducted in three phases: (1) the initial item development; (2) the instrument testing, re<sup>fi</sup>nement and data collection phase; and (3) the model testing and con<sup>fi</sup>rmation phase.

## Phase 1: initial item development

It is important to determine whether constructs should be measured formatively or re<sup>fl</sup>ectively early in the measurement development process (Hardin et al 2008a, b). Following Hardin et al (2008a, b), the psychological constructs are best measured using re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators. In addition, covariance among re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators is expected, and the re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators can be added or dropped without the alteration of conceptual meaning. According to DeFleur & Ball-Rokeach (1989, p. 311), MSD relations envision a cognitive psychological process that increases the probability of one’s being affected by particular media content. In addition, the indicators (<sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs) of each second-order construct are conceptually correlated. Third, dropping an indicator from the measurement model does not alter the meaning of the construct (second-order construct). For example, dropping either selfunderstanding or social-understanding from understanding dependency relations does not alter the conceptual domain of ‘questions of meaning and knowledge’ (DeFleur & Ball-Rokeach, 1989, p. 306). Therefore, the measurement of MSD relations (motives) in this study is operationalized as re<sup>fl</sup>ective second-order constructs.

The measurement items (see Appendix) were adopted from the existing literature and adapted to the SNSs context. The items were measured on a 7-point Likert scale with anchors from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). Moreover, to control for an explanation of results due to extraneous factors, gender, age, Internet experience and Facebook tenure were also incorporated into the model as control variables. First, gender and age have been shown to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on users’ acceptance of technology (Venkatesh et al, 2003). Second, because online social networking involves Internet use, lack of Internet experience impedes individuals from engaging in online social networking. Hence, Internet experience is proposed as a control variable on continuance intention. Third, Zheng et al (2013) proposed that tenure had a positive effect on the continuing intention to consume. Hence, it may be possible that individuals who have lengthy tenure on Facebook will perceive it to be valuable for exchanging social support with others and may have a stronger motivation to maintain their relationship with the SNSs.

## Phase 2: instrument testing, re<sup>fi</sup>nement and data collection

Instrument testing and re<sup>fi</sup>nement A pre-test and pilot-test were conducted using data collected from Facebook users in Taiwan (see Appendix). The results indicate that the measurement items ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the criteria for reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity.

Data collection The research model was tested with data collected from Facebook users in Taiwan. Facebook was chosen as the target SNSs site because it is the second most popular site in the world, with more than a billion members (Alexa, 2014 traf<sup>fi</sup>c rankings; Facebook Newsroom, 2014). Furthermore, Facebook is the dominant player in the SNSs industry, growing daily. To target Facebook users, a hyperlink leading to the survey website was posted on several local bulletin board systems, online forums and virtual communities. However, there is a potential for selfselection bias because participants volunteered for the study. These volunteers may have been biased toward a more positive outlook on SNSs usage. The self-selection bias is also discussed in the limitations.

The purpose of this study and a guarantee of con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality were declared on the <sup>fi</sup>rst page of the questionnaire. After we <sup>fi</sup>nished the data collection process, 32 randomly selected respondents were contacted via e-mail to obtain their contact information to mail them the incentives. To increase the fun and entertainment of completing the questionnaire, this study conducts a special incentive distribution consisting of three different incentive levels: U.S.\$18 (2 persons), \$11 (10 persons) and \$7 (20 persons). The incentive distribution was conducted after the a collection process was completed. Therefore, the different incentives did not cause method bias. After the data collection, there were 657 valid responses for the data analysis.

Usable responses were collected from 294 female respondents (44.7%) and 363 male respondents (55.3%). The majority of the respondents were aged 20–29 (70.3%), followed by 30–39 (18.0%), under 20 (7.8%) and over 40 (4.0%). The majority had a university education (65.4%), followed by graduate school (21.5%), high school (7.6%) and junior college (5.5%). Approximately 82.5% of the respondents had used the Internet for more than 7 years, with 83.4% having more than 1 year of Facebook tenure.

## Phase 3: model testing and con<sup>fi</sup>rmation

This study uses a two-step approach recommended by Anderson & Gerbing (1988) to conduct the data analysis. The aim of the two-step approach is to establish reliability and validity of the measures before assessing the structural relationships within the model. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>rst step involves the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the measurement model and the second step validates the structural relationships among the latent constructs.

According to Gefen et al (2000), covariance-based SEM (e.g., LISREL, AMOS) is superior to components-based SEM (e.g., PLS-Graph, SmartPLS) in terms of parameter estimates, model <sup>fi</sup>t and model analyses. Therefore, we used LISREL 8.51 to analyze the measurement model and the structural model.

Measurement model Con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) was applied to test the adequacy of the measurement model. The adequacy of the measurement model was evaluated on the criteria of model <sup>fi</sup>t, reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity.

To ensure our SNSs usage model has suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently good model fit. five model-fit indices were used to assess the measurement model: normed chi-square $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { D F } )$ , adjusted goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t index (AGFI), non-normed <sup>fi</sup>t index (NNFI), comparative <sup>fi</sup>t index (CFI) and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). For our CFA model, $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { D F }$ was 2.18 (χ<sup>2</sup> = 1964.28; DF = 900), AGFI was 0.858, NNFI was 0.955, CFI was 0.962 and RMSEA was 0.042. The <sup>fi</sup>t indices surpass the recommendations suggested by Jőreskog & Sőrbom (1993) and Chau (1997), suggesting adequate model <sup>fi</sup>t.

Reliability of the measurement items was examined using composite reliability (CR) values. It is suggested that CR values should be above 0.70 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981) to indicate adequate reliability. Table 4 shows that all of the CR values were well above 0.7 thresholds. To establish convergent validity, all indicator loadings on their corresponding constructs exceed 0.70 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). For the research model, all indicators had loadings above 0.7 except for PSI5. (see Appendix). However, it was still above the minimal standard of 0.6 suggested by Bagozzi & Yi (1988). In addition, Hair et al (2010) also indicated that when the sample sizes are greater than 350, factor loadings of 0.30 can obtain a power level of 80% at a 0.05 significance level. Therefore, in a sample of 657, a factor loading of 0.688 is acceptable. Another criterion for evaluating convergent validity suggests that the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct should be 0.5 or greater (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Table 4 shows that all of the values of AVE range from 0.528 to 0.907, indicating adequate convergent validity.

## Discussions and implications

## Summary of the results

There are two major <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study: (1) MSD relations can be considered individual motivations and can be successfully integrated into U&G theory, and (2) habit exerts a negatively moderating effect on the relationship between grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention in the SNSs context.

As predicted, the three second-order constructs, understanding, orientation and play dependency relations, are multidimensional in nature and positively affect parasocial interaction. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that individuals use SNSs to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a variety of understanding, orientation and play motivations, such as gaining insights into themselves, keeping up with world events, obtaining guidelines for their behaviors, learning how to interact with others and entertaining friends or themselves. The more they rely on SNSs to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll those motivations, the more they use them and the greater the perception of intimacy they develop with their resource providers. Prior studies have also found that people engage in parasocial interaction with the media because they want to gratify their information, guidance and entertainment motivations (Levy, 1979; Rubin et al, 1985; Conway & Rubin, 1991). Comparing the importance of the three SNSs usage motivations, play dependency relations play a dominant role, followed by orientation dependency relations and understanding dependency relations. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are similar to those reported by Wedbush Securities (2011), who indicated that 37% of their members play games on Facebook. According to EContent (2011), playing games and streaming videos have become frequent online social activities. In other words, as individuals focus on various forms of play, SNSs are gradually becoming entertainment platforms (EContent, 2011).

Parasocial interaction has a positive effect on grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, which in turn has a positive effect on users’ SNSs continuance intentions. The <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with that of Levy & Windahl (1984), which indicates that personal involvement in<sup>fl</sup>uences individuals’ sense of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation regarding media use. In our study, SNSs users who perceived that they enjoyed a greater intimacy with their ‘friends’ obtained more grati<sup>fi</sup>cation from their usage experiences. Furthermore, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation can be seen as a predictor of continuance intention. In this research, the higher the level of individual grati<sup>fi</sup>cation received from using a given SNS, the higher the individual’s continuance intention toward an SNS.

In terms of the interaction effect, this study examines the differing effect of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation on continuance intention depending on the particular level of habit. On the basis of the results, the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the moderating effect is 0.10 (P<0.001) and the effect size of the interaction effect is 0.03, which is a small effect. The small effect seems to indicate that grati<sup>fi</sup>cation is more affective oriented than cognitive oriented. Therefore, it continues to play an important role in shaping continuance intention. In other words, habit may have less of a moderating effect on the relationship between affective-oriented grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention.

With respect to the control variables, we found that gender, age, Internet experience and Facebook tenure did not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence SNSs continuance intention. Although prior studies found that gender, age, Internet experience and tenure had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on stickiness intention or the intention to continue to consume (e.g., Li et al, 2006; Zheng et al, 2013), these variables were not examined in the SNSs context. Another possible explanation is that grati<sup>fi</sup>cation has a strong effect on SNSs continuance intention (β = 0.68, P<0.001). Therefore, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation may have a suppressive effect, that is, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation may weaken or conceal the strength of the effect of the control variables on SNSs continuance intention.

## Implications for theory

On the basis of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research, we would like to share several points that are worthy of consideration in theory development. First, this study integrates two mass communication theories, U&G theory and MSD theory, to examine individuals’ online social network behaviors. Many studies have investigated individual motivation regarding SNSs behavior using U&G, including Kim et al (2011), Smock et al (2011), Baek et al (2011) and Lee & Ma (2012). However, the absence of any theoretical consideration of an individual’s motivation has led to a gap in the development of the theory. Previous research has either classi<sup>fi</sup>ed the different types of SNSs motivations (e.g., Johnson & Yang, 2009) or examined the effect of motivations on behavioral intention or usage (e.g., Smock et al, 2011; Lee & Ma, 2012). To address this knowledge gap, this research uses MSD relations to elaborate the role of motivation. In other words, this study identi<sup>fi</sup>es the media usage motivations from the theoretical framework of MSD.

Second, this study further operationalizes MSD relations as re<sup>fl</sup>ective, second-order variables for comprehending SNSs user motivations more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally (i.e., the six levels of dependency relations between the individual and the medium). In other words, this study conceptualizes the motivations from the personal and social perspectives. Previous studies have usually treated MSD relations as unidimensional measures (Ball-Rokeach et al 1984; Grant et al 1991; Ballantine & Martin, 2005). Although a unidimensional measure of MSD relations has been shown to have a high level of internal consistency among media effects (Ball-Rokeach et al, 1984), this approach cannot evaluate the nature of the relationship between the individual and the medium with a high degree of precision. Our results indicate that MSD relations are multifaceted, providing a meaningful addition to the theory.

Third, by examining the relationship among MSD relations, parasocial interaction, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention in the SNSs context, this study highlights the linkage among motives, media behavior, grati<sup>fi</sup>cations and repetitive behavior, which is emphasized in U&G theory.

Fourth, our results show the negative moderating role of habit between grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention. Many studies have considered the moderating role of habit in online contexts, including Khalifa et al (2002) and Khalifa & Liu (2007). However, these studies explored the positive role of habit as it affects satisfaction and behavioral intention or stickiness and investigated it in an online shopping context. Prior research had paid less attention to the individual’s grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and has not examined the negative moderating role of habit in the SNSs context. According to the results, the interaction effect size is 0.03, and the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between the interaction term (i.e., habit×grati<sup>fi</sup>cation) and continuance intention is 0.10 (P < 0.001), which is lower than those by Khalifa et al (2002), that is, the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between the interaction term (i.e., habit × satisfaction) and stickiness/repurchases is 0.215 (P < 0.001), and the effect size is 0.048. This may be because satisfaction is more cognitive oriented, and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation is more affective oriented. Therefore, habit may have a small suppressant effect on the relationship between affective-oriented grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuance intention in the SNSs context.

## Implications for practice

Our study offers several implications for practice. Regarding orientation dependency relations, SNSs should provide a ‘video calling’ function for meeting and talking with real people online. They should offer ‘face-to-face-to-face’ applications (multi-person video chat), which can group people in a chat room to help individuals solve their current problems or give someone suggestions immediately for enhanced interpersonal interaction. With respect to play orientation, although various games are already available on Facebook, most of them are not multiplayer based and are played individually. For instance, consider Candy Crush Saga, the popular application on Facebook. Players in Candy Crush Saga can ask ‘friends’ for more lives when they fail to meet the level’s objective or ask ‘friends’ to ‘unlock’ the episodes for them. However, this ‘help’ from ‘friends’ is asynchronous. Players may send the request to ‘friends’ and wait for their responses. In other words, players and their ‘friends’ cannot play the game at the same time. Therefore, SNSs should offer multiplayer games, which can provide a relaxed space for people to have fun with their ‘friends’. To understand dependency relations, SNSs should actively provide users with information that addresses their interests. For example, they can look for videos or articles, which their users will like, so that when the users log onto the website, there is always something interesting to watch, read or share with their ‘friends’. Therefore, to attract users and increase their continuance intention, SNSs providers should pay attention to these preferences when they design and develop websites.

Individuals’ arrival at a state of ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of their goals through purposeful media consumption can be seen as grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. Their feelings of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, which are derived from pleasurable experiences, can contribute to SNSs-related repetitive behaviors. Therefore, gratifying their members is an important goal for SNSs providers. In pursuit of this goal, SNSs should provide gratifying experiences for users, such as making the user interface more attractive or developing new IT features to be embedded in the SNSs. In addition, because grati<sup>fi</sup>cation is a pleasureand emotion-oriented concept, SNSs managers should provide novel, fun support tools (e.g., photo sharing, message sharing, video sharing), services and games, which can intensify the positive experience of using the SNSs.

Finally, habit can be seen as an alternative to grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. For that reason, understanding the individuals’ habitual behaviors and discovering the reasons of their habit formation become important to SNSs providers. According to Martin (2008), convenience is the critical factor in habit formation. Therefore, SNSs providers need to make the interfaces of SNSs as intuitive as possible and design the layout with ease of use in mind. Online users may not switch from their current SNSs if the site is visually appealing, intuitively designed and provides various types of applications and games. Most importantly, SNSs managers should pay attention to developing more compatible, value-added and complementary applications or services, such as services that are mobile-compatible or that synchronize members’ SNSs contact lists or personal information with their mobile devices. In addition, SNSs providers should offer more support tools or valuable applications, such as calendars or recommendation systems that recommend articles, information or websites to members. In addition, privacy and security considerations may affect users’ habit formation. Habitual use may not arise if online users are frustrated or disappointed by slow video downloads or requests to install additional players or applications.

## Limitations

Despite our careful research design, there are still several limitations. First, the data were collected from a single SNSs site: Facebook. Although it is the most popular SNSs site in the world, the generalizability of the model and <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other SNSs requires additional research. Second, the respondents to the survey were limited to Taiwanese Facebook users. According to Kim et al (2011), the cultural contexts (American vs Korean) signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly differentiate the motivations for using social networks. Therefore, this study may have limited applicability to other countries. Third, as the data are cross-sectional, the statistically supported relationships can only be viewed as correlational. Fourth, our study may have been affected by self-selection bias. The sample of this research consists of active users. Users who have already stopped using Facebook may have different perceptions of the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the three motivations (i.e., understanding, orientation and play dependency relations), parasocial interaction, grati<sup>fi</sup>- cation and habit. Thus, the results of this study should be interpreted as explaining the continuance intention of current online users only. Additional research is necessary if future studies seek to extend our results to non-users or disaffected users. Finally, we did not examine the moderating role of of<sup>fl</sup>ine intimacy. According to Turel & Serenko (2012), individuals employed the SNSs to stay in touch with close friends and relatives (89%). In other words, a high percentage of people use SNSs to maintain pre-existing social connections. Ross et al (2009) also indicated that Facebook demonstrated an of<sup>fl</sup>ine-to-online trend, that is, the majority of Facebook friends are met of<sup>fl</sup>ine and then added later. Therefore, the of<sup>fl</sup>ine intimacy may strengthen the effect of users’ parasocial interaction. However, this study does not examine the moderating effect of of<sup>fl</sup>ine intimacy on the relationship between parasocial interaction and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation.

## Future research

According to Hill (1987), the need for social contact is an essential human motivation, driven by the need to seek and enjoy relationships with others. Baumeister & Leary (1995) also state that ‘human beings have a pervasive drive to form and maintain at least a minimum quantity of lasting, positive, and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interpersonal relationships’ (p. 497). Therefore, people may use an SNS to gratify their desire to af<sup>fi</sup>liate with others. Individuals desire social contact with others through SNSs for at least four reasons. First, they may <sup>fi</sup>nd social contact stimulating or exciting (positive stimulation). Second, social contact may generate attention or praise. Third, people may obtain emotional support from others when feeling low. Finally, through engaging in social contact with others, people can acquire opportunities for social comparison (Hill, 1987). Therefore, future researchers may divide the af<sup>fi</sup>liation motivation into four sub-constructs (positive stimulation, attention, emotional support and social comparison) when examining individual motivations for using an SNS. ## Conclusion

To better understand SNSs continuance intention, this study expands the horizons of U&G research, which has been applied regularly to traditional media use, by integrating MSD theory. Drawing upon Ball-Rokeach et al’s (1984) six types of MSD relations at the individual level, we recognized suitable resources for enhancing indivi duals’ parasocial interaction. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that SNSs users strive for understanding, orientation and play dependency relations, and ful<sup>fi</sup>lling those goals fosters users’ parasocial interaction and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, and subsequently fosters their continuance intention. We believe that the model proposed in this paper is not conceptually limited to SNSs but should also be applicable to other online services. Certainly, more effort should be directed to further reveal the complex relationships among motives, media behavior, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and repetitive behavior. We hope that the model proposed in this study can lay a useful foundation for future work in this important area.
