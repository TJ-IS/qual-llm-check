# BRIDGING THE SERVICE DIVIDE THROUGH DIGITALLY ENABLED SERVICE INNOVATIONS: EVIDENCE FROM INDIAN HEALTHCARE SERVICE PROVIDERS<sup>1</sup>

Shirish C. Srivastava

Department of Operations Management and Information Technology, HEC Paris, 1 Rue de la Libération, Jouy en Josas Cedex, 79351 FRANCE {srivastava@hec.fr}

G. Shainesh

Department of Marketing, Indian Institute of Management Bangalore, Bannerghatta Road, Bangalore, 560 076 INDIA {shaineshg@iimb.ernet.in}

The digital divide is usually conceptualized through goods-dominant logic, where bridging the divide entails providing digital goods to disadvantaged segments of the population. This is expected to enhance their digital capabilities and thus to have a positive influence on the digital outcomes (or services) experienced. In contrast, this study is anchored in an alternative service-dominant logic and posits that viewing the divide from a service perspective might be better suited to the context of developing countries, where there is a huge divide across societal segments in accessing basic services such as healthcare and education. This research views the prevailing differences in the level of services consumed by different population segments (service divide) as the key issue to be addressed by innovative digital tools in developing countries. The study posits that information and communication technologies (ICTs) can be leveraged to bridge the service divide to enhance the capabilities of service-disadvantaged segments of society. But such service delivery requires an innovative assembly of ICT as well as non-ICT resources. Building on concepts from service-dominant logic and service science, this paper aims to understand how such service innovation efforts can be orchestrated. Specifically, adopting a process view, two Indian enterprises that have developed sustainable telemedicine healthcare service delivery models for the rural population in India are examined. The study traces the configuration of three interactional resources—knowledge, technology, and institutions—through which value-creating user centric objectives of increasing geographical access and reducing cost are achieved. The theoretical contributions are largely associated with unearthing and understanding how the three interactional resources were orchestrated for service-centric value creation in different combinative patterns as resource exploitation, resource combination, and value reinforcement. The analysis also reveals the three distinct stages of service innovation evolution (idea and launch, infancy and early growth, and late growth and expansion), with a distinct shift in the dominant resource for each stage. Through an inductive process, the study also identifies four key enablers for successfully implementing these ICT-enabled service innovations: obsessive customer empathy, belief in the transformational power of ICT, continuous recursive learning, and efficient network orchestration.

Keywords: Service innovation, developing countries, service divide, healthcare, process view, service science, service systems, social entrepreneurship, society, digital divide, India, institutions

## Introduction

The proliferation of information and communication technologies (ICTs)<sup>2</sup> is often seen as a panacea for achieving multiple policy objectives. Realizing the transformational power of ICTs, governments are making significant investments in ICT infrastructures (Dutta and Mia 2010). But the overall growth of ICT resources in a nation may not always signify a balanced economic growth. Inequitable distribution of ICT resources amongst different societal groups, commonly referred to as the digital divide, may in fact serve to intensify existing social stratifications (Avgerou and Madon 2005; Ching et al. 2005; Wei et al. 2011). In their three-level digital divide framework, Wei et al. (2011) demonstrate how a divide in the access to digital resources may result in a digital capability divide that leads to a divide in the experienced outcomes. Similar to most prior digital divide research, this framework is grounded in a goods-centric view, which emphasizes provision of digital goods such as computers for achieving the anticipated outcomes (Dewan et al. 2010; Hsieh et al. 2008; Madon 2005). Such conceptualization of the digital divide is valuable in scenarios where (1) governments have sufficient resources to provide the necessary digital goods, and (2) the society in general is capable enough to transform the digital goods into desired outcomes. However, this view may have limited applicability in developing countries where the majority of the population does not even have access to basic capabilities such as healthcare and education (Dreze and Sen 2013; Sen 1985, 1988, 1989). In such contexts, fostering these basic capabilities has greater salience than focusing on developing digital capabilities. Such basic capabilities can be conceptualized as services that one system provides to another through value-creating provider-user interactions (see Rai and Sambamurthy 2006; Vargo and Lusch 2004). ICT can certainly be leveraged to provide these basic services to the disadvantaged segments of a society (Madon 2004, 2005), but to do this effectively, it would be useful to conceptualize the divide in the society from a service-centric view (i.e., according to the level of basic services consumed by different societal groups). In this research, which is contextualized in a developing country setting, we therefore extend the digital divide perspective to that of a service divide to better understand the role of ICT in facilitating equitable development. We posit that in developing countries, bridging the service divide is of greater concern than bridging the digital divide. But bridging the service divide entails significant challenges, and examining how this can be done effectively will be of value to both research and practice.

In many developing countries, including India (the context of the current study), governments have inadequate resources for providing basic services such as healthcare and education to the society at large. This void portends a business opportunity for private entrepreneurs, who therefore take on the task of delivering such services—albeit for profit (Khanna and Palepu 1997). These entrepreneurs tend to service the more profitable urban segments, which further accentuates the service divide, with the majority of the population— especially in rural areas—having minimal or no access to these basic services. However, the private entrepreneurs include some social entrepreneurs who are not driven by profit alone, but also by a desire to serve disadvantaged sections of society. Such entrepreneurs provide basic services by innovatively designing contextualized yet economically sustainable solutions (Mair et al. 2007). Although highly contextualized, the service innovations often involve extensive use of ICTs to facilitate value creation for the targeted population segments (Barrett and Davidson 2008; Rai and Sambamurthy 2006; Srivastava et al. 2013). Clearly, understanding the value-creating mechanisms facilitating such service innovations will have theoretical and practical relevance. But value creation in a developing country’s complex societal set up also depends on multiple contextual contingencies, which must be suitably incorporated in the theorization (see Bamberger 2008; Johns 2006; Krishna and Madon 2003).

Using service-dominant logic and concepts from the service science literature, we posit that the value creation in such a scenario requires combining available ICTs with other available resources. Such value-creating combinations do not arise solely through the presence of tangible resources owned by the service provider (social entrepreneur) and/or the service user (society), but are orchestrated through valuecreating interactions across the provider and user systems. Resources, both tangible and intangible, that facilitate valuecreating interactions across the provider and user are termed interactional resources. For example, shared knowledge belonging to either the provider or the user system that is used to govern interactions between them can facilitate value creation. Various combinations of interactional resources across the provider and user service systems provide the different value-creating mechanisms for service innovations. In this study, we theorize the value-creating mechanisms involving three interactional resources—knowledge, technology, and institutions—orchestrated across the service provider and user systems for creating the desired value.

The study examines telemedicine initiatives by two large private healthcare service providers in India that address the healthcare service divide by providing rural populations with affordable quality eye care. We use the case studies to investigate two questions:

1. How can interactional resources be orchestrated across the provider and user service systems to create service innovations aimed at bridging the service divide in the context of developing countries, specifically India?

2. What are the key enablers that facilitate delivery of ICTenabled service innovations to bridge the service divide in the context of developing countries, specifically India?

Our research is expected to make several contributions. First, we conceptualize the digital divide through desired outcomes (a service perspective) as a service divide, and we propose that, in the context of developing countries, this conceptualization is more effective for better equipping the society with basic capabilities such as healthcare and education by leveraging ICT. Building on the digital divide literature, we propose that the role of ICT in bridging the service divide in developing countries needs to be explicitly theorized in order to better appreciate the challenges in facilitating inclusive growth. Second, our study advances work on digitally enabled service innovation by theorizing the process of service-centric value creation. Specifically, using the case studies, we delineate and trace value-creating mechanisms as they evolved for the two initiatives. We also identify the key enablers facilitating service-centered value creation. Third, we contribute to the service science literature by conceptualizing interactional resources as a shared operationalization of resources individually owned by the provider and user service systems. Specifically, we identify and describe the mechanisms through which these shared interactional resources are leveraged to implement valuable ICT-enabled service innovations. The practical value of this research lies in demonstrating the applicability of digitally enabled service innovations to provide quality service to previously underserved segments of a society.

## Background Literature and Theoretical Framework

## Goods- and Service-Dominant Logics

Value creation is the key reason for any economic exchange. Traditional models of value creation are based on goodsdominant (G-D) logic, in which a firm’s output is the source of value creation. G-D logic is based on a push philosophy, viewing produced goods as the source of value for the consumer. The value produced is thus “value-in-exchange,”

where the production of goods is the starting point for value creation and the goods are expected to create value for the consumer when they are consumed. G-D logic thus views producers and consumers as distinct entities with distinct roles in the value creation process, with the producer playing the key role, conceptualized “as a series of activities performed by the firm” (Vargo et al. 2008, p. 146). The consumer is only expected to benefit from the value thus created.

Vargo and Lusch (2004, 2008) proposed an alternative view, a service-dominant (S-D) logic in which the roles of producers and consumers in the value creation process are intertwined rather than distinct. The focus is on the requirements of the service consumers (users) and the offerings of service producers (providers) that aim at creating “value-inuse.” Value is created jointly and reciprocally through mutual interactions, facilitating integration of resources among the producers and consumers (Vargo et al. 2008). This new pullbased S-D logic has a user-centric perspective in which the producer and the consumer together create value by designing processes that mutually leverage available resources (Sheth and Parvityar 2000; Teo et al. 2006). However, service provision entails greater use of intangible resources such as knowledge and skills for value creation, in addition to tangible resources such as technology. Thus, in contrast to G-D logic, S-D logic emphasizes an appropriate configuration of resources—intangibles along with tangibles—to create value for consumers. Figure 1 summarizes the two logics.

## Bridging the Digital Divide for Equitable Development

The term digital divide originally referred to the gap in computer ownership between certain ethnic groups in the United States and is now used in a more comprehensive manner to describe the differential access to digital goods and capabilities between social groups (Kauffman 2005). Digital goods and capabilities have not been uniformly distributed, and the gulf between “digital haves” and “digital have-nots” is often viewed as a major impediment to equitable development (Kauffman 2005; Venkatesh and Sykes 2013). It is feared that ICT-challenged segments of a society may be further disadvantaged, exacerbating societal gaps (Avgerou and Madon 2005; Ching et al. 2005; Dewan and Riggins 2005). Bridging the digital divide is now widely recognized as a means to facilitate the progress of communities, regions, and nations in both developed (see Agarwal et al. 2009; Hsieh et al. 2011, 2008; Wei et al. 2011) and developing (see Venkatesh and Sykes 2013) countries.

As discussed, previous digital divide research predominantly views the role of ICT for development from the G-D logic perspective (i.e., the divide in the access to digital goods is seen as contributing to the divide in digital capability, which in turn affects the experienced service outcomes). While this perspective works well for most of the developed world, it often falls short of incorporating the contextual intricacies of the developing world, where the bulk of the population does not have access to even basic services.

## Developing Countries: Rethinking the Digital Divide with Service-Dominant Logic

Developing countries differ from the developed world in two major ways that should be taken into account when theorizing the digital divide for the developing world. First, governments in developing countries generally have limited resources, and this affects the provision of basic services such as healthcare and education to large sections of the population. Social security systems that ensure such basic services for all in developed countries are largely absent in developing countries. Hence, developing countries depend heavily on private entities for provision of these services. Second, in addition to profit-oriented private entrepreneurs who tend to service only the profitable urban markets, social entrepreneurs driven by a desire to serve disadvantaged sections of the society are also emerging in developing countries. Such entrepreneurs attempt to provide accessible and affordable services to the masses by innovatively designing economically sustainable business models (Mair et al. 2007). In the absence of well-developed, structured, formal institutions, these social entrepreneurs must continually orchestrate available resources to provide basic services on a sustained basis, and ICT is generally one of the prime resources leveraged to make the service delivery efficient. These social entrepreneurs thus view technology as a tool for providing basic capabilities rather than as an instrument for enhancing digital capabilities.

Given the contextual specificities of the developing world, we propose the S-D logic perspective of a service divide to theorize the divide in the quantity and quality of basic services consumed by different segments of the population. Reframing the digital divide with S-D logic provides a customercentric demand perspective, in contrast to the traditional producer-centric supply perspective. Appendix B shows the key differences between the two perspectives.

In S-D logic, value is collaboratively created by the service provider (producer) and the service user (consumer). The service provider and service user generally create value within their own interactive configurations of mutual exchange, called service systems (see Spohrer and Maglio 2008) and service science aims to systematically examine the processes through which service systems use their own resources in conjunction with the resources of other service systems to create value (Maglio and Spohrer 2008; Spohrer et al. 2007; Vargo et al. 2008). Therefore, from a service science perspective for a holistic understanding, it is imperative to theorize and examine the processes that leverage resources across service systems to enable value creation in the context of our study.

## Resources for Value Creation: Existential and Interactional Perspectives

From the G-D logic perspective, the resources that enable value creation are primarily owned by the producer, who utilizes these resources (such as land, labor, and capital) to create value for the consumer. In contrast, from the S-D logic perspective, value creation is a collaborative process, and the resources enabling value creation are owned by both the provider and the user service systems. Value creation is a dynamic and interactive process which utilizes resources that are unique to the value-creating relationship rather than resources that are independently owned by provider and user service systems. Hence, the unit for value creation is the provider–user relationship rather than individual service systems.

In contrast to G-D logic, S-D logic can categorize resources (people, technology, and information) that enable value creation in two ways (see Cova and Salle 2008, Gallan et al. 2013; Vargo et al. 2008). First, they can be viewed as passive sources for value creation that are individually owned by specific provider and user service systems. Such resources, termed existential resources, have clear ownership boundaries governed by explicit property rights. Second, they can be viewed as active assets specific to the interactional relationship between provider and user service systems. For example, knowledge, technology, and institutions can be shared between the provider and user systems to facilitate integration of people and information in the value-creating relationship. The interactional resources that are shared between provider and user service systems have existential resources embedded within them and thus offer another perspective for describing provider and user service system resources. Viewed from an existential perspective, resources are owned by specific service systems (e.g., people attributable to the provider or people attributable to the user system). In contrast, viewed from an interactional perspective, resources are shared across provider and user systems (e.g., knowledge shared by the two systems). Because the value creation process among service systems is embedded in the networks of dynamic interactions between the systems, we adopt the interactional view of resources to theorize the processes of service innovation in this research.

## Service Innovations: Leveraging Interactional Resources

Value creation within relationships between provider and user service systems is dependent on the interactional use of resources (see Sarker et al. 2012). Building on the interactional view of shared resources, we posit that value is created in the interactional relationship between the service provider and service user systems when interactional resources are orchestrated in value-creating configurations. We posit that in addition to ICT (technology), which is the key interactional resource in most value-creating provider– user relationships, social entrepreneurs in developing countries need to continually search for and orchestrate contextually available knowledge and institutional resources to deliver the desired value to society (see George et al. 2012; Radjou and Prabhu 2012; Radjou et al. 2012). Hence, we posit that in the context of developing countries, social entrepreneurs need to innovatively combine three interactional resources—knowledge, technology, and the situating institutions—in value-creating mechanisms to effect the desired service innovation. Knowledge comprises the skills, competencies, and understanding that provider (social entrepreneur) and user (target societal segment) service systems have for acknowledging, comprehending, and responding to the dynamic contextual imperatives instrumental in value creation. Technology includes all information and communication technologies that assist in value creation by utilizing and integrating elements of provider and user service systems. Institutions in the context of a society comprise the socially and legally constructed entities that provide the contextualized framework for interaction between provider and user service systems within a given community and transcend the individual agency. Institutions thus comprise not only the formal laws and informal practices but also the agencies that implement them such as the related governmental and nongovernmental bodies, private organizations, and community groups. In our study, institutions imply both the formal and informal procedures/practices and also the organizations/ groups implementing them.

In this study, we first analyze the research context to arrive at the value proposition desired from the service innovation to foster the identified basic capability. Next, using a case study methodology and adopting a process view, we examine how value-creating interactional resources facilitate healthcare service innovation in developing countries. Specifically, we trace the value-creating mechanisms at play, and through a careful analysis of our case study data, we extract and identify the conditions enabling the value creation. ## Research Design and Methodology

This research aims at theory building by adopting a service divide perspective. We use a qualitative case-based methodology and a process view to analyze data related to two ICTenabled ophthalmic healthcare initiatives for rural India to unearth the patterns of interactional resources constituting the service innovations, and we also examine how these patterns evolve over time (see Chakraborty et al. 2010; Sarker et al. 2012; Silva and Hirschheim 2007). Consistent with the case study approach, we have provided an overview of some of the streams of research that contributed to our theory building, namely, literature on S-D logic, ICT and the digital divide, the role of ICT for bridging the service divide, and related literature on service systems and service innovation. Based on our preliminary framework for service innovation (Figure 2), we identify the mechanisms through which the three interactional resources (knowledge, technology, and institutions) create value. Finally, we situate each of our findings within the literature to contextualize the emergent theoretical concepts before concluding with theoretical and practical implications.

We adopted a literal replication strategy (Eisendhardt 1989; Kirsch 2004; Yin 1994) and chose cases for their similarities vis-à-vis elements that are important for this research. Hence, we expect to find comparable results for the two cases. The data for our research came from specialized ophthalmic telemedicine initiatives undertaken by two large private healthcare providers located in the south of India. These organizations, Madurai-based Aravind Eye Care Systems (AECS) and Bangalore-based Narayana Nethralaya (NN), an associate of the Narayana Hrudyalaya (NH) group of hospitals, were established with the primary objective of providing affordable quality eye care. They were motivated by the need for sustainable healthcare models to supplement governmental efforts. We investigate how the two organizations designed and implemented their service delivery models to reach marginalized segments of the population. Because these cases are atypical initiatives for providing services to the masses, our extracted findings can assist aspiring social entrepreneurs.

Our study utilizes a mix of primary and secondary data to analyze the research problem. We conducted a total of 69 interviews, 45 face-to-face and 24 telephonic (Appendix C). Of the 69 interviews, 22 were conducted at AECS and 47 at NN/NH. The sample questions used as guidelines for conducting interviews during first contacts with respondents appear in Appendix D. Most key respondents were interviewed multiple times to clarify new facts as they emerged during the data collection process. In addition to telephonic interviews, we had at least one face-to-face interview with every interviewee but one. We took detailed notes during all interviews. We also had email exchanges with officials in both organizations for an in-depth understanding of the underlying processes, mechanisms, and interactions associated with service innovations. We followed an iterative process of data collection, with data collection and analysis continuing handin-hand for approximately 11 months in total: from September 2010 to January 2011 in the first phase, and from September 2011 to February 2012 in the second. We also sought clarifications in September and October of 2012 about the patterns emerging from our data. Open access to the two organizations allowed us to move back and forth between the data and the research sites as required. We also visited the field sites to observe the telemedicine operations (Appendix E).

We took detailed observational notes during the site visits, which formed part of the data for our analysis. For secondary data, we went through the organizations’ activity reports, websites, published articles, internal reports, and video clips (Appendix F). We used a process view to analyze the data, aimed at unearthing the patterns of interactional resources that constitute the service innovations (see Chakraborty et al. 2010; Lyytinen and Newman 2008; Markus and Robey 1988; Newman and Robey 1992). We first analyzed within-case data and then searched for emergent cross-case patterns.

## Healthcare in India: Description of the Context and Identification of the Value Propositions for the Service User

Before presenting the case details, we explain the context and formulate the value propositions expected to be created by the service innovations so as to populate the right side of the preliminary research framework (Figure 2). India produces the largest number of medical doctors worldwide. Every year, approximately 35,000 doctors graduate from India’s 330 medical colleges, yet the country faces an acute shortage of doctors. India’s large population accounts for its current dismal doctor:population ratio of 1:1700 compared to the world average of 1:666 (Table 1), and thus access to medical care in India is scarce.

Exacerbating the problem, most qualified doctors work in urban centers after completing their education. Rural India, which includes approximately 70 percent of the Indian population, thus has limited access to healthcare professionals. The disproportionate geographical distribution of medical professionals and facilities in urban centers results in an acute geographic healthcare service divide. A recent report by the Ministry of Health (MOH), Government of India, makes it clear that the government is cognizant of this disparity:

Over 75 per cent of the human resources and advanced medical technology, 68 percent of an estimated 15,097 hospitals and 37 percent of 623,819 total beds in the country are in the private sector. Most are located in urban areas. Of concern is the abysmally poor quality of services being provided at the rural periphery by the large number of unqualified persons. (MOH 2010, p. 21)

Thus, innovation that provides the rural Indian population with access to qualified medical expertise and facilities would clearly be very valuable. Hence, improving geographical access (accessibility) to medical facilities for the rural population is the first value proposition we expect ICT-enabled service innovations to offer.

Another major impediment to healthcare service access in India is its high cost compared to what the bulk of the Indian population can afford. India’s public healthcare spending is among the lowest in the world, at 20 percent, while private spending accounts for over 78 percent of healthcare expenditures and the remaining 2 percent is contributed by international/external sources (NHAI 2005, pp. 2-3). This high share of out-of-pocket expenditures for healthcare creates a major impediment for health-seeking behavior in India:

As public spending on health has remained low, private out-of-pocket expenditures on health are among the highest in the world. Healthcare, far from helping people rise out of poverty, has become an important cause of household impoverishment and debt. The average national health indicators, though showing improvements in recent decades, hide vast regional and social disparities. Although some privileged individuals enjoy excellent health outcomes, others experience the worst imaginable conditions. Health disparities are being exacerbated by unequal economic growth, growing commercialization of healthcare and poor regulation of costs and quality of care. (Patel et al. 2011, pp. 448-449)

Other studies confirm the regional and income disparities. Kumar et al. (2011, p. 4) succinctly summarized the reasons for the healthcare service divide in India, which has been growing:

Health services in the public sector that can be accessed free or for a nominal fee are grossly inadequate. As a result, most Indians access private healthcare that is expensive, unaffordable, unreliable, and impoverishing. Good-quality healthcare in the private sector is also not available, particularly in rural and other remote parts of India. The absence of financial protection and the rising costs of treatment have been dissuading people from accessing much needed healthcare. In 2004, 28% of ailments in rural areas went untreated because of financial reasons—up from 15% in 1995–96. Similarly, in urban areas, 20% of ailments were untreated for financial reasons in 2004—up from 10% in 1995– 96. 47% of hospital admissions in rural India and 31% in urban India were financed by loans and the sale of assets.

The high cost of healthcare coupled with low individual income levels makes quality healthcare unaffordable for large segments of the population, creating a sharp economic divide in terms of healthcare access. Hence, the second value proposition that any related service innovation should offer is to reduce cost for the solution and make it affordable (affordability). We therefore focus on these two identified value propositions and examine how interactional resources are leveraged to improve the accessibility and affordability of healthcare service.

Venkataswamy (Dr. V) founded AECS in Madurai to fulfill a long-cherished dream—the creation of a private nonprofit eye hospital that would provide quality eye care at an affordable price (Rangan 2009). His mission was to eradicate unnecessary blindness, and Dr. V pioneered a high-volume, cost-effective, high-quality eye care system by adopting a delivery model based on McDonald’s standardization principles that train people anywhere in the world to efficiently produce the same product. From its humble beginning in a rented house with 11 beds in the mid-1970s, AECS has grown to become one of the largest eye care delivery systems in the world and has conducted almost 4 million surgeries over a span of three and a half decades. In 2011–2012, AECS’s nine hospitals<sup>3</sup> treated about 2.8 million outpatients and conducted more than 300,000 surgeries. Consistent with Dr. V’s philanthropic philosophy (“You serve God by serving humanity”), AECS is based on an innovative business model in which almost three-quarters of the hospital beds are for patients who receive free treatment (Appendix G), and yet the business as a whole generates modest surplus.

Dr. V believed that high volume was the key to achieving low cost and making eye care affordable, thereby providing eyesight for millions in India. This entailed proactively reaching out to patients in rural areas rather than waiting for them to visit hospitals in urban centers. To this end, AECS organized weekend “eye camps” with the help of its teams of doctors and support staff, who set up temporary makeshift eye clinics in rural areas for routine eye checkups and simple procedures. Patients requiring further specialized treatment or surgery were taken to AECS’s base hospitals, and three to four days after treatment (typically cataract surgery), they were transported back to their villages. The surgeons at AECS each perform more than 2,000 surgeries every year, compared to the national average of 400 surgeries per surgeon. This high level of productivity enhances capacity utilization and drives down costs. While the outreach through eye camps successfully increased the volume of patients to optimally utilize the hospital resources (infrastructure, equipment, staff, and doctors), it also incurred costs associated with travel to villages, doctors’ time, and the necessary resources to set up temporary clinics. Hence, AECS continuously sought innovative ways to reach the rural poor through the use of new ICTs that could further reduce costs and balance eye care services between paying patients and those for whom the services were free.

## The Case Problem and the Digitally Enabled Service-Centric Solution

More than 12 million people in India are blind. Cataracts account for nearly 75 percent of this blindness, but most medical resources and infrastructures are located in urban areas and are often inaccessible to more than two-thirds of the population. Commenting on this service divide, AECS’s Executive Director explained,

Our country has approximately 12,000 trained ophthalmologists, but most practice in urban areas, thereby the majority of the population residing in the rural areas does not have easy access to eye care. Our biggest challenge is to make eye care affordable for the patient and the community. At the same time, it should be sustainable for AECS. How do we make this happen when most cannot pay? About 60 percent of our patients do not pay, while we charge market rates from those who can afford. The building blocks of the AECS model include a strong value system committed to the ideals and mission of Dr. V, a very effective delivery system, and innovations to serve the large underserved population in the face of resource scarcity of capital and people, dispersed population, low affordability, and poor logistics.

Grounded in this organizational philosophy, AECS’s telemedicine initiative aimed to efficiently reach the rural masses and thereby provide them quality service at affordable prices. AECS Senior Manager of Systems and IT recalled the genesis of the tele-ophthalmology initiative:

The majority of our patients for cataract surgery are sourced from eye camps. In the mid-1990s, our utilization studies found that eye camps reach only 7 percent of the people who needed eye care. So we started looking for a better touch point option for eye care in rural areas in the face of a shortage of ophthalmologists willing to stay in these remote locations. Telemedicine emerged as a potential solution, and we planned for a permanent setup in the form of a vision center in rural areas to create a trusted service touch point for the villagers.

In 2004, the first vision center (VC) was piloted at Ambasamudram in Southern Tamil Nadu by connecting to AECS’s base hospital at Theni through a wireless network using WiFi 802.11b technology. This was a low-cost technology, but antennas had to be adjusted to the line of sight and only had a range of approximately 60 km. In the last few years, AECS has switched to broadband connections, which are more economical than dedicated wireless networks. To achieve effective utilization of the VCs, AECS uses a geographical information system (GIS) to identify new VC locations that will cover populations of approximately 50,000 within a range of 5 to 10 km. Between 2005 and 2012, the number of VCs and tele-ophthalmic consultations grew as shown in Table 2. Each VC is connected to one of the base hospitals at Theni, Madurai, Coimbatore, Tirunelveli, or Pondicherry, providing real-time transmission of digitized information (voice and images). Each base hospital has a dedicated center for teleophthalmic consultations, staffed by a specialist who consults across all the VCs served by the base hospital.

Each VC is staffed with a coordinator, a technician, and an eye health fieldworker. The fieldworker is trained to conduct household surveys, measure vision, identify extra-ocular defects, identify curable and incurable blindness, and record all information at the village level. The fieldworker counsels and refers the curably blind to the VC and provides community-based rehabilitation for the incurably blind. As pointed out earlier, the health-seeking behavior of people in emerging countries is dismally low, so the fieldworker’s role in counseling people to seek proper eye care when needed is very important.

The trained technician at the VC diagnoses common eye problems, dispenses spectacles, and treats minor injuries after consulting with the base hospital ophthalmologist using lowcost broadband connectivity. The technician has the patient sit in front of a digital camera fitted on a computer, thus enabling the patient to speak to the specialist at the base hospital who provides real-time consultations. Depending on the case, the technician may also conduct slit-lamp examinations, refraction tests, and fundus photography, transmitting the reports and images to the base hospital specialist. The center coordinator manages patient registration with electronic medical records (EMR) on the networked computer, provides optical services, maintains accounts, manages the inventory of supplies, provides counseling, and coordinates referrals with the base hospital. The EMR is a permanent history of the patient that enables the technician at the VC and the ophthalmologist at the base hospital to access medical records efficiently.

This real-time teleconnectivity decouples eye care service into three interconnected but distinct components: (1) the patient examination (by the VC technician), (2) diagnosis (by the specialist in the base hospital), and (3) dispensation of medicine and/or spectacles (by the VC technician), as shown in the figure in Appendix H. This decoupling facilitates effective utilization of the specialists’ time and skills, resulting in an overall systemic efficiency.

The centers operate six days a week, from 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. Contact telephone numbers for the technician and coordinator are listed, so that patients can reach them at any time for emergencies. Consultation charges are a nominal INR 20 (\~ US 40¢). This digitally enabled service delivery system has helped the rural population access eye care service through a sustainable, sophisticated, scalable, and interactive model.

Highlighting the cost savings for patients, the AECS Systems Manager emphasized that only 10 percent of the patients visiting rural VCs require further examination or treatment at a base hospital. Thus, VCs provide quality low-cost eye care service access for the rural poor at their doorsteps. The majority—90 percent of the patients—save travel and other costs associated with the patients’ and attendants’ loss of remuneration while visiting the base hospital. Based on information collected during the interviews, we estimated the average cost savings for eye care patients visiting the VCs in 2009–10 to be substantial: INR 38.80 million (US \$780,000; Appendix I). We also used data provided by AECS to estimate the total investment needed to set up a VC: approximately US \$10,790 was needed in 2011, compared to US \$8,704 in 2007 (Appendix J). Despite the 24 percent increase in the investment to set up VCs, the nominal consulting fee charged to patients at the VCs has remained constant since the time of inception.

The initial investment for new VCs is provided by AECS with support from funding agencies. The VCs are expected to recover their operational expenses and break even, but their financial performance varies significantly, as Appendix K shows for the VCs connected to the base hospital at Madurai. In general, new VCs do not generate sufficient revenue for the first few years (as seen in the case of Singampunari and Sattur, which were set up in 2010; Appendix K). Management keeps a close watch on the cumulative investment recovery so that the VCs break even overall (with a 99.96% recovery in the case of the Madurai-hospital-based VCs). Some of the key performance indicators for the centers include the number of patients reached, the number of patients identified with problems and treated, and the cost recovery for sustainability. For the key stakeholders, the performance measures include access to eye care in rural areas, awareness creation, transformed health-seeking behavior, and sustainability. During an interview, the Senior Manager of the Outreach Project explained the benefits:

The vision centers have helped us reach larger volumes of patients and provide them with quality service. The major benefit of the ICT-enabled connectivity is that when patients talk to our doctor at the base hospital, it increases their trust. The technician tests the eye, but talking to the doctor increases patients’ confidence in the treatment, making it comprehensive and trustworthy. The consultation at the center helps improve the technician’s skills. She gains knowledge and develops her clinical skills. For patients, unnecessary referrals to the hospital, and the associated costs and efforts, are minimized. So these centers help everyone.

## Case Study 2: Tele-Ophthalmology at Narayana Nethrayala (NN)

## History and Business Philosophy

NN is part of the NH group, a pioneer in cardiac care and a leading adopter of telemedicine reputed for social initiatives such as micro-health-insurance programs for the poor in rural areas. After working for several years in the United Kingdom, Dr. Devi Shetty, the group’s founding chairman, returned to India in 1989 and cofounded a nonprofit organization, the Asia Heart Foundation (AHF). The AHF set up the Rabindranath Tagore Institute of Cardiac Sciences, a 150- bed heart hospital to provide affordable cardiac care to the masses, in Kolkata. In 2001, Dr. Shetty set up a 280-bed NH hospital in Bangalore. By 2010, the NH group had become one of the largest healthcare organizations in India, with over 5,000 beds. The group’s two heart hospitals perform approximately 12 percent of all heart surgeries in India. NH performs the world’s largest number of heart surgeries on children, and its pediatric cardiac surgery unit, with 80 critical-care beds, is the world’s largest pediatric cardiac surgery intensive therapy unit (ITU).

NH is driven by Dr. Shetty’s belief that no one should be deprived of the best healthcare because of insufficient funds. The NH healthcare economic model is based on developing integrated “health cities,” each with approximately 5,000 beds at full capacity and focusing on cardiac care, oncology, neurology, nephrology, orthopedics, and super-specialty eye care. The expected large volume of patients reduces costs through better utilization of hospital equipment, lower prices for supplies because of greater bargaining power with vendors, and higher productivity of key human resources, including doctors and paramedics. The lower costs achieved through economies of scale and scope make these specialized services affordable to the masses. NH uses a hybrid pricing approach with richer patients charged more than poorer patients, which ensures financial sustainability. However, the economies achieved through high volume help lower even the prices for “full paying” patients, who pay less than the market price.

Similar to AECS, NH understood that the bulk of its potential patients reside in rural areas and recognized the need to leverage ICT to reach these patients. Commenting on the use of technology, the chairman of NH explained,

ICT is the only way to create access to good quality healthcare for the masses. I can sit in my hospital in Bangalore and provide services to patients sitting in a village hospital thousands of kilometers away. I will never be able to travel to these places.

Since 2001, doctors at NH have provided remote consultations to more than 53,000 patients through 143 telemedicine centers, including 53 centers located on the African continent. Cardiologists at NH have used a transtelephonic electrocardiogram (TT-ECG) network since 2004 to diagnose, provide opinions, and recommend treatment plans based on ECG reports sent by physicians from across the country (Appendix L). By virtue of being part of the NH group, NN benefits from many synergies, especially in terms of the technologies used. Currently, NN is one of the largest tertiary eye care centers in Karnataka. Established as an eye clinic in 1982, NN now operates out of two large specialty eye care hospitals in the city of Bangalore. NN is also a strong believer in the power of technology, as reflected in the thoughts of NN’s vice chairman:

Being a part of the NH group, it is natural for us to use Internet and communication technologies to expand our reach. Our tele-ophthalmology projects have created a huge impact among the poor who cannot afford advanced screening services.

We will describe one of NN’s telemedicine initiatives that focuses on Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP), providing specialized eye care to rural patients.

## The Case Problem and the Digitally Enabled Service-Centric Solution

ROP is a potentially blinding condition that is prevalent among preterm infants and is the largest cause of infant blindness in developed countries. Dr. Anand Vinekar, head of the Department of Pediatric Retina at NN, described the magnitude of the problem in India:

Every year, more than 2.3 million [8.8%] of the over 27 million births in India are born preterm or underweight, i.e., less than 2,000 grams. Almost 47 percent [1.08 million] of these infants are prone to ROP, and over 15 percent [0.16 million] require specialized treatment. ROP is curable, but requires early screening and timely treatment within four weeks of birth to prevent irreversible blindness. The initial ROP screening for preterm infants requires specialized retinal cameras costing over US \$110,000, which is unaffordable for most hospitals in rural India. The procedure requires consultation with a specialist retinal surgeon. But the country has just about 400 retinal surgeons and only 15 centers for comprehensive ROP screening and treatment. Most surgeons practice in the large cities, thus skewing the demand–supply gap further in the rural areas. The high cost of the screening equipment coupled with an acute shortage of retinal surgeons in India puts a large number of preterm and underweight babies born in rural India at risk of getting blind due to ROP.

Thousands of children in India turn blind every year due to poverty, an acute shortage of diagnostic facilities for ROP, lack of awareness among parents as well as doctors, and low access to quality healthcare. The problem is compounded by the small window of four weeks after birth for detection and treatment of ROP.

In early 2008, NN leveraged the power of the Internet to initiate the Karnataka Internet Assisted Diagnosis of ROP (KIDROP) project (Vinekar 2011), adopting a “triple-T strategy” involving Tele-ROP, training ophthalmologists, and talking to pediatricians and gynecologists to create awareness, and setting up early screening and treatment systems in rural and semi-urban areas. The Tele-ROP component provides ROP screening in rural areas through digital imaging of infants’ retinas using a specialized retina camera (RetCam). A dedicated team comprising a trained technician, a manager, a driver, and an ophthalmologist travels with the RetCam to hospitals in the rural and semi-urban areas on a fixed, weekly schedule. A sample weekly schedule for one of the ROP teams is shown in Appendix M.

The technician uses the RetCam to image the eye in the neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) of partner hospitals, instantly uploading the images over broadband to a secure server accessible to the retina specialist in Bangalore for diagnosis. The specialist also has real-time access to the images on an Apple iPhone using an application developed by i2i Telesystems, the project’s Bangalore-based technology partner. The technician is trained to image, to save, retrieve, and analyze the images, and to detect ROP using computational pattern recognition. This innovation replaces the retina specialist with a trained nonphysician for screening in underserved rural locations and uses broadband and mobile networks to instantly transmit images to the specialist in the city for quick diagnosis. The specialist’s report is transmitted to the rural technician and, if required, KIDROP team experts or locally trained ophthalmologists provide treatment at the rural center. The KIDROP program currently covers 36 NICUs at partner hospitals within an approximate 160 km radius from Bangalore. The program has also been expanded to 36 hospitals in North and Central Karnataka under a public–private partnership scheme with the Government of Karnataka and the Government of India’s Ministry of Health. An innovative technique identifies, tracks, and screens suspected ROP cases. In rural areas, most mothers do not know the exact date of their baby’s conception, making it difficult to identify preterm babies. The ROP team therefore uses birth weight as an objective criterion for short-listing infants for ROP screening. Each partner hospital maintains a “Red ROP” register, recording the weight of all babies born at less than 2 kg. A red card (Appendix N) is handed to the parents of underweight infants, who are asked to visit the hospital for a check-up three to four weeks after the child’s birth. A tearaway slip with the mother’s mobile phone number is pasted in the Red ROP register. Mothers are phoned with reminders about scheduled KIDROP team visits to ensure that the babies are brought to the hospital for the ROP screening and necessary medical attention. Extensive networks of mobile phone services in rural areas of India enable easy contact and followup with mothers of ROP-suspect babies.

The program began in April 2008, and within the first 30 months (through November 2010), the program conducted over 3,200 screenings across 24 centers within the 160-km radius around Bangalore (Vinekar 2011). By December 2011, they had conducted over 10,000 screenings. Of the patients, 62 percent received the service at no cost, 16 percent were given a heavy subsidy, and the rest were charged the full cost. Fees collected from those who can afford to pay help to cover the operational costs, thus allowing the program to break even. On average, approximately 550 screenings are conducted every month, and the team travels 2,000 km each week. Thus, KIDROP leverages technology innovatively to provide access to high-quality specialized eye care to ROPvulnerable rural infants at a very low cost. Travel and associated costs to bring an infant from a village 100 km away to Bangalore are greater than INR 630 (\~ US \$12.50) per visit. In contrast, the monetary cost of a visit to the nearest center, for example, the Government Hospital in Mandya, is only INR 170 (\~ US \$3.40; Appendix O), accompanied by substantial savings in time and effort. A major benefit of the nearest center’s proximity is a reduction in follow-up attrition and hence in the risk of blindness.

## Results and Interpretation

## Mechanisms for Value Creation

We first analyzed the data for the two cases using a process view to discover value-creating instances in terms of reduced service cost (affordability) and improved geographical access (accessibility). We then arranged the identified value-creating instances (29 for AECS and 36 for NN) in chronological stages for each of the initiatives (Appendix P for AECS; Appendix Q for NN). Next, we identified the associated value-creating interactional resources for each value-creating instance. The configurations of the three interactional resources, namely knowledge (K), technology (T), and institutions (I), have evolved continuously for both initiatives. Finally, through our data analysis, we identified three valuecreating mechanisms: resource exploitation, resource combination, and value reinforcement, as explained next.

## Resource Exploitation

Resource exploitation is the value-creating mechanism in which a single interactional resource (knowledge, technology, or institutions) is leveraged to create the desired value (either reducing cost or improving geographical access) for the user. For example, for AECS (Appendix P, value-creating instance # 7), knowledge is leveraged to reduce cost:

An initial screening by the VC technician saves time for the doctor in the hospital, who can also consult with more patients in the seven to nine VCs telelinked to the hospital. An awareness program facilitates early detection of potential ailments and saves costs for patients and society.

In another example from NN (Appendix Q, value-creating instance # 32), technology is leveraged to improve geographical access:

Tele-ROP version 2.0 is explored to include image enhancement tools, vascular analysis, multitasking, integrated videoconferencing with image analysis, a tablet platform, and more. This helps improve both the quality of service and geographical access.

## Resource Combination

Resource combination is the value-creating mechanism in which two or three interactional resources are combined (as dyads or triads) to create the desired value for the user. For AECS (Appendix P, value-creating instance # 21), a dyadic combination of technology and institutions increases geographical access:

AECS partners with Marratech to customize its Manager edition software for teleconsultation based on H.263 audio and video standards for smooth transmission of audio and video data along with case sheets and images. A hub-and-spoke model enables all VCs to interact with the ophthalmologist at the base hospital, thereby increasing geographical access.

For NN (Appendix Q, value-creating instance # 20), a dyadic combination of knowledge and technology reduces cost:

An Apple iPhone application released by i2i with a complete Tele-ROP solution enables live reading and reporting. The ROP specialist does not have to travel to remote locations but can read the images in real time for diagnosis and reporting. This saves the ROP specialist’s time, thereby resulting in cost savings for the patient.

For AECS (Appendix P, value-creating instance # 4), a triadic combination of knowledge, technology, and institutions reduces cost:

Two vision care centers (VCs) are set up at Ambasamudram and Andipetti, both connected to Aravind Eye Hospital. Wi-Fi–802.11 B, developed by the University of California, Berkeley, is used for wireless transfer of images, and two-way audio-video communication is used for consultation between the patient in the rural VC and the doctor in one of Aravind’s urban eye hospitals. This saves patients the cost of commuting to the city and prevents loss of wages/earnings of patient/attendant incurred by travel to the city.

For NN (Appendix Q, value-creating instance # 10), a triadic combination of knowledge, technology, and institutions is also leveraged to reduce cost:

NN partners with i2i Telesolutions to develop an Internet-based picture-archiving communication system (PACS). Development begins by using an existing PACS as the platform for a tele-PACS where multiple specialists simultaneously view and report on a single-image system. The older PACS used e-mail attachments, which was slow and did not solve security or storage issues. The tele-PACS saves the ROP specialist’s time, resulting in cost savings for the patient.

## Value Reinforcement

Value propositions in service innovations are often described with more than one parameter. For example, we have described the value proposition for the rural eye care user in terms of two parameters: cost reduction and improved geographical access. Frequently, the value proposition parameters are related to each other. For example, cost reduction can be leveraged to increase geographical access, and vice versa. We term this mechanism of value creation value reinforcement, where the value created through one parameter can be leveraged to create value through another parameter. For example, for AECS (Appendix P, value-creating instance # 11), geographical access is leveraged to reduce cost:

The locations of VCs are chosen to serve rural populations of approximately 50 to 60 thousand within a travel radius of 5 to 10 km. Only approximately 10% of the patients who visit VCs need to travel to the base hospital for further treatment. This saves substantial costs for the remaining 90% of the patients and their attendants.

Similarly, for NN (Appendix Q, value-creating instance # 25), cost reduction is leveraged to increase geographical access:

The low cost realized through economies of scale and scope further helps enroll additional partner centers and hospitals. Differential pricing for poor patients further increases numbers and geographical access. The project leader also has the discretion to waive a patient’s fees, which helps provide access for many more patients.

Although both AECS and NN leverage all three mechanisms of value creation, we observe that resource exploitation and resource combination are leveraged most often. Moreover, resource combination is the dominant mechanism for AECS, while NN utilizes more resource exploitation. A closer analysis reveals that NN has primarily leveraged institutional resources during the latter stages of its project evolution to scale up its operations and reach out to more users; NN’s institutional relationships with government bodies and different organizations have facilitated this growth. In contrast, AECS has expanded through the resource combination mechanism, with technology as the primary interactional resource. To explain this difference, we note that although both organizations use tele-ophthalmology to provide eye care service to their patients, they provide different kinds of service. AECS’s VCs are permanent village facilities for providing general eye care, so deeper penetration into the rural population is necessary for the project to make an impact and remain sustainable. NN’s ROP service is a very specialized service for screening and treating ROP-vulnerable babies, and breadth of coverage is of prime importance. To make an impact, NN must reach out to patients in villages throughout India. Thus, NN concentrates on institutional partnerships with the country and state governments and other bodies to scale up its operations and facilitate the ROP project’s sustainability.

The idea and launch stage includes the conception of the project and its commencement. During the late growth and expansion stage, concerted efforts are made to grow the number of centers and served locations through geographical expansion. Comparison of the interactional resources and their configurations within and across the two cases reveals two important patterns. First, all three interactional resources are important during the entire process of evolution for both AECS and NN. Second, the relative importance of different interactional resources changes across the different stages in both cases. There are also notable similarities and differences in the emergent patterns across AECS and NN, the latter possibly due to contextual differences between the two service innovation projects.

We observe that for both projects, knowledge is more important than technology and institutions during the initial idea and launch stage. This is because the service provider needs to first understand the user requirements and explore the available knowledge that might help provide the service innovatively. For both AECS and NN, technology plays a primary role in expanding service provision during the infancy and early growth stage, where the focus is on stabilizing operational processes along with increasing geographical outreach. But we also observe that institutions play an equally important role for NN during this period, possibly because of the nature of the service NN provides. As previously discussed, NN aims to cover as many infants as possible in a wide geographical area, and its growth thus depends to a large extent on partnerships with hospitals, governments, and institutions that want the infants in particular regions to be screened for ROP vulnerability. This is reflected in the valuecreating instances in which institutions are as important as technology.

Although the interactional resources leveraged for creating value in the first two stages of evolution are relatively similar for AECS and NN, the dominant interactional resource during the third stage differs markedly. Recall that the late growth stage signifies expansion and growth. As explained in the previous section, the difference in the types of service innovations that AECS and NN offer explains their different growth trajectories. While AECS uses technology as the primary interactional resource in the third stage to scale up its operations, NN leverages institutions in order to grow. AECS had partnered with the government of India during the second stage, in 2007, to form the Tripura Vision Center (TVC) by replicating its VCs at Community Information Centers (CICs), and 40 VCs are now operational in the state of Tripura, serving a population of 3.7 million. Perhaps AECS could also leverage institutional alliances in the third stage, in addition to technology, to scale up its operations. But this would require some rethinking, as the AECS project is a continuing eye care facilitator in rural areas and requires a deep understanding of regional idiosyncrasies along with diverse eye care competences.

## Enablers of Value Creation

To identify the enablers of value creation, we used an iterative recursive approach, moving from our interpretation to the data and vice versa several times before abstracting the four major themes shown in Appendices P and Q. First, we individually examined each of the identified value-creating instances (29 for AECS and 36 for NN) to discover its enabling theme. We then filtered and consolidated the enabling themes that initially emerged from the data interpretation through a process of iteration and reconciliation to produce the four broad enablers for service innovations across the two cases: obsessive customer empathy, belief in the transformational power of ICT, continuous recursive learning, and efficient network orchestration. Appendices P and Q list the enablers for each value-creating instance. We observe that many of the instances are facilitated by more than one enabler. Although all four enablers have been instrumental in creating value for both AECS and NN, we observe that efficient network orchestration is a particularly dominant enabler for NN. We discuss each enabler in turn.

## Obsessive Customer Empathy

AECS and NN are driven by an unrelenting effort to understand the needs of rural users. We observe that the two service providers are exemplars of customer centricity, in terms of both understanding and satisfying the users’ desires and needs, when we consider the resources placed at the users’ disposal (Sheth et al. 2000). An obsessive empathy for customers leads to value creation for both the customer (service user) and the firm (service provider). As truly customer-centric businesses, AECS and NN take users’ needs, desires, and resources as the starting point for all aspects of their strategy formulation, design, and implementation. Shah et al. (2006) underscored the critical role of leadership commitment for both initiating and sustaining initiatives related to customer centricity, and in our data we observed a clear vision regarding customer centricity among the top management at both AECS and NN. The executive director of AECS explained the organization’s approach to customers:

Our value system has helped us build a very patientcentric organization. Every year we recruit and train about 300 young women from the rural areas for administrative and paramedical tasks. They are one of the key pillars that make the AECS efficient while being empathetic to the poor. The high productivity of our surgeons and the system is enabled by our entire team, from the front-line staff to the top management, which has a strong sense of pride in serving the patients.

Similarly, NN’s deep understanding of the rural ground realities and the factors inhibiting parents from screening their newborn infants for ROP led them to bring the RetCam closer to patients rather than waiting for patients to come to the hospital. NN recognizes that the diagnostic window of four weeks for ROP prevention might be too short a timeframe for parents residing in rural areas to visit hospitals in urban centers, and also that parents and entire families will face even greater hardships if avoidable blindness stemming from ROP is not prevented.

Because solutions for rural users in developing countries must be inexpensive, AECS and NN both replaced expensive equipment with low-cost innovations and used the latest technologies to improve responsiveness. AECS replaced the expensive fundus camera (\~ US \$25,000) with a simple digital camera with an annular ring (\~ US \$300) to screen patients’ eyes at the VCs and capture the images for transmission to the base hospital. Similarly, NN partnered with a technology provider to develop an Apple i-Phone application so that a specialist can quickly review the patient images identified as critical by the technician, speeding up both diagnosis and treatment. As the ophthalmic specialist at Bangalore elaborated,

The i-Phone gives me tremendous flexibility, as I do not have to be in front of my laptop to see the images. I can quickly view images flagged by my technician screening the baby at Mandya even if I am in the operating theater at my hospital in Bangalore.

This novel application is an example of reverse innovations: innovations originating in emerging countries that have potential applications in developed markets (Govindarajan 2010).

Another customer-centric business innovation that can be adapted to other situations is the way that both AECS and NN focused on high volumes to reduce operational costs and support sustainable pricing models. The unique contribution of this pricing model is that cross-subsidization that attracts a large number of poor patients also benefits the wealthy, who gain from the systems’ productivity and effectiveness. NH’s chairman explained this business model:

We need improvements and innovations that lower costs to make health services affordable and accessible to the poor. The combination of process improvements, lower construction costs, bulk buying of supplies, and better negotiating power with equipment vendors sustains our business even at lower prices. With increase in volume, we gain further economies of scale. The productivity gains help us provide healthcare at comparatively lower prices even to paying patients.

This genuine obsessive customer empathy is important not only for social entrepreneurship initiatives like those in this study, but also for other businesses (Dev and Schultz 2005a, 2005b; Teo et al. 2006). Empathetic customer-centric obsession driven from the perspective of solutions clearly emerges as one of the key enablers for effective ICT-enabled service innovations.

## Belief in the Transformational Power of ICT

The value-creating instances in Appendices P and Q clearly reveal both AECS’s and NN’s strong belief in using ICT to facilitate their service innovations. Both organizations leverage the capabilities of ICT to improve their effectiveness in reaching out to customers and reducing costs. AECS is built on an efficient delivery process that relies extensively on ICT to reduce costs and standardize processes for handling large volumes. AECS’s senior systems manager discussed the key role of ICT in reaching out to rural areas:

Apart from regular applications for automation of our processes throughout the network and at the vision centers, we use IT to enhance patient care by remote screening diabetic retinopathy using V-SAT [Very Small Aperture Terminals] link, communicating with patients through SMS [Short Message Service] to remind them about medication and review visit, intranet for information access to manage patient flow, and through information kiosks to provide patient information about diseases, treatment options, charges, outcomes, hospital facilities. We are currently expanding our electronic medical record system to enhance documentation and use this information for planning and improving patient care.

Similarly, NH’s chairman is a firm believer in ICT as the fundamental enabler for providing excellent healthcare service to excluded segments of society:

Our concept of prosperity should not be based on materialism but on creating access. We cannot wish away poverty in our country. Even for the poor, we can create a system which will provide them access to world-class healthcare with dignity. Such systems will bank on creatively using information and communication technologies.

This statement also implies that a service divide perspective is imperative for facilitating the development of emerging countries like India. Bridging the service divide through ICT is one of the keys to inclusive growth. Although the belief in the transformational power of ICT generally stems from top management, it becomes ingrained as a part of the organizational fabric through their clear communication to all stakeholders (Armstrong and Sambamurthy 1999; Liang et al. 2007; Purvis et al. 2001; Sharma and Yetton 2003).

## Continuous Recursive Learning

As can be seen in Appendices P and Q, AECS and NN both emphasize the role of organizational learning and continuous adaptation in improving service delivery and effectiveness. When AECS realized that its eye camps were reaching only 7 percent of the target population, it was quick to identify and adopt tele-ophthalmology as a way to create a permanent presence in rural communities, thereby increasing both its reach and the target population’s trust. Similarly, when NN learned the ground realities in rural India, it quickly adapted. For example, the prime criterion used to identify ROPvulnerable infants in developed countries is the pregnancy conception date. When NN discovered the impossibility of correctly determining pregnancy conception dates in rural India, it adopted another novel criterion, birth weight, to identify premature babies. This innovative objective measure for assessing ROP risk was easily obtained to screen newborns. As another example, NN created a cost-effective way to track ROP-risk-prone babies through red and green cards with the parent’s mobile number and a Red-ROP register. This is also an innovative adaptation that takes advantage of an external ICT resource (mobile phones) which has a high penetration even among India’s rural population.

Thus, both organizations are not only knowledgeable about their work but are also intimately aware of the enfolding context (see Bamberger 2008; Johns 2006), which helps them leverage resource complementarities (such as mobile phones) to implement service innovations that make their project efficient (Collis and Montgomery 1995; Zhu 2004). Moreover, the customer-centric cultures that pervade the two organizations enhance innovativeness throughout their entire business systems and not merely in goods and services (Parsons 1991). These organizations leverage their customer centricity by incorporating continuous learning and improvement as integral parts of their operations (Ellinger et al. 2002; Teo et al. 2006; Zahay and Griffin 2004). As the director at AECS said, “Fostering a culture of continuous innovation is one of the building blocks of our system. It is also a big challenge.”

Both AECS and NN have strived to learn about the local context and constraints in order to come up with innovative business models and approaches. Both organizations have a clear focus on reducing costs and increasing service outreach, and both were inspired by global giants. Dr. Shetty calls his NH model “the Walmart approach,” while Dr. V of the AECS was inspired by McDonald’s standardization model. Clearly, the two organizations have used continuous recursive learning to suitably modify these models to the ground realities in India.

## Efficient Network Orchestration

Partners, key stakeholders who support and sustain the initiatives, have played a key role at every stage of NN’s and AECS’s evolution and are especially critical for scaling up the reach of their services, as shown in Appendices P and Q. Partners include individuals as well as institutions ranging from educational organizations, not-for-profits, corporations, businesses, associations, and media to government bodies at the local, state, and national levels. These partners provide critical human, physical, and financial resources, including complementary skill sets, last-mile access, and local knowledge and relationships that help bridge access for the rural poor (George et al. 2012).

For example, Dr. Anand Vinekar at NN evangelized the need to screen infants for ROP with presentations at meetings and conferences organized by pediatricians’ and ophthalmologists’ associations, connected with several hospitals to conduct ROP screening, and built momentum by publishing results and case studies in medical journals, attracting the attention of mainstream business journals and the local, state, and central governments. The rapid growth of centers was facilitated by new hospitals signing up and offering use of their pediatricians, trained nurses, and NICUs for ROP screening, and by government funding through an awardwinning public–private partnership project. Dr. Vinekar elaborated on the partnerships:

Our expanding partner network is one of our most important assets and also an endorsement for KIDROP. When we started in 2008, even many doctors were not aware of the severity of ROP. Our partner hospitals have helped us reach patients in places where we could have never imagined setting up a facility. They believe in our mission and have been very generous in sharing their facilities, staff, and doctors. In addition, their credibility among the locals helps ensure follow-up and compliance with the treatment regimen. Now we are also seeing the fruits of our network in other ways. Almost every month, we receive calls and queries from organizations who want us to offer ROP screening in their facilities.

At AECS, institutional partners provided the technology to set up the first few VCs, and local partners, especially businesses, schools, colleges, and the panchayat (the village-level government body), play an active role in conducting eye care awareness programs. As the Executive Director at AECS explained,

AECS has a long history of collaborating with local bodies, corporates, NGOs, governments, multilateral agencies, for example, WHO, and associations such as Lions Club International. Our partners are resource multipliers. We partner with others for technology, expertise, funding, and access. We have managed hospitals funded by corporates and charitable foundations, received capital grants for expansion, funding from government for setting up the first vision center, and we work very closely with the local community for all our outreach programs.

Thus, network orchestration helps both NN and AECS collaborate with their partners for sharing resources and local relationships, leveraging their expertise to provide affordable access, and scaling their innovative service offerings (Radjou and Prabhu 2012; Radjou et al. 2012).

Because managing a multiplicity of partnerships is challenging, both NN and AECS have dedicated project teams to identify and manage partnerships. The project team structure enables autonomy and creativity while assuring that funding permits sustainability. With support from top management, these teams help expand the scale and scope of activities by mobilizing organizational and network resources. The implementation of new project-based business models has helped these innovative efforts succeed and grow (George et al. 2012).

## Theoretical and Practical Implications

Based on our research findings, we present a revised theoretical model (Figure 3) that can be used as the point of departure for future research on service innovations. Two broad observations about the applicability of this model based on the two case studies of service innovation in teleophthalmology are important. First, both initiatives have been successful in reaching out and providing eye care services to traditionally marginalized segments of the Indian society, and both are based on financially and operationally sustainable models. To achieve these goals, both service providers have focused on leveraging three interactional resources of provider and user service systems: knowledge, technology, and institutions. Although the two initiatives are generally similar in terms of the ICTs used, there is a major difference in the kind of health service they provide. The AECS teleophthalmology model focuses on general eye care services for all population segments and referrals for cataract surgeries for the older population, whereas the NN tele-ophthalmology model targets a very specialized group of patients: infants at risk of ROP. This shows that ICT-enabled innovations can be leveraged to bridge the service divide not only for general services but also for highly specialized, complex services. Second, both initiatives focus on providing ICT-enabled service rather the ICT itself to the masses. This servicecentric view of the divide between the service haves and have-nots demonstrates the importance of focusing on the service divide rather than the digital divide, especially in the context of emerging countries, to secure equitable growth.

## Theoretical Implications

Our research has significant theoretical implications. First, it contributes to the broad stream of literature on the digital divide by extending it to the context of developing countries (see Agarwal et al. 2009; Hsieh et al. 2011, 2008; Wei et al. 2011). Because the bulk of the population in developing countries does not have access to basic services such as healthcare, it is necessary to rethink the role of ICT for these contexts. We posit that in such contexts it is more effective to conceptualize the divide in terms of the basic services consumed by different societal segments. The mechanisms for leveraging ICT to bridge the service divide extend existing digital divide models by using the SD-logic and the associated service-centric perspective of service required rather than accessibility to digital goods and capabilities. This shift also answers the call of some IS researchers to address ICT planning in emerging countries from the demand side rather than the supply side (Avgerou and Madon 2005).

Second, we extend the research on digitally enabled service innovation by unearthing patterns of resource configurations enabling service-centric value creation. Our research views technology as an interactional resource that can be leveraged with other interactional resources such as knowledge and institutions to create innovative service systems to benefit traditionally excluded segments of a society. Using a process view to analyze data related to two ICT-enabled ophthalmic healthcare initiatives for rural India, we uncover the patterns of resource configurations enabling service-centric value creation (Figure 3), We delineate three service-centric value creation mechanisms—resource exploitation, resource combination, and value reinforcement—in which the three interactional resources (knowledge, technology, and institutions) configure in different value-creating patterns. The analysis also reveals three distinct stages of service innovation evolution (idea and launch, infancy and early growth, and late growth and expansion), with a distinct dominant resource for each stage. Our study also uncovers four enablers for service innovations, with the theoretical model (Figure 3) summarizing the modalities for materializing such service innovations.

This study contributes to research on service innovations in general and ICT-enabled service innovations in particular, especially in the context of emerging countries. Based on our discussion of the service-centric approach through two case studies, we conclude that desired innovation outcomes should be formulated based on the end user’s requirements (e.g., Gallouj and Weinstein 1997, Ordanini and Parasuraman 2011). This is especially important for socially driven service innovations in developing countries with severe resource constraints. Our study also identifies two value propositions that service innovations in developing countries should generally offer: improving geographical access and reducing delivery cost. Appendix R shows these value propositions may be universal across developing countries for a host of services, and we expect that ICT-enabled service innovations can provide these value propositions. However, our study does not examine inhibitors of service innovations, so future research should address factors that may inhibit inclusive service delivery, such as culture, language, and customs.

Third, we contribute to the service science literature by conceptualizing interactional resources as shared operationalizations of existential resources owned by the provider and user service systems. Specifically, we conceptualize social entrepreneurs as service providers and society as service users in a developing country, which provides a framework for examining the process of value creation by leveraging three interactional resources—knowledge, technology, and institutions—across provider and user service systems. Our study demonstrates the usefulness of this framework in casting the role of social entrepreneurs as supplementing rather than replacing governmental efforts. Moreover, viewing the digital divide from a service-centric perspective provides enhanced flexibility for visualizing outcomes. Leveraging this approach, the development of digital capabilities can also be conceptualized as a digital outcome or service. The flexibility offered by a service-centric approach is especially important for emerging countries, where service consumption varies significantly across different segments of society. Braa et al. (2007) noted the need for a flexible approach when they highlighted the importance of developing flexible standards for health information systems in developing countries.

Fourth, similar to the views of de Vries (2006) on recombinative innovations, we posit that digitally enabled service innovations are the result of recombining the interactional resources of knowledge, technology, and institutions, so that technology should not be considered in a vacuum when designing innovative applications. Rather, cognizance of the enfolding context is of utmost importance for designing innovations that leverage existing knowledge and institutions (see Bamberger 2008, Johns 2006). This study shows that ICT-enabled, socially driven service innovations in emerging countries are generally recombinative in nature. Importantly, service innovations related to provision of basic services should not focus exclusively on personalization but should also focus on inclusion of underserved societal segments: the service itself may not be new, but the mode in which it is delivered is innovative and reaches new user groups. Future research can integrate the service-oriented perspective with the literature on recombination of resource configurations to better understand the service benefits that can be leveraged through ICT-enabled service innovations. In particular, there has been a growing concern in recent years for a focus on digital inclusion projects related to society-wide critical healthcare issues (Reilly and Gomez 2001; Walsham et al. 2007). The current study advances this research agenda with respect to the use of ICT in the healthcare sector (Oborn et al. 2011), which we believe has immense potential for emerging countries (Braa et al. 2007; Miscione 2007).

## Practical Implications

Our research also has significant practical implications. First, our study has clear practical implications for policy makers in emerging countries. The service-centric perspective on the digital divide encourages these governments to reconsider whether the digital divide is the real threat to their equitable growth. In developed countries where formal institutions are fairly mature and income levels are high, overcoming the digital divide by providing ICT goods and digital capabilities may be the key to equitable growth (see Kauffman 2005). In contrast, emerging countries can use ICT-enabled service innovations to bridge the ever-increasing service divide and thus contribute to the general welfare of the population by enhancing their basic capabilities, which can then be expected to impact their economic welfare. Governments in emerging countries can also formulate policies to regulate and encourage social entrepreneurs who attempt to foster basic capabilities such as healthcare and education for the society through innovative business solutions. Future research needs to investigate the scalability and sustainability of such endeavors.

Second, by highlighting the importance of adopting a servicecentric perspective, our study emphasizes focusing on the actual needs of end users to initiate the design of both private and public service delivery solutions. Specifically, we have shown that effective service solutions are the result of innovative recombinations of multiple resources such as knowledge, technology, and institutions. Hence, service providers should adopt a customer-centric service perspective so that other contextual interactional resources such as knowledge and institutions can be leveraged to deliver technology-driven solutions to user-desired objectives. Our descriptions of the value-creating mechanisms of service innovations can help service providers visualize how their enterprises might design and implement user-centric solutions. Our description of resource configurations also highlights challenges entrepreneurs may face as they implement user-centric ICT initiatives. For example, although the end users in our study are rural individuals, several agencies at different levels of analysis (enterprise, village, and country) were involved in the actual service delivery process. Finally, the four key enablers for delivering effective ICT-enabled service innovations that we have extracted from our case studies provide useful directions for both private and public organizations contemplating the delivery of innovative ICT solutions to their customers.

Third, our study encourages policy makers and governments to focus on implementing innovative ICT-driven solutions to mitigate the effects of imbalanced growth. While this may be a routine policy issue for developed countries where income levels are higher and public institutions are well developed, it is a critical issue for emerging countries where wealth is increasing but the distribution of resources is skewed because of weak formal institutions. Innovative ICT tools have the potential to be significant service levelers and hence to contribute to balanced growth in the developing world.

Fourth, our study highlights a key role that private enterprises can play in partnership with governments in the developing world, which generally have limited resources: such partnerships can facilitate serving the needs of the majority of the population. Social entrepreneurship initiatives such as those we have examined are not intended to replace the government’s efforts; rather, they are aimed at assisting governments through different institutional arrangements such as public– private partnerships (PPPs). Moreover, these initiatives clearly demonstrate that such efforts need not be charitable. These initiatives rely on innovative business models that help them to not only break even but also realize modest profits. Prahalad’s (2004) bottom-of-the-pyramid (BOP) proposition underscored the need to innovatively design business models that can serve the poor and also be profitable. Our study has found that innovative leveraging of interactional resources can deliver sustainable if not profitable social solutions. Extending the BOP proposition, Khanna and Palepu (2010) claim that the service divide in emerging markets is actually an opportunity for businesses and private enterprises. The onus is on entrepreneurs to design innovative and sustainable business models with novel service delivery arrangements to tap these opportunities to assist underserved population segments.

## Conclusions

Taking a service-centric view, this study extends the digital divide literature by contextualizing it to the developing world environment. Specifically, the study posits that in the developing world, the role of ICT in bridging the service divide (unequal access to and consumption of basic services) rather than the digital divide (unequal access to digital goods and capabilities) should be examined. In developing countries where accessibility to basic services such as healthcare and education for the majority of the population is a major challenge, bridging the service divide by innovatively leveraging ICT in conjunction with other resources can be an initial step toward equitable growth. Grounding the discussion in service-dominant logic and concepts from the service science literature, this research theorizes the value creation process across provider (social entrepreneur) and user (society) service systems as leveraging interactional resources (knowledge, technology, and institutions) in different configurations to realize valuable service outcomes. Specifically, we unpack the black box of service innovations that facilitate digitally enabled inclusive healthcare service delivery by two private enterprises in India, revealing different value mechanisms for bridging the service divide through value-creating configurations of three interactional resources, showing how the relative importance of the interactional resources evolves as the projects develop, and delineating four enablers that facilitate such service innovations. This research makes modest, albeit useful, contributions to the growing literature on digitally enabled service innovation, in addition to the literature on the digital divide in developing countries.

## Appendix R

## Identifying Value Propositions for Service Innovations in Developing Countries

Table R1 compares the disparities across two developed and two developing countries in their rural (and thus urban) population percentages, GDPs (gross domestic product) per capita corrected to purchasing power parity (PPP), and Gini coefficients.

We observe that the rural–urban disparity is higher in developing countries compared to the developed world, indicating an observable geographic divide. Further, GDP per capita (PPP) in developed countries is substantially higher than in the developing countries, indicating a systemic economic divide between the developed and developing nations. Moreover, we also observe a significant inequality in the distribution of wealth within the developing countries as indicated by the Gini coefficients. Although many developed countries also have a huge economic disparity within the country, those governments have better resources for the provision of basic services to citizens, and the average GDP is much higher. Hence, the acuity of the economic divide is generally less in developed countries compared to that in developing countries. The value propositions that service innovations could offer for providing basic services such as healthcare in developing countrie are improving geographical access and reducing service delivery cost.
