# Product Information Websites: Are They Good for Consumers?

PANOS M. MARKOPOULOS, RAVI ARON, AND LYLE H. UNGAR

PANOS M. MARKOPOULOS (markopoulos.panos@ucy.ac.cy; corresponding author) is on the faculty of the Department of Business and Public Administration, University of Cyprus. He holds a Ph.D. in computer and information science from the University of Pennsylvania. His professional experience includes the Athens Stock Exchange, IBM Research, and McKinsey & Co., where he consulted for the top management of leading corporations in Europe, the United States, and Canada. His research focuses on the strategic use of information technology by firms in the delivery of information-intensive services. His research has appeared in Management Science, Information Systems Research, and Harvard Business Review.

He has consulted for many companies, ranging from start-ups to Google.

ABSTRACT: Product information websites have become ubiquitous in supporting B2C E-Commerce. This study explores their impact on firm profitability, consumer surplus, and social welfare. Using an analytical model, we show that firms take advantage of such infomediaries and reduce their own information investments, increasing their profitability. Surprisingly, we find that the existence of these websites may actually reduce social welfare. Contrary to the common belief that product information websites are good for buyers, we show that they may be hurting consumers, even when they seek to maximize consumer surplus as their principal goal. These findings question the uncritical acceptance of infomediaries as beneficial to markets in general, and buyers in particular, especially when the infomediaries assume roles that substitute the information disclosure investments that sellers freely choose to make.

KEYWORDS AND PHRASES: B2C eCommerce, consumer surplus, electronic markets, infomediaries, information dissemination, information economics, product information.

When consumers must decide which products or services to buy, they do not have to rely exclusively on information provided by the sellers of these products or services. Increasingly they turn to other consumers’ reviews, or to third-party infomediaries. These third-party infomediaries—the focus of this study—offer a variety of information services to consumers, sometimes complementing, but often substituting for the information that product sellers themselves also provide [21].

Consumers often wish to know whether a particular product fits their idiosyncratic needs or tastes, or their particular use case. For example, before purchasing a mechanical keyboard, many consumers want to know exactly how typing on the keyboard sounds, or how the tactile sense feels when pressing the keys. Product fit to consumer tastes has become very important in electronic commerce, as most product returns—a considerable cost to the seller—are caused by a mismatch with buyers’ tastes, rather than by issues with product quality [24]. This is why many websites, smartphone apps, and cross-platform services segment their users according to their preferences and encourage them to shop by embracing their individual tastes, rather than conforming to the opinions of the majority. For example, the online radio service Pandora informs its listeners about which song characteristics (e.g., repetitive chorus) the service takes into account when choosing a song. Pandora refrains from making any claims about a song’s quality, and only informs music listeners on issues of fit. In this study we focus on third-party infomediaries that provide product information that can help consumers assess this fit.

The key questions that we address here are tied to product information disclosure and acquisition in the presence of truthful sellers and third-party infomediaries that focus on being an alternative source of product information to consumers.<sup>1</sup> First, we ask how much firms should invest in informing consumers and how they should price their products in the presence of third-party infomediaries. We then ask whether these infomediaries increase or decrease social welfare and consumer surplus.

We find that sellers will free ride on the information provided by infomediaries, and reduce their own information investments in order to increase their profitability. We show that a firm captures maximum profits under moderate levels of information availability, when the market parameters are such that the firm and the infomediaries provide neither too much, nor too little product information. Surprisingly, we find that infomediaries may reduce social welfare. Even more surprising is our finding that the information provided by infomediaries can actually reduce consumer surplus, even if the infomediaries’ incentives are perfectly aligned with buyers’ incentives (e.g., with a buyer-operated infomediary). Furthermore, this occurs in regard to products for which it is costly to disseminate information, precisely the types of products for which buyers would need infomediaries’ help the most.

The intuition is that when most of the benefits from increased information availability accrue to the firm, the extent of information disclosed by the firm would be greater in the absence of infomediaries than when infomediaries are present in the market. In the presence of infomediaries, the firm free rides on the information disclosed by infomediaries, to lower its own costs and increase profits. Furthermore, the total information available to buyers in this case is less than the information available when there are no infomediaries. Thus the infomediary’s presence causes overall information availability to decrease, hurting consumers and lowering social welfare. In the cases where most of the benefits from improved information accrue to the buyers, we show that it is the infomediary that prefers to disclose more information than the seller, thus benefiting buyers and increasing total welfare.

These results are counterintuitive and novel. Indeed, ours is the first study to analytically evaluate the welfare implications of product information websites. Moreover, our findings carry clear and actionable social policy implications. For example, we recommend that product information websites reliably commit to not acting as substitutes for the information that product manufacturers provide on their own accord, and only verifying or correcting the manufacturers’ own claims.

We do not claim that these results also apply in cases where infomediaries resolve quality-related (as opposed to fit-related) uncertainty. Models related to quality information have significantly different competitive dynamics where production cost [1] and signaling [19] become very important, and sellers have strong incentives to lie about their quality, so that issues of trust become important.<sup>2</sup> Furthermore, we restrict our analysis to product information that is only used to resolve product fit prior to purchase. Many infomediaries offer after-purchase product information that has the potential to enhance buyer utility. Such infomediaries may increase consumer surplus, even for buyers who have already purchased the product and cannot generate new profit for the seller. Again, the competitive dynamics in this case differ significantly.

Numerous infomediaries help consumers assess product fit, often using technology to better convey information [13]. For example, a photographer who needs to ascertain whether a Nikon camera lens is suitable for his/her purposes, may be able to find this information at Nikon’s website, where the firm offers detailed information for some, Alternatively, the photographer can visit DxO Labs (dxomark.com), which puts lenses through standardized tests and often provides even more information than Nikon on Nikon lenses. Similarly, a consumer who wants to see exactly what a certain Samsung mobile phone looks like, can visit Samsung’s own website, which includes detailed photos from different angles. Alternatively, the consumer can visit phonearena.com, which includes true 3D product views, and in addition a size comparison with standard objects, such as an AA battery, so that the consumer can better visualize the phone’s true size.

In these and many other examples, we find sellers and infomediaries offering similar information to consumers. While the infomediaries have different forms, ranging from websites (Yelp for restaurants), to newsletters (LEDs Magazine for LED-related products), to social media groups (patientslikeme.com for treatments and drugs), and mobile apps (148 apps for iOS applications), we will refer to them as either infomediaries, or product information websites—their most common form.

One potential explanation for the proliferation of product information websites is that consumers increasingly demand that they be better informed about the products that they subsequently purchase. Indeed, consumers are becoming more likely to seek product information prior to product purchase [18]. Further, consumers seem to appreciate factual product information more so than traditional advertising techniques. Indeed, the top three website features that make consumers more likely to purchase clothing and accessories online [10] are related to factual information:

● Image zoom—the ability to zoom in and see the item up close.

● Search filters—the presence of search filters that allow consumers to zero in on their desired products based on well-specified search parameters.

● Rotation of 360 degrees—the ability to rotate the item, for an arbitrary point of view.

When sellers or infomediaries wish to provide more information about product fit, they usually have to invest in some technological or other means to do ${ \mathrm { s o . } } ^ { 4 }$ For example, Apple’s iTunes allows consumers to listen to samples of music albums, and many video game developers distribute playable demo versions of their games [36]. Rich media that incorporate video, sound, and interactive elements are also being increasingly employed. The online clothing retailer Asos offers catwalk videos that help consumers visualize the clothing item in real life, and the shirt retailer T.M. Lewin uses hover boxes that allow zooming in to the shirt picture to observe the fabric weave pattern. The Wall Street Journal reports that when the shoe retailer Zappos tested showing its products with and without a video description, it observed a 10 percent increase in sales for the shoes that included video [25].

More advanced technology examples abound. The seller of prescription eyeglasses Warby Parker is among many retailers that have experimented with virtual try-ons. The technology superimposes a consumer’s photo with the company’s products, creating the illusion of standing in front of a mirror and trying on different pairs of glasses [20]. IKEA uses augmented reality technology to let customers see how the company’s products fit in a room. This is done via a smartphone app that can render furniture in 3D in real time [34]. Microsoft created an HTML5 web page so that users of Android and iOS phones can “sample” Microsoft’s mobile operating system, by making their phone appear as if it is running Windows Mobile. Other innovative technologies used to reduce consumer uncertainty include recommendation technologies [27] such as collaborative filtering [2], and data analytics. For example, Netflix uses collaborative filtering [7] to recommend movies that match a customer’s taste, and data analytics to customize TV-series trailers according to a customer’s demographic information [3].

## Related Literature

A stream of research related to this study deals with the effects of product sampling and trial on consumer choice of products. Researchers in the domains of consumer psychology and consumer behavior have studied product trials and sampling. More recently, researchers in information systems have also looked at sampling and trials of information goods. Kempf [12] investigates the nature of product type—hedonic versus functional—on consumers’ emotional responses to trial; Srinivasan and Till [32] study the impact of consumers’ brand perception on their search behavior and perception of credence attribute performance evaluations; Girard and Dion [8], study the impact of consumer risk perceptions and purchase intentions on search behavior, especially for products characterized by the dimensions of experience and credence.

Researchers in information systems have looked at aspects of sampling, trials, and experience of information goods. Jiang and Benbasat [11] study the phenomenon of virtual product experience (VPE), which enables consumers to explore and experience different features and functions of products, and show that VPE increased consumers perception of the usefulness of the virtual shopping experience in evaluating/experiencing product attributes. Consumer reviews that provide information about consumers experience of products can be seen as a partial substitute for sampling of products. An interesting variant of this idea is explored by Mudambi and Schuff [23], who look at the usefulness of product information in the form consumer reviews to buyers. They contrast two kinds of products—search and experience products—and characterize the information attributes of reviews that make them useful to consumers. Chellappa and Shivendu [4] argue that piracy of information goods can be seen as experiencing the good in the first stage of a two-stage decision-making model. They find that employing appropriate pricing strategies could help to internalize any potential benefits of piracy through sampling. In a paper allied to the nature of the problem in our study, Wang and Zhang [35] study the impact of sampling on the sale of information goods. They find that under certain conditions, when infomediaries (third parties) provide free samples of information goods, the monopolist can actually be better off. These studies are different from ours in that, first, they address the extent to which sellers can benefit from enabling product trials and sampling of information products, whereas our model does not restrict itself to information products. Second, some of these studies look at the value of thirdparty information either in the form of free samples [35] or in the form of consumer reviews to consumers. We do not restrict our analysis to a particular kind of information —samples or reviews—and instead study the impact of infomediaries on equilibrium outcomes of price, profitability and welfare characteristics. Third, we consider the strategic investment aspect of disclosure, when information may be expensive to disseminate widely, and extend these studies’ findings by showing that an infomediary may reduce a seller’s incentive to invest in expensive information disclosure (e.g., in costly trials and samples).

Further related to our analysis is the work of Chen and Xie [5, 6] who have looked at how third-party information sources affect firms’ marketing strategy, and have studied the possibility of infomediaries both substituting and complementing seller information. We extend their work by focusing on the impact of the infomediaries on consumer surplus and social welfare. Sun [33] also studies the question of a seller’s incentives to disclose non-quality-related information, but without considering the impact of infomediaries.

Disclosure of taste-related information by infomediaries has also been studied by Kwark et al. [14], who find that the role of infomediaries is to educate consumers about their own needs and tastes. In contrast, we focus on the infomediary role of informing consumers about the details of the product’s horizontal attributes.

In summary, extant research has not addressed the market microstructure wherein buyers receive information from both sellers and strategic infomediaries, and where sellers can free ride on the information disclosed by the infomediaries. As a result of filling this gap in theory we uncover a novel and counterintuitive finding: third-party infomediaries may be reducing consumer surplus and social welfare when acting in such roles.

## A Model of Information Disclosure with Infomediaries

## Model Preliminaries

We model a monopolistic market where a single firm offers a single-purchase good for which buyers are imperfectly informed before purchase. The market is modeled as a

Salop circle [29] of unit size, which contains all possible product types. The exact product type, that is, its actual location on the circle, is denoted by <sup>d</sup> (Table A1 in the Appendix includes all key notation). The firm supplies the good with constant marginal cost, normalized to zero. The product price is denoted by $p ,$ and the product quality by <sup>v</sup>.

Consistent with the modeling primitives used by Salop [29], the utility that a buyer whose ideal product type is z receives from consuming the product is given by:

$$
V (p, d, z) = v - p - t | d - z |,\tag{1}
$$

where <sup>t</sup> is the fit cost parameter, which denotes the intensity of buyers’ preferences for their ideal product type, and the product $\boldsymbol { t } \cdot | \boldsymbol { d } - \boldsymbol { z } |$ is the buyer’s fit cost. All buyers are assumed to be risk neutral and demand one unit of the good, subject to their utility <sup>V</sup> being positive. Buyer preferences are uniformly distributed around the circular market’s circumference.

Buyers do not know the exact product type offered by the seller. Their knowledge depends on the information that the firm or an independent infomediary has released about the product. The true product type offered by the firm is $d ,$ but the buyers cannot observe it. On the basis of available information, buyers form beliefs about the true product type being uniformly distributed in the interval $[ d _ { A } , d _ { B } ]$ , which contains the product’s true type, that is, $d \in [ d _ { A } , d _ { B } ]$ . The interval $[ d _ { A } , d _ { B } ]$ , of size $a = d _ { B } - d _ { A }$ is termed the firm’s uncertainty interval.<sup>6</sup> The variable <sup>a</sup> is the measure of uncertainty that exists in the market about the product’s taste-related parameter and is the minimum of $\cdot _ { a _ { \nu } }$ and $a _ { i } ,$ which respectively denote the information that has been provided by the seller and the infomediary about the seller’s product: $a = \operatorname* { m i n } ( a _ { \nu } , a _ { i } )$ . In the Online Appendix we show that our results remain qualitatively the same, even if we model product uncertainty by making use of an imperfect signal, as opposed to an uncertainty interval.

In order for the market to be able to operate at all, we must have at least some buyers with positive expected utility $\nu - p - F ( a , x ) > 0 .$ , which translates to the requirement that $a \leq 4 \nu / t .$ . In the rest of the study we make this assumption.

We plot Equation (2) on the right-hand side of Figure 2. We see that as long as there is some uncertainty in the market $( a > 0 )$ , the expected fit cost can never be zero. Note that for $x \geq a / 2 ,$ , that is, if the buyer’s ideal location <sup>z</sup> is outside the seller’s uncertainty interval, then uncertainty (the size of <sup>a</sup>) has no impact on the buyer’s fit cost. Intuitively, as we increase uncertainty, we worsen the worst-case scenario for the buyer, that is, the product may actually be even farther away from the buyer’s ideal. However, at the same time, we improve the best-case scenario, which is that the product is even closer to the buyer’s ideal. On average, Equation (2) says that a risk-neutral buyer would be indifferent in this case. This contrasts with a buyer for which the ideal location <sup>z</sup> is inside the seller’s uncertainty interval, with $x < a / 2$ . In this case, increasing <sup>a</sup> would worsen the worst-case scenario for the buyer (as before) but would not improve the bestcase scenario, which remains that the seller’s product is exactly located at <sup>z</sup>.

Also note that, whereas a buyer’s utility after consumption depends on the product’s actual type <sup>d</sup>—Equation (1)—the buyer’s decision on whether or not to purchase the product depends on the expected fit cost. This is because, prior to purchase, the product’s true type <sup>d</sup> is unobservable, and the best possible information that the buyer has about the product is summarized in the position and size of the seller’s uncertainty interval. Only after the product is purchased and used, does a buyer learn its true type.

We model the above as a multistage game as follows:

● Stage 1: The firm chooses the amount of information to release $a _ { \nu }$

● Stage 2: The infomediary observes $a _ { \nu }$ and chooses its own information disclosure $a _ { i }$

● Stage 3: The firm chooses product price

After the firm and the infomediary make their choices, each buyer buys either one (if utility is positive) or zero units of the good.

The seller discloses first, since only it has access to its product’s information before the product is released. It also has the incentive to do so: disclosing first allows it to act strategically and free ride on the infomediary’s information disclosure. It is thus natural for our model to have the seller as the Stackelberg first mover. We must note that for some products (e.g., electronics) infomediaries may provide product information even before product launch. However, this information tends to be mostly about product specifications, that is, about the free component of product information. Because this entails no disclosure cost, letting the infomediary disclose before the seller would not change our results. In the Discussion section, we return to this issue by arguing that the infomediary must try to reliably commit to a disclosure level, before the firm does. In the Online Appendix we also study simultaneous disclosure, which we show does not affect our results qualitatively.

As the most flexible of the decision variables, pricing is decided last. To better understand this, it is useful to see it as the firm being able to simply increase or reduce its price after the infomediary disclosure.<sup>8</sup> It would not seem natural to forbid the firm to do so. In contrast to this, information cannot be taken back as a response to a price change.<sup>9</sup> Nevertheless, our results remain qualitatively the same, even if the firm commits to a price up front, as happens with many products whose price is announced at launch and does not change until the next product generation (e.g., smartphones).

Finally, the buyers’ information retrieval process is assumed to be simple: the buyers are able to costlessly learn the interval of size <sup>a</sup> inside which the seller’s product is located, either from the seller, or from the infomediary, if the infomediary released more information than the seller $( a _ { i } < a _ { \nu } )$ . Given the model, the buyers will then form the rational expectation that the product is uniformly located inside this uncertainty interval.

## Model Details and Players’ Incentives

Since there is no established way to model information disclosure cost in the spatial differentiation literature, we proceed to explain our modeling choices in some detail.

Part of the product information can be communicated to the buyers at trivial cost through product specs that are easy to understand, such as size, color, weight, and so on. For convenience we assume such costs to be zero.<sup>10</sup> However, part of the product information is difficult and expensive to communicate. It may for example require extensive products tests, or actual experience of using the product, perhaps over a prolonged period. In this case, the seller or the infomediary must invest in order to, among other things, put the product through thorough testing (CNet.com), better organize and present available information by use of interactive technology and rich media (Zappos), provide real or virtual product samples (Amazon, Netflix), and so forth. The magnitude of the component of the product’s information that is expensive to communicate is denoted by $a _ { x } .$ 11

A seller can reduce buyer uncertainty all the way down to $a _ { x }$ at zero cost by communicating the component of the product’s information that is free to disclose. However, below $a _ { x }$ it becomes increasingly expensive to reduce buyer uncertainty.<sup>12</sup>

A seller information disclosure cost function $c _ { \nu } { \left( a _ { \nu } \right) }$ that is consistent with the above discussion is (see also Figure 3):

$$
c _ {v} (a _ {v}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} m _ {v} \Big (\frac {1}{a _ {v}} - \frac {1}{a _ {x}} \Big), & a _ {v} <   a _ {x} \\ 0, & a _ {v} \geq a _ {x} \end{array} \right.,\tag{3}
$$

where $a _ { \nu }$ is the amount of information that the seller releases (lower $a _ { \nu }$ means more information), and the $m _ { \nu }$ parameter determines the information disclosure cost for a given disclosure level $a _ { \nu } .$ . Markets with higher $m _ { \nu }$ have products whose information is more difficult to communicate than markets with lower $m _ { \nu } ,$ for any required level of specificity.

For the infomediary, who invests in information disclosure after the seller, the cost $c _ { i }$ to reduce uncertainty to the level $a _ { \nu } ,$ already disclosed by the seller, is zero.<sup>13</sup> Indeed, once a seller reduces uncertainty to $a _ { \nu } ,$ the infomediary can costlessly achieve the same level of information disclosure simply by referring buyers to the information that has been made available by the seller. However, the cost of providing additional information, beyond $a _ { \nu } ,$ , matches the seller’s information disclosure cost. Therefore, the cost $c _ { i }$ for the infomediary to reduce buyer uncertainty to $a _ { i }$ is given by:

$$
c _ {i} (a _ {i}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c} m _ {i} \Big (\frac {1}{a _ {i}} - \frac {1}{a _ {x}} \Big), a _ {i} <   a _ {v} \\ 0, a _ {i} \geq a _ {v} \end{array} , \right.\tag{4}
$$

where, again, $m _ { i }$ is a parameter that determines the information disclosure investment cost, for any given level of disclosure $a _ { i }$

We will analyze the model under the simplifying assumption that $m _ { \nu } = m _ { i } = m$ This is the neutral case in which the nonzero parts of the cost functions of the infomediary and the seller are identical (neither party has a cost advantage when conveying product information to buyers), so that none of our results and policy recommendations about how infomediaries should operate, can be attributed to differences in efficiency in conveying product information to buyers. In the Online Appendix we discuss and show how $m _ { \nu } > m _ { i }$ and $m _ { \nu } < m _ { i }$ impact our results: we explain that $m _ { \nu } < m _ { i }$ (the seller has a cost advantage over the infomediary in conveying product information to buyers) strengthens our results, while $m _ { \nu } > m _ { i }$ (the infomediary is more efficient than the seller in ascertaining and disseminating product information) has the opposite effect. In the extreme case $m _ { \nu } \gg m _ { i }$ the infomediary can only improve social welfare and consumer surplus with its operation.

Equation (4), displayed schematically in Figure 3, assumes that when the infomediary reduces consumer uncertainty down to $a _ { i } < a _ { \nu } ,$ , he does not benefit from the efforts of the seller to reduce uncertainty down to $a _ { \nu } .$ . This is reasonable if we assume that an entirely new process is required to obtain and disseminate more information than $a _ { \nu }$ . For example, the infomediary may be required to put the product through completely different tests or through a completely different use case, in order to reach the $a _ { i } < a _ { \nu }$ level of information. Alternatively, we could have assumed that the infomediary does benefit from the efforts of the seller to reduce uncertainty down to $a _ { \nu } ,$ and that the infomediary only incurs the same marginal cost to reach information level $a _ { i } < a _ { \nu }$ . For example, the infomediary may be required only to perform additional product tests, and not entirely new ones. In that case, Equation (5) would be a more reasonable assumption for the infomediary’s disclosure cost (see also Figure 4). While we use Equation (4) for the remainder of this study, in fact both Equations (4) and (5) yield exactly the same equilibrium. We will present the intuition for this when we discuss the robustness of our model below.

$$
\text { alternative }: c _ {i} (a _ {i}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} m \Big (\frac {1}{a _ {i}} - \frac {1}{a _ {x}} \Big) - c _ {v} (a _ {v}) = m \Big (\frac {1}{a _ {i}} - \frac {1}{a _ {v}} \Big), & a _ {i} <   a _ {v} \\ 0, & a _ {i} \geq a _ {v} \end{array} \right..\tag{5}
$$

The seller’s objective is to maximize profit. For the infomediary we consider a range of different incentives in the Online Appendix. Here we consider the most stringent possible circumstances. It is relatively easy to show that the infomediary may reduce social welfare and even buyer surplus, when it is pursuing its own interests. We would least expect these results, especially the reduction of buyer surplus, to be true when the infomediary is buyer operated, because such an infomediary would be actively seeking to prevent buyer surplus from reducing. The objective of a buyer-operated infomediary [30] would be to maximize total buyer utility minus its information disclosure cost. In other words the infomediary will release more product information as long as the buyer surplus generated exceeds the information disclosure cost. In the Online Appendix we show that our results remain qualitatively the same for a wide range of infomediary objective functions, including product information websites that charge the firm a commission, or others that profit mainly through advertising. We also show that our results remain qualitatively the same, when multiple infomediaries compete for seller referral fees.

To reiterate, while we consider a range of infomediary incentives in the Online Appendix, here we assume an infomediary whose incentives are aligned with those of the buyers as the most conservative modeling assumption. Our finding that the infomediary may reduce buyer surplus becomes most interesting (and significant) when we show that even the infomediary whose incentives are aligned with those of buyers and who does not seek to benefit at their expense, ends up lowering buyer surplus.

Given price $p ,$ the distance x from the center of the uncertainty interval of the last buyer who expects to receive positive utility from buying the product is given by solving $\nu - p - F ( a , x ) = 0$ , where $F ( \boldsymbol { a } , \boldsymbol { x } )$ is given by Equation (2). Solving for x, and taking into account buyers on both sides of the seller’s uncertainty interval, we derive the demand function for the seller, which has two branches, due to the two branches of $F ( \boldsymbol { a } , \boldsymbol { x } )$

$$
D (a, p) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 2 \frac {v - p}{t}, & \frac {v - p}{t} \geq \frac {a}{2} \\ 2 \sqrt {a \frac {v - p}{t} - \frac {a ^ {2}}{4}}, & \frac {v - p}{t} \leq \frac {a}{2} \end{array} \right..\tag{6}
$$

The firm’s profit function is given by:

$$
\Pi (a _ {v}, p) = p \cdot D (a, p) - c _ {v} (a _ {v}).\tag{7}
$$

Note that the demand that the seller faces depends on the level of information available to buyers $^ { a , }$ but the information disclosure cost $c _ { \nu } { \left( a _ { \nu } \right) }$ depends on the amount of information that the seller releases. The two may not be the same if the infomediary invests in releasing information $a _ { i } < a _ { \nu } ,$ in which case the seller would have incurred cost $c _ { \nu } { \left( a _ { \nu } \right) }$ , but the buyers will make their purchase decisions based on $a = \operatorname* { m i n } ( a _ { i } , a _ { \nu } ) = a _ { i } .$

Total buyer surplus is given by (see also the Appendix):

$$
U _ {B} (a, p) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {p ^ {2} + v ^ {2}}{t} - \frac {t \cdot a ^ {2}}{1 2} - \frac {2 \cdot p \cdot v}{t}, & \frac {v - p}{t} \geq \frac {a}{2} \\ \frac {4 v - 4 p - a \cdot t}{6} \sqrt {\frac {a \cdot (4 v - 4 p - a \cdot t)}{t}}, & \frac {v - p}{t} \leq \frac {a}{2} \end{array} \right..\tag{8}
$$

As discussed, the infomediary tries to maximize total buyer surplus minus the disclosure cost. Its utility $U _ { i }$ is:

$$
U _ {i} (a _ {i}) = U _ {B} - c _ {i} (a _ {i}).\tag{9}
$$

We take social welfare to be the efficiency with which the market allocates its resources [28, 31]. Specifically, we measure the efficiency with which products are allocated to buyers in aggregate, minus any wasteful costs associated with the allocation (in our case, the cost of disclosures). Starting with $\begin{array} { r } { - c _ { i } ( a _ { i } ) + \sum _ { a l l B u y e r s } ( \nu - y \cdot t ) } \end{array}$ , and by adding and subtracting <sup>p</sup> inside the summation, we derive the social welfare as:

$$
S _ {M} = U _ {B} (a, p) + \Pi (a _ {v}, p) - c _ {i} (a _ {i}).\tag{10}
$$

Equation (10) intuitively says that social welfare equals the buyer surplus plus any seller profit, minus the disclosure cost, with any transfer payments canceling out.

Proposition 1 shows that if buyer uncertainty is below a certain threshold $( a \leq \nu / t )$ the seller’s optimal price is not affected by uncertainty. It is easy to verify this as the threshold that places the marginal buyer just outside the seller’s uncertainty interval, so that, as we discussed for Equation (2), the marginal buyer is no longer affected by uncertainty. If, however, the information released in the previous game stages by the infomediary and the seller is not enough to place the marginal buyer outside the uncertainty interval, then, as the second branch in Proposition 1 shows, the seller must also consider the amount of buyer uncertainty, when setting product price.

For $m > m _ { m a x } ,$ the information that the infomediary would disclose on its own is too little from the seller’s perspective. The buyers capture too little of the efficiency gain from more information, and the buyer-operated infomediary is unwilling to incur too much disclosure cost. It is preferable for the seller to incur the information disclosure cost and benefit from the resulting revenue increase.

For $m _ { m i n } < m < m _ { m a x }$ the seller would, in the absence of an infomediary, prefer a lower level of buyer uncertainty than what results in a market with an infomediary. However, now the seller finds that the infomediary disclosure would be “good enough.” The buyers capture enough of the efficiency gain from more information for the buyer-operated infomediary to be willing to make a more substantial investment in disclosure. It is now preferable for the seller to avoid the information disclosure cost, even if it means lower revenue as a result. Thus, the seller takes advantage of the infomediary, and chooses not to disclose any information, setting $a _ { \nu } = a _ { x } = 0 . 2$ . The infomediary then reduces buyer uncertainty to levels that range from $a _ { i } \cong 0 . 0 7$ to $a _ { i } \cong 0 . 1 0$ , which is less information disclosure than what the seller would have chosen in the absence of the infomediary. However, the seller saves on information disclosure costs and increases its profit.

Finally, for $m < m _ { m i n }$ the optimal disclosure lever for the infomediary switches from $a _ { i } = a _ { i h } > \nu / t$ to $a _ { i } = a _ { i l } < \nu / t$ (see Figure 5), as the peak at $a _ { i l }$ becomes higher than the peak at $a _ { i h }$ , and we see a discontinuous increase in the amount of information that becomes available to buyers. What happens is that, for $m < m _ { m i n } ,$ information is cheap enough to disclose that the infomediary chooses a level of uncertainty that is low enough for the marginal buyers to be outside uncertainty intervals (as we discussed for Proposition 1, this occurs when $a _ { i } < \nu / t )$ . As we showed earlier, the seller would never disclose more information than $\nu / t ,$ since he would only be improving the expected utility of supramarginal buyers, leaving the marginal buyers indifferent about the disclosure. Thus, for $m < m _ { m i n }$ the level of information availability continues to be determined by the infomediary.

While our model is in general intractable as an oligopoly, we expect similar results to hold in competitive markets as well, in cases where the seller side receives most of the benefits from increasing information availability.

When $m < m _ { m i n }$ , as per Proposition 2, the infomediary discloses $a _ { i } = a _ { i l } < \nu / t .$ As we discussed before, the seller would never provide more information than $\nu / t ,$ , as it cannot improve its profit by doing so (the marginal buyer no longer benefits from reduced uncertainty). Since the buyer population always benefits from increased information availability (Equation [8] is decreasing in <sup>a</sup>), the infomediary’s presence improves buyer surplus for $m < m _ { m i n }$ . In addition, since information disclosure beyond $\nu / t$ does not impact the firm, the socially optimal amount of information is simply the amount that maximizes buyer surplus, minus disclosure cost, that is, exactly the target information availability for the infomediary. This can be seen in Figure 6 where we can see that the infomediary discloses exactly the socially optimal amount for $m < m _ { m i n } .$ , and as can be seen in Figures 7 and 8, the infomediary causes consumer surplus and social welfare to increase.

When $m > m _ { m a x }$ (when disclosure is costly) only the seller discloses (see Proposition 2) and thus the infomediary does not contribute to buyer information, and consequently has no effect on the market.

Finally, the intuition as to why the infomediary reduces social welfare and buyer surplus when $m _ { m i n } < m < m _ { m a x }$ , is that the seller prefers to forgo disclosure investments, and lets the infomediary disclose instead. As we explained previously, when disclosure costs are moderate $( m \in [ m _ { m i n } , m _ { m a x } ] )$ , in the absence of the infomediary the seller would have disclosed even more information, so that the presence of the infomediary causes overall information availability to decline. The seller’s effort to extract additional profit by free riding on the infomediary’s disclosure, results in an even greater reduction in buyer surplus (Figure 7), which in turns leads to lower levels of social welfare (Figure 8).

Looking at the impact of product information websites on buyer surplus and social welfare across a wide range of information disclosure costs, we see that product information websites are beneficial only when information about the products is cheap to disseminate. On the other hand, when information is expensive to disseminate, the infomediaries’ presence cannot help buyers and it may actually make buyers worse off by enabling seller free riding.

Unfortunately, the implications of these results for the operation of product information websites are disheartening. When information is cheap to disseminate, it will tend to be plentiful, and arguably buyers will not need infomediaries as much. When information is expensive to disseminate, information availability will be low and it would be precisely then that buyers need the most help when collecting information online. Thus, the infomediaries’ negative impact on buyer surplus and social welfare when $m > m _ { m i n }$ is arguably more important than their positive impact when $m < m _ { m i n }$ . In the Discussion section we will return to these findings and offer our social policy recommendations.

## Model Robustness: Alternative Modeling Assumptions

When we introduced our model, we explained that our results remain the same when we use Equation (5) for the infomediary disclosure cost, that is, when we assume that the infomediary only has to incur the marginal information disclosure cost, beyond $a _ { \nu }$ . We can now see why. First, it is the marginal cost of information disclosure that determines the optimal information dissemination level for the infomediary, so that the infomediary behaves the same way in both cases. Second, even under Equation (5), it is still the case that the seller will only disclose $a _ { x }$ when free riding, so that the cost to the infomediary from Equation (5) becomes exactly equal to the cost given by Equation (4). The social welfare as well as the consumer surplus remain the same in both cases. Furthermore, in the Online Appendix, we also study the case where the seller or the infomediary may have a cost advantage when disclosing information (when $m _ { \nu } > m _ { i }$ and $m _ { \nu } < m _ { i } )$

In addition, our results remain qualitatively the same when we change other key assumptions in our model. Specifically, in the Online Appendix we study alternative business models for the infomediary that range from advertising-based revenues to charging the seller a per-sale commission, and we address the case where multiple infomediaries compete for referral fees. We also show that our results do not depend on the sequential nature of the game, or on the way that buyer uncertainty is modeled; we discuss an alternative model of buyer uncertainty similar to the model of Markopoulos and Clemons [17].

Thus, our results appear to be robust across a variety of different modeling assumptions and competitive regimes and apply just as well to different models of buyer uncertainty.

## Discussion and Policy Recommendations

The tendency of sellers to disclose less when buyers can obtain product information from third parties is well documented. Chen and Xie [6] showed that sellers often reduce information investments when third parties publish their own product reviews. In their study, sellers of running shoes reduced their advertising when a magazine published an independent review of their product. Sellers of high-quality running shoes reduced their advertising spend by 30 percent, while the reduction in the case of lower quality running shoes exceeded 70 percent. In this study we showed that in such cases, where sellers free ride on third-party information, consumers may end up with less information than they would have received in the absence of the third party.

Consider for example DxO Labs, the provider of information on cameras and lenses, which we mentioned earlier. Its operation may be allowing camera and lens manufacturers to invest less in informing consumers about their products. Specifically, lens manufacturers may be publishing fewer and less detailed MTF curve diagrams—an importance source of information on a number of lens characteristics. Indeed, the authors have observed that DxO often publishes MTF curves first, and photography news websites and photographers’ blogs quickly reproduce this information, reaching many photography aficionados either directly, or through news feeds (e.g., RSS) and social media (e.g., Twitter). The result is that the lens manufacturer now has little incentive to produce its own MTF curves, often leaving DxO as the only source of this type of information. Even if the lens manufacturer originally intended to produce even more detailed MTF diagrams, the marginal benefit of such an investment is now considerably lower. Photographers may end up receiving less information.<sup>14</sup>

There is an interesting social policy recommendation to be made. Infomediaries who act as alternative sources of product information must try to commit to a level of disclosure up front. For example, society will be better off if DxO Labs publicly commits that it will cease providing MTF curve information for lens manufacturers that fail to do so, and will only verify or correct the MTF curves that lens manufacturers themselves provide. If this commitment is enforced by DxO Labs and other infomediaries, lens manufacturers will be compelled to cease free riding, they will provide more information, and buyers will benefit from increased information availability.

Such recommendations may be surprising. We tend to think of infomediaries as providing valuable services to consumers, for example, in verifying seller’s claims, or even in correcting claims of sellers that may be exaggerating or that may be plainly untruthful about their products. Interpolating from these roles and also naively thinking that more information cannot possibly be hurting consumers, we may be inclined to uncritically accept infomediaries as beneficial to markets in general, and buyers in particular, provided that the infomediaries do not try to mislead or extract monopolistic rents from other market participants. We do not dispute that infomediaries can offer valuable services to the consumer. But we do raise questions about the infomediaries’ ability to increase consumer surplus by substituting for seller roles, especially in providing product information to buyers that wish to assess product fit.

If we were to offer an informed guess, we would argue that product information websites may be socially valuable when they fulfill roles that complement and add value to, rather than those that substitute for, information provided by product sellers. These roles go beyond those that we study in some detail in this paper (see Figure 9). If we look at the purchasing process from the consumer’s point of view, we find examples of value-added activities that the product information websites can assume, and that may help increase consumer surplus and social welfare. Throughout the process, such activities include helping buyers to objectively identify their needs, educating buyers about the product category and about the different options and sellers available, fact-checking seller claims, helping buyers assess the risk of transacting with different sellers, and enhancing buyer utility by providing useful product tips and information that helps product owners get the most out of their purchase (effectively raising product quality).

In some of these cases, the infomediaries assume complementary roles, that is, roles that the sellers themselves cannot or will not fulfill. For example, an infomediary may be increasing consumer utility by informing buyers about different seller options. Consumer utility may increase via the resulting increased competition between sellers, and even social welfare may benefit as the average misfit cost reduces by the expanded market coverage. In other roles the infomediaries may simply be more efficient than the seller in providing the relevant information. Such examples may include advice on proper product use, and care tips that ensure product longevity. The infomediaries may be especially adept at this last role for two reasons. First, they may be more cost-efficient in creating consumer communities where product owners can exchange such qualityenhancing product information. And second, such information would tend to increase utility for all buyers, even those who have already purchased the product—a population that sellers of non-repeat-purchase goods may not necessarily care strongly about.

## Conclusions

We used a game theoretic model and focused on the impact of product information websites on firm profitability, buyer surplus, and social welfare. Specifically, we modeled the dynamics of information revelation as a multistage game where both the seller and an infomediary invest in information disclosure.

We first showed that, in accordance with Chen and Xie’s paper [6], the presence of an infomediary allows the seller to free ride and reduce information disclosure investments, as the infomediary’s information disclosure becomes a substitute for the information that the seller would provide on its own accord. We showed that the presence of the infomediary could reduce buyer surplus and social welfare, even when the infomediary explicitly sets its goal as to make the buyers better off. Moreover, this occurs in regard to products for which it is costly to disseminate information, that is, it occurs exactly in those markets where independent product information websites could be of greatest value to buyers.

Our findings were shown to be robust to alternative modeling assumptions, including different infomediary business models. Combined with the increasing importance of product information in electronic retailing, and the proliferation of third-party information sources, our results seriously question the uncritical acceptance of infomediaries as beneficial to markets in general, and buyers in particular.

Clearly more work is needed to address these questions. Our study is but a first step that covers a small subset of the different infomediary roles, which we highlighted in the previous section for researchers who want to extend this research and build on its insights.

## Stage 1: Solving for Optimal Information Disclosure by the Seller

At the game’s first stage, the seller knows that the infomediary will, in the next stage, choose to disclose information according to Equation (12) and that, subsequently, the seller will price according to Proposition 1.

We first note that the seller will never choose $a _ { \nu } < \nu / t ,$ since, from our previous discussion, the seller’s profit for $a _ { \nu } < \nu / t$ will always be lower than profit for $a _ { \nu } = \nu / t$ . There are two possible choices for $a _ { i }$ . The first is $a _ { \nu h }$ , which maximizes the second branch of $\Pi ( a _ { \nu } , a _ { i } = a _ { \nu } )$ , with $a _ { i } = a _ { \nu } ,$ and does not have an analytic formula. The second possible equilibrium solution is $a _ { \nu } = a _ { x }$ , where the seller will only release the free component of the product information and incur zero information disclosure costs. Any other choice of $a _ { \nu }$ will be dominated either by $a _ { \nu h }$ or by $a _ { x } .$ For example, consider the seller choosing some $a _ { \nu } \geq \nu / t$ . If in the next stage $a _ { i } ^ { * } ( a _ { \nu } ) < a _ { \nu }$ , then the seller would have been better off having chosen $a _ { \nu } = a _ { x }$ , in order to forgo the information disclosure cost. $$
a _ {v} ^ {*} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} a _ {v h}, & \Pi \big (a _ {v h}, a _ {i} ^ {*} (a _ {v h}) \big) > \Pi \big (a _ {x}, a _ {i} ^ {*} (a _ {x}) \big) \\ a _ {x}, & \Pi \big (a _ {v h}, a _ {i} ^ {*} (a _ {v h}) \big) \leq \Pi \big (a _ {x}, a _ {i} ^ {*} (a _ {x}) \big) \end{array} \right..\tag{13}
