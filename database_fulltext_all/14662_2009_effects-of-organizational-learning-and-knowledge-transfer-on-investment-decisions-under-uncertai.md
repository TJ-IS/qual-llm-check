Ab stract: Because uncertainties around innovative technologies resolve over time, investments in such technologies are often made in stages so that organizations can use the knowledge gained from earlier stages to decide the next step. Previous studies usually assume that once some uncertainty is resolved, it becomes common knowledge within the investing organization. We develop a game-theoretical model to study how different parties within an organization gain and transfer knowledge about new technologies while investing in these technologies, and how the learning process may affect the investment decisions. We show that managers with incentives misaligned with the organization may transfer their knowledge untruthfully and distort the learning process of decision makers. Such behavior may lead to inefficient investment decisions. We also study the effect of uncertainty on the misreporting problem and the investment decisions. Mechanisms to mitigate or prevent untruthful knowledge transfer are also proposed. In particular, powerful incentive schemes may alleviate, but not prevent, the misreporting problem; punishing managers who are caught misreporting may deter the misreporting behavior, but in practice such mechanisms are difficult to implement.

Key words and phrases: economic analysis, game theory, investment under uncertainty, knowledge transfer, organizational learning, signal jamming.

Investments in research and devel opment (R&D) of a new technology are often made when the potential value of the technology is still uncertain. Investing before uncertainty is fully resolved may provide firms with strategic advantages such as preempting competitors; however, it also kills the option to wait and may lead to regret (see, among others, [12, 18, 22]). As a result, firms often make R&D investments in multiple stages, so that at each stage they can decide whether to continue to invest based on the information gained earlier [21, 24].

What has largely been ignored is that the process in which uncertainty around a new technology resolves is also one through which the investing organizations learn about the potential of the technology. Previous studies usually assume that once some uncertainty is resolved, it becomes common knowledge within the investing organization. In other words, the details of organizational learning—how participants learn about a new technology and how the knowledge is transferred from one participant to another within an organization—have not been scrutinized.

This paper focuses on how organizations gain knowledge about new technologies while investing in these technologies, and how the learning process may affect the investment decisions. We posit that learning about the potential of an unproven technology and transferring this knowledge effectively within an organization present new challenges to investment decisions under uncertainty. We develop a multiperiod game-theoretical model to describe and explore the effects of learning and knowledge transfer processes on investment decisions.

Individual and organizational knowledge often take the form of beliefs about reality [1, 25]. In our model, a participant’s knowledge about a new, unproven technology involves a belief in the potential value of the technology. Some new technologies are more likely to be technically and commercially successful than others. Players have initial beliefs about how likely the new technology will succeed. Further, as more information is revealed over time, players update their beliefs. One key feature of our model is that players learn about the potential of a technology through different conduits, and thus update their beliefs in different patterns. Specifically, all players hold the same initial belief about a new technology’s potential value before an investment is made. Once a firm invests in the R&D of this technology, players in the firm can either observe or get reports about the progress of the project, and revise their beliefs about the potential of the technology accordingly.

In large organizations, major investment decisions are often made at the highest corporate level based on reports from lower-level managers. Lower-level managers usually have access to firsthand information regarding the progress of projects. Decision makers rely on these managers, who provide updated information about the projects. Such information asymmetry between decision makers and lower-level managers may not be a serious concern when the projects are routine and reported outcomes can be easily verified. However, when a firm is investing in a novel technology and there is uncertainty around the potential of the technology, managers with direct access to the project may understand the technology much better than decision makers, and the decision makers may not be able to fully observe the progress of the project. This has been well documented in the product development processes of many industries, including software [11], aerospace [6], automotive [26, 34], and electronics [33]. Practices such as offshore sourcing of the development of new products and systems may reinforce the discrepancies between decision makers and lower-level managers [7]. In such situations, it is critical for managers to truthfully report the progress of a new technology project so that decision makers can make well-informed decisions.

When a new technology is not as promising as expected or the project progress is less than satisfactory, managers may not want to report the true status of the project for a sundry of reasons. For example, they may fear that such reports will be interpreted as their failure as managers rather than the technology’s lack of potential. Or they may want to entrench their positions in the organization by continuing working on the project and fear that a negative report will lead to the abandonment of the project. Research has shown that concealment of problems is quite common in R&D efforts [15, 27, 30]. For example, Ford and Sterman [14] observe that the project team leaders at a defense contractor were known as a “liar’s club” because at their weekly meeting everyone withheld knowledge about the problems and delays in their subsystems.

Our model describes the interactions between two parties—a decision maker and a project manager. The decision maker and the manager have the same knowledge (belief) about a new technology at date 0. At date 1, the manager gains new knowledge and acts as a knowledge sender, who transfers his or her knowledge to the decision maker, a knowledge receiver. If the manager untruthfully reports the progress of a new technology project, it may cause the decision maker to make inefficient decisions.

Our results show that the possibility that the decision maker may not learn the true knowledge may result in two types of inefficient investment decisions. Before an investment is made, the decision maker anticipates possible misreporting from the manager and thus lowers his or her expectation about the value of the investment, which leads to underinvestment. Once the investment is made, however, the decision maker has to rely on the manager’s report when choosing whether to continue with the investment, and may continue when it should be abandoned.

Our research contributes to several strands of literature. It has been recognized that aligning participants’ incentives with organizational objectives is an important dimension for the design of information and knowledge management systems [3]. Building on the sender–receiver framework for knowledge transfer proposed by Lin et al. [23], we study how information asymmetry and misaligned incentives between parties may challenge knowledge transfer in the setting of investment decisions.

March [25] describes how organizations learn from individuals’ knowledge and notes that it is possible that in equilibrium all individuals and the organization may share the same but inaccurate beliefs. We use game theory to explicitly model the behavior of different parties in an organization and show how undesirable knowledge transfer outcomes may happen.

Our research also contributes to the literature on investment under uncertainty. We highlight the importance of parties’ learning behavior when organizations make investments under uncertainty, which has been vaguely alluded to in the real options theory and the strategic investment literature.

Our model falls into the broad category of sender–receiver games (see [2, 10, 17, 28]) and, in particular, signal-jamming games (see, among others, [16, 19, 29]). Our model has a signal-jamming feature because the manager’s report may “jam” the transmission of the true signal about the type of the technology, which may lead to the decision maker’s suboptimal decisions. It must be noted that our model departs from the principal–agent literature in the following important ways. First, while the manager has private information about his or her type, in our model this private information is not what the decision maker is trying to find out; the decision maker is trying to learn about the type of the technology, which is crucial to his or her investment decisions. Second, the information structure in our model evolves over time: the decision maker and manager hold symmetric beliefs about the type of the technology at date 0, but asymmetric beliefs emerge at date 1, when the decision maker and manager gain information in different ways.

## Model Setup

There are three dates—0, 1, and 2—and the time intervals between two adjacent dates are called period 1 and period 2. We assume that there is no discounting between the time periods. At date 0, a firm endowed with one unit of asset decides whether to invest in the R&D of a new technology. If the firm decides not to invest, the unit asset will have a deterministic value at date 1, which we normalize to 1. We assume that the opportunity to invest in the new technology only occurs at date 0. Therefore, if the firm does not invest at date 0, it will no longer have the option to invest at date 1, and thus the value of the asset will remain the same (at the value of 1) throughout both periods.

If the firm invests in the new technology at date 0, the project may last two periods until date 2. Let v denote the realized value of the asset at date 2 if the firm chooses to invest at date 0. In period 1 (the time period between date 0 and date 1), the firm engages in the R&D efforts. If the R&D efforts meet the period 1 goals, which we call a success or a good outcome, the value of the asset becomes $g$ at date 1. If the R&D efforts do not make satisfactory progress in period 1, which we call a failure or a bad outcome, the asset is worth b at date 1. We assume that investing in a new technology leads to a higher value if it succeeds but a lower value otherwise.

## Results

We now study the case where the decision mak er cannot observe the period 1 outcome directly and has to rely on a report from the project manager. We solve the model using backward induction. Based on D’s choice at date 0, there are two subgames: the one where D chooses to invest in a new technology (called the “investment” subgame), and the one where D chooses not to invest (called the “no investment” subgame). We first characterize the equilibrium in the investment subgame, and then discuss D’s investment decision at date 0.

## Misreporting and Distorted Learning

Because this is a game with incomplete information, the equilibrium concept we use is the perfect Bayesian equilibrium with the intuitive criterion (PBE for short). The intuitive criterion is a widely adopted equilibrium refinement mechanism for ruling out equilibria with unreasonable beliefs [8]. Generally, we assume that there exists a PBE for the investment subgame. In any PBE, M knows D’s updating process. As a result, after observing the true period 1 outcome and before deciding whether to report truthfully, M is well aware of the consequences of his or her choice. Because he or she gains a personal benefit from the continuation of the project, M will always truthfully report a good period 1 outcome. When the period 1 outcome is bad, he or she misreports (or cheats) if and only if his or her expected gain from cheating outweighs his or her cost.

Similarly, we use the hat symbol for D’s beliefs based on reported outcomes rather than true outcomes. For example, the notation $\gamma _ { 1 g }$ still represents the believed probability that the technology is a high type updated at date 1 based on a true period 1 success, while $\hat { \boldsymbol { \gamma } } _ { 1 \hat { g } }$ is D’s updated belief about the probability of the technology being a high type based on a reported period 1 success. The following equations show how D updates his or her belief about the new technology being a high type upon receiving a report on the period 1 outcome:

If the reported outcome is good:

$$

$$

If the reported outcome is bad:

$$
\hat {\gamma} _ {1 \hat {b}} = P \left(\beta = \beta_ {h} | \hat {b}\right) = \gamma_ {1 b} = \frac {\left(1 - \beta_ {h}\right) \gamma_ {0}}{\left(1 - \beta_ {h}\right) \gamma_ {0} + \left(1 - \beta_ {l}\right) \left(1 - \gamma_ {0}\right)}.
$$

Note that $\gamma _ { 1 \hat { b } } = \gamma _ { 1 b }$ because the manager will never misreport a period 1 success. The next proposition shows that, although M may misreport the period 1 outcome and distort D’s learning process, D may still gain some knowledge.

Proposition 3 is a strong result in the sense that it holds for all possible functional forms of the distribution function of the private benefit to the manager, $F ( \cdot )$ , as long as F(·) is continuous and monotonically increasing.<sup>5</sup>

From Proposition 3, we see that when $\hat { \boldsymbol { \eta } } _ { 1 \hat { g } } g + ( 1 - \hat { \boldsymbol { \eta } } _ { 1 \hat { g } } ) \boldsymbol { b } \leq 1$ , the period 1 outcome does not benefit D at all because he or she completely ignores M’s report. In this case, a project that has yielded a true period 1 success will be abandoned due to the high likelihood of M’s misreporting, leading to inefficient decisions.

We also see that when $\hat { \Pi } _ { 1 \hat { g } } g + ( 1 - \hat { \eta } _ { 1 \hat { g } } ) b > 1$ , D can partially learn from the reported period 1 outcome and the optimal strategy for D at date 1 is to continue with the R&D efforts when receiving a good report. From D’s point of view, he or she makes the optimal decision based on all of the information available to him or her, including the report he or she gets from M and his or her knowledge about the likelihood of M’s cheating. When the report of a good period 1 outcome is untrue, D is misled to inefficiently continue with the new technology investment. But, when $\hat { \Pi } _ { 1 \hat { g } } g + ( 1 - \hat { \eta } _ { 1 \hat { g } } ) b > 1$ , it is in D’s best interests to continue with the R&D upon receiving a good report, even though he or she is aware of the possible cheating by M and of the fact that continuation in the event of cheating is inefficient.

This may explain why even though it is well known that managers usually report good news and hide the problems in their projects, decision makers still often choose to continue funding the projects.

Nevertheless, the possibility of M’s cheating makes the date 0 expected value of the project lower than in the case of perfect learning. The next subsection discusses D’s investment decision at date 0.

## Investment Decisions Under Distorted Learning and Possible Misreporting

We have considered how M’s behavior may distort D’s learning process at date 1 in the investment subgame. We now analyze D’s investment decision at date 0 when he or she expects distorted learning.

If $\hat { \Pi } _ { 1 \hat { g } } g + ( 1 - \hat { \eta } _ { 1 \hat { g } } ) b < 1$ , from Proposition 3 we know that D always abandons the new technology investment at date 1 in the investment subgame. Therefore, under this condition, the date 0 expected value of the investment is simply given by $\boldsymbol { \mathfrak { n } } _ { 0 } { g } + ( 1 - \boldsymbol { \mathfrak { n } } _ { 0 } ) \boldsymbol { b }$ In other words, the value of the investment in this case equals to that in the case of no learning. Based on Assumption 2, we know that D should choose not to invest at date 0. Because no investment is made at date 0, it should be noted that the case of a project with a true period 1 success being abandoned does not occur in equilibrium.

If $\hat { \Pi } _ { 1 \hat { g } } g + ( 1 - \hat { \eta } _ { 1 \hat { g } } ) b > 1$ , in the investment subgame, D chooses to continue with the new technology investment upon receiving a report of success, and discontinue otherwise. Therefore, the date 0 expected value of the investment is given by $\eta _ { _ 0 } g [ \eta _ { _ 1 g } g + ( 1 - \eta _ { _ 1 g } ) b ] + ( 1 - \eta _ { _ 0 } ) b [ \varphi ( \eta _ { _ { 1 b } } g + ( 1 - \eta _ { _ { 1 b } } ) b ) + ( 1 - \varphi )$ . We denote this by $V _ { D L } ^ { I }$ (the subscript DL indicates distorted learning). The investment decision at date 0 thus depends on the value of $V _ { D L } ^ { I }$

Proposition 4 (Optimal Investment Decision at Date 0 in the Case of Distorted Learning): (a) $I f \hat { \Pi } _ { _ { I \hat { g } } } g + ( I - \hat { \Pi } _ { _ { I \hat { g } } } ) b < I ,$ , at date 0, D chooses not to invest in the new technology investment; and (b) $i f { \hat { \Pi } } _ { I { \hat { g } } } g + ( I - { \hat { \Pi } } _ { I { \hat { g } } } ) b > I ,$ at date 0, D chooses not to invest in new technology $i f V _ { D L } ^ { I } < I _ { ; }$ , where $\begin{array} { r } { V _ { D L } ^ { I ^ { - } } = \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { o } g l \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { _ { I g } } g + ( I - \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { _ { I g } } ) b J + } \end{array}$ $( I - \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { o } ) b [ \boldsymbol { \phi } ( \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { _ { I b } } g + ( I - \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { _ { I b } } ) b ) + ( I - \boldsymbol { \phi } ) J ,$ , and to invest $i f V _ { D L } ^ { I } > I ;$ at date 1, D chooses to continue with the new technology investment upon receiving a good reported outcome, and discontinue otherwise, while M’s strategies at date 1 are as described in Proposition 1.

In the perfect learning case, investing in the new technology has a higher expected return than not investing. Under distorted learning, however, the decision maker expects possible misreporting from the manager once the new technology investment is chosen, and consequently the decision maker discounts the value of the new technology investment ex ante. Proposition 4 shows that the expectation of possible cheating by M may lower the expected value of the investment to such an extent that causes D to shun away from the new technology. Therefore, comparing to the case of perfect learning, the misreporting problem may lead to underinvestment.

It should be noted that there are two cases in which D does not invest at date 0. First, in the case of $\hat { \Pi } _ { 1 \hat { g } } g + ( 1 - \hat { \eta } _ { 1 \hat { g } } ) b < 1$ , the misreporting problem is so severe that D expects no learning at date 1, and thus the expected value of the project is the same as that in the “no learning” case, which means D should not invest at date 0. Note that in this case, if the project started at date 0, D would abandon the project regardless of any report at date 1 anyway. Second, when $\hat { \Pi } _ { 1 \hat { \varrho } } g + ( 1 - \hat { \eta } _ { 1 \hat { \varrho } } ) b > 1$ , D does expect to gain some knowledge from M’s report, but when $V _ { D L } ^ { I } < 1$ , the value gained from the possibly distorted knowledge is not sufficient to justify the investment.

Proposition 4 shows that the anticipated misreporting problem lowers the expected value of an investment, and as a result projects that would be undertaken under perfect learning may not be funded, leading to underinvestment. Remarkably, this proposition also shows that the case where D invests at date 0 but simply abandons the project regardless of M’s report at date 1 does not happen in equilibrium. This is because if at date 0, D anticipates no learning at date 1, he or she would not undertake the project. Therefore, once a project gets funded, the inefficiency of the date 1 decision stems solely from the fact that some projects are not properly abandoned, due to misreporting.

We discuss the case of distorted learning $( \hat { \boldsymbol { \eta } } _ { 1 \hat { \boldsymbol { g } } } { \boldsymbol { g } } + ( 1 - \hat { \boldsymbol { \eta } } _ { 1 \hat { \boldsymbol { g } } } ) { \boldsymbol { b } } > 1 )$ a little further. For any given parameters g $, b , \beta _ { l } , \beta _ { h } ,$ , and $\gamma _ { 0 } ,$ and the function $F ( \cdot ) , V _ { D L } ^ { I }$ is a function of $p _ { m } .$ —that is, $V _ { D L } ^ { I } = V _ { D L } ^ { I } ( p _ { m } )$ . It is also intuitive and easy to prove that $V _ { D L } ^ { I } ( p _ { m } )$ is strictly increasing in $\boldsymbol { p } _ { \boldsymbol { m } }$ . This is because the higher $p _ { { } _ { m } } .$ , the less likely that M misreports, and therefore the higher the value of learning and hence the value of the investment. When ${ p _ { { \scriptscriptstyle m } } } \to \bar { p }$ and thus $F ( p _ { { \scriptscriptstyle m } } ) \to 1$ , we see that $V _ { D L } ^ { I }  V _ { P L } ^ { I }$ , which means that when M almost never cheats, the value of the project under distorted learning is close to that under perfect learning.

Let $\tilde { p } _ { { } _ { m } }$ be the solution to $V _ { D L } ^ { I } ( p _ { m } ) = 1$ . When $p _ { { } _ { m } } < \tilde { p } _ { { } _ { m } } , V _ { { } _ { D L } } ^ { I } ( p _ { { } _ { m } } ) < 1$ , D will avoid the new technology investment. Note that even though M may in fact be honest, D still decides not to invest when $p _ { { } _ { m } } < { } \tilde { p } _ { { } _ { m } }$ . In other words, the existence of managers who are more likely to misreport can make companies shun away from new technology projects. It is worth noting, however, that this result is based on the assumption that the type of the manager of each firm is private information to the manager only. If a decision maker has information about the exact type of his or her manager, such as through auditing or past experience, the decision maker then knows that he or she will either learn perfectly (in the case of $p < { p } _ { m } )$ or learn nothing (in the case of $p > p _ { m } )$ at date 1. Interestingly, knowing the type of the manager does not automatically lead to efficient investment decisions. Of course, if D knows the type of M at date 0, he or she can hire only those M with $p < { p _ { m } }$ to ensure perfect learning, and thus can make efficient decisions at both date 0 and date 1. If, however, D only learns at date 1 that M’s type is $p > p _ { \eta }$ , this new information will cause D to abandon the investment, which may be inefficient if the outcome is in fact a success.

## The Effect of Uncertainty

In this sub section, we address a further question: How do the above results change when the technology investment is more uncertain?

To study the effect of uncertainty, we hold the expected value of the investment with no learning constant and vary the returns from successful and failed R&D efforts. Recall that $V _ { _ { N L } } ^ { I } = \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { _ 0 } g + ( 1 - \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { _ 0 } ) b$ . Let $\Delta = \{ g , b | \eta _ { \scriptscriptstyle 0 } g + ( 1 - \eta _ { \scriptscriptstyle 0 } ) b \equiv R \}$ ; thus for a given level of $\boldsymbol { \eta } _ { 0 } ,$ a higher $g$ implies a lower b and means a more uncertain technology for any set of $( g , b ) \in \Delta . ^ { 6 }$ We then analyze how the value of perfect and distorted learning would change with the level of uncertainty.

## The Effect of Uncertainty in the Case of Perfect Learning

First, we look at the effect of uncertainty in the case of perfect learning. Intuitively, when the technology is more uncertain (i.e., a good outcome is worth more and a bad outcome is worth less), in the event of a period 1 failure, there is always the option to abandon the investment to protect the company from further losses; if period 1 is a success, however, the company can continue with the investment and reap even higher value in the next period. Overall, as the following lemma shows, when the firm can perfectly observe the period 1 outcome, the benefit from the upside of a more uncertain project outweighs the cost of the downside.

Lemma 3 (Effect of Uncertainty in the Case of Perfect Learning): With perfect learning, the date 0 expected value of the new technology investment, $V _ { _ { P L } } ^ { I } = \boldsymbol { \eta _ { o } } g / \eta _ { _ { I g } } g \ + \ ( I - \boldsymbol { \eta _ { _ { I g } } } ) b J \ + \ ( I - \boldsymbol { \eta _ { _ { O } } } ) b ,$ , is an increasing function of g for $( g , b ) \in \Delta = \langle g , b | \eta _ { o } g + ( I - \eta _ { o } ) b \equiv R \rangle$

The above lemma shows that with perfect learning, higher uncertainty raises the value of the investment. Because we hold the value of the investment in the case of no learning constant, this result means that the value of learning (without misreporting) increases with uncertainty.

This result is consistent with the real options literature. In our model, the option to continue or stop the R&D efforts can be viewed as a growth option and a shutdown option. Under perfect learning, the firm can fully capture the value of these options. The real options literature has shown that for both types of real options, the value increases with uncertainty [21].

## The Effect of Uncertainty on the Manager’s Behavior

In the case of distorted learning—that is, when there is possible misreporting from the manager—the effect of uncertainty is unclear. On one hand, higher uncertainty leads to higher values of the options to continue and to stop the project. On the other hand, uncertainty may affect M’s behavior and the learning process. We first look at how M’s action changes with uncertainty.

Proposition 5 (Effect of Uncertainty on the Manager’s Behavior): Suppose at date 0, D chooses the new technology investment. Let $( g , b ) \in \Delta = \{ g , b | \mathfrak { n } _ { o } g \ +$ $( l - \mathfrak { \eta } _ { o } ) b \equiv R \}$ , and define

$$
k = \frac {\left(\beta_ {h} - \beta_ {l}\right) \gamma_ {0}}{\beta_ {l} + \left(\beta_ {h} - \beta_ {l}\right) \gamma_ {0}} \cdot \frac {\left(\beta_ {h} - \beta_ {l}\right) \left(1 - \gamma_ {0}\right)}{\left(1 - \beta_ {h}\right) + \left(\beta_ {h} - \beta_ {l}\right) \left(1 - \gamma_ {0}\right)}.
$$

When $b < R / 2 + ( I - R ) / ( 2 k )$ , (a) the likelihood that M cheats upon a bad period 1 outcome is an increasing function of g; and (b) when $b > R / 2 + ( I - R ) / ( 2 k )$ the likelihood that M cheats upon a bad period 1 outcome is a decreasing function of g.

Proposition 5 shows that, when the return to a failed project is very low, the more uncertain the project (a lower return for a failure, in other words, a higher return for a success), the more likely the manager will cheat (reporting a success when the actual outcome is a failure). The reason is as follows. For M, there are two effects of cheating when a period 1 failure occurs: on one hand, he or she gains a private benefit, which he or she would not get if he or she reports truthfully; but on the other hand, he or she expects lower incentive compensation, which is the cost of cheating. Therefore, when b is low, the expected cost of cheating is also low and actually decreases with uncertainty; therefore, higher uncertainty increases M’s propensity to cheat. Conversely, when b is high, the cost is relatively high and also increases with uncertainty, so higher uncertainty would discourage M from cheating.

In sum, the effect of uncertainty on M’s behavior is ambiguous: higher uncertainty may either discourage or induce M to misreport. However, innovative technologies are often characterized by a high value in the event of a success but a close-to-zero value otherwise. Therefore, it is more likely that uncertainty usually increases the manager’s propensity to cheat in new technology investments.

## The Effect of Uncertainty on Investment Decision Under Distorted Learning

We know that a higher level of uncertainty raises the value of the abandonment option and the growth option. Therefore, when a higher level of uncertainty discourages M from misreporting, obviously the expected value of the investment increases with the level of uncertainty, because more uncertainty not only makes the options more valuable but also leads to a higher degree of learning.

When a higher level of uncertainty increases M’s propensity to cheat, the influence of uncertainty on the investment decision is not straightforward. As uncertainty increases, on one hand, the misreporting problem becomes more severe, lowering the expected gain from learning; on the other hand, the abandonment and growth options become more valuable, raising the expected value of the investment. Because comparing these two effects is analytically intractable, we use simulations instead. It turns out that, for all parameter sets that we have tried, the effect of higher option value always dominates the effect of less learning, and therefore the value of the investment increases with uncertainty. ## Possible Remedies

Recal that M misreports the period 1 outcome when it is unobservable to D because (1) M’s incentive is not perfectly aligned with D’s, and (2) M has private information about his or her personal benefit. Therefore, remedies to this issue should focus on various ways to align the incentives of the manager and to make the manager’s incentives more transparent. We first examine whether the incentive compensation can alleviate the misreporting problem:

Proposition 6 (Powerful Incentives May Alleviate Misreporting): A higher fraction of the value of the asset received by M at date 2 (i.e., higher a) may alleviate the misreporting problem, and thus may lead to a higher expected value of a project at date 0 and a higher level of learning at date 1.

The effectiveness of powerful incentives may be limited. The fraction of the asset value that M receives tends to be small, and as long as $p _ { _ m } \equiv \alpha b [ 1 - \pmb { \eta } _ { _ { 1 b } } g - ( 1 - \pmb { \eta } _ { _ { 1 b } } ) ] <$ p, the misreporting problem persists.

Another remedy is to ex ante explicitly announce the punishment to the manager if cheating is discovered later on. Note that in our model, if the outcomes in both periods are bad, leading to a realized value of b<sup>2</sup>, this is evidence that M has cheated. Therefore, D can announce ex ante that if b<sup>2</sup> happens, M’s payoff will be reduced by d.

Proposition 7 (Punishment for Cheating May Deter Misreporting): Suppose D announces ex ante that if b<sup>2</sup> happens, M’s payoff will be reduced by d. In any PBE in which D continues the new technology investment upon a reported period 1 success and discontinues upon a reported failure, the marginal type of M is determined by $p _ { _ m } \equiv \alpha b { } [ { } I - \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { _ { I b } } g - ( I - \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { _ { I b } } ) b { } J + ( I - \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { _ { I b } } ) d .$ When

$$
d \geq \frac {\overline {{p}} - \alpha b \left[ 1 - \eta_ {1 b} g - (1 - \eta_ {1 b}) b \right]}{1 - \eta_ {1 b}},
$$

M always truthfully reports and perfect learning is achieved, leading to efficient investment decisions.

Proposition 7 shows that if D can credibly announce and enforce a sufficiently severe punishment for cheating behavior that gets exposed, misreporting can be prevented. However, in practice, this mechanism may be difficult to implement, because decision makers are often reluctant to punish managers, and therefore the announcement may not be credible.<sup>7</sup>

Overall, theoretically, contracts can be designed to mitigate or prevent misreporting, but there are limitations to such contracts in practice, and the empirical literature does show that such misreporting exists in many industries (see [14]).

## Concluding Remarks

Information technol og y (IT ) investments face the challenge of committing irreversible investments when uncertainty abounds, and there has been a growing stream of research on IT investments using real options analysis (see, among others, [4, 5, 9, 13, 31, 32]). While real options analysis suggests that abandonment options can be as valuable as growth options, it is observed that firms often escalate commitment to troubled IT projects [20].

This paper studies how an investment decision may be hampered by the learning and knowledge transfer processes within an organization. Uncertainty not only makes the overall evaluation difficult but also makes the organizational learning process more complicated. The key message of this paper is that the novelty of a technology often causes asymmetric learning processes of parties within an organization, and when parties with asymmetric knowledge also have misaligned incentives, inefficient investment decisions may occur.

In our model, managers learn about the potential of a new technology based on the intermediate outcome they observe. Decision makers, however, have to learn from the managers, who transfer knowledge to decision makers. Therefore, managers as knowledge senders have a chance to interfere with the learning process of the decision makers. If they have such an incentive, managers can “jam” the decision makers learning process by misreporting the observed outcome.

When managers (knowledge senders) do not transfer their knowledge truthfully, the decision makers are unable to correctly update their beliefs; thus, their knowledge is tainted. This is detrimental to firms because firms may forgo valuable investment opportunities due to these concerns. We propose several remedies that may alleviate the misreporting issue.

This research also highlights the importance of the management of new technology investments. For example, developing proper incentive schemes for managers, building a corporate culture that encourages early detection of problems, and so forth can be very important for companies to reap expected values from investments that are uncertain in nature.
