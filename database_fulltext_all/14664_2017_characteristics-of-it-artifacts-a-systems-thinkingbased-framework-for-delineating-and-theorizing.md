# Characteristics of IT artifacts: a systems thinking-based framework for delineating and theorizing IT artifacts

Sabine Matook\* & Susan A. Brown<sup>†</sup>

\*The University of Queensland, UQ Business School, 3 Blair Drive, 4072 Brisbane, QLD Australia, email: suebrown@eller.arizona.edu, and <sup>†</sup>The University of Arizona, Eller College of Management, Management Information Systems Department, 1130 E. Helen St., 430 W, Tucson, AZ 85721, USA, email: suebrown@eller.arizona.edu

Abstract. The information technology artifact (ITA) has been suggested as the core of information systems (IS) research, and the research community has been encouraged to deeply engage with the ITA. Various studies highlight, however, that the ITA continues to receive only limited attention and thus, little foundation exists for IS researchers to delineate and theorize about the ITAs studied. In this paper, we develop a framework that can be utilized as a language for articulating and theorizing the ITA in IS research. Our framework builds on the multi-faceted theoretical paradigm of systems thinking from which we derive several concepts and appropriate them to the context at hand, resulting in a seven-dimensional framework of characteristics for ITAs. In a literature survey of research on enterprise systems and enterprise resource planning systems in top IS journals, we show how ITA characteristics are currently included to present details of the relevant ITA instance and identify theoretical relationships between ITA characteristics and outcomes. We conclude the study by demonstrating the use of the ITA framework for delineating and theorizing the ITA in IS research. © 2016 Blackwell Publishing Ltd

Keywords: IT artifact, systems thinking, systems characteristics, information systems research

## INTRODUCTION

Over a decade ago, Orlikowski & Iacono (2001) called for deeper engagement with the information technology artifact (ITA). They showed that in information systems (IS) studies, the ITA was either nonexistent, taken for granted or treated as unproblematic. By discussing prior IS research views on the ITA, Orlikowski and Iacono illustrated <sup>fi</sup>ve broad meta-categories for IS researchers to theorize about the ITA and subseguently include resulting ITA-driven theories in their studies. Since then, much discussion has focused on the ITA suggesting it as the core of the IS discipline (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003), arguing for a macro view on the transformationa impact of the ITA (Agarwal & Lucas, 2005) and conceptualizing ITAs on their effects (Markus & Silver, 2008). However, little research has actually responded to Orlikowski and Iacono’s call for deeper engagement, as various studies repeatedly report a lack of ITA theorizing in IS research (for example, Ayanso et al., 2007; Akhlaghpour et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2011).

Some have argued that the lack of focus and subsequently, the lack of ITA theorizing has resulted in multiple ‘errors of inclusion’ (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003) being made by journals and conferences as they publish articles that do not belong in the IS literature. Our view is somewhat different. We suggest that ITA details can be found and theoretical relationships developed, but neither is done consistently across different studies nor is it obvious to the reader when it is done. Hence, we argue that instead of making ‘errors of inclusion’, IS research has such a varied level of detail and terminology for talking about the ITA that researchers are unsure how to present and theorize it in their research. Indeed, IS researchers would articulate numerous details of the ITA and include the ITA more explicitly in their theorizing process if they had a mechanism for doing so, for example by means of a theoretically derived framework. Prior research has developed such How To frameworks in a number of areas, for example for grounded theory (Urquhart et al., 2010), mixed-methods studies (Venkatesh et al., 2013) and positivist case study research (Dubé & Paré, 2003). These frameworks not only help authors to better explain their methodology, but they also assist reviewers and editors in evaluating it. Thus, it is our objective to create a conceptual framework – in the form of a typology – that IS researchers can utilize as a language for articulating and theorizing their ITA. A conceptual framework is one of three modes (the others are theories and models) of research formulation that enables contributions to knowledge in a discipline, in our case to IS (Shapira, 2011). In addition, readers, reviewers and editors can use the framework as an assessment tool for determining the extent to which a deep ITA engagement occurs in the research at hand.

The absence of such a framework poses three important concerns. First, it leads to assump tions made by researchers that may or may not be valid. For example, when differences in <sup>fi</sup>nd ings about enterprise resource planning systems (ERP) exist, should researchers assume that they are because of the context (e.g. users) because these studies all examine the same ITA (e. g. ERP)? With a language for talking about the ITA, researchers might be able to identify elements beyond the context that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the results. Second, without a common language, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to compare results across studies with different technologies. Should researchers un derstand <sup>fi</sup>ndings obtained from studies examining ERP and data warehousing differently be cause they are different ITAs, therefore any differences are because of the ITA? These two concerns lead to the ultimate problem – the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in building a cumulative tradition that would be founded on theoretical similarities and differences of ITAs rather than on speci<sup>fi</sup>c features of the ITA being studied. By drilling down into the characteristics of ITAs, one would gain a genera understanding of the ITA being studied, and how to include the ITA in theorizing in IS research. Consequently, we would be in a better position to comprehend the impacts of certain ITA char acteristics on dependent variables of interest.

Our ITA framework builds on systems thinking, a multi-facetted theoretical paradigm, that conceptualizes the world with its real and imagined elements as systems (Checkland, 1981; von Bertalanffy, 1972). Building on this theoretical foundation allows us to use systems thinking concepts to capture the structure and behaviour of ITAs internally and as they relate to thei environment (Ackoff, 1971). The result is a seven dimensional ITA characteristics framework for which systems thinking concepts are appropriated to the context of ITAs.

To explore how current research delineates and theorizes ITAs, the framework is applied in a literature survey of articles in major IS journals. We focus this literature survey on the ITA of en terprise systems/enterprise resource planning systems (ES/ERP) as they are of great interest in IS research and have been investigated in multiple prior studies (Nevo et al., 2010). Our results identify the way IS researchers de<sup>fi</sup>ne, articulate and theorize their ES/ERP artifacts. In so doing, we show that in some articles a relationship between ITA characteristics and organizational and user outcomes is made, although typically it is not obvious to the reader. We conclude our study with two examples of how IS researchers can use the framework in their own work. First, we demonstrate, with an ERP customer success story, how the framework can be used to delineate a speci<sup>fi</sup>c ITA instance. Second, we use Wixom & Watson’s (2001) data warehouse success model to present how the characteristics can become part of an ITA-driven theory. We conclude by discussing the positive potential implications the ITA framework offers for IS research.

## BACKGROUND

## Debates and de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of IT artifacts in IS research

Although IS research has focused on theoretical issues regarding the ITA for some time (see, for example Weber, 1987), the debate peaked with the articles by Orlikowski & Iacono (2001) and Benbasat & Zmud (2003), and continued with responses and assessments of the state of IS research (for example, Agarwal & Lucas, 2005; Robey, 2003; Galliers, 2003). The debate over the ITA was accompanied by the proclamation of an identity crisis in IS. Orlikowski & Iacono (2001) demonstrated with their <sup>fi</sup>ve views (i.e. tool, proxy, ensemble, computational, nominal) that IS research has no deep engagement with the ITA (see Appendix 1 for de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the views). Consequently, Orlikowski and Iacono call for research to include the ITA in the theorizing process “…to understand many of the IT critical implications … for individuals, groups, organizations, and society” (p. 133). But <sup>fi</sup>ve years later, Orlikowski & Iacono (2006) seem disappointed in the progress when they “point to what appears to be a blind spot in our <sup>fi</sup>eld, and that is that despite our common interest in information technology, much of our research takes it [the ITA] for granted or ignores it [the ITA] when conducting empirical investigations or building theoretical models” (p. 288). At regular intervals, an assessment of the state of IS research and ITA theorizing is conducted (for example, Ayanso et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2011; Akhlaghpour et al., 2009), and the results are consistent with Orlikowski and Iacono’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

One could, however, interpret the results obtained by Orlikowski & Iacono (2001) and the other studies as evidence of the challenges researchers experience when theorizing the ITA in their work. Not only is IS a diverse <sup>fi</sup>eld that provides many different methods for undertaking research and theorizing the ITA (Robey, 1996), but the ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nitions also vary substantially (see Table 1), adding another layer of complexity to potential theorizing.

Table 1 shows that the ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nitions capture a range of meanings: IT application (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003), design science (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Hevner et al., 2004), and a combination of hardware and software (Srinivasan et al., 2005). In fact, information technology (IT), as combined hardware and software, is mentioned in a number of de<sup>fi</sup>nitions as the foundation for the ITA. ITalso ful<sup>fi</sup>ls the requirement of material or physical existence (Gregor & Hevner, 2013) and IT is embedded within a social context connecting humans and institutions (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2006). Furthermore, the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions suggest that the ITA can be used to achieve a task (Goldkuhl, 2013). The goal orien tation on task achievement is important because it assumes a purposeful creation of the ITA, pointing to an arti<sup>fi</sup>cial and not natural phenomenon.

The design science understanding of the ITA is more inclusive than any other ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nition because it considers objects or processes that are not necessarily hardware-based or software-based to be ITAs. In the design science research tradition, a conceptual model, a software development method, or a vocabulary can each be considered an ITA (March & Smith, 1995). To moderate the difference between the design science understanding and the combined hardware and software understanding of the ITA, we introduce the notion of ITA-in-development and ITA-at-completion. Those artifacts that contribute to the development of hardware and software would be considered ITA-in-development. Indeed, March & Smith (1995) note tha “design science creates artifact” (p. 254). These ITAs relate to speci<sup>fi</sup>c design science artifacts, for example the constructs (vocabulary and symbols), models (abstractions and representations) and methods (algorithms and practices) (Hevner et al., 2004). Lee et al. (2015) capture this more inclusive view of design science by proposing the concept of an IS artifact.

For our study, we draw on Goldkuhl’s (2013) ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nition that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the understanding of the ITA-at-completion. We chose Goldkuhl’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition because it captures a core principle of the theoretical lens we draw on – systems thinking – by conceptualizing ITAs as integrated wholes. It also presents the ITA as a material object, comprised of hardware and software, and having a usage purpose. Based on other ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nitions (see Table 1), these are important elements of the ITA and Goldkuhl’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition acknowledges them. Finally, we believe that Goldkuhl’s argumentation represents a careful weighing of the different de<sup>fi</sup>nitions that exist in prior research to arrive at a harmonized ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nition that represents the most recent ITA thinking.

## The theoretical lens of systems thinking

We now turn to the literature on systems thinking as a theoretical basis for creating a framework that allows us to delineate and theorize the ITA. The decision to draw on systems thinking is motivated by various IS researchers, for example Saunders & Wu (2003) call for expanding the ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nition through systems concepts. In addition, a number of distinguished IS researchers have evoked systems theory as a foundational theory for IS research (Galliers, 2006; Hirschheim, 1983: Checkland, 1988), Indeed, Saunders & Wu (2003) see the “systems concept … [as] absolutely central to the IS discipline” (p. 563.) More importantly, systems thinking offers a theoretically grounded way to capture the different manifestations of systems in the world though the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a common set of characteristics. We take advantage of this when de<sup>fi</sup>ning the ITA characteristics.

## Overview of systems thinking

Systems thinking is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a speci<sup>fi</sup>c way of thinking to understand and resolve complex and uncertain real-world problems (Godfrey et al., 2010). Checkland (1981) adds that systems thinking “implies thinking about the world … by means of the concept ‘system’ ” (p. 3). Using systems thinking enables research to examine structures and behaviours of systems (Hirschheim, 1983). At the core of systems thinking is the understanding of a system as an organized whole that interacts with the environment for the purpose of exchange (e.g. energy, matter) (von Bertalanffy, 1972). Indeed, real-world systems exist in different forms and shapes with different levels of complexity interacting with the environment. Because of exchanges between the system and the environment, systems are conceptualized as open, able to reach a steady state, and “may even develop towards states of increased order and organization” (von Bertalanffy, 1956, p. 3). Through a set of common characteristics, researchers are able to capture, compare and understand systems (Midgley, 2003). These characteristics, however, are not a property of only a system part (Boulding, 1956).

Systems thinking originated as general systems theory (GST) from the natural sciences (von Bertalanffy, 1972). Although GST is one of, if not the best known, example of systems thinking theory, one needs to distinguish GST from systems thinking in that “GST is concerned with a general theory of systems” aiming to unify science whereas systems thinking “attempts to study the behavior and properties of actual systems” (Hirschheim, 1983, p. 818). Thus, systems thinkers understand the systems concepts as a conceptual apparatus, a body of knowledge (Barton et al., 2004), that includes a wide range of theories with different objectives and foci on systems, including general systems theory (von Bertalanffy, 1972; Boulding, 1956), cyber netics (Beer, 1972), soft systems methodology (Checkland, 1981), critical systems thinking (Ulrich, 1988; Jackson, 1982) and the viable systems approach (Barile & Polese, 2010). We use systems thinking in this paper because this theoretical lens has a more modest and realistic objective than that of GST. However, a number of systems thinking theories build on GST and thus, some concepts we utilize for creating the ITA framework relate closely to GST.

## Key concepts in systems thinking

Examining the structure and behaviour of systems enables us to derive key concepts in systems thinking. A comprehensive de<sup>fi</sup>nition conceptualizes a system as “a representation of an entity as a complex whole open to feedback from its environment” (Ryan, 2008, p. 28). It is im portant to note that understanding the system as complex still implies varying degrees of complexity, with some systems having higher complexity and other systems having lower complexity (Klir, 1991). In fact, a system that one would de<sup>fi</sup>ne as not complex would be an independent system where the entities have no relations among each other or with the environment (Ackoff, 1971). This means complexity relates to interdependence of the system elements and their interacting aspect and as a result, is focused on the relations among the system parts (von Bertalanffy, 1972; Anderson, 1999). In addition, complexity is closely related to connectivity of the system elements which, in concert with the interdependence and the interacting aspect, lead to the emergence of other systems characteristics including self organization, co-evolution, emergence, non-linearity equilibrium, chaos and path dependence (Mitleton-Kelly, 2003; Kauffman, 1993; Kauffman, 1995). The entity “is composed of at least two elements and a relation that holds between each of its elements” (Ackoff, 1971, p. 662). The aspect of representation acknowledges that a system “ab stracts away many details of the entity”, and as a result is based on simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed assumptions tha mean a system “stands in for an entity [however], … it is always constructed and used by an agent (or observer)” (Ryan, 2008, p. 27).

From a systems thinking perspective, the system’s elements are parts that are connected and arranged in an organized manner forming a whole (Angyal, 1969). The system cannot be understood by studying the parts in isolation because it is only in combination that the unique properties exist (M’Pherson, 1974). Consequently, the whole is more than the sum of the parts (von Bertalanffy, 1972). In the whole, every part is either directly or indirectly connected to every other part and thus, the parts are interdependent (Kramer & de Smit, 1977). This aspect manifests our <sup>fi</sup>rst systems thinking concept, that of wholeness of a system and organized system parts.

Second, systems have boundaries that separate them from the environment. A system boundary speci<sup>fi</sup>es what an observer considers part of the system and what is part of the sys tem’s environment (Kramer & de Smit, 1977). Because systems can transact with the environment, the system’s openness (i.e. open, closed, isolated) indicates the degree of system integration with the environment (Kramer & de Smit, 1977). Thus, a change in the environment can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the parts in the system and may lead to a change of the entire system (Ackoff, 1971). This aspect manifests our second systems thinking concept, that of system boundaries and the system’s environment.

Third, systems can be captured as “systems of systems” in which systems and system parts are connected in a hierarchical structure (Boulding, 1956). Depending on the level, every system exists in relation to a lower layered sub-system and a higher layered super-system. The hierarchical levels correspond with the complexity in such a way that lower layered systems or system parts are less complex than higher layered systems (Boulding, 1956). Complexity captures the scale of sub-systems and system parts at any given level measured “by the number of entities [parts] involved in the system (variables, states, components) and the variety of relationships among the entities” (Klir, 1991, p. 115). Organized in a sub-system and super-system hi erarchy, the wholeness of the system highlights the importance of the relations among the parts, i.e. relationship complexity (Simon, 1996). It is for that reason that the whole is more than the sum of the parts and blocking the relations results in closed systems or isolated parts (Ackoff, 1971). Thus, relations are fundamental to understanding complexity because they capture the interdependencies and the interacting aspect of the parts (von Bertalanffy, 1972). This aspect manifests our third systems thinking concept, that of hierarchical order and system complexity.

Fourth, to account for behaviours of systems, one needs to examine the state of a system. System state is expressed by the properties of the system at a certain point in time (Ackoff, 1971). Systems seek to maintain a balanced state (i.e. equilibrium) at any moment in time as the system performs best in a balanced state (Feibleman & Friend, 1945). Stimuli from the environment affect the system and may trigger a change in system state (Miller, 1965). Once a system has been moved out of the balanced state through the stimuli, it utilizes feedback to ei ther return to the original state or to move to a different – again balanced – state (Beer, 1972). This aspect manifests our fourth systems thinking concept, that of system state and change of state.

Finally, system behaviour is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced through feedback exchanged among the system’s parts and the environment. A system receives inputs in the form of information, energy and matter from a sub-system or the environment, transforms them into outputs and transfers the out puts to other parts of the system or the environment. The transformation is enabled through positive or negative feedback that is exchanged between the system and the environment (Miller, 1965). Feedback is used for self-regulation of systems resulting from interactions with the environment (von Bertalanffy, 1956). This aspect manifests our <sup>fi</sup>fth systems thinking con cept, that of material transformation and feedback.

## A SYSTEMS THINKING FRAMEWORK FOR IT ARTIFACTS

## Overview of the IT artifact characteristics framework

This section presents the framework of ITA characteristics that is based on systems thinking. It represents a theoretically derived typology (McKelvey, 1982). Different from theories or models, conceptual frameworks do not present testable predictions (Shapira, 2011). Indeed, frameworks are created when the research domain or the research phenomenon – in our case the ITA – is in an “initial stage of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c inquiry” (Shapira, 2011, p. 1314). Conceptual frameworks “help organize empirical observations, … make sense of the <sup>fi</sup>eld and [assist to] understand its boundaries, major <sup>fi</sup>ndings, and challenges” (Shapira, 2011, p. 1314). Extensive efforts have been undertaken in IS to create conceptual frameworks using classi<sup>fi</sup>cation concepts as a means to establish a structure and provide a conceptual order of ITAs (Nevo et al., 2010; Ein Dor & Segev, 1993; Gorry & Morton, 1971). Yet, these conceptual frameworks allow differentiation across ITAs (e.g. an ERP and a DSS) but fail to differentiate among the same ITA (e.g. ERP in company 1 and ERP in company 2). Furthermore, as the preceding discussion about ITA research in IS illustrated, much has been published about the need for and importance of the ITA but no scienti<sup>fi</sup>c tool, as far as we are aware, exists that would equip researchers to ei ther delineate or theorize including the ITA. Thus, the research phenomenon of ITA is still not well understood and the creation of a conceptual framework by means of a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation schema/typology can address this.

A classi<sup>fi</sup>cation schema can emerge as one of two forms: a deductive typology or an inductive taxonomy (McKelvey, 1982). The two can easily be confused and it is important to distinguish the “theoretically derived typologies from empirically derived taxonomies” (Fiss, 2011, p. 388). In a typology, the dimensions are axiomatic representations of a general nature and because of the theoretica grounding the dimensions are expected to specify the object (i.e. ITA). In contrast, a taxonomy is based on observations and thus, the schemas are derived by capturing existing objects. When creating a typology, an n-dimensional space is generated (Bailey, 1994) in which the dimensions represent the multiple characteristics of an ITA. Using the typology, ITAs can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed, grouped and differentiated along any combination of these characteristics. It should be noted that we do not propose, nor do we believe, that the current framework is a <sup>fi</sup>nal and exhaustive compilation. In addition, certain combinations of the characteristics may occur in practice more often while others may be mutually exclusive. Our typology, however, should be seen as a theoretically grounded starting point for IS researchers to delineate the ITA instances examined. The emergence of the ITA characteristics from the systems thinking concepts is summarized in Table 3. The systems thinking concepts do not, however, map one to one to the ITA characteristics, as we have combined concepts to arrive at characteristics and derived multiple characteristics from one concept, thus resulting in a sevendimensional framework.

## System parts, wholeness, system boundary and environment ➔ integration and connectivity

The systems concept of wholeness postulates that systems encompass interrelated parts (von Bertalanffy, 1956). The system parts can have varying degrees of connectivity within the sys tem. This connectivity is required to bring together the various sub-systems that, in combination, function as a system (Mehta, 2009). We refer to this characteristic as integration that expresses the level of aggregation of parts into a whole. This dimension can range from highly integrated to highly fragmented. Furthermore, the concept of system boundary suggests that systems parts can have relations to the environment (Kramer & de Smit, 1977). We refer to this characteristic as connectivity, which is the level of linkage between system elements and the environment, thus focusing on the system boundaries. This dimension can range from highly connected to highly isolated (see Table 3).

This concept postulates that systems are complex as expressed by the number of their rela tions among the system parts (Klir, 1991). By building a hierarchy of complexity among systems, lower layered systems are less complex than higher layered systems because the former have fewer relations among their parts. As such, complexity captures the fact that the parts mutually rely on each other for the functioning of the system (von Bertalanffy, 1972). We can utilize the description of a system (i.e. parts and relations) as a proxy for determining its complexity (Backlund, 2002) because humans perceive something as complex when it surpasses their cognitive abilities (Langefors, 1995). Hence, this characteristic captures the complexity of the ITA as experienced by users. The dimension of complexity ranges from highly complex to less complex (see Table 3).

## System state and history of system state ➔ state

The concept of system state postulates that systems strive toward a balanced state and thus, stimuli from other system parts or the environment may lead to a new state (Ackoff, 1971). To reach the balanced state, the system relies on state information. We refer to this characteristic as state because an ITA’s interactions with users or other components vary depending on the availability of information from previous states. Hence, the dimension for this characteristic ranges from stateless (where no state information is available) to stateful (where information is at hand) (see Table 3).

## Transformation and feedback-induced change of a state ➔ adaptation and self-adaptation

The concept of feedback and system state also impacts the system’s ability to change based on the feedback received from other system parts or the environment. In this transformation process, either the system’s structure or the system’s behaviour is altered to reach a balanced state (Ackoff, 1971). Adaptation has three aspects to it: First the general ability of the ITA to adapt, second, the ability of the ITA to self-control adaptation, and third, actions to adapt. A sys tem’s ability to change requires a general ability to change that we capture as the ITA’s ability for adaptation. Second, a system’s control over the change as an ITA may initiate change on its own based on feedback, which we capture as the ITA’s ability for self-adaptation. Finally, the actions that may be carried out to perform the transformational change process, which we de<sup>fi</sup>ne as con<sup>fi</sup>guration and customization. These actions are relevant for both adaptation and self-adaption. Adaptation ranges from static to dynamic and self-adaptation ranges from non-adaptive to adaptive. It is important to note that ITAs can only be adaptive when they have some dynamic capabilities. In turn, a static ITA cannot be adaptive; however, not every dynamic ITA is adaptive (see Table 3).

## Feedback ability ➔ synchronicity

In systems thinking, the concept of feedback refers to a system’s ability to receive inputs, pro cess them and provide outputs to other system parts and the environment (von Bertalanffy, 1956). The system uses different relations it has with other system parts and the environment for these exchanges. For an ITA, we refer to this characteristic as synchronicity to highlight the ITA’s ability to undertake exchanges of information, either simultaneously through an open channel or with a delay in time. The dimension ranges from synchronous to asynchronous (see Table 3).

## Detailed presentation of IT artifact characteristics

In the following, we present more details of the ITA characteristics’ seven dimensions. We enrich our presentation with examples and explanations of how the characteristics unfold for various ITAs.

## Integration

The degree of ITA integration, ranging from fully integrated to highly fragmented, has various organizational implications. Company-wide integrated ITAs have more organizational impact than fragmented solutions because the less integrate d ITAs only incorporate one depart ment or location (Noble, 1995). It is important because an integrated ITA can reduce the <sup>fl</sup>uctuations in capacity and inventory planning (Caggiano et al., 2006). Integrated ITAs include data from various sub-systems that were combined in an overarching system to form one functioning ITA. Examples of integrated ITAs (as commonly implemented) are client-server implementations and data-warehouse solutions (March & Hevner, 2007).

In contrast, fragmented ITAs are implemented in such a way that they are more independent of other system parts. These ITA instances are implemented as centralized standalone systems typical for accounting information systems in small and medium enterprises (Prasad & Green, 2015). Despite the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts an organization can gain from having a highly integrated ITA (e.g. access to various information/data resources), many organizations have experienced signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenges in implementing integrated systems (Robey et al., 2002). Thus, companies are becoming mindful of the risks and consequences of failed ITA implementation projects. As a result, companies may opt for a ‘<sup>fi</sup>t-for-purpose’ integration. For example, decision support systems can be implemented along the dimension of integrated to fragmented depending on their intended users – one primary user or many different users in an organization (Mora et al., 2002).

## Connectivity

The degree of ITA connectivity ranges from highly connected to highly isolated, considering the connections between the system’s parts and other systems in the environment. Con nected ITAs provide and require inputs from their external partners. Online-supported ITAs that utilize Internet platforms are highly integrated with external ITAs to enable information exchange between organizations and trading partners or customers. These connected ITAs are part of distributed networks that “harnesses the creative solutions of […] individuals through what amounts to an open call for proposals” in the case of crowdsourcing platforms (Brabahan, 2008, p. 76). Electronic marketplaces are another example for highly connected inter-organizational networks where organizations connect through intermediaries with buyers and sellers (Matook & Vessey, 2008; O’Reilly & Finnegan, 2010).

In contrast, isolated ITAs are implemented in such a way that they are highly disconnected from the environment. These ITAs do not possess any network connection (Rustad & Eisenschmidt, 1995) because they are either safety-critical ITAs or store data that is considered con<sup>fi</sup>dential and highly sensitive (e.g. national security data, patient medical history, in formation about child abuse or adoption cases (Air Force Health Study, 2015)). As a result, these isolated ITAs are shielded from any security attacks (Axelrod, 2011). Examples of this kind of system are ITAs operated by defence contractors, the pharmaceutical industry and certain government agencies (e.g. Queensland Department of Communities, Child Safety and Disability Services). Access to the information is, in most cases, only possible when physically in the building because the computers have no email or Internet connection, “are stored on a stand-alone hard drive” and/or can be “located in a room secured with a cyberlock” making these ITAs highly isolated from the environment (Air Force Health Study, 2015, chapter 5).

## Complexity

The degree of complexity captures the complexity of the ITA as a function of the number of system’s relations involved. Hence one ITA can be of high complexity while another can be of low complexity. Examples for different degrees of complexity are presented in the IS literature when highly complex and less complex ITAs are examined. A common example for a highly complex ITA is enterprise resource planning systems (Robey et al., 2002) because they are composed of various interdependent modules, which typically require substantia implementation and training efforts, that in turn may disrupt organizational business processes (Bharadwaj et al., 2007). In contrast, less complex ITAs have fewer relations because of having fewer parts which may be done to accommodate potential users with “only limited knowledge of computer usage” (Nobre et al., 1997, p. 35). For example, Nobre et al. (1997) examined a geographic information system used for public health surveillance. and noted that the ITA instance had only a few relations among the three system parts and thus, kept user training to a minimum. In addition, less complex ITAs also emerge through development approaches with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant user participation, as demonstrated by an inpatient psychiatric clinic IS (Tanner et al., 1994). A team of clinicians developed an ITA instance on their own without any assistance from the organization’s IT department. This ITA is coined as less complex because the focus was only on inpatient documents to “improve the chances for long-term acceptability” (Tanner et al., 1994, p. 621).

## Synchronicity

The time aspect of communication and message transfer between ITAs, namely synchronous and asynchronous, is a criterion that enables differentiation between ITAs (DeSanctis & Gallupe, 1987). With a synchronous system, both the sender and receiver have to be available at the same time, whereas in asynchronous communications the actors can access information on their individual schedules. Thus, asynchronous communication indicates independence between ITAs because no constant communication channel needs to be open. Email systems are examples of asynchronous communication, whereas instant messaging systems are examples of synchronous communication systems (Nah et al., 2002). Ngwenya & Keim (2003) investigated synchronous and asynchronous group support systems (GSS) and found that asynchronous GSS facilitate higher decision quality because the synchronous GSS created time pressure and lead to information overload. Dennis et al. (2008) also highlight that one single ITA instance can be appropriated in such a way that it can be used for both synchronous or asynchronous communication.

## Adaptation

Adaptation captures changes of the ITA that range from static to dynamic. Static ITAs undergo minimal changes. An example of a static ITA would be typical back-end applications in banks as they have minimal <sup>fl</sup>exibility, do not scale and cannot be customized. Conceptualizing internet websites as ITAs, differences in adaptation exist such that static websites are utilized to disseminate one-directional information to a broad user base (Meijer, 2001). Implementing static websites is preferred when information does not change often and the purpose is to educate the audience, as demonstrated by research on how to close the knowledge gaps in diabetes care (Nobel, 2006).

In contrast, dynamic ITAs are found in situations where frequent changes need to be sup ported. With advancements of e-commerce applications, the presented content changes dy namically to re<sup>fl</sup>ect product availability, such as with accommodation booking engines and airline websites (Cantoni et al., 2011). These ITAs are able to scale to handle more users with the same system performance. Through these dynamic abilities, customer service and performance can be enhanced.

## Self-adaptation

Self-adaptation captures the ITA’s capability to adapt on its own based on feedback (Chuang & Yadav, 1998). Thus, the level of self-adaptation indicates the extent to which the ITA is internally responsive to stimuli from other system parts or the environment. Being adaptive is particularly important in e-commerce to improve customer service. Advanced search engines, knowledge-based systems and learning systems can adapt to changes either by improving their internal parts or by creating new relations among the parts. The ITA’s learning ability allows the ITA to perform the same task more effectively in the future (Deng & Chaudhury, 1992). At an online web store, adaptation is based on information in the user’s pro<sup>fi</sup>le, but also through ongoing shopping actions (Ardissono et al., 2001). The feedback the ITA receives through browsing an adaptive website is used to infer users’ preferences and autonomously suggest products for which a high likelihood of purchasing is predicted (Menczer et al., 2002).

Furthermore, adaptive negotiation agents in online marketplaces learn to negotiate on behalf of consumers, which enhances the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the marketplace (Lau et al., 2008) and enhances the shopping experience of the consumer (Menczer et al., 2002). Comparing performance and satisfaction of non-adaptive and adaptive websites, Te’eni & Feldman (2001) demonstrated that users achieved better performance regarding search time and information accuracy with a self-adaptive ITA; no differences were found, however, regarding user satisfaction for different self-adaptation conditions.

## State

The state information relates to the system’s ability to maintain state. Whether or not an ITA has a record of prior connections is deemed to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in understanding how the ITA interacts with other system parts or the environment. Stateful ITAs remember previ ously performed events in a given interaction sequence because the speci<sup>fi</sup>c instance of an ITA has the capacity to store state information. E-commerce ITAs, when implemented as stateful systems, allow identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of customers when they return and are commonly implemented for online shopping carts (Liu et al., 2005). A stateful electronic marketplace keeps a record of various state information (e. g., order process) and there is no need to re-enter user information (e.g. address and bank details), resulting in more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient ITA use (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013). However, retaining the information to enable stateful connections could be associated with security issues and possible distrust on the part of the customer (Miyazaki & Fernandez, 2001).

In contrast, a stateless ITA does not keep track of any state information. As a result, information has to be requested for each interaction state (Fowler, 2003). For example, the majority of html pages (e.g. the websites of university faculty) are implemented stateless because no record is kept of general web-sur<sup>fi</sup>ng (Schulzrinne, 1996). Stateless systems are advantageous because they can respond without preceding state information that would require potentially larger storage capacity (Osrae et al., 2007).

## LITERATURE SURVEY

The literature survey seeks to understand the extent to which IS research publications in clude characteristics to describe the ITA they study and to demonstrate that variations of ITA characteristics have an impact on different outcome variables. To achieve these objectives, we chose to survey the IS literature and in particular, published articles in premier IS journals. The literature survey method is comparable to case survey research (Larsson, 1993), as a secondary analysis method where an analysis and synthesis of <sup>fi</sup>ndings in published case studies is conducted (Newig & Fritsch, 2009). The case survey method has recently been used by Rivard & Lapointe (2012) to analyse published cases in the context of user resistance to IT implementation. Although our focus is not only on qualitative case studies but rather ITAs in any published journal article, the method provides guidance for a sys tematic selection, coding and analysis of the material (Larsson, 1993).

## Selection of the articles on the IT artifact ES/ERP system

To conduct the analysis, we examined articles published from 2008 to 2012 in six major IS journals listed in the AIS Senior Scholars’ Basket of Journals (i.e. European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association of Information Systems, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly). We restrict our focus to a particular ITA – Enterprise Systems (ES)/Enterprise Resource Planning systems (ERP) – because to present all ITAs identi<sup>fi</sup>ed across the <sup>fi</sup>ve years in the six journals would take more space than available to us.

After the literature sources were determined, we carried out the selection process. First, we conducted a keyword search for ES/ERP articles through the AIS eLibrary and if the journal was not accessible there, we went to the journal’s home page. Our keywords included enterprise resource planning, ERP, enterprise system, enterprise software, ES and enterprise information system. After eliminating articles that appeared multiple times in the search results, we had a total of 63 articles. Next, we excluded all articles that were editorials, and for which we determined that the ES/ERP was not the ITA of concern, rather it was mentioned in passing or served to set the scene. After this process, we arrived at 28 articles (see Table 4 <sup>fi</sup>rst column).

## Coding of the articles on the IT artifact ERP system

We used the ITA characteristics framework (Table 4) to code the articles. In addition, we captured the ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nition and the impact the ITA characteristics have on various factors. The coding process was undertaken in iterations of coding and discussions between two coders.

Initially, each coder read each article independently and made notes on the printed article. Tex passages that related to the de<sup>fi</sup>nition, characteristics and outcomes of an ES/ERP were highlighted. It is important to note that the coder identi<sup>fi</sup>ed an ES/ERP characteristic only if it was clearly and ex plicitly mentioned in the article. Any text passages that would require making assumptions about the meaning of a characteristic were ignored.

To aid the coding and to enhance correctness and completeness, an additional keyword search for the characteristics was done using the automatic search function of Adobe ReaderX (10.1.4). The two coders interacted very closely throughout the entire coding process. After the initial coding of a number of papers was done, the coding was discussed, areas of agreement noted and issues of disagreement resolved. Similarly, as done by Rivard & Lapointe (2012), disagreement was resolved through a consensus approach and the coding of the next set of articles did not start before a consensus between the coders was reached. Disagreement was mostly related to differences in supporting text passages for an IT artifact characteristic. However, across the entire coding process few instances of disagreement were noted.

## De<sup>fi</sup>ning the IT artifact in light of systems thinking concepts

Almost all of the analysed articles provided a de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the ITA as presented in Table 5, only two exceptions were noted, i.e. Wang & Ramiller (2009) and Xue et al. (2011). De<sup>fi</sup>nitions are a foundational element of a theory as they enable a shared understanding, clarify the research objective under study, and help avoid confusion in meaning (Sutton & Staw, 1995; Weick, 1995). Because so many of the articles included a de<sup>fi</sup>nition, we infer that the authors saw the importance of clarifying the ITA instances studied.

A closer examination of the ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nitions in light of systems thinking illustrates that some of the systems thinking concepts are present. The wholeness and systems parts concept is re<sup>fl</sup>ected by de<sup>fi</sup>ning ES/ERP as packages (e.g. Benlian & Hess, 2011; Klaus & Blanton, 2010; Staehr, 2010), sets (Kauffman & Tsai, 2009) and suites of modules (Alvarez, 2008). These terms imply that an ES/ERP is composed of multiple elements that in combination aim to meet organizational needs (Strong & Volkoff, 2010), whereas the ITA-supported business processes represent the relations among the system parts. The concept of boundaries and environment can be found in the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions when referring to the ITAs as enterprise-wide (Morris & Venkatesh, 2010), across the entire organization (Berente et al., 2010), or across the enterprise (Davis et al., 2009). With these terms, the authors highlight that the ITAs connect different departments in an organization, but also external partners from the environment with the organi zation. The concept of hierarchical order can be inferred from the notion of composition because the articles state that ES/ERP are composed of tables that represent a sub-system (Alvarez, 2008) and from the description that the supra-system ‘hospital information systems includes a ‘radiology information system’ as a sub-system (Ayal & Seidman, 2009). The concept of systems state is only subtly included when referring to “processing of the core transactional data of the organization” (Staehr, 2010). In addition, it can be assumed that the ITA-supported business processes in the organization are the means to receive and provide feedback. Hence, the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions re<sup>fl</sup>ect the systems thinking concepts and as such, provide evidence to support the choice of this theoretical lens.

Based on the ES/ERP de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, a comprehensive understanding of the ITAs studied can be formed and functional features and system capabilities for ES/ERP elicited. However, the de<sup>fi</sup>- nitions are diverse, and it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess how similar the examined ES/ERPs are. Furthermore, the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions stress software as a key element of the ITA but pay less attention to the necessary hardware. Nevertheless, Goldkuhl’s (2013) ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nition still applies for ES/ERP because “without hardware, the software is just symbolic expressions” (p. 93). In fact, we would argue that the hardware is taken for granted by the authors and thus, not mentioned explicitly. It is further possible that the hardware is omitted because the ITA is not included in the theoriz ing process and hence, the hardware seemed less relevant. Yet, some authors (e.g. Berente et al., 2010) de<sup>fi</sup>ne the ES/ERP as “a unique form of information technology” and consequently, conform to our ITA understanding.

We will explore this issue further when examining the characteristics used to describe the ES/ERP.

## Details of IT artifact characteristics included

Analysing the articles also demonstrated characteristics from the ITA framework were used to describe the ES/ERP. As illustrated in Table 5, the most commonly included characteristics are integration (27 articles), complexity (16 articles) and adaptation (12 articles). The high usage frequency of the three characteristics suggests that they are the most relevant aspects for ES/ERPs. Appendix 2 provides excerpts from the articles to show how the articles used the three characteristics. Based on our detailed analysis and the keyword search, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any of the other ITA characteristics in the articles (i.e. connectivity, synchronicity, selfadaptation, state), suggesting that the characteristics are not perceived by the authors as relevant aspects of ES/ERPs. For example, connectivity was not found as a characteristic because ES/ERPs are ITAs that focus on the business processes within one organization, as explained in many of the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions in Table 4. Indeed, Chellappa & Saraf (2010) aptly write that “prior research in IS has largely … focused more on corporate implementation of enterprise systems” (p. 850). Similarly, ES/ERPs normally have the ability to be adapted, albeit not all articles mentioned it. However, organizations may not appreciate if the relevant ITA self-adapts because of the high levels of integration with other organizational ITAs and the complexity of the system.

Degree of integration. Most articles discuss the internal integration of the ES/ERP instances, either in general as being integrated into the existing application landscape (Benlian & Hess, 2011) or speci<sup>fi</sup>cally with certain ES/ERP modules (e.g. the manufacturing module is the core module and was integrated with sales, purchasing, inventory and <sup>fi</sup>nance modules (Liu et al., 2011)). Integration was also discussed referring to data such that once ITAs are integrated, they become more consistent, especially regarding use and working with <sup>fi</sup>nancial data (Berente & Yoo, 2012). We also found examples where the degree of integration is reported as being low, which means that not all organizational sub-systems are part of the ITA. For example, Häkkinen & Hilmola (2007) found that “not all locations within the corporation were using the new ERP system” (p. 86).

Degree of complexity. In general, ES/ERPs are ITAs consisting of many relations among their modules (e.g. parts). Hence, many articles mention that ES/ERPs are more complex than any other artifacts in the organization (Klaus & Blanton, 2010). Additionally, the complexity of a particular module (i.e. the <sup>fi</sup>nancial module) is determined as the highest or most complex module among all other ERP modules, viz., materials management, human resource management and campus management (Sasidharan et al., 2012). Despite that the ES/ERP instances are presented as complex, Liu et al. (2011) directly state that different ERP instances “in organizations vary in terms of complexity” (p. 188).

Degree of adaptation. This characteristic refers to the dynamic changes in a system’s state. Not many articles discuss how and to what extent ES/ERPs can and did change and thus, only a few examples are available to analyse this ITA characteristic. Nevertheless, the <sup>fl</sup>exibility to change an organization’s ES/ERP instance is seen as crucial because of the turbulent environment a company operates in and the frequent shifting of business requirements (Kauffman & Tsai, 2009). Overall, an ES/ERP can be changed through con<sup>fi</sup>guration and customization actions organizations perform to tailor their speci<sup>fi</sup>c ITA instances to align with organizational needs (Strong & Volkoff, 2010). To be able to change an ES/ERP is seen as a bene<sup>fi</sup>t because it provides companies with “the <sup>fl</sup>exibility to make discrete changes without disrupting the entire business process” as the CIO of Subaru is quoted by Kauffman & Tsai (2009). However, Newman & Zhao (2008) caution that too much customization may impair the day-to-day operations, especially if the customized system is not stable and breaks down.

In summary, three out of seven ITA characteristics were used to describe the ES/ERP. The analysis showed that the ES/ERP possessed characteristics in the entire range, which means the ITAs were presented as complex, but of varying degrees, integrated or fragmented, dynamic or static. Because of these variations in ITA characteristics, it is important that authors present them to inform readers what ITA is studied. Yet, the description of the characteristics was not prominent in the articles we surveyed and no comprehensive presentation of the ITA characteristics could be found. In fact, the characteristics we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed were only mentioned in passing or to explain a research outcome in the respective articles.

## Impact of IT artifact characteristics on users and the organization

The analysis of the articles also showed that the different degrees of an ITA characteristic can lead to different outcomes. This means an ITA characteristic impacts users and the organization. When a characteristic has an impact, a relationship between an ITA characteristic and another variable exist. This relationship may manifest as a theoretical connection where the ITA becomes part of the theorizing process. Table 5 provides examples for the three characteristics However, it should be noted that these theoretical connections (with the exception of Seddon et al., 2010) were not made explicit, but rather are an outcome of our data coding and analysis. Next, we discuss the impacts and theoretical connections authors ascribed to the ITA characteristics and examine the extent to which the characteristics are part of their theorizing (see Table 6).

Impact of the degree of integration. In the articles examined, we found support that the extent of integration has implications for an organization’s performance, pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and market responsiveness. For example, Wagner et al. (2010) suggest a link between the extent to which an ITA is integrated and system performance with this interview quote “It’s integrated – it’s slower, it’s a pain in the ass.” (p. 285). Further, Seddon et al. (2010) explain that higher degrees of integration result in larger organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Impact of the degree of complexity. In comparing the complexity between purchased ES/ERP and home-grown ITAs, it is suggested that purchased ES/ERP are more complex whereas home-grown ITAs are less complex because of the difference in the number of interdependent relations. Home-grown ITAs may only have functionalities the organization needs, whereas purchased ES/ERP may offer a much larger portfolio of features resulting in more relations. In addition, more complex ES/ERPs may cause users to experience confusion because of the large number of relations (Klaus & Blanton, 2010). Furthermore, Kauffman & Tsai (2009) note that “unnecessary IT complexity adds costs, reduces effectiveness, and stalls innovation” (p. 188). Thus, complexity may be a factor to be considered in a research study that examines effectiveness and innovation, especially as … “mangers underestimate the complexity of the ERP system” (Staehr, 2010, p. 215) when planning and budgeting a project.

Impact of the degree of adaptation. The changes in an ES/ERP state impact organizational outcomes and IT project parameters. State changes and an ability to adapt are enabled through ES/ERP con<sup>fi</sup>guration which has an impact on the implementation costs that can easily be in the millions of dollars (Morris & Venkatesh. 2010). The adaptation exten may also reveal interesting insights into user satisfaction and ITA resistance, particularly if the ES/ERP does not operate reliably. In extreme situations, the adaptation may cause an ES/ERP to be removed because “less than optimal hardware con<sup>fi</sup>guration [needs] to be replaced” (Staehr et al., 2012, p. 451).

## AN IT ARTIFACT CHARACTERISTICS FRAMEWORK FOR IS RESEARCH

This research has drawn on systems thinking to develop a conceptual framework of ITA characteristics that can be used to delineate ITAs and to include them in the theorizing process of IS research. With the framework, we seek to contribute knowledge to the IS discipline. Indeed, existing classi<sup>fi</sup>cation schemas do not support researchers in differentiating between the same type of artifact (e.g.

ERP in company 1 and ERP in company 2), but rather allow for differentiating between different ar tifacts, e.g. ERP and DSS. As Shapira (2011) states, conceptual frameworks are most valuable when the domain or phenomenon of interest is not well understood, mature or <sup>fi</sup>lled with confusion. In this section, we present examples of how to use the framework for these two purposes.

## Delineating the IT artifact using the framework

In the literature survey, we applied the framework ex-post to published articles. In doing so, we showed that ITA characteristics are utilized in presenting details of ES/ERP. These descriptions were, however, neither prominent nor comprehensive, but rather scattered throughout the article. This means that readers would be asked to collect the different information pieces to develop a comprehensive understanding of the ITA under investigation. Consequently, an example would be helpfu that illustrates how to apply the framework in order to delineate ITAs in a more accessible manner.

We leverage SAP’s customer success stories, available online on the vendor’s website. (SAP, 2014). These stories describe the reasons for implementing an ERP, details on the solution, challenges mastered and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts achieved. We chose this approach because a success story presents an existing ITA instance and the vendor and client, knowledgeable about the ITA, provide a description of it. This approach has been used by Seddon et al. (2010) to provide examples for judgments regarding organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using ES (see their appendices B and C). SAP provides hundreds of customer success stories and we randomly selected one – PlayCorp Group of Companies (SAP, 2014). This company implemented mySAP All-in-One (SAP Business Warehouse), speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the SAP apparel and fashion solution from Supply Chain Consulting. In the customer success story, PlayCorp and SAP describe how the ERP was implemented to master the company’s growth, improve ef-<sup>fi</sup>ciency and manage the daily operations with suppliers and customers. We have used their descriptions to determine the ITA characteristics and present text excerpts in Table 7.

Based on the information we extracted from the customer success story, one could formulate a comprehensive ITA description of this ERP that could read like this:

We examine an ERP that consists of multiple components including a data warehouse reporting tool and personal digital assistants (PDAs) that exchange information in a synchro nous manner. The ITA is fully integrated and manages the daily operations of the company. The ERP is not customized because the company assumes it will make updates and maintenance easier. Integration of the system reduced the complexity of the ITA by reducing the number of relations.

This brief ITA description could be included in an article’s methodology section as it presents the application domain in which a theory is developed or tested.

## Theorizing the IT artifact using the framework

Providing an explicit description of the ITA is only the <sup>fi</sup>rst step in giving the ITA more prominence in IS research. A subsequent step is leveraging the ITA framework as a resource for researchers who wish to incorporate the ITA into their theorizing. By including characteristics of the ITA in the theorizing, researchers can build more upon each other’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings as it increases the transparency of the ITAs examined.

This study <sup>fi</sup>nds that in the examined ES/ERP articles, this theorizing process is not completely obvious although multiple examples for ITA-driven theorizing were found. The most obvious example is Seddon et al. (2010) who formulated a hypothesis on the relationship between ITA integration and organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. In most cases, the theorizing takes place because the authors explain, justify or elaborate their <sup>fi</sup>ndings in regards to a particular ITA. Consequently, we suggest including the ITA in the a-priori theorizing process by leveraging the ITA characteristics as variables. In this way, research can move away from the black box view and explicate the unique characteristics of the underlying technology. We even believe that the framework could enable contributions of IS research to reference <sup>fi</sup>elds such as management, marketing or economics.

To demonstrate how the framework could be applied, we use Wixom & Watson (2001) data warehouse success model We chose this model because an inspection of the outcome variables in Table 6 showed that ES/ERP implementation time and implementation costs were affected by two ITA characteristics, namely complexity and adaptation. Consequently, we looked for a research model that included time and cost as variables and Wixom and

Watson’s model offers this within the ‘project implementation success’ construct which includes adherence to the project schedule (i.e. on-time), the project budget (i.e. on-cost), and the technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations (i.e. on-specs).

As per Figure 2, project implementation success depends on resources, user participation and team skills. However, these factors do not represent data warehouse characteristics. Following our line of thinking, one could suggest a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed research model that includes the characteristics of complexity and adaptation as representing the data warehouse and the other variables as people-related factors (i.e. user participation and team skills) and resource-related factors. Whether or not ITA characteristics have direct effects (as proposed in Figure 2), or may even moderate the relationship between people/resources-related fac tors and implementation success (as proposed in Figure 3), would need to be developed more fully. However, our modi<sup>fi</sup>ed research model represents a sociotechnical worldview because the technology side and the social side are present (Goldkuhl, 2013). Furthermore, by theorizing the ITA as suggested, researchers might be able to compare results across ITAs (e.g. complex ITAs) and establish patterns based on the ITA characteristics; rather than focusing on a particular ITA. The extent to which this inclusion changes the results of a study is an empirical question. What it would provide is enhanced clarity regarding whether or not the particular ITA matters to the study.

## Summary: recommendations, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risks of using and not using the ITA framework

Use of the framework for delineating the IT artifact

This research developed a systems thinking based framework of ITA characteristics that can be used to delineate the instances of an ITA under investigation. Just as researchers commonly provide the details of their theories, subjects, data collection approach and analytical techniques, with this framework, details of an ITA can be presented. We have provided an example in section 4.1 of how researchers can use the framework for presenting a detailed de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the ITA they study. Details can also be presented as part of the presentation of a paper’s empirical sample demographics that are normally included in a paper’s results section. Including the characteristics allows other researchers to decide if they examined the same type of ITA or an ITA with the same label but instantiated very differently. The details also provide greater explanatory power regarding different <sup>fi</sup>ndings about an ITA when the same dependent variables within the same contexts are examined (e.g. use, software quality, performance). In addition, the use of the ITA framework may allow for more nuanced <sup>fi</sup>ndings to emerge. As a result, IS research can move away from the black box approach and expli cate the underlying technological structures.

## Use of the framework for theorizing the IT artifact

The systems thinking based framework of ITA characteristics can also be integrated into the theorizing processes of a research study. We have suggested in section 4.2 two ways that can be done, either as independent variables or as moderating variables. Including ITA characteristics in a research model answers the call for “developing conceptualizations and theories of IT artifacts; and incorporating such conceptualizations and theories of IT artifacts expressly into our studies” (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001, p. 130). With such an application of the ITA framework, IS researchers can create theories that truly belong to the IS discipline. The proposed ITA characteristics framework may be another step in enabling the creation of a cumulative tradition for IS research, something that was recommended at the very <sup>fi</sup>rst International Conference on Information Systems (Keen, 1980). By including certain charac teristics in the theorizing, we improve our understanding of technology-led transformations in organization, but also in the larger society (Agarwal & Lucas, 2005).

IS research has its root in systems thinking but also in other theories such as information theory, theory of semiotics, structuration theory, actor-network theory and change theory (Avison & Elliot, 2006). These theories may offer similarly fruitful approaches for delineating the ITA and allowing researchers to create mechanisms to include the ITA in their theorizing.

With these different theories, researchers may focus on the relationship between the ITA and its actors (e.g. users and organizations) and the issue of hierarchical nesting of ITAs (e.g. Internet –personal information management software (Microsoft Outlook) – Email and Calendar).

## Recommendations on how not to use the framework

This framework enables us to present descriptive details about an ITA and to create and extend theories where technology matters by incorporating ITA characteristics as variables in a research model. It is not our intention that the framework becomes a hammer, such that editors and reviewers use the lack of ITA characteristics as a tool for rejecting manuscripts. It is quite possible that authors may not have or be able to share clear details regarding their ITA in certain circumstances (e.g. proprietary systems). Thus, although we believe strongly that discussing the ITA characteristics can be valuable for theorizing, we also recognize that there are times when it is simply not possible to include them. We encourage editors and reviewers to ask for them, but to be reasonable in their expectations and willing to accept when authors cannot provide some or all of them. We would rather have no ITA characteristics mentioned than have them inaccurately reported out of mandate.

## Limitations of the research

There are two primary limitations of our study that need to be acknowledged. First, it could be argued that the ITA framework is not comprehensive. Our framework development has drawn on and is thus closely aligned with key concepts of systems theory. The collection of individual theories subsumed under this umbrella allowed for capturing ITA in different situations and performing different behaviours. We felt this choice was appropriate as IS researchers have suggested using systems theory for ITA research. Further, by drawing on theory from systems thinking, certain assumptions have been made. We implied that the characteristics are valid for all types of systems and thus, also applicable for any representation of an existing ITA. We also assumed that these characteristics allow for differentiation among ITAs of the same type because each implementation of an ITA possesses a unique combination of these characteristics.

The second limitation is the selection of ES/ERPs as the focal ITA. We used ES/ERPs because there is substantial research published about these particularly ITAs. We do not believe that this choice impacted our conclusions and we would expect to <sup>fi</sup>nd similar results for other ITAs. Prior research has shown that between 22 percent and 33 percent of articles published in MIS Quarterly and Information Systems Research do not include an ITA or related issues like IT capabilities, IT practice, IT usage or IT impact (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). In contrast, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that researchers are aware of the need to define and theorize the ITA under examination and the relevance of ITA characteristics for the study context. We believe the lack of an apparatus to do so may be the reason for the variations in clarifving ITA characteristics across the articles

## Future research

The current study offers a number of directions for future research. First, we hope this paper will provide a foundation for work that theorizes the ITA and seeks to reconcile the varying notions of what constitutes an ITA. It might be impossible to <sup>fi</sup>nd an ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nition that is equally valid and useful for all IS research traditions. Perhaps a viable <sup>fi</sup>rst step would be to identify different IS research traditions, their understanding of the ITA, the boundaries of each ITA, and areas of overlap and similarity in ITA understanding. Our ITA de<sup>fi</sup>nition based on Goldkuhl (2013) focuses narrowly on the manifestation of hardware and software as an integrated whole. However, the ITA understanding of the design science research tradition in cludes artifacts that do not <sup>fi</sup>t Goldkuhl’s (2013) de<sup>fi</sup>nition, for example algorithms or conceptual models. We considered these artifacts as an ITA-in-development because they serve as foundational elements in the processes of creating hardware- and software-based ITAs.

Second, ITA theorizing might also continue by utilizing a different theoretical base. In this study, we have built on systems thinking to develop the framework, however, different theories may broaden our understanding of the ITA. Other possible perspectives that may allow for theorizing about the ITA are adaptive structuration theory (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994) and sociomateriality (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014) as they acknowledge the complex and intertwined nature of socio and material.

Third, we encourage IS researchers to demonstrate an impact of one or more characteristics on organizational outcomes. Hence, one could design a study that compares the results when one research model is tested repeatedly on an ITA (e.g. ERP or DSS) that varies in ITA characteristics (loosely vs. highly integrated). Any differences in the dependent variable may be interpreted as resulting, at least in part, from the variations of the ITA characteristics.

## CONCLUSION

In this study, we developed a seven dimensional framework that includes characteristics for ITAs. We offer this framework as a language for researchers to present and theorize the ITAs they study. Our ITA framework builds on the theoretical work in systems thinking and utilizes this lens for deriving ITAs characteristics. We examined characteristics of Enterprise Systems/Enterprise Resource Planning systems (ES/ERP) presented in published research in six IS journals during the years 2008 to 2012. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate how three characteristics (i.e. complexity, integration and adaptation) were used to delineate ES/ERP instances. Further, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate indirect ITA theorizing by illustrating a link between ES/ERP characteristics and outcomes. We conclude the study with two examples that show how to utilize the framework for delineating and theorizing ITAs.
