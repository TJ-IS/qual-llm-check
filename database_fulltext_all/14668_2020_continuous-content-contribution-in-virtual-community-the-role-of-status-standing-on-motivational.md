## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Virtual community Status-standing Motivational mechanism Self-interest motivation Prosocial motivation

## A B S T R A C T

A central concern for the long-term vibrancy of virtual communities is how to efectively motivate continuou contributions from community members. To address this challenge, a series of motivation factors that range from self-interest driven to prosocial-oriented motives have been identified by scholars. As members participate in continuous contributions and community activities alike, their status or positions within the virtual communities would change, which may influence their cognition and behavior. While status-seeking can lead to greater contributions and a better performance, research has yet to study in a comprehensive manner how an individual's status-standing shape one's recognition and behavior in a systematic way. To fill this research gap, a comprehensive theoretical model based on the status theory of collective action is proposed to explain how status standing moderates the impact of diferent types of motivations (self-interest vs. prosocial) on continuous contributions in virtual communities. Our findings suggest that improved status-standing can enhance the motivational incentives of virtual rewards and peer recognition, while boosting the prosocial motivation of opinion leadership to contribute content in virtual communities. Overall, this study not only suggests a comprehensive view of our current understandings regarding contribution behavior in virtual communities, but also yields deep insights into how motivational mechanism of virtual communities can be reasonably designed and properly used to promote continuous contributions.

## 1. Introduction

A central concern to sustain the long-term vibrancy of virtual communities (VCs) is to motivate continuous voluntary contributions from community members [11,26]. This challenging issue has prompted information systems (IS) and marketing scholars to devote considerable eforts to studying the motivational antecedents that encourage content contribution [11,40]. Scholars have concluded that the contribution behavior of VC members can be driven by diferent sets of motivational factors that range from self-interest driven to prosocialoriented motives [12,47]. As members participate in continuous contributions and community activities alike, their status or positions within the VCs can change. Indeed, most VCs have employed diferent forms of status hierarchy systems; these systems typically distinguish members by awarding them with improved status-standings (e.g., beginners, experts, etc.) for their community participation and contribu tions.

Sociologists have long documented that people's status-standing influences their cognition and behavior [13,37]. In VC literature, however, status-standing has been predominantly treated as a desirable goal that members seek to improve [16]. From this perspective, statusseeking can lead to greater contributions [27] and better performance within communities or groups [28]. What has been largely understudied in VC research is how an individual's status-standing can shape one's recognition and behavior in systematic ways. We propose that, as members earn improved status-standing, their motivations to contribute (e.g., self-interest or prosocial driven) may shift. which influences subsequent contribution behavior. Thus, the present study investigates how status-standing moderates the impact of various motivational factors on the continuous contributions in VCs. To do so, we outline a theoretical model based on the status theory of collective action (STOCA) - a sociological perspective that explains the collective contribution behavior of an individual, where the motivation to contribute is based on the contributor's status-standing [13,43]. Our model was tested against large-scale data collected from Dianping, a leading VC based in China.

We first review prior work on motivational factors for VC content contributions and discuss the status theory of collective actions, upon which we develop our hypotheses and put forward an integrative model. We then report on the study context, methodology, analytical strategy, and empirical results. Finally, we discuss the findings and implications of our study, present research limitations, and suggest potential directions for future work.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Virtual community status and continuous contribution

Researchers have suggested that VCs have a social structure that lead to the emergence of status hierarchy [17,30]. As VC members participate in continuous contributions, repeated interactions, and community activities, they may acquire measures of distinction over time. Specifically, VC members are awarded improved status for con tributing to the communities, often recognized by virtual badges, titles, or other ranking indicators [16]. For example, Yahoo! Kimo Knowledge + employs a status hierarchy of five standings – beginner, postgraduate, expert, master, and knowledgist. These titles are awarded to members according to the number of questions they answer and the corresponding acceptance rate of their answers [32]. A similar hierarchy has been widely employed by various VC platforms. Extant research has examined the processes of how a status hierarchy comes into being in VCs and the evolution of the hierarchy therein [16,30]. Status in this study refers to “an individual's relative standing in a group based on prestige, honor, and deference” [44].

Social scientists have long demonstrated that, just as an individual's position, status, and role can change, so do his/her cognition and values [13,37]. In group settings, when people receive diferent status-standings, their personal preferences and group behavior change [13,43]. The literature implies that achieving a certain status-standing may influence a member's motivation to contribute. However, past research on VC content contributions has mainly considered status-standing as a desirable goal to achieve, e.g., VC members seek to improve their status within the community; very few have examined the dynamic where members' values and motives shift along with their change in status [16]. Accordingly, we investigate how member status might moderate the influences of diferent types of motivational factors on contribution behavior. In order to better understand the impact of a participant's status, we draw on the status theory of collective action.

## 2.2. Status theory of collective action

Status theory of collective action (henceforth STOCA) is a socio logical perspective that explains contributions in collective action situations. Collective action situations are defined as situations where groups desire the production of public goods that would benefit all members, and that the public good requires contributions from individuals to be produced [43]. Particularly, STOCA highlights the role of an individual's status-standing in contributing public goods. STOCA has been used to explain community contributions in several prior studies. For instance, Simpson et al. [37] have showed that introducing status diferences in groups can efectively facilitate the organization of more successful collective actions and can help resolve the free-riding problem. Kilduf et al. [28] have found that disagreement about statusstandings within a group can negatively afect group performance. Moreover, Ridgeway and Nakagawa [35] have designed an incentive system that aims to encourage lower-status members to respect higher status members and participate in collection actions in the groups.

In a VC context, a higher status commands more benefits for in dividuals, such as more respect by other community members, better access to scarce resources and specific privileges, and so on [28].

Consequently, the pursuit and maintenance of status can drive certain behaviors such as content contribution in VCs [32]. The central thesis of STOCA is that “the greater the status an individual receives for past contributions, the more his/her group motivation will increase and the more the individual will tend to view the group positively” [44]. The group motivation is defined as “the extent to which the individual values the group's interests relative to their own” [43]. Compared to lower-status contributors, higher-status contributors value the group interests more, relative to their own [44]. Therefore, an individual with high statusstanding would possess greater group-based, prosocial motivation, whereas an individual with low status-standing would pay more attention to self-interest-driven motivations. This claim is supported by empirical evidence showing that individuals who are at a higher status appear to be more collectively minded, while lower-status individuals seem to be more self-interested [13,44]. According to STOCA, the im proved status can shift one's focus from the micro-individual level (e.g., self-interest) to the macro-group level (e.g., group productivity, cohesion, and solidarity [44]) factors in decision-making. Accordingly, a VC member who contributes content to the community achieves advanced in status-standing. The improved status may increase one's group motivation to assist the collective goods relative to one's own goods.

STOCA can serve as an appropriate theoretical explanation for us to investigate the interplay between members' status-standing and their continuous contribution behavior. First, contributing content to VCs can be viewed as a collective action situation. Much past research has suggested that the content contributed by VC members is essentially public good that is consumed by and can benefit all members [12,16]. The public goods in VC rely on members' voluntary contribution to produce. Second, VC members earn improved status-standing in the community based on their past contribution behavior. Status hierarchies can be found in almost all VC platforms. A member's status is typically indicated using symbols or titles that appear along members usernames and in their profiles [13]. Members often share an understanding of the diferentiated levels of the status hierarchy within a given community. Third, the STOCA aims to apply to collective action situations where group members have mixed motives [44]. In such situations, members have common interests in the production of public goods but are also tempted to free-ride. Thus, various types of motives, such as the self-interest-driven or prosocial-oriented motives, coexist, which reflects well the diferent motivations of VC members to con tribute content.

Based on the central thesis of the theory, we revert to the focal context to better understand how diferent types of motivation play a role in encouraging continuous content contributions. In particular, according to the theory, we distinguish motives as self-interest-driven and prosocial-oriented.

## 2.3. Motivations for virtual community contribution

A growing body of literature has identified the factors that influence contribution in VCs, as shown in Appendix A, the factors associated with contribution behavior can generally be classified into self-interestdriven and prosocial-oriented. Early researchers have extensively studied the self-interest motivational factors that lead individuals to contribute content [41]. Self-interest motivations are ones that serve an individual's own interests, and can be economically-driven or psychologically-based [14]. Within economics, discussions of self-interest are generally related to tangible benefits. A ‘self-interested’ person is someone who cares about maximizing profits. Prior research has suggested that VCs do not provide any immediate financial reward to contributors, but provide virtual rewards to their contributors [12,31,32]. Virtual rewards refer to tangible incentives, such as digital vouchers, virtual gifts, virtual currencies, or points [10,21,32], which have utility benefits to individuals. What kind of virtual rewards ofered depends largely on the nature of the community or community policy [46]. According to the economic theory that posits that rational individuals would generally be driven by utility, it could be conjectured that virtual rewards are an important driver to stimulate people's con tribution behaviors [9,31]. Self-interest motivation is not restricted to the pursuit of tangible benefit, but can take other forms including the pursuit of intangible benefit, such as the innate psychological needs [15]. According to Rocha and Ghoshal [36], people pursue the positive feeling of doing good and thus are psychologically dependent on others' welfare, such as desire to be praised. From this perspective, contributors engage in VC contribution for the purpose of image enhancement, such as desire to be seen positively by others. Peer recognition is considered as an intrinsic utility to fulfill an individual's innate psychological needs of gaining attention and boosting self-worth, which, conceptually, is typically diferent from virtual rewards [12,26]. This means that people's satisfaction is the key driver. Relating to the focal context, when an individual contributes content and receives positive feedback or approval (e.g., “likes”) from other VC members, it is deeply important to the contributor. This is also empirically sup ported by previous studies such as Jabr et al. [24] and Jin et al. [26].

A growing consensus indicates that contributing content to VCs can be viewed as a collective action situation. In other words, contributed content in VCs is essentially a public good that can benefit all VC members [12]. Such a public good relies on group motivation, such as prosocial motivation [43]. Prosocial motivation is a sociologicallydriven motive that reflects a person's willingness to help others and support a group [13]. In some studies, researchers also refer it as “the desire to expend efort in order to benefit others” [18]. In the field of social psychology, research shows that moral duty and social responsibility are two key aspects that linked to prosocial motivation, which afect one's tendency to perform prosocial behavior [48]. According to social learning theory [4], if someone wants to know why people act pro-socially, he/she should consider their learning history, namely considering observational learning or modeling that comes from watching the actions of others. In VCs, research has suggested that social learning may activate the sense of obligation and duty, which further leads to the awareness of generalized reciprocity [26]. Individuals who accumulate a growing stock of abilities by learning also lead to the easy access of their own knowledge and enhance their productivity [26]. As Brown and Duguid [8] point out, the central issue in learning is becoming a practitioner not learning about practice. Accordingly, VC members who are motivated by the social learning tend to contribute content in order to help others [41].

Social responsibility is the component of the self-concept related to help and contribution; when it is activated, people experience their identities as oriented toward positively influencing others, and they are more likely to voluntarily contribute [19]. With regards to VC content contribution, some members who undertake social responsibility and commit to give advice and information with others, are typically function as subject matter experts or opinion leaders in the community [29]. Research has shown that individuals who assume opinion leadership are more likely to construe themselves as part of the community and link community value to self-value [6]. They are more willing to act collectively and even shoulder the responsibility to collaborate with and organize other members. Thus, VC members who are motivated by the role of opinion leadership tend to contribute content to create common values for the community and maintain group cohesion [6]. In sum, our literature review reveals two primary types of motivational factors that underlie content contribution in VCs, as summarized in Table 1.

Although prior studies have identified a series of motivational fac tors, from self-interest driven to prosocial motivations, that affect contribution behaviors, the role of status has been largely ignored. According to STOCA, the improved status-standing might reinforce a member's pro-social or group-based motivations, so that he/she care more about the community and is more willing to take responsibility to ensure its success. However, the theory has not been tested meticulously to determine how status influences motivations that range from the self-interest to the prosocial [13,44]. Thus, such social and hierarchical individual diferences have been largely understudied by IS scholars, but could be useful for understanding collective contribution behavior in virtual groups and communities [43]. The following will theorize how the moderation efects happen, based on the theory and prior literature.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

In this section, we describe how we make use of STOCA and past VC research on contribution motivations to develop a research model for our study (Fig. 1), which will help illustrate how the influence of different motivations on continuous contributions is contingent on statusstanding. Specifically, we propose that improved status will moderate subsequent contributions. In other words, as an individual is rewarded with improved status, he/she values the group more; this, in turn, leads to greater subsequent contributions driven by group motivations. Our hypotheses thus specify an important moderator – status-standing – as guided by STOCA, which has been largely understudied in the IS literature.

Once members earn status rewards for their contribution, the individual's group-based motivations are reinforced [43]. The group motivations would be weighted by high-status members as counteracting external incentives for contribution. In addition, individuals that rank high in status often view the interests of a group as their own selfinterests [43]. As such, members with higher status are likely to contribute to VCs without much self-benefits. Conversely, low-status members are often driven by a task-specific goal orientation and are, thereby, they are less likely to align their self-interests with those of the community [23]. In this case, they may be more concerned about whether their contribution can satisfy their self-interest rather than benefit the community, and thus they tend to be driven by virtual rewards to perform an action.

H1. Status standing negatively moderates the influence of virtual rewards on continuous contributions. (i.e., the impact of virtual rewards on continuous contributions is stronger for low-status than for high-status members).

Members with lower status-standing tend to pay more attention to their own goods relative to the collective goods when making decisions to contribute public goods. Lower status members are usually more eager to prove themselves as valuable members by gaining the recognition of others [42]. Thus, their contributions are more likely to be driven by peer recognition that satisfies their own psychological needs. Conversely, as an individual's status-standing increases, his/her motivation to contribute to the collective good increases relative to his/her own good. Higher status members thus possess greater prosocial and group motivation, and appear to be more collectively minded [13,43]. Therefore, compared to their lower status counterparts, higher status members' contribution decisions are less likely to be motivated by peer recognition. Taken together, we predict that status-standing will weaken the incentive efect of peer recognition.

H2. Status standing negatively moderates the influence of peer recognition on continuous contributions (i.e., the impact of peer recognition on continuous contributions is stronger for low-status members than for their high-status counterparts).

While prior research has suggested that social learning motivates continuous contributions [26,41], we posit that the strength of this motivational impact depends on status-standing. Specifically, members with a higher status have a stronger group identity, and show a greater sense of obligation and duty [7,22]. Being collective-minded, higher status members tend to make future returns from the content they have learned. Thus, when higher-status members obtain others' content, as contributed through social learning, they are also more likely to comply with a generalized reciprocity to respond due to their strong sense of obligation. Conversely, lower-status members show less communitybased considerations when making decisions [44], and thus are less likely to be driven by generalized reciprocity. As such, when they receive content in VCs, they may not feel as obligated to return the favor. Therefore, we predict that status-standing will strengthen the impact of social learning in motivating continuous contributions.

H3. Status standing positively moderates the influence of social learning on continuous contributions (i.e., the impact of reciprocity on continuous contributions is stronger for high-status members than for their low-status counterparts).

As mentioned, higher-status members possess more prosocial group motivation in contributing to collective actions [44]. We posit that status-standing can strengthen the impact of prosocial motivation on contributions. Specifically, as a member's status advances in the community, he/she increasingly cares about the group and, therefore, becomes more willing to serve as an opinion leader [23]. Thus, the member's higher status would lead him/her to play the role as an opinion leader more actively, which leads him/her to make more content contributions to the VC. Relatively speaking, a low-status member may prefer to listen to the opinions of others and is possibly less likely to care about guiding others.

H4. Status standing positively moderates the influence of opinion leadership on continuous contributions (i.e., the impact of opinion leadership on continuous contributions is stronger for high-status members than for their low-status counterparts).

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Research context and data collection

Dianping (dianping.com; a Chinese equivalent of Yelp) was selected as the research context for this study. Dianping is a leading VC in China, in which members share comments and reviews on restaurants and entertainment businesses. Founded in April 2003, Dianping currently has > 250 million active members, and > 5.8 million active local merchants across 300 cities in China. We chose Dianping as the context to test our hypotheses because: (1) it is one of the earliest word-of mouth communities that has already been cultivated as a mature VC; (2) since our unit of analysis is review contributors, Dianping has an ideally large base of active members that enable the selection of contributors with diversified characteristics, such as status-standing; and (3) the website has the typical VC components, including members' profile pages, member-generated reviews, and representative social networking functionalities.

A Dianping member's profile page (Fig. 2) contains basic personal information, such as gender, location, education, hobbies, and registration time, etc. Members can contribute reviews about businesses, which are visible to everyone. On a member's profile page, the past reviews contributed by the member are listed. The contributed reviews may receive “likes” from other members; the total number of “likes” received is also displayed on the page. In addition, members can follow other members to form social networks, and they may also be followed by others. The follower-followee relationship is not necessarily mutual. That is, a member who has hundreds of followers may follow only a few people (followees) or vice versa. Once a member contributes new comments, the comments are automatically pushed to his/her followers through the feed flow [20,26].

Dianping employs a status hierarchy of eight standing levels. Members earn their status standings based on their activities in Dianping, including managing profiles, adding and editing businesses information, recommending businesses, uploading photos, and content contribution. Once a member is awarded a certain status, a corresponding title (e.g., five star) and its badge icon (e.g., five yellow badges with ribbons) are displayed along with the member's ID.

In line with prior studies, such as Burtch et al. [9] and Ba and Wang [2], we randomly selected 4000 Dianping members. Due to the setting of some members' privacy protection, we cannot crawl their specific data. Except them, 3869 members were selected. We tracked and collected each member's activities from December 1, 2017 to March 1, 2018 (e.g., 90 days) using a Python Scrapy crawler. Because our study focuses on the behavior of continuous content contribution, we restricted our analysis to only active members who had participated continuously to contribute content. To capture this sample, we followed prior research and set a 30-day time unit [11]. The whole timeline was divided as three stages, namely, “Dec.1st, 2017 to Jan. 1st, 2018” as T1, “Jan.1st, 2018 to Feb.1st, 2018” as T2, and “Feb.1st, 2018 to Mar.1st, 2018” as T3. In our specific context, a 30-day period is a moderate and preferred time unit to study review contribution behavior because, if the time unit is too short, it might not capture the activities of some active yet infrequent review contributors [16,24]. Likewise, if the time unit is too long, it might not capture important information about the change of members' behavior [11]. We selected T1 and T2 in our analysis in order to avoid the festival influence. Specifically, Chinese Spring Festival falls on February 28, 2018 (a seven-day holiday), which may afect the accuracy of the results. Accordingly, in the main analysis section, we kept the T1 and T2 only. Furthermore, to test the robustness of the findings, we further used T2 and T3 for another round of analysis in the robustness check section. We observed each member's activities in each period. Consistent with prior research [20,26], active contributors are defined here as those who wrote at least one review in at least two periods. In our final sample, 2524 members who met this criterion were included in the analysis.

## 4.2. Measures

Given the possible associations among our study variables, we measured the dependent variable (henceforth DV) and independent variables (henceforth IVs) in two consecutive time periods to reduce potential endogenous problems [27]: the IVs and moderator (i.e., status-standing) were measured in T1 (i.e., Dec. 1st, 2017 to Jan. 1st, 2018), and the DV and control variables (i.e., tenure, gender, login frequency) were measured in T2 (i.e., Jan. 1st, 2018 to Feb. 1, 2018). Furthermore, in our robustness checks, we also used two diferent period to measure DV to retest the model, namely February 1, 2018 to March 1, 2018 (T3) and January 1, 2018 to March 1, 2018 (T2&3).

## 4.2.1. Dependent variable

Continuous contribution. Following prior research that uses the number of comments written as a proxy for content contributions [16,24], our DV continuous contribution was measured by the number of newly written comments in the time period of T2.

## 4.2.2. Independent variables

Virtual rewards. The Dianping community uses a virtual reward program to incentivize contributors. To collect a virtual reward, members need to use their own contribution in exchange for it [32]. Once a member's contribution reaches certain levels, he/she will be rewarded D-coins (Dianping Coins). The D-coins earned can be exchanged for certificates that can be used toward an in-store purchase of products and services, or for the prize draw. From the platform, we can ascertain how many D-coins a participant obtains. In this paper, the calculation of D-coins depends on the Dianping points. When the points reach a certain level, the community will be rewarded with the corre sponding D-coins. Although users may consume a certain amount of Dcoins to exchange for corresponding products or services, this does not afect the total number of points. Therefore, regardless of whether a member exchanged his/her D-coins for meal certificates, we can calculate the total number of D-coins awarded by the community according to the corresponding points. In this study, we employed the amount of D-coins a content contributor earned in the time period of T1 to measure virtual rewards.

Peer recognition reflects a member's perception that his contribution has received attention or some sort of acknowledgement from other members [12,24,26]. Members usually use information or content to receive recognition from other members. In VCs, when members contribute content, they always expect to get a response or recognition from their followers or someone else [24,26], which is expressed by votes of usefulness [12]. In Dianping, a content contributor can receive “likes” from other members, which reflects the recognition or response to the content that they contribute. We therefore used the number of likes that a content contributor receives in the time period of T1 to measure the level of peer recognition.

Social learning occurs as a result of observing both the behavior of others and the environmental outcomes of certain behaviors [26]. In dividuals who accumulate a growing stock of abilities by learning can thus be more easily linked to their own knowledge system and can possibly further enhance their productivity. In the Dianping context, a member actively seeks out other members to follow (i.e., followees) because those followees are typically considered to be useful information resources to the member. The member automatically receives the reviews and activity information of the followees. The more followees a member has, the more information he/she receives from others in the community. Thus, we used the number of followees that a content contributor has at the end of T1 to measure social learning.

Opinion leadership reflects the degree to which a VC member provides knowledge and advice to other members in a preferred way with relative frequency [25]. Opinion leadership is composed of two distinct dimen sions: social connectedness and interpersonal influence [33]. In VC, members can form an undirected network (user A following user B does not automatically mean that B follows A) with their followers, and a user's number of followers is an informative social signal [40]. When a member contributes a review, it is automatically supplied to his/her followers. In this regard, the more followers a member has, the more opinion leadership he/she assumes. Accordingly, we used the number of followers a content contributor has at the end of T1 to measure opinion leadership.

Status-standing served as the moderator in our analysis; it reflects a member's relative position in the status hierarchy in a given community [44]. The Dianping community employs a status hierarchy of eight levels. We employed a content contributor's level at the end of T1 to measure status-standing, which ranged from 1 to 8, with a higher score suggesting a higher standing in the hierarchy. It is noteworthy that status is not assigned based on the tenure of membership or the total time spent online; rather, it is awarded based on content contributions and community participation (e.g., adding and editing business information, etc.). Therefore, the status-standing measure here resonates with the concept of status in the STOCA [44].

## 4.2.3. Control variable

This paper contains three control variables that have been suggested to influence content contribution behavior. First, year was a measure of a member's tenure of membership. Tenure of membership reflects platform loyalty, which could afect a platform user's future behavior [12,46]. Second, gender was included in our analysis because gender has been found to afect contribution behaviors [46]. Third, our model included login frequency, which reflected platform use intensity [17]. It was measured by a member's monthly average number of location checkins from his/her registration dav to the end of T2

As our variables of virtual rewards, peer recognition, social learning and opinion leadership are power-law distributed, we applied a natural logarithm transformation to them. The correlation coeficients among the IVs are < 0.7; the VIF values for all of these IVs range from 1.02 to 2.47, with an average value of 1.47. Thus, multicollinearity was not considered a problem. ## 4.3. Mediation efect tests

To verify the efectiveness of status standing as a moderator variable, we performed mediation efect tests. For example, status standing may be related to the IVs of peer recognition and opinion leadership. If this is the case, it is not valid to test status standing as a moderator because of the causal relationships between the moderator and the IVs. To avoid this, we should justify that there is no such causal relationship. According to Baron and Kenny [5], if peer recognition is a mediation variable between status standing and continuous contributions, the following conditions should hold: (1) the status standing is shown to afect the future contribution in Path $\operatorname { A } ; ( 2 )$ the status standing afects peer recognition in Path B; (3) the peer recognition afects continuous contributions in Path C. ## 5. Results

## 5.1. Estimation results

The results are presented in Table 5. Model 1 only considered the main efects for NB; Model 2 added control variables based on Model 1; while Models 3, 4, 5, and 6 tested the moderating efects by sequen tially adding interaction items. In addition, the significance of regres sion coeficients of all variables are not change, indicating that these models have a good stability.

The results show that, as predicted, status standing has a negative moderating efect on virtual rewards $( \beta = - \ 0 . 0 7 3 0 , p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and peer recognition $( \beta ~ = ~ - ~ 0 . 1 8 1 , p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 5 )$ . That ${ \mathrm { i } } s ,$ status standing weakens the influence of virtual rewards and peer recognition on continuous contributions. The regression results also suggest that status standing has no significant moderating efect on social learning $( \beta = 0 . 0 5 7 7 , p = 0 . 5 4 4 )$ . Perhaps most significantly, we found that status standing has a positive moderating efect on opinion leadership $( \beta ~ = ~ 0 . 0 3 1 2 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 1 )$ ). In other words, the status standing strengthens the influence of opinion leadership on continuous contributions. Therefore, H1, H2, and H4 are supported, but H3 is not supported (see Fig. 3).

## 5.2. Robustness checks

As with all studies, in order to determine the robustness of the findings, we performed several additional analyses. Coinciding with previous studies like Chen et al. [11], which argues that potential unobserved individual characteristics may significantly influence final results, we included a set of variables for consideration. Specifically, according to Jin et al. [26] and Guan et al. [20], we added the selfdisclosure variable into our model to verify its robustness. Self-disclosure measures the extent to which a member discloses his/her personal information to the community, which, in prior studies, is considered as a correlate variable relating to community participation [20,26]. In this context, Dianping members could choose to release several kinds of information, such as their birthday, occupation, personalized label, and so on. In our study, as with previous works such as Jin et al. [26], we also used the count value by summarizing how many items a focal participator discloses. As shown in Table $^ { 6 , }$ the findings demonstrate that our results are robust.

Analyzing data with alternative models is also an efective way to check the robustness [26,27]. Accordingly, in line with studies such as Guan et al. [20] and Chen et al. [11], we also performed our analysis by using diferent estimation models. In particular, considering that the DV is a count variable in nature, we adopted the Poisson Model and Ordinary Least Squares Model. As shown in columns 2 to 5 of Table $^ { 6 , }$ we learn that the results are also robust. In addition, we further checked the results by using alternative sample periods, as suggested by Chen et al. [12]. In particular, we selected the IVs during the period from January 1, 2018 to February1, 2018 (T2), and the DV from February 1, 2018 to March 1, 2018 (T3) to retest the model. In addition, we also selected the IVs during the period from December 1, 2017 to January 1, 2018 (T1), and the DV from January 1, 2018 to March 1, 2018 (T2&3) to retest the model. The consistency of the results further indicates the robustness of our findings.

In addition, the positive moderation of status standing on the re lationship between opinion leadership and continuous contributions shows that, when members are motivated by their role as opinion leaders, they tend to shoulder more responsibility to guide others [29]. Specifically, they devote more time and efort as meaningful con tributors. In addition, as a member's status advances in the community, he/she is increasingly invested in the group and, therefore, becomes more willing to serve as an opinion leader [23]. Thus, the member's higher status would lead him/her to play the role of an opinion leader more actively, which results in more content contributions to the VC.

However, interestingly, we typically did not find the positive/ne gative moderating efect of status standing on the relationship between social learning and continuous contributions. This may be due to the dual influence of status standing. On the one hand, members with a higher status have a stronger group identity, which strengthens their sense of obligation and duty [7,22]. Accordingly, they are more likely to accumulate a growing stock of abilities by social learning to access of their own knowledge and enhance their productivity. On the other hand, when individuals are relatively high in status standing, they tend to exert influence on the viewpoints and decisions of other members, and are unlikely to be afected by others [37]. However, social learning means that members also need to acquire knowledge from others, which may expose one's inabilities, rather than promote contribution.

## 6.1. Limitations and future research

Before we discuss how this research holds implications for both theory and practice, as with all prior studies, the following will outline limitations with the aim of encouraging future research on this topic. This study mainly centers around the short-term objective data col lected from the Dianping platform, which may, to some extent, hinder the generalizability of the final findings. As suggested in prior studies, diferent types of virtual communities, such as a Q&A community [12,20], a healthcare community [10] or an open source software community [41], may provide specific types of motivation mechanisms to incentivize the participants to contribute. Therefore, much broader context-oriented and longer-term panel data studies that focus on extensive motivation mechanisms are required for further explorations.

Moreover, in this study, we aware that the R-squared value is relatively small. Although studies like Wooldridge [45] suggest that low R-squared in social science studies are common, and a seemingly low R squared does not mean that an OLS regression equation is useless, a higher R-squared would be better to explain the potential influences of various motivations on the continuous content contribution. Relating to this study, we conjecture that low R-squared of our study may due to the unobserved individual characteristics like self-eficacy, community identification, attitude toward sharing, sense of self-worth, sense of belonging, and ideology and altruism as suggested in previous studies [20,26,41,46]. Therefore, we call for future research to explore the issue in relation to the contributed knowledge quality and the unobserved individual characteristics, thus extending the current knowl edge and ofering practical guidelines to community managers.

## 6.2. Implications

This study ofers several important implications for extant knowl edge and practices. First, the findings broaden the virtual community related literature. Status representation has been widely applied in the design of games or VCs, through the form of medals or titles, which is demonstrated by sociologists to not only bring diferent cognitive perceptions to participators, but also to their value systems [13,37]. In past studies, scholars have paid attention to how status design could in centivize participants to generate more contributions [16,27] and re lated performance [28]. Moreover, scholars have not yet explored in a comprehensive manner how status design impacts the varying types of motivations diferently. The undertaking of this study extends our knowledge about how status standing influences diferent types of motivations in VCs.

Second, this study contributes to the status theory of collective action. Status theory of collective action, from a sociological perspective, is used to interpret how people with diferent statuses in focal systems have diferent levels of motivation to contribute, whereby participants with a higher level of status would be less likely to be free-riding and more active contributors to the collective actions of groups [28,35,37]. However, the theory is not meticulously tested to determine whether and how status influences motivations varying from the self-interests to the prosocial [13,44]. Although previous studies have shown that the efects of incentives on individuals vary depending on an individual's status standing [42], scholars have neglected to specify the intricacies involved in the motivation-behavior linkages. The findings of this study show how various types of motivations, say self-interest driven to prosocial-oriented motives, impact participators' continuous content contribution behavior, thus extending the current knowledge.

Third, Chen et al. [11] suggest that the failure to efectively map behavioral theories onto VC design may be due to the fact that most experimental behavioral methods cannot consider the multiple motivational factors of a VC, which should be determined simultaneously. Considering the intrinsic public good of VCs, continuous contributions to VCs is of the utmost importance, as it ensures its long-term success and survival. This study suggests a comprehensive view about how to promote continuous contributions more efectively, and how status standing moderates the impact of diferent types of motivations (selfinterest vs. prosocial) on continuous contributions in VCs. These results reveal deep insights into how the motivational mechanisms of VCs can be reasonably designed and properly used to better promote continuous contributions.

Virtual rewards and peer recognition seem to be efective at encouraging members to contribute. This indicates that members are more inclined to contribute if they realize that the community can give reasonable rewards and timely recognitions for their contribution. In addition, virtual rewards and peer recognition appear to be particularly efective at motivating low-status members. However, the current VC design employs a “winner-take-all” mechanism, which may not be so efective [38]. In this case, only high-status members can be awarded while the rest receive very few recognitions. Thus, VC members with a low status are more prone to being insuficiently motivated. An improved mechanism should try to balance the incentivizing efects of members at diferent statuses wherever possible. For example, community operators should consider the following two issues with careful attention: (1) how to broaden access to virtual rewards to attract new or low-status members; (2) how to increase the opportunities for current members to gain recognition. Meanwhile, community managers should be fully aware that the follow mechanism may foster free-riding habits. In such cases, VC managers can identify free-riders by algorithms, and then use a push mechanism to guide them to contribute. Community operators should make use of technology to strengthen social interac tions between users and their followees, and constantly cultivate a sense of reciprocal awareness in the community to play an active role in controlling free-riding behavior.

Moreover, our findings show that opinion leadership is an important motivator to promote continuous contributions in VCs. The more followers a member has, the more likely that he/she will contribute to the community. Opinion leadership appears to be particularly important as status continues to rise. Thus, when contributors have a higher status, community managers can strengthen their opinion leadership. VCs can promote opinion leadership among high-status members through a variety of ways. For instance, the Dianping community sets the weight of comments to increase the exposure of members who have a higher status and gives them opportunities to showcase their contributions. The Monthly Star Program employed by the Dianping community can raise opinion leadership, enhancing the contributors' identification to the community and strengthening their status within it. In addition, community operators can hold periodic face-to-face activities by inviting high-status members to share their experience, which is con ducive to enhanced social interactions and opinion leadership.

Furthermore, the improved status-standing might reinforce a member's pro-social or group-based motivations, so that he/she cares more about the community and is more willing to take responsibility to ensure its success. In reality, many VC operators do not apply diferent incentive weights that are contingent on status standing. Rather, all members are perceived as roughly the same, without realizing that diferences in status can lead to diferent preferences and group beha viors. Our results show that status standing negatively moderates the influence of self-interest motivation (e.g., virtual rewards and peer recognition) on continuous contributions, whereas it positively moderates the influence of pro-social motivation (e.g., opinion leadership). If the goal is to induce more high-status members to contribute, then encouraging members to get more followers may be a more efective intervention than attaining more “likes” and virtual currency. However, in cases where members are low-status, the weight of “virtual rewards” and “peer recognition” should be strengthened, and the efect of “opinion leadership” can be downplayed. Thus, appropriate and efective guidance can help members decide where to devote their eforts and how to accumulate community experience, thereby reducing the lack of motivation. In addition, a buddy matching algorithm that connects members and encourages social exchanges can be useful in that high. status members can steer low-status members toward pro-social beha vior. VC operators could re-consider or re-design their motivation me chanisms to align with our findings, which would encourage members with diferent status standings to engage and contribute maximally.

## 7. Concluding remarks

The present study attempts to improve current understandings of VC contributions by developing and empirically testing a theoretical model that integrates diferent types of motivations, members' status-standing, and continuous contributions; to do so, this research builds upon the status theory of collective action to explain how VC contributors' status standing moderates their motivations to make continuous contributions. An integrative model is proposed and validated against largescale data collected from a leading VC in China. Overall, our findings suggest that improved status-standing can compensate for the motivational incentives of virtual rewards and peer recognition, while boosting the prosocial motivation of opinion leadership to contribute content in VCs.
