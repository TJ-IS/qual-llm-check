# The roles of psychological climate, information management capabilities, and IT support on knowledge-sharing: an MOA perspective

William J. Kettinger<sup>1</sup>, Yuan Li<sup>2</sup>, Joshua M. Davis<sup>3</sup> and Lynda Kettinger<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Management Information Systems Department, Fogelman College of Business & Economics, University of Memphis, Memphis, TN, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Division of Business, Mathematics and Sciences, Columbia College, Columbia, SC, U.S.A.; <sup>3</sup>Department of Marketing and Supply Chain Management, School of Business, College of Charleston, Charleston, SC, U.S.A.; <sup>4</sup>Memphis, Tennessee

Correspondence: William J. Kettinger, Management Information Systems Department, Fogelman College of Business & Economics, University of Memphis, Memphis, TN 38152-3120 U.S.A. Tel: +1 901 678 4547; E-mail: bill.kettinger@memphis.edu

## Abstract

Leveraging the motivation-opportunity-ability (MOA) theoretical framework and past research on psychological climate, this study analyzes three antecedent factors driving an individual’s knowledge-sharing (KS) within organizations: knowledge-sharing psychological climate as motivation, information management capability as ability, and organizational information technology support as opportunity. An empirical examination reveals that a motivating psychological climate has a primary impact on KS behavior, and the impact of perceived information management capability on sharing is mediated by the psychological climate. Perceived organizational use of information technology to support knowledge work bears strong influence on information management capabilities but not on sharing, suggesting that investment in IT does have indirect payoffs. The study is the first to position the opportunity→ability→motivation causal network in an individual’s KS behavior. The findings suggest that managers need to consider the pre-requisite roles of IT-enabled opportunities and workers’ information management abilities when building an all-important motivating climate to share.

## Introduction

Knowledge is a strategic resource that forms the basis for sustained competitiveness of organizations (Prieto & Easterby-Smith, 2006). Importantly, because organizational knowledge resides primarily within individual knowledge workers (Grant, 1996), it must <sup>fi</sup>rst be adequately managed and shared before its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be reaped by the organization (Foss et al, 2010). Knowledge-sharing (KS) often does not occur, as it is encumbered by a number of hurdles (Staples & Webster, 2008). Therefore, managers must identify effective methods for ensuring opportunities to share, enhancing employees’ abilities to share, and nurturing the work environments that motivate individuals to share their knowledge (Argote et al, 2003; Siemsen et al, 2008).

IS research highlights a variety of factors that drive an individual’s KS, such as need for reputation, organizational incentives, and technological support (e.g. Huber, 2001; Kock & Davison, 2003; Taylor, 2004; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Belanger & Allport, 2008; Wang & Haggerty, 2009). While various driving factors have been explored, few studies have pulled them together into a single explanatory model (Wang & Noe, 2010). Scholars suggest that drawing together factors into a single, integrative model will help improve the understanding of KS (Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005; Prieto & Easterby-Smith, 2006); to this end, the motivation-opportunity-ability (MOA) framework was adopted in KS research (Siemsen et al, 2008). This framework suggests that sharing is driven by three primary factors – motivation to share, opportunity to share, and ability to share – and lacking of any of these factors would cause bottlenecks in KS.

While the MOA framework has extant use in organizational behavior research, it has not been a focus in the IS literature. Reasons for this may be that speci<sup>fi</sup>c concepts constituting opportunity, ability, and motivation to share knowledge have not been developed or operationalized. Second, the relationship among MOA factors is still unresolved in the KS context. While research in other <sup>fi</sup>elds has indicated motivation plays a key role in directly predicting KS, the direct effects for opportunity and ability are less consistent across the literature (e.g., Siemsen et al, 2008; Reinholt et al, 2011). This suggests a potential indirect impact of opportunity and ability on KS that could be mediated by a motivation factor. Responding to literature, we employ the MOA framework to identify speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors constituting opportunity, ability, and motivation to share knowledge in the context of individuals working in IT-enabled organizations. As will be discussed in the theoretical basis section, we focus on an individual’s psychological climate for KS as the motivation factor, his/ her information management capabilities as the ability factor, and perception of organizational IT support as the opportunity factor, and study the impacts of the factors on KS. We illustrate that each factor is a solid proxy of the corresponding components in the MOA framework.

This research is guided by the following question: How does an individual’s psychological climate (motivation), perceived information management capabilities (abilities), and perceptions of the organization’s supportive information technology (opportunity) integrate and contribute to KS behavior? By answering this question, this study attempts to contribute to research on intra-organizational KS in three important ways. First, we propose a theoretical model to predict an individual’s sharing based on the MOA framework (Macinnis et al, 1991) and examine knowledge-sharing psychological climate (KSPC), perceived IT support (ITS), and personal information management capabilities (IMC), an understudied ability in IS research, as underlying drivers of KS. Second, we examine both direct effects of each driver and the potential causal relationships among the drivers, as recent research applying the MOA framework to KS examines the interaction effects of the MOA factors but not the mediating effects among them, which may provide insights on their causal order. Third, responding to calls for multidimensional perspectives, we include known motivational factors into a newly developed individual-level KSPC construct. The measurement of climate as a multidimensional construct composed of several related and organizationally relevant dimensions holds promise for managers in designing a more effective climate for KS.

In the next sections, we <sup>fi</sup>rst elaborate the theoretical basis of the study, and develop the research model and the hypotheses. The research method for testing the model is reported, and the results and implications of the study are discussed.

## Theoretical basis and research mode

The MOA framework (Macinnis & Jaworski, 1989; Macinnis et al, 1991) is a well-established theoretical base for understanding work performance (Blumberg & Pringle, 1982; Argote et al, 2003). It posits that motivation, opportunity, and ability are direct and interrelated antecedents of behavior. Motivation is a force directing individuals toward speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals, and it encompasses a willingness, readiness, and desire to engage in a behavior (Macinnis et al, 1991). Ability is the extent to which an individual possesses the necessary skills and abilities required to commit an action (Macinnis et al, 1991). Opportunity represents the extent to which the situation is conducive to accomplishing a particular action (Macinnis & Jaworski, 1989), and it encompasses the contextual factors that facilitate or hinder a particular behavior (Macinnis et al, 1991; Gruen et al, 2006). The framework has been employed as a conceptual basis for organizing knowledge-management practices in the workplace (Argote et al, 2003), and it has been applied to examine knowledge-sharing (Siemsen et al, 2008; Reinholt et al, 2011).

Drawing upon the MOA framework, we study three particular factors that constitute motivation, opportunity, and ability to share one’s knowledge. First, we study an individual’s KSPC as the motivational factor. Prior research has shown that the organizational context can have an important motivational impact on KS, as social settings often in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge workers’ attitudes and beliefs (Alavi et al, 2005; Boh & Wong, 2013). Studies focused on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the organizational context have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a number of isolated factors that motivate KS behavior, including social norms (Kankanhalli et al, 2005); perceived organizational support (Cabrera et al, 2006); social ties (He & Wei, 2009); and incentive structures (Siemsen et al, 2007). Climate has been highlighted as an important motivational factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing innovation, solution reuse, and decision-making performance (Barkhi & Kao, 2011). The concept of psychological climate is receiving increased attention from IS researchers, and consistent with our focus, is an individual-level construct as opposed to organizational climate. More recently, researchers have advocated for multi-dimensional perspectives of the organizational KS environment, arguing that the conventional focus on isolated dimensions of the environment provides a limited view of how those dimensions affect KS (Boh & Wong, 2013). In line with this notion, we take a multi-dimensional approach to capturing the KS environment by focusing on the individual’s perceptions of the KS climate. While we recognize that additional motivational factors might be important in speci<sup>fi</sup>c KS contexts, the importance of establishing an initial multi-dimensional measure of psychological climate re<sup>fl</sup>ecting key motivational components is an important research concern.

We recognize that a precondition to effective KS is having an understanding of the information to be shared. Perceptions of useful information depend on the ability to effectively draw out relevant information and present it to a recipient. At the core of this is the sender’s ability to manage organizational and personal information well (Marchand et al, 2000, 2001). Therefore, we position an individual’s perceived IMC as a key ability driving KS behavior. Past research has generally captured an individual’s ability in the KS context through knowledge-based constructs such as knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (Kankanhalli et al, 2005) and self-rated expertise (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). While there is value in this approach, we sought to more explicitly capture ability in the KS context by understanding information management processes necessary to share knowledge. Employing this information management lifecycle concept (Marchand et al, 2000, 2001) decouples information management processes (e.g., collecting, organizing, and processing) from perceptions of the information resource being managed. By doing so, IMC captures component information abilities that underpin KS behavior. While past work has established self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy as an important driver of KS, little is known about how the strength of one’s information management abilities impacts KS (Alavi & Leidner, 1999); thus, we examine this factor in the current study.

Finally, we study perceived ITS as the opportunity factor. ITS, such as the use of Knowledge Management Systems or other collaborative systems, is generally accepted to provide opportunity for KS (Argote et al, 2003; Ma & Agarwal, 2007). While some have examined individuals’ perceptions of the availability and quality of KM systems and their impacts on KS (e.g. Cabrera et al, 2006), we argue it is perceptions of an organization’s use of IT to support knowledge work that is important for sharing. Prior research has typically scoped the ITS construct to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT application within isolated knowledge exchange contexts (Kock & Davison, 2003; Belanger & Allport, 2008). As a result, this stream lacks a broader depiction of ITS as an opportunistic foundational factor that considers areas such as daily operations, work processes, innovation, and decision-making that are all essential for knowledge workers in relation to KS. This depiction of perceived organizational use of ITS as opportunity is also more aligned with individual-level information management processing and use (IMC).

## Psychological climate as motivation

Climate is considered a manifestation of organizational culture that can be conceptualized as either an organizational or a psychological (individual-level) variable (Durcikova et al, 2011). Psychological climate in particular provides an informative basis for hypothesizing how interpretations of the work environment motivate subsequent behaviors ( Jia et al, 2008). It refers to one’s perception and interpretation of the workplace climate (Barkhi & Kao, 2011), and it captures an individual’s cognitive constructions of the situation, which result in personalized, subjective interpretations of that situation in terms that are psychologically meaningful ( James et al, 1978; Jia et al, 2008). Literature shows that a worker’s perceptions of his/ her organizational environment take motivational signi<sup>fi</sup> cance through a process of valuation, whereby cognitive representations of the workplace are interpreted in light of personal values (Brown & Leigh, 1996). Consistent with this understanding, past studies have used the term ‘motivational climate’ to refer to perceptions underpinning psychological climate (Ntoumanis & Biddle, 1999) and have shown psychological climate to serve as an important individual factor motivating a variety of workplace behaviors including decision-making (Barkhi & Kao, 2011), job involvement (Brown & Leigh, 1996), and solution innovation and reuse (Durcikova et al, 2011).

Psychological climate can be assessed either as a molar concept or by tailoring it to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context (Carr et al, 2003). In organizational research, scholars recommend a tailored approach to capturing psychological climate, <sup>fi</sup>tting the construct to the context under investigation (Brown & Leigh, 1996; Durcikova et al, 2011). Following this recommendation, we tailor the psychological climate concept to the IT-supported KS context and develop a new construct – KSPC – to represent an individual’s perceptions of the practices, procedures, and goals that get rewarded, supported and expected with regard to KS. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne KSPC as a knowledge worker’s cognitive representations of the knowledge-sharing environment in the work unit. As an intra-individual factor, KSPC serves as a perceptual mediator through which objective individual and environmental factors impact KS behaviors ( James et al, 2008). Consistent with the psychological climate literature (Parker et al, 2003), we expect it to have an impact on an individual’s KS behavior.

Perceptions of the work environment should drive behavior through the creation of normative belief systems. Knowledge management (KM) researchers suggest that organizational norms stimulate sharing by reducing the perceived costs and efforts for sharing (Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Olivera et al, 2008). Taking a cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t perspective, this suggests that KSPC motivates KS outcomes. For example, to the extent that employees are receiving feedback about goal-related performance, are expected to proactively utilize knowledge to solve problems, and are encouraged to interact with other employees for solving problems, then their KS climate impressions will be greater. Therefore, we hypothesize a direct impact of employees’ KSPC on sharing:

H1: Knowledge-sharing psychological climate (KSPC) is positively associated with knowledge-sharing (KS).

Perceived information management capability as ability In the KS context, ability is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent of one’s skills and pro<sup>fi</sup>ciencies required to share knowledge (Siemsen et al, 2008). We suggest that perceived IMC best describes such skills and pro<sup>fi</sup>ciencies. Perceived IMC consists of a set of interrelated individual competencies for effective management of information, including: collecting, organizing, processing, and maintaining (Marchand et al, 2000, 2001). This multi-dimensional view of IMC moves beyond single areas of competence such as virtual competence (Wang & Haggerty, 2009) and encompasses the whole information management lifecycle. While the original IMC conceptualization was developed at the organizational level, the core tenets of the IMC constructs are applicable to the individual level. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, collecting consists of identifying and gathering information from key sources both inside and outside of a person’s workplace; organizing includes indexing, classifying, and arranging information in a systematic manner for future access; processing deals with the assessment and analysis of information for decision-making and value creation; and maintenance keeps information updated for enhanced organizational performance. While each taps into a different aspect of the capabilities needed for information management, they are not unrelated. As information is managed in a continuous and iterative lifecycle, which requires a seamless integration of all the competences, the four competences are interdependent and constitute a higher-level capability of intra-individual information management.

Ability, as captured by perceived IMC, is fundamental to KS for two reasons. First, it enhances the perceived value of held knowledge (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Second, it makes knowledge more modi<sup>fi</sup>able and transferrable. KM research suggests that expertise in encoding, storing and retrieving information enables knowledge transfer among distributed team members (Oshri et al, 2008). Active collection of information can greatly enrich an employee’s knowledge repository, which makes sharing feasible and encourages further KS activity. Competence in organizing information is also important in categorizing and formalizing knowledge, which is necessary to convert knowledge into codi<sup>fi</sup>ed, communicable forms (Huber, 2001). An important task in information processing is the analysis of information sources to derive useful knowledge for decision-making. This can improve both the quantity and the quality of possessed knowledge, and enhance KS activity. Finally, keeping information up-to-date is important for the value of knowledge held and the effectiveness of KS. All these competencies are interrelated and together they constitute one’s ability to share knowledge. Following this rationale, we hypothesize:

## H2: Perceived information management capability (IMC) is positively associated with knowledge-sharing (KS).

We also expect a direct relationship between perceived IMC and KSPC, which presents the mediating role of KSPC. The psychology literature describes psychological climate as an intervening variable, where the point of intervention is within the individual, and mediates between situational attributes and individual attributes ( James et al, 2008). Since individuals perceive situations in terms of their personal meaning, the operations and transformations someone performs on information have a direct impact on psychological climate ( James et al, 2008). Translated to the KS context, these research <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that KSPC is an intermediate variable between individual characteristics such as perceived IMC, and KS activities.

Organizational research also suggests that an individual’s IMC functions as a tool to make sense of the organizational environment (Boland et al, 1994). Enhanced IMC allows one to possess more valuable information to improve the awareness of the situation. Thus, we expect KSPC to play an intermediate role between perceived IMC and KS activity. Stated formally, we hypothesize:

H3: Perceived information management capability (IMC) is positively related to knowledge-sharing psychological climate (KSPC).

## Perceived information technology support as opportunity

ITS refers to perceptions of the organization’s use of IT resources to facilitate various organizational activities from operation, business process, innovation, to decisionmaking (Ma & Agarwal, 2007). ITS captures the tools and resources provided to workers for information access and collaboration across the <sup>fi</sup>rm, which provides greater opportunity for KS (Argote et al, 2003). Drawing from past research (e.g., Marchand et al, 2000, 2001), perceived ITS is considered here to be an essential enabler of KS activities. IT resources facilitate workplace behavior across four domains: operations, work processes, innovation, and decision-making (Marchand et al, 2000, 2001). ITS of operations provides the basic approach through which an employee communicates with others, shares best practices, and improves work performance. ITS of work processes links individuals across functional areas, providing opportunities for sharing cross-functional knowledge. IT can also enhance the speed and process of innovation, which depends heavily on the communication of new ideas. Finally, ITS provides a mechanism to exchange ideas in the course of collaborative decisionmaking. A uni<sup>fi</sup>ed, highly integrative IT infrastructure supports knowledge work across various organizational activities and suggests the existence of a higher-order ITS construct.

Prior research suggests that providing employees with tools and resources for information access and collaboration has a direct effect on knowledge exchange in team settings (Gupta et al, 2009). These tools can reduce time and effort required for sharing (Olivera et al, 2008) by helping individuals form and sustain social networks that can create trust often required to share expertise (Huber, 2001), and by showing someone ‘where knowledge is and who has it’ (Van Den Hooff & Huysman, 2009). Thus, an organization’s ITS use reduces perceived external barriers to KS such as physical distance and temporal asymmetry (Argote et al, 2003; Ma & Agarwal, 2007). By reducing these perceived costs, ITS can increase the overall perceived value of KS, positively in<sup>fl</sup>uencing KS activities. Based on this rationale, we expect a positive relationship between an individual’s perceived organizational use of IT and KS activities, and we hypothesize:

## H4: Perceived information technology support (ITS) is positively associated with knowledge-sharing (KS).

Since perceived IMC comprises the behavioral aspects of an individual’s KS ability and perceived ITS captures the organization’s use and breadth of technological infrastructure made available for KS, the two are closely related. Without adequate technical support for the primary IMC, the information handling workload can become too voluminous to handle manually, and result in information overload and poor performance. On the other hand, the application of information technology such as collaborative technology can change information behavior, which in turn restricts or enhances the functions of IT (Kock & Davison, 2003; Belanger & Allport, 2008). In addition to a direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence, a substantial portion of the impact from ITS on KS may be mediated through supporting encoding, storage and retrieval processes (e.g. transactive memory) among team members (Choi et al, 2010). Olivera and colleagues (2008) propose that information technology’s role in supporting individuals’ contribution behaviors is through facilitating information processing and communication tasks, thus reducing cognitive effort. When knowledge possessed by an individual is converted from tacit forms into explicit forms, it becomes more transferrable to others (Alavi & Leidner, 2001); technology should be made available to support the conversion and transfer of knowledge (Huber, 2001; Belanger & Allport, 2008). Thus, while perceived IMC is distinct from perceived organizational use of IT, the degree and effectiveness of IMC is substantially enhanced through leveraging available ITS. Based on this rationale, we hypothesize:

H5: Perceived information technology support (ITS) is positively associated with perceived information management capability (IMC).

## Operationalization of constructs

A survey was conducted to collect data to empirically test the research model. The measurement items were either adapted from existing literature or developed in this research. As mentioned in the hypotheses development, both perceived IMC and perceived ITS are multi-dimensional constructs; therefore, we adapted previously validated measures in organizational research (Marchand et al, 2001) to the current study’s context and developed re<sup>fl</sup>ective items to measure each of the four dimensions for perceived ITS and the four dimensions for perceived IMC at the individual level (see Table 1).

New items were developed for the KSPC construct through a standard scale development process. Some measures of psychological climate available in literature (e.g., James et al, 1978) relate to general routine jobs in organizations and were not designed for speci<sup>fi</sup>c behaviors, such as KS. Consequently, further research on psychological climate developed and tailored the measures in particular contexts with varied dimensions (Brown & Leigh, 1996; Barkhi & Kao, 2011). As none of the psychological climate measures <sup>fi</sup>t well with the KS context, we developed a new scale to measure its multiple dimensions. First, a search on cross-disciplinary literature on KM identi<sup>fi</sup>es a number of candidate factors for KSPC, such as rewards and interpersonal relationships (Osterloh & Frey, 2000; Kankanhali et al, 2005), proactiveness and leadership (O’dell & Grayson, 1998), openness to failure and training (Garvin, 1993), supervisor support (Cabrera et al, 2006), and goal awareness (Gagne, 2009). These factors were further investigated since identifying speci<sup>fi</sup>c climate factors should be tailored in collaboration with organizational members in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c study context to increase their ecological validity (Payne, 2000). To corroborate the possible dimensions of the KSPC construct, we conducted a series of on-site interviews at a large multinational company that we call HighTech (pseudonym).

HighTech is an industry leader in supplying power conversion products and communication subsystems for the computing and communication industry. It was founded in the 1990s based on the merger of two other companies, each of which had grown from various merger and acquisitions during the previous decade. It is a knowledge-based <sup>fi</sup>rm that relies heavily on effective knowledge creation and management to survive severe competition, and its facilities and workforce are stretched around the globe. Its clients are typically leaders in communication infrastructure or the enterprise computing industry.

In terms of market position, many of its product divisions operate between a <sup>fi</sup>rst and second tier supplier to the communications and computing industry. At the time of this research, the company was rethinking its decentralized KM activities and considering options for achieving greater KS within and across its operating divisions globally, which provided an excellent opportunity to gain a deeper perspective on the study’s key perceptual factors.

The interviews focused on general KS behaviors as well as perceptions surrounding the KS climate. We designed a series of open-ended questions, such as: How effectively is the company communicating knowledge across and within its global design facilities? What are the facilitators and inhibitors to sharing knowledge with the company’s worldwide design facilities? And, how can the company improve knowledgesharing within its worldwide design facilities? The responses helped to recognize the factors that underlie the KS climate in this setting. Seventeen senior managers, middle managers, and chief engineers participated in the initial interviews. Using the statement analysis technique (Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005), we analyzed the interview notes by counting the frequencies of the factors mentioned, and compared with <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the literature. This crossexamination process based on both literature and on-site interviews revealed <sup>fi</sup>ve key dimensions of KSPC.

<sup>Rewards</sup> Rewards refer to the organizational mechanisms provided as incentives to share knowledge (Huber, 2001). From the economic perspective, such mechanisms compensate for and therefore encourage people’s efforts to share their valuable knowledge with others.

Interpersonal relationships <sub>The familiarity and comfort</sub> associated with close interpersonal relationships can garner trust between individuals and promote mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial KS activity (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Poor social relationships may result in distrust and act as barriers to successful sharing and collaboration (Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005). In terms of psychological climate, the quality and quantity of interpersonal relationships bear strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on an individual’s general perception of a trustworthy work environment (Staples & Webster, 2008).

<sup>Proactiveness</sup> Sharing new and tacit knowledge is rooted in a mentality of proactive and innovative belief, and that the subsequent behavior is valued in the organization ( Jeppesen & Frederiksen, 2006). Employees who have a propensity to proactively seek out and apply useful information are the most productive (Ma & Agarwal, 2007).

<sup>Openness to failure</sup> Organizational learning emphasizes the importance of past experience, since a productive failure can lead to insight, understanding, and thus be an addition to the knowledge base of the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Garvin, 1993). A climate open to failure enhances trust between knowledge workers, which is closely tied to organizational KS (Alavi & Leidner, 2001).

<sup>Goal awareness</sup> Goal awareness is the extent to which individuals are informed of their work performance and use performance information and feedback to improve their work. The process of providing relevant performance feedback can facilitate KS by effecting work motivation (Gagne, 2009). From a motivational perspective, goal awareness has the potential to impact positive perceptions of a KS climate.

Similar to the measurement of perceived IMC and perceived ITS, we developed re<sup>fl</sup>ective items to measure each of the sub-dimensions of the higher-order KSPC construct based on interview notes and the literature. We also developed re<sup>fl</sup>ective items to measure the unidimensional KS construct. Initial survey items were then presented to several IS faculty members and Ph.D. students at a U.S. research university and a selected group of managers at HighTech to verify the face validity of the items and provide feedback for improvement. The survey questionnaire was also piloted at HighTech with 22 respondents to solicit further feedback. This procedure highlighted an issue regarding the use of the word ‘knowledge’ in certain items. ‘Information’ and ‘knowledge’ were used interchangeably among many managers, along with other terms such as ‘lessons’ and ‘best practices’ to refer to speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge transferred. To cope with the use of alternative forms of knowledge by practitioners and to capture more relevant responses, we use the term information as a substitute wherever suitable, a term consistent with much KS research (Wang & Noe, 2010). We clearly stated on the cover page the purpose of the study and the individual emphasis of the measurement items. Each manifest item was measured with a 7-point Likert scale anchored at 1 (indicating strongly agree) and 7 (indicating strongly disagree). The control variables were each measured by a single item.

To determine the directions (i.e., re<sup>fl</sup>ective or formative) of the relationships between the higher-order constructs (including KSPC, perceived IMC and ITS) and their subdimensions, we followed the approach outlined by past research ( Jarvis et al, 2003; Petter et al, 2007). Assessment of the content and interrelationships among the <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions of the KSPC construct indicated that the <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions are formative indicators, since each dimension is conceptually distinct (i.e., non-substitutable) and taps into a separate aspect of KSPC, and together they render a modular KSPC construct. The addition of each dimension (assuming it is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant) would improve the understanding of the overall KSPC, while the removal of the dimension would impair the understanding. The same assessment was applied to the dimensions for perceived IMC and for perceived ITS. As mentioned above, information is managed in a continuous and iterative lifecycle requiring a seamless integration of all the competencies, so that the four IMC dimensions are interdependent and correlated, and each is a re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicator of the perceived IMC. Similarly, the four dimensions of the perceived ITS construct are each an aspect of the overall organizational IT environment and are often integrated and correlated, so that the four dimensions are re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators of perceived ITS as well. The <sup>fi</sup>rst-order factors including the sub-dimensions of KSPC, IMC and ITS and also the unidimensional KS construct are each measured with a group of re<sup>fl</sup>ective items, which, as mentioned above, was controlled in the scale development.

## Survey administration

An anonymous questionnaire was delivered to 254 employees in four divisions/locations (two in Europe and two in North America) from various product divisions of the company. The process was carefully controlled so that individuals involved in the initial interviewing and piloting processes did not participate in the survey. Ultimately 174 completed questionnaires were returned, leading to a response rate of 69%. Means and standard deviations of the measurement items are reported in Table 1. To assess non-response bias, we compared the respondents with all individuals invited, analyzing differences in job positions and divisions of the individuals. ## Data analysis and results

As formative sub-dimensions are included and the major purpose of data analysis is to test the predictability of the model, the Partial Least Squares (PLS) technique was used for model estimation (Chin, 1998). Anderson and Gerbing’s (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988) two-step approach was followed to test the measurement model <sup>fi</sup>rst and then the path model with the latent constructs and hypotheses. As second-order factors exist in the model (i.e., KSPC, perceived ITS and IMC), in order to use PLS to test the model, factor scores of the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order factors (i.e., the subdimensions) were generated from the measurement items and used as raw data to estimate the second-order factors.

## Measurement model validation

For the re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs, the measurement model was tested for the psychometric properties of the items including internal reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. Cronbach’s alpha was <sup>fi</sup>rst used to examine the internal reliability of the items. Three items in three dimensions of the perceived IMC construct had low itemto-the-total correlations and were dropped. Cronbach’s alpha was re-calculated using the remaining items. The re-calculated values, shown in Table 1, are all above 0.6, indicating suf<sup>fi</sup>cient internal reliability.

To test the convergent validity of the constructs, all the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order factors, including the sub-dimensions of the perceived ITS, IMC and KSPC constructs and the KS construct, were estimated using PLS with correlations among the factors. Based on the results, convergent validity was examined using the composite reliability and average variance extracted (AVE). As shown in Table 1, the composite reliability ranges from 0.818 to 0.957, and the AVE ranges from 0.601 to 0.882, all higher than the recommended values. Along with the factor loadings that all exceeded 0.5, the results indicate suf<sup>fi</sup>cient convergent validity.

Discriminant validity was assessed using the comparison of the square roots of the AVE against the inter-factor correlations; Table 2 shows that for each factor the square root of the AVE is greater than the correlations of the factor with other factors, indicating suf<sup>fi</sup>cient discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). In addition, the loadings and cross-loadings of the items (see Table A1), calculated from the factor scores generated by PLS and standardized raw data, showed that none of the items loaded higher on any other factors than on the intended one, providing extra evidence of discriminant validity.

## Common method variance testing

As the individual-level study was conducted in a single corporation, there might be a potential problem of common method variance (CMV) in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the results. To test for the presence of CMV, we <sup>fi</sup>rst followed a popular approach, Harman’s single-factor test (Podsakoff et $^ { a l , }$ 2003), by entering all variables into an exploratory factor analysis without rotation; substantial common method bias is signaled by the emergence of either a single factor or a factor that explains a majority (i.e., over 50%) of the total variances. The analysis revealed multiple factors in the results with the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor accounting for only 24.95% of the total variances. The results suggest that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of CMV on the results is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. In this case. the correlation between perceived ITS and KS (r = 0.11) was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the smallest observed correlation among all of the manifest variables. ## Structural model testing

The PLS test with bootstrapping process (resample size = 200) was conducted to estimate the research model. The loadings of the re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators of perceived IMC, ITS and KS, weights of the formative indicators of KSPC, path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, and variances explained (i.e. R<sup>2</sup>) in the endogenous variables are shown in Figure 2 and in Table 3. The path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients between KSPC and KS (β = 0.557, t = 6.21), between perceived IMC and KSPC $( \beta = 0 . 4 6 2 , ~ t = 5 . 0 2 )$ , and between perceived ITS and IMC− − (β = 0.454, t = 7.03) are all signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 0.01 level, providing support to H1, H3 and H5. The path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients between IMC and KS ( β = 0.036, t = 0.46) and between ITS and KS ( β = 0.020, t = 0.21) are both insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, rejecting H2 and H4. None of the three control variables are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in this research. The full model explains 32.0% of the variance in KS. Meanwhile, the factor loadings of the re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators all exceeded 0.5. For the KSPC construct, goal awareness, proactiveness, and interpersonal relationship dimensions are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 0.01 level; however, openness to failure and rewards were not insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. With insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant variables and paths removed, the clean model still explains 30% of variance in KS.

A potential reason for the non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact of perceived IMC is the mediating role of KSPC; we conducted a series of tests to verify the strength of the mediating effect. Therefore, we use all the approaches to test the mediating effect. For the causal steps approach, we follow the classical method by Baron and Kenny (Baron & Kenny, 1986). We <sup>fi</sup>rst tested the main effect of IMC (the independent variable) on KS (the dependent variable), <sup>fi</sup>nding a path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of 0.250 (t = 3.49), and then tested the main effect of KSPC (the mediator) on KS, <sup>fi</sup>nding a path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of 0.592 (t = 10.86); both were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The impact of IMC on KSPC was also tested, showing a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant result− − ( β = 0.496, t = 7.24). When the three variables were tested together in a partial mediating model, the direct impact of IMC on KS diminished $\left( \beta = - 0 . 0 2 ; ~ t = - 0 . 5 0 \right)$ while the other paths remained signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The results suggest that KSPC is a full mediator of perceived IMC.

Overall, these test results support the intermediate role of perceived IMC (i.e., ability) in the causal chain from perceived ITS (i.e., opportunity) to KS.

## Discussion

This paper addresses the need for an integrative model that explains the factors driving individual KS in an IT-enabled organizational context and the need to clearly specify the components of the factors. Rooted in the MOA theoretical framework and prior research on psychological climate, we examined the impact of KSPC (motivation), perceived IMC (abilities) and perceived organizational ITS (opportunity) in the process of KS. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings present a unique yet parsimonious view of an individual’s KS, positioning the factors in an O→A→M nomological network that is useful for future research and practice. This paper contains several important contributions.

First, in the context of IT-enabled individual KS, we recognize the possibility of intermediate relationships among the MOA factors as they create a causal chain which can be described as OAM. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we hypothesized and found intermediate roles of KSPC and perceived IMC. Consistent with past research, the motivational factor (psychological climate) played the key role in predicting KS. This study addresses calls in previous literature to clarify the nomological network leading to shared knowledge (e.g., Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005; Prieto & Easterby-Smith, 2006; Wang & Noe, 2010).

Similar to previous literature, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that neither opportunities nor abilities have a direct effect on KS. However, our study extends the highly cited Siemsen et al 2008 study in terms of clarifying the interrelationship among the MOA factors (see Appendix B for post-hoc analysis). Importantly, our study shows that although motivation is the primary predictor of behavior, opportunity and ability provide important preconditions to the motivation, which provides a new way of thinking about these factors in the original MOA framework. Future research may explore the directions and relationships of these variables in other organizations and contexts.

Second, to our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to explore a person’s perceived IMC as a facilitating role in the causal order resulting in KS. While we <sup>fi</sup>nd that neither perceived ITS nor IMC directly affects sharing, their roles are in<sup>fl</sup>uential to the process. When people perceive a strong IT infrastructure and a supportive environment for IT use, they are likely to be con<sup>fi</sup>dent in their IMC. This capability does not result solely from training on IT use; it is also re<sup>fl</sup>ected as a con<sup>fi</sup>dence workers have about an informational understanding of their job – a holistic view of information management that allows them to better collect, organize, and use the data being presented to them. When they perceive this ITS and the capabilities to manage their job information well, they are more likely to cue into a motivating KSPC.

Third, this study is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to incorporate psychological climate into the KS domain and, to the best of our knowledge, we are the <sup>fi</sup>rst in MIS to develop, empirically test and validate a new KSPC construct as an individual-level variable. We support and extend prior work on climate’s impact on KM (e.g. Hoegl et al, 2003; Durcikova et al, 2011). Our approach incorporated KSPC as a higher order factor comprised of speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>rst-order factors, or perceptions of workplace practices that may promote sharing. Conceptualizing climate in this manner allows the KS climate to be examined in relation to other perceptions or antecedents of KS and to sharing behavior, and can help managers to target intervention efforts on the most relevant unit or organizational attributes and where to assess its effects (Schulte et al, 2006).

In our study organization, goal awareness, proactiveness, and interpersonal relationships each had a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant effect, but rewards and openness to failure did not. A potential reason for the distinct impacts is the absolute importance (i.e., the loading) of the indicators (Hair et al, 2011). The PLS results show that each sub-dimension of the KSPC construct has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant loading $_ { ( P < 0 . 0 5 ) }$ although only the loading of interpersonal relationship (0.808) is salient (i.e., over 0.5) while the other loadings (ranging from 0.273 to 0.483) are not, despite their signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. Particularly, rewards ( 0.273) and openness to failure (0.341) have the lowest loadings, suggesting that relative importance (i.e., weight) of the sub-dimensions plays a stronger role in determining the latent KSPC construct (Hair et al, 2011). In fact, when the subdimensions of KSPC were modeled as re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators, the structural model explains only 22% of the variance in KS as compared with 32%. We conclude that the roles of the sub-dimensions of the KSPC construct are determined by their relative importance or weights.

Our measures of rewards re<sup>fl</sup>ect only KS-related rewards in general, not speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of incentives (such as monetary or intrinsic motivations), and this vagueness may have yielded our lack of effect in terms of both relative importance and absolute importance. This result is not surprising, as it con<sup>fi</sup>rms previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings that rewards sometimes may not induce KS (Osterloh & Frey, 2000; Cabrera et al, 2006; He & Wei, 2009). As the types of rewards that lead to KS remain unclear (Gagne, 2009; Wang & Noe, 2010), we suggest that future research needs to be more speci<sup>fi</sup>c to determine the impacts of speci<sup>fi</sup>c reward types. It is also possible that rewards have an indirect effect, and still help to create a supportive KS environment (Cabrera et al, 2006). Thus, we recommend removing this dimension from the KSPC construct, and encourage more empirical evidence in <sup>fi</sup>eld settings that clari<sup>fi</sup>es types of recognition and incentives that may link to individual KS behavior.

The result of openness to failure is surprising. A possible reason is that we developed only two items to measure this dimension, which may interpret its weak effect. In fact, the weight of this dimension (0.22) is marginally signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (t = 1.76) at 0.1 level, suggesting that this factor plays a role in nurturing a facilitative KS climate, although compared with other KSPC dimensions, its role is relatively less important. Instead of removing this dimension from the KSPC construct, we suggest that future research may re<sup>fi</sup>ne the measurement and investigate the role of openness to failure in KS.

Climate researchers suggest that the interpretation of climate systems depends on the dimensions included (e.g., Schulte et al, 2006). We followed methods appropriate for assessing a speci<sup>fi</sup>c climate (i.e., KSPC) and used empirically derived scales based on our research setting. Other dimensions such as communication or supervisory and peer support could be important in other industries. A dimension related to the perceived clarity of the work unit’s or organization’s goals for sharing can assess the extent to which individuals perceive the goals of the organization to be linked to sharing knowledge, and may contribute to a positive sharing climate. Future research may also explore KS climate systems to determine if they have a facilitative role with other climate dimensions and measures and in other settings.

Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>nding clearly positions psychological climate as a direct motivating and mediating factor promoting KS. A positive KS climate includes having available feedback regarding work performed (i.e., goal awareness) and a work situation that promotes continuous use of relevant information to allow individuals to have in<sup>fl</sup>uence over their work (i.e., proactiveness). It also implies that an individual can perform his/her work in a supportive work situation where he or she feels a sense of community or connection to fellow workers (i.e., interpersonal relationships). Together, these practices and routines may increase motivation for sharing by satisfying an individual’s relatedness, competency and autonomy needs and through a sense of sharing norms (Gagne, 2009). To better understand the dynamics of KS, future research should include this KS motivational construct to determine if such motivation is a prerequisite for sharing behavior in the researcher’s unique context.

## Managerial implications

Our results suggest that organizations should create a climate for KS around each knowledge worker, and IT and HR units must be in concert to do so. For the IT department, an effective climate <sup>fi</sup>rst requires ensuring an infrastructure of information technology services that are viewed by knowledge workers as supporting their decision-making, problem-solving, and daily operations. Strategies to promote positive perceptions that the organization is good at using IT are needed, such as including periodic assessments of organizational use for each functional area in their IT service quality program. For the HR department, managers should screen and select people that not only know how to use IT systems but also have holistic abilities to manage their information well to do their jobs and to share with others.

Research shows that climates provide a ‘psychological identity of an organization for employees, who come to share this identity through their interactions with others and their work environment’ (Schneider et al, 2006, p. 117). To the extent employees come to share this identity (e.g. KS is valued), climate will then guide their behavior. A climate for sharing behavior emerges when employees are surrounded with meaningful ‘social cues’ that sharing knowledge is supported and expected. In this regard it is the job of general managers to focus on a whole climate system as opposed to single factors if they want to improve KS. The climate measures developed in this study could be adapted and used to periodically assess employees’ perceptions of their KS environment. Multiple interventions in concert among IT, HR, and general managers should be perceived as inter-related and reinforcing.

## Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. HighTech is a large, decentralized multidivisional corporation and its management was concerned with the unbalanced KS across its autonomous product divisions worldwide. As the data were collected from one corporation’s business divisions, the study does suffer from limitations of a nested design. Therefore, the external validity of the results cannot be assured and the study should be replicated across multiple <sup>fi</sup>rms and industries before our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be generalized. However, as employees’ perceptions of the work environments may differ across organizations, our focus on one organization enabled us to control for varying KS climates and ITS systems, enhancing the study's internal validity. We believe that this company context offers some variance in practice similar to a multi-site sample. To verify this point, we conducted a series of analyses to compare the unbalance in KS across divisions. To simplify the tests, we only compared the two divisions in North America with the two divisions in Europe in our sample. The results show signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference only in KS across the divisions, but not for ITS, IMC or KSPC. This suggests that at least for the dependent variable, the sample does not appear to suffer from the single organization context.

Second, the cross-sectional design does not allow for longer-term causal inferences; it is possible that the process of regularly sharing knowledge increases an employee’s perceived IMC, or in<sup>fl</sup>uences their perceptions of the KS climate. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the directions of relationships found in our study. Third, in addition to self-reported KS behavior, supervisors, peer ratings or objective measures of sharing would provide a better understanding of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Finally, future studies leveraging the MOA framework in the organizational KS context may consider combining the factors in this study with additional motivation, opportunity, and ability constructs to present a more comprehensive view of the individual and/or contextual mechanisms that underlie sharing behavior. For example, organizational networks and social ties are recognized to create sharing opportunities by facilitating mutual learning and reducing distance (Argote et al, 2003; Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005). Recent research suggests that persons in large, open organizational settings who are centrally positioned in networks are more likely to share when combined with motivation and ability (Reinholt et al, 2011). Network centrality combined with perceived ITS may be examined for their roles in supporting someone’s ability to share. ## Conclusion

Getting people to share their knowledge within organizations remains a complex challenge. This study bridges MIS, organization behavior and psychology research to further understand an individual’s KS process. We argue that managers need to consider the pre-requisite roles of ITenabled opportunities and workers’ information management abilities when building an all-important motivating climate to share. We believe that the O→A→M casual network unearthed in this study will provide an important theoretical basis for testing other potentially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant additional variables. We hope future research will continue to explore the interplay of individual and organizational factors, and particularly the impact of KSPC, in promoting KS.

## Appendix B

## Post-hoc analysis: extending Siemsen et al (2008)

As a post-hoc analysis, we compared our study with that of Siemsen et al (2008). In their study, Siemsen et al compared four alternative models of MOA including: (1) the linear model where motivation, opportunity, and ability directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence behavior; (2) the multiplicative model where, in addition to the direct impact of motivation, opportunity, and ability, their two-way and three-way interactions also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the behavior; (3) the constraining factor model (CFM) where, in addition to the direct impact of motivation, opportunity, and ability, the weakest factor (called constraint) among the three factors also in<sup>fl</sup>uences the impact of the other two factors; and (4) the combined model which is a combination of all the above factors. The four competing models in Siemsen et al’s study share the same assumption: the direct impact of all the factors (including their interactions) on behavior. Siemsen et al <sup>fi</sup>nd that not all the MOA factors have a direct impact on behavior: motivation has a direct impact on behavior in all competing models; opportunity has no impact at all; and ability shows an impact only in certain models.

To compare our study with each of the alternative models in Siemsen et al’s study, we conducted a series of regression analyses based on the latent construct scores;

the results are shown in Table B1. First, the analysis on the linear model with KSPC, ITS, IMC, and control variables as independent variables and KS as the dependent variable shows that only KSPC is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $\left( \beta = 0 . 5 8 , \ P { < } 0 . 0 0 1 \right)$ and the model explains 38.7% of the variance in behavior (F(6,153) = 16.13, P<0.001). Second, the analysis on the multiplicative model shows that only KSPC is signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant $( \beta = 0 . 5 6 , P { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and none of the other antecedents or interactions are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 0.05 level. The model explains 40.1% of the variance in behavior (F(10,149) = 9.99, P<0.001). Third, the analysis on the constraining factor model shows that KSPC is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ( β = 0.43, P = 0.02) but none of the other antecedents or constraining factors are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The model explains 41.8% of the variance in behavior (F(14,145) = 7.44, P<0.001). Finally, the combined model explains 42.1% of the variance in behavior (F(18,141) = 5.70, P<0.001), but none of the predictors reached signi<sup>fi</sup>cance at 0.05 level. The series of analysis con<sup>fi</sup>rms Siemsen et al’s <sup>fi</sup>nding that the constraining factor model provides a better explanation of the phenomenon than the linear model and the multiplicative model. Nevertheless, except for motivation’s role, the roles of the other two antecedents remain unclear in Siemsen et al’s models. As we discuss in the paper, our path model extends this highly cited work by highlighting the interrelationship among the factors (i.e., O→A→M).
