# Using institutional theory with sensemaking theory: a case study of information system implementation in healthcare

## Abstract

Institutional theory has proven to be a central analytical perspective for investigating the role of social and historical structures of information systems (IS) implementation. However, it does not explicitly account for how organisational actors make sense of and enact technologies in their local context. We address this limitation by exploring the potential of using institutional theory with sensemaking theory to study IS implementation in organisations. We argue that each theoretical perspective has its own explanatory power and that a combination of the two facilitates a much richer interpretation of IS implementation by linking macro- and micro-levels of analysis. To illustrate this, we report from an empirical study of the implementation of an Electronic Patient Record (EPR) system in a clinical setting. Using key constructs from the two theories, our findings address the phenomenon of implementing EPRs at three levels: the organisational field, the organisational/group, and the individual/socio-cognitive level. The study shows how a rationalised myth of an efficient EPR system has travelled from the organisational field to the hospital ward and on to individual doctors. The findings also provide evidence of a strong human agency by showing how doctors enact their work practices and shape the use of the EPR system. The study contributes to IS research by showing the need to address macro-level structures, as well as individual interpretations and practical use situations, in order to identify how and why information systems are adopted by users. ## Introduction

n information systems (IS) research, there has been growing interest in using institutional theory to analyse how institutions are changed, maintained, and reinforced during IS implementation. A large number of studies (Barley, 1986; Avgerou, 2000; Silva and Backhouse, 2003) have looked at the social, historical, and contextual influences on how IS are put into use and show how technologies can be carriers of change in existing institutional structures. The implementation of IS is considered to be mediated by normative, regulative, and culturalcognitive institutional pressures (Liang et al., 2007) where systems carry institutional logics influencing organisational practices (Gosain, 2004).

While institutional theory is well suited to explain the effects or outcomes of institutional pressures (Suddaby and Greenwood, 2009), it does not explicitly take into account the question of how social practices are internalised and reproduced through human actions (Barley and Tolbert, 1997). Fligstein (2001) goes as far as arguing that institutional theory considers organisational actors as being passive recipients or ‘cultural dopes’ who use readily available scripts provided by government, professionals, or other institutional carriers to structure their actions. Other scholars (e.g. DiMaggio, 1988) criticise institutional theory for lacking explanatory power of why actors act as they do and what interests motivate them (e.g., to adopt technology). In line with this, we maintain that, whereas institutional theory provides powerful explanations of the influences of institutional structures on IS implementation, it does not specifically address how the human agency influences the social practices from which the institutions are created. To extend our understanding of IS implementation, we thus argue for the use of complementary theories to the institutional perspective, and suggest juxtaposing institutional theory with the theory of organisational sensemaking to add focus to the details of local practices and the human agency that inform and respond to institutions in IS implementation.

With its origin in social psychology, sensemaking emphasises local and subjective micro-level processes and is primarily a process theory (Weick, 1995). It focuses on how IS implementation is influenced by the organisational actors who are going to use the IS (Barley, 1986; Orlikowski and Robey, 1991) from a subjective and practice-based viewpoint (McKinley et al., 2000; Weber and Glynn, 2006). It is useful when investigating how and why organisational actors appropriate, act, and make sense of IS in the local context (Jensen and Aanestad, 2007). The focus is on micro-level actions, but reasons for acting are not only attributed to individual agencies, but also to institutional constraints, organisational premises, and traditions.

In this paper, we explore the potential of using institutional theory with sensemaking theory as interpretive lenses for analysing IS implementation in organisations. We suggest that each theoretical perspective has its own explanatory power and that a combination of the two theories facilitates a much richer interpretation of IS implementation by linking micro–macro levels of analysis. To illustrate this, we draw from a case study in a Danish hospital ward in which an Electronic Patient Record (EPR) system has been adopted by a group of doctors. The study describes the institutional context into which the EPR system was introduced, and shows how this context influenced and was influenced by the doctors’ sensemaking of the system.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows: next, we introduce the theoretical underpinnings of institutional theory and sensemaking theory with a special emphasis placed on a combination when studying IS implementation. We then describe our methodology and research context. Based on the case study of an EPR implementation, we analyse and discuss the explanatory power of the two perspectives and highlight the advantages of combining them. We conclude the paper by providing implications and suggestions for future research.

## Theoretical underpinnings

Only few studies in other research disciplines have sought to combine institutional theory and sensemaking theory to investigate social phenomena (Weick et al., 2005; Weber and Glynn, 2006). Furthermore, to our knowledge, few previous studies within the IS literature have made the combination addressing multiple levels (see Currie, 2009).

An important distinction between the two theories is the level of analysis addressed. While organisational institutionalism (new institutional theory) (Greenwood et al.,

2008) primarily focuses on macro-level structures addressing the organisational field level and organisational level of analysis, sensemaking theory primarily addresses microlevel processes (Weber and Glynn, 2006), although it occasionally focuses on organisational-level phenomena. We posit that a combination of the two theories is fruitful in providing a multi-level analysis of IS implementations in organisations, as each theory, although extendable upwards or downwards in levels, has its explanatory power in either macro- or micro-level processes; thereby, the two theories complement one another (Weber and Glynn, 2006). Furthermore, both theories are logically compatible due to their common origin and philosophical tradition (Berger and Luckmann, 1966; Schutz, 1967), and both can be categorised as social theories (Kjærgaard and Vendelø, 2008; Currie, 2009) addressing related phenomena (Weber and Glynn, 2006).

Below, we outline the application of each of the theories in the IS literature to show their explanatory power on different levels of analysis, and then discuss the value of combining the two theories.

## An institutional perspective on IS implementation

Institutional theory is a multi-level construct spanning the individual, the organisational, and the organisational field levels of analysis; however, it has rarely been applied as such in IS research (Currie, 2009). Rather, it has been used as a theoretical lens to investigate a variety of topics related to IS implementation at different (and isolated) levels such as the implementation and use of CT scanners at the organisational subsystem level (Barley, 1986), the transformation of an enterprise at the organisational level (Avgerou, 2000), and the diffusion of mobile services at the organisational field level (Knutsen and Lyytinen, 2008).

Institutional theory provides us with three key constructs: rationalised myths, isomorphism, and institutional logics that we consider useful when investigating IS implementation as a social phenomenon.

Rationalised myths are part of the institutional context, and they are often disguised as rational arguments used by organisations to ‘maximize their legitimacy and increase their resources and survival capabilities’ (Meyer and Rowan, 1977). Meyer and Rowan (1977) argue that rationalised myths and taken-for-granted rules lead to isomorphism (structural similarity), where the formal structures of organisations need to conform to society to obtain legitimacy. DiMaggio and Powell (1983) ‘move’ the focus on isomorphism from society level to the organisational field level and introduce the concepts of coercive, normative, and cognitive institutional pressures. They argue that these pressures lead to isomorphism where organisations live in an iron cage. Liang et al. (2007) postulate that cognitive, coercive, and normative institutional pressures impact the assimilation of enterprise systems. For instance, the normative pressures in an organisational field where suppliers, customers, consultants, and professional associations collectively assess and endorse IS innovations (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997) will shape the implementation and assimilation of enterprise systems by providing institutional norms that guide top managers (Liang et al., 2007).

Institutional pressures are normally exerted from the society and organisational field on organisations and individuals as a top-down process; however, bottom-up processes also impact structures in the organisational field and society. This emphasises the reciprocal interaction between levels where macro-structures in society are bridged by organisational fields to micro-structures in organisations or even ‘down’ to the individual actor level, and vice versa (Scott, 2008). The reciprocal interaction between levels is an important aspect, which allows for ‘connecting’ macro-level structures with local subjective sensemaking processes, as we will elaborate further.

There has been much emphasis placed on isomorphism within institutional theory (Greenwood et al., 2008), but this focus has more recently progressed to address the effects of different, often conflicting institutional logics on individuals and organisations. We therefore extend the core constructs presented above with a third construct, institutional logics that ‘y shape rational, mindful behaviour, and individual and organisational actors have some hand in shaping and changing institutional logics’ (Thornton and Ocasio, 2008). Institutional logics link institutions and actions and provide a bridge between macro-structural perspectives (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983) and microprocess approaches (Zucker, 1977). Multiple institutional logics are ‘available’ for organisations and individuals (Scott, 2008), and the embedded agency in institutional logics presupposes partial autonomy for individuals and organisations (Thornton and Ocasio, 2008). Although institutional logics have the embedded agency, we still lack a detailed understanding of how individuals, as well as organisations, choose between the available multiple logics, often contradictory, and then ‘edit’ the roles and scripts (Weber and Glynn, 2006) embedded in institutional logics. This is where sensemaking theory serves as an appropriate approach.

Institutional theory has been applied in the field of healthcare (see Scott et al., 2000) in which a special emphasis has been on the three constructs that we accentuate. With respect to healthcare IS studies, the wellknown study by Barley (1986) shows how the adaptation of identical CT scanner technologies has resulted in divergent changes in the two departments. The contradictory results emphasise the complicated reciprocal interaction between the institutionalised organisation and the IS artefact as carrier of institutional logic. A study by Miscione (2007) stresses how the routines and practices that are inscribed in telemedicine technology have to be embedded in the current local institutional context in order to improve local health conditions. In addition, a collection of diverse types of organisations engaged in competitive and cooperative relations is analysed by Currie and Guah (2007). The study shows conflicting institutional logics between the public sector ethos of professionalism and self-regulation and the private sector ethos of government policy, regulation, and efficiency (ibid.: 244–246).

Whereas the institutional perspective is useful in describing and explaining how political pressures, institutional constraints, and professional traditions construct the context in which IS are implemented, it is less useful in describing how individuals respond to these institutional pressures. We suggest complementing institutional theory with sensemaking theory (Weick, 1995) to ascertain the interplay between action and interpretation at the microlevel where meaning is created, thus guiding further action and interpretation.

## A sensemaking perspective on IS implementation

Sensemaking originates from the field of organisation studies and is defined as the ‘making of sense’ (Weick, 1995), where sense refers to meaning and making refers to the activity of constructing or creating something. It focuses on the relationship between cognition and action in organisations, specifically addressing cognitive and social mechanisms for dealing with unexpected events – for example, the introduction of new technology. Although sensemaking is an ongoing process, the need to make sense is intensified in circumstances where organisational members face new or unexpected situations, where there is no predetermined way to act, and where a high degree of ambiguity or uncertainty is experienced (Weick et al., 2005). Relevant to this study, IS implementation may interrupt organisational members’ current projects or ways of working. This causes what Weick refers to as a ‘shock’ that triggers an intensified period of sensemaking (Anderson, 2006) and that informs action.

In IS research, sensemaking has primarily been used to examine social aspects of IS implementation (Jasperson et al., 2005; Vaast and Walsham, 2005). For instance, Orlikowski and Gash (1994) suggest that ‘to interact with technology, people have to make sense of it; and in this sensemaking process, they develop particular assumptions, expectations, and knowledge of the technology, which then serve to shape subsequent actions toward it’ (Orlikowski and Gash, 1994: 175). An example of its use in IS research is provided by Bansler and Havn (2004) who studied the implementation of groupware technology in an organisational setting and argue that a sensemaking perspective helps managers to clarify the values, needs, and priorities of users when implementing IS. Similarly, Henfridsson (1999) shows how technologies are enacted in specific organisational contexts through actors’ continuous meaning production and actions.

In line with these studies, we focus on three constructs from sensemaking theory: bracketing, enactment, and identity to highlight the micro-level mechanisms at play when implementing IS in organisations. When organisational actors interact with a technology, they single out items and/or events in order to connect and make sense of it; this is also known as bracketing (Weick, 1979). In the bracketing process, the users of a given technology identify specific cues that signify desired preferences and ends. It is most likely that users bracket different cues, and this means that the same technology does not necessarily lend itself to similar interpretations by different groups of actors. Technologies may thus be equivocal in nature, meaning that they imply ‘[s]everal possible and plausible interpretations’ (Weick, 1990: 2). The bracketing process is ongoing where the technology is contextualised, managed, and adapted to the specific context of use.

Following the process of bracketing, the cues are related to the repertoire of frames, or certain institutional logics, that an individual holds. It is the connection of the cues to existing frames that creates meaning, and here we see an obvious link to institutional structures. In this way, the individual creates the reality that he or she responds to in a process of enactment (Weick, 1995). The meaning that the users create will guide their future actions and attention in the situations they face, and this continuous process of enactment may even lead to self-fulfilling prophecies, as individuals only see what they expect.

The third construct, identity, is relevant for our analysis as ‘who we think we are as organisational actors (identity) shapes what we enact and how we interpret’ (Weick et al., 2005). For example, introducing new technology in an organisation may constitute the stimulus that the organisational actors try to place in a frame (e.g., the professional identity of the actors). Through this process, they attempt to relate their interpretations of the technology to the expectations they have of their roles and responsibilities and thus their identity. The identity thereby forms the sensemaking, but the sensemaking also informs the identity by confirming or questioning the existing understandings of identity (i.e., who we are).

The constructs of identity, bracketing, and enactment have been applied to studies in healthcare. For instance, Apker (2004) emphasises the importance of identity construction in managed care by arguing that sensemaking among nurses generates interpretations of change that are grounded in their care giving role. Similarly, in a study by Jensen and Aanestad (2007), we see how healthcare professionals’ sensemaking of an EPR adoption process is related to their conceptions of the technology and to their role and identity. Kohli and Kettinger (2004) investigate how technology is enacted in practice by studying the interdependencies between actors, technology, and contextual factors. They show how the doctors’ professional identity and autonomy shape, as well as are shaped by, IS implementation.

## Research methodology

To illustrate the value of using institutional theory with sensemaking theory, we draw from a qualitative, interpretive case study of the implementation of an EPR system by a group of doctors in an orthopaedic surgery ward in a Danish hospital. The empirical case study was conducted by one of the authors as part of a previous research project (Jensen, 2007).

Healthcare has in the last decades faced increased digitalisation of its work practices, especially with the introduction of EPR systems, which makes it particularly relevant to study IS implementation in this context (Jensen and Aanestad, 2007). Also, healthcare is a highly institutionalised field with a long history of different institutional logics (Currie and Guah, 2007), including a strong professional tradition and a high degree of professional autonomy leading to more explicit and expressive attitudes among actors when studying sensemaking processes (Strauss et al., 1997; Sandelowski, 2000).

## Research setting

As a consequence of the political agenda and national IT strategies for healthcare, the management of a medium sized hospital in Denmark decided in 2003 to implement a standard off-the-shelf EPR system in the entire hospital. In the years that followed, the EPR system was implemented at the gynaecological/obstetrics department, the organ surgery department, and the orthopaedic surgery department. Our empirical study took place in the orthopaedic surgery ward, which has an average of 3000 admissions (emergency and planned) per year and consists of a standard ward, an outpatient clinic, and a secretaries’ office. In the ward, orthopaedic doctors and nurses make up the permanent staff, providing treatment and care in collaboration with staff from other departments: outpatient, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, rehabilitation therapy, and radiology. Ten consultant surgeons and one managing consultant surgeon are employed in the ward. They are specialised in hip alloplastics, shoulder, knee, and foot surgery. In this study, we focus on the surgeons and their experiences and reactions to the EPR implementation.

## Data collection

To study how the EPR system became institutionalised at a society level and to explore the patterns of enactment in the local clinical practices, we draw on three main sources of data: observation studies, semi-structured interviews, and written material.

The participant observation took place during two periods: one at the beginning of 2005 and another at the end of 2005. One of the authors followed the group of doctors in their everyday practice, studying the use of the EPR system in the ward rounds, at conference meetings, and at patient examinations in the outpatient clinic. During the observations, she had the opportunity to talk to the doctors about their expectations, actual experiences with, and reactions to, the EPR implementation. Insights from the observations and informal conversations were noted down as accurately as possible.

To gain in-depth insight into the doctors’ perceptions about the use of the system, ten semi-structured interviews were conducted, each lasting from 60 to 90 min. The interviews were guided by a number of questions. Some questions related to the EPR discourse at an organisational field level were: What led to the implementation of the EPR system? What was the political discourse in Denmark? Other questions related to the specific EPR implementation in the hospital/ward and the doctors’ reactions: How was the EPR system implemented – who was involved, when, and why? How did you experience the implementation? How did the EPR implementation influence your work practices, roles, and responsibilities? What are important values in your work?

The interviews were taped and transcribed verbatim in order for the doctors to approve them. Initial findings about recurring themes from the individual interviews were also reported back in writing to the doctors, and were then followed by a focus group interview that served to elaborate and explain the data from the individual interviews as well as provide an opportunity for the doctors to discuss and reflect upon the findings.

To complete the data collection, we accessed a variety of written material and documentation in order to understand the political and institutional decisions to implement EPR, as well as the public discourse about EPRs. This material included the national IT strategies and regulations for healthcare in Denmark, project plans, organisation charts, information from websites, EPR user manuals, evaluation studies of EPR implementations, and public debates in newspapers, radio, TV, and on the Internet. We also attended conferences and seminars on healthcare related issues over a number of years.

## Data analysis

We coded our interviews and field data (observations and written material) by relying on established methods of handling qualitative data (Huberman and Miles, 1994). First, we carried out a close reading of the observations, field notes, and written documents to sort out important from unimportant data. Second, we looked for relations and contradictions in the data. We then worked at identifying general themes and at categorising them along the questions that guided the study. Finally, we reviewed the emergent themes for each transcript and were able to construct a storyline presenting the process of the EPR implementation at three levels: the organisational field level, the organisational/group level, and individual/socio-cognitive level. We present the case study below.

## Empirical findings: case study of an EPR implementation

The case study first describes the broader picture of the EPR implementation (i.e., at a macro-level) by looking at the contextual and political influences on the EPR discourse in Denmark; it then elaborates on how the EPR system was implemented in the orthopaedic surgery ward, and finally, reflects on the doctors’ perceptions of, and actions and reactions to, the implementation at a micro-level.

## EPR systems on the political agenda in Denmark

We find the first strategic attempts to implement EPR systems in the Danish healthcare sector in an Action Plan for Electronic Patient Records that was published by the Danish Ministry of Health in 1996. The objective of the plan was to promote the development of EPR systems by providing financial as well as other support facilities to local and regional EPR implementation projects. As a result of this action plan, EPR implementation projects were initiated in 13 hospitals. A majority of these hospitals implemented standard off-the-shelf systems, whereas others implemented module-based solutions, including medicine, booking, clinical process and laboratory modules, among others. Yet other hospitals built their own systems.

In the later national IT strategies for healthcare from 2000 to 2002 and from 2003 to 2007, the Danish Ministry of Interior and Health gave a mandate that all Danish hospitals needed to adopt an EPR system before 2006 or as soon as possible after this date. The records introduced were to be based on a national standard for the benefit of the patients, the healthcare professionals, and the society in general. An EPR Observatory was established to gather and disseminate evaluations of the ongoing EPR projects. It was also the Observatory’s task to clarify the need and to provide guidelines for the development of national EPR standards.

The main reasons for implementing EPR systems, as presented in the national strategies and in the public discourse, were to solve many of the problems that the healthcare sector was facing: long waiting lists, decreasing budgets, an increase in the number of patients, and complaints about the quality of treatment and care. It was expected that EPRs would improve patient quality and efficiency, and enhance the sharing of data among healthcare professionals. While the government tried to create and communicate a positive picture of the visions, targets, and progress of the EPR strategy, newspaper headlines reflected disappointment in EPR systems: ‘Hospital paralyzed due to EPR error’, ‘Millions of Danish kroner feared wasted. EPR implementation in counties has stopped’, and ‘Electronic records cause problems’.

EPR system implementation in the orthopaedic surgery ward The EPR system introduced in the orthopaedic surgery ward was a standard off-the-shelf system and represented an electronic version of the patient’s paper record. It contained progress notes, nursing notes, physiotherapists’ notes, diagnoses, medicine schemes, X-rays, data on temperature and blood pressure histories, laboratory data, etc.

The hospital management promoted the EPR system as an indispensable tool for work optimisation and as a way to provide the best possible patient treatment and care. The project plan listed the following benefits to be gained as (a) instant access to real-time data in the electronic record, (b) the possibility of patient treatment relying on only one set of information, (c) more secure medicine prescriptions, and (d) improved quality of treatment because of a common documentation system.

For the majority of doctors in the orthopaedic surgery ward, the EPR system provided prospects for better performance and direct clinical benefits to their professional work. They expected the system to support and facilitate their documentation procedures by serving as their daily tool for retrieving patient information and for documentation purposes. The doctors would be able to access real-time patient information from different locations.

The doctors also anticipated that the EPR system would facilitate, as well as secure, the medicine prescription procedures. For instance, the system would enable the doctors to type in medicine prescriptions directly, as opposed to dictating them to secretaries. The prescriptions would only be contained in the system and not be transferred to other systems or paper forms, thereby reducing the risk of errors. The EPR would also make it possible to establish standard prescription procedures for some patient groups (e.g., patients who had a hip replacement) so that the doctors did not have to prescribe each drug separately.

## Doctors’ interpretations and actual use of the EPR system in practice

Many of the expected benefits were realised in practice. The EPR system led to more secure medicine prescription procedures, and the standard prescription function in the system eased their work with respect to some patient groups. Furthermore, the doctors were able to access patient information instantly.

At the same time, some of the doctors criticised the system for leading to unnecessary and too time-consuming work tasks that they did not consider to be part of their responsibility. One example was related to medicine prescription. Even though the EPR implementation had improved the medicine prescription procedures, the doctors now had to spend time on entering prescriptions into the system, a task earlier carried out by secretaries on the basis of doctors’ dictations. This meant that they were spending ‘valuable time on this procedure instead of treating the patients’. They did not consider this activity to be part of their work and some thus continued to dictate.

Another criticism related to doctors needing to retrieve X-rays in the EPR system, whereas previously the nurses had put them up on the board in the outpatient clinic prior to patient consultations. Similarly, doctors were now in charge of referring patients for appointments with occupational therapists or physiotherapists, a task also previously performed by nurses. Both tasks were considered to be too time consuming, and some doctors continued to ask the nurses to perform these activities.

A further criticism was that many doctors felt that their professional authority was challenged since they had not been consulted in the decision-making process of the EPR system. This led to some degree of scepticism. One doctor stated, ‘Of course it is not possible for everybody to participate in this decision making process, but it would have been nice to have the impression that you were involved somehow’.

The doctors also felt that their professional identity as specialists was challenged with the EPR implementation. As expressed by one surgeon: ‘We are craftsmen. We need a hammer and a chisel to do our job. This [using a computer] is not really of interest to us’. Some doctors believed that the EPR led to more control, as they were now obliged by the system to document each time they checked test results, whereas earlier they received the results on paper.

## Analysis of case study

The case study presents the process of implementing the EPR system on three levels: the organisational field level, the organisational/group level, and the individual/sociocognitive level. For illustrative purposes, we present these levels as being distinct and easily separable which is only an approximation, as they are reciprocal in nature.

Organisational field level: changing the institutional environment of Danish healthcare – a rationalised myth about an efficient EPR system

In the first part of the case description, we described the political agenda behind EPR implementations in Denmark. The action plan and the national IT strategies reflected a new era with considerable changes in the institutional environment of healthcare, that is, a change from paperbased patient records to electronic records and changes in how the clinical tasks were to be accomplished. We witnessed the creation of a rationalised myth around the EPR system communicated in the plans and strategies for healthcare and in the public discourse. The system represented an artefact worth implementing, based on its potential to make the clinical work more efficient. Politicians and hospital managers considered instant access to patient data, more secure medicine prescription procedures, and better quality in patient treatment as important benefits. Considerable investments were made to initiate 13 EPR projects and the EPR Observatory was formed to evaluate and monitor the EPR development and to provide guidelines for its future.

The national IT strategies for healthcare can be considered as having been coercively imposed by the government (Dobbin, 1994). They reflected a coercive pressure to not only implement EPR systems, but ‘implement EPR standards before 2006’. We also saw a normative pressure such as ‘EPRs as a technological trend’. These pressures created legitimacy for the hospital to be ‘compliant’ with the wider societal or state expectations (Scott, 2008). This caused a standardisation of working procedures through implementation and use of EPRs, leading to a higher degree of isomorphism among hospitals (the organisational field level) and isomorphism among wards in a single hospital (the organisational level).

The myth that the healthcare professionals would optimise working procedures in hospital wards prevailed (i.e., a claim of efficiency). This was a powerful and wellinstitutionalised myth, meaning that it would probably endure even if (and when) doctors and other healthcare professionals expressed serious concern about the efficiency actually achieved. This is also supported by Meyer and Rowan who claim that ‘[q]uite apart from their possible efficiency, such institutionalised techniques establish an organisation as appropriate, rational, and modern y Their use displays responsibility and avoids claims of negligence’ (Meyer and Rowan, 1977: 344).

The technological innovation in the form of the EPR system can be seen as an object carrying inscribed institutional logics (Gosain, 2004) into hospitals. The public discourse reflected scepticism regarding the systems, which might be explained by functional arguments such as errors in the systems, but also by institutional arguments, such as the power relations between doctors and nurses changing (see also Currie and Guah, 2007). It was within this context (i.e., what Weick terms as ‘frame’) of changing institutional structures that the doctors’ sensemaking of the EPR system, related to its benefits and drawbacks, took place.

Organisational/group level: institutional logics in the local context As the case description moved from a national to an organisational/group discourse, we were able to investigate the local implementation of the EPR system in the orthopaedic surgery ward, including the meaning constructions (Weick, 1995) that were formed by doctors as a professional group. The hospital and the orthopaedic surgery ward were subject to the institutional pressures that existed at the national level. Czarniawska and Joerges (1996) designate this as the ‘travels of ideas’, meaning that the idea of ‘the efficient EPR systems’ travels from place to place – or, as in this example, from the healthcare discussion on the organisational field level into this specific hospital, and further on to the healthcare professionals in the orthopaedic surgery ward using the system. The positive expectations towards the EPR as expressed by the doctors showed how the rationalised myth about the systems ‘travelled’ into the hospital. Their early meaning creation was compliant with the discourse at a national level.

The case study showed evidence of conflicting institutional logics (Currie and Guah, 2007), the institutional logic representing the rational myth about an efficient EPR system and a cost efficient organisation, that is, a private sector ethos. This was further backed by ‘real functional benefits’ enforcing the institutional argument. The doctors were inclined to accept the premises of the system as a way to serve the best interest of patients. But this meant that they had to perform what they perceived as being administrative tasks in addition to what they had done in the past. A conflicting institutional logic was the doctors’ understanding of the typified role of doctors: doctors treat patients and do not perform administrative tasks. This clash of institutional logics showed that multiple, and often conflicting, institutional logics lived side by side as contradictory forces. So where we saw how the institutional logic regarding an efficient system was shaped at the organisational field level, the logic was enacted in practice at an organisational/group level.

The EPR implementation can be seen as part of an ongoing sensemaking process (Henfridsson, 1999). In the beginning, there was a high degree of ambiguity and uncertainty among doctors because of conflicting logics, since their work responsibilities were questioned and challenged. The case study shows evidence of actions where doctors refused to accept the logics inscribed in the system (e.g., that it was now their job to retrieve X-ray pictures). The work-arounds were a solution to the institutional misalignment between the system and their understanding of the typified role (orthopaedic surgeons) with typified actions (treat the patients) (Gosain, 2004). In this way, the study shows how the doctors’ cognitions (i.e., thoughts and interpretations about the technology) and actions (i.e., comply with the technology or work-arounds) were related. The doctors made sense of their work practices in relation to the EPR and some of their enacted practices reinforced existing structures (as we describe in greater detail below).

Individual/socio-cognitive level: the doctors as active players in the EPR implementation process

One of the criticisms of institutional theory is that actors may be considered as passive recipients of institutions, using readily available scripts to structure their actions (Fligstein, 2001). We find evidence of a stronger agency in our case study (Vaast and Walsham, 2005). Although the doctors acted within a specific frame, where certain institutional logics prevailed (e.g., they had not themselves made the decision to implement the EPR system nor to choose the specific system), they actively formed/enacted the course and the outcome of the implementation. They were the primary users and they interpreted, created, as well as determined, the use of the system in practice, which to some extent is related to pre-established conventions of use and ways of thinking. In some cases, the institutional logic built into the system did not seem to ‘make sense’ to the doctors who maintained their routines. They used it in ways that challenged its intended operation and created work-arounds through which they reinforced the old way of working. The doctors were active in bracketing the flow of cues (Weick, 1995) about the system continuously available to them. In this way, they ‘discovered’ the system that they either partly embraced or rejected based on their own assumptions and expectations. These examples put emphasis on the human agency by showing how the doctors actively chose among the different and sometimes conflicting institutional logics at play (Weick et al., 2005).

The empirical findings also show evidence of the equivocal nature of the EPR system (Weick, 2001), lending itself to different interpretations among the doctors. Some of the doctors were in favour of the system, while others claimed that it hindered them in focusing on their primary job of treating patients. Too much time was used on what the doctors termed ‘administrative tasks in front of the computer’. They focused on the many features of the system, which supported this belief of the misuse of their time. The doctors were also uncertain about the long-term consequences of the EPR system. In the early process of the implementation, multiple interpretations thus competed for legitimacy and were open for debate.

As mentioned, the doctors viewed the control mechanism (checking test results) as a challenge of their expertise, professional autonomy, and clinical freedom. The doctors’ continuously engaged in processes of association with, and dissociation from, the system in relation to their professional identity (Weick et al., 2005). We argue that the EPR highly influenced the doctors’ perceived identity and authority. Whether or not the doctors followed the procedures set out by the system or created so-called workarounds, it challenged their status in the organisation. The doctors thus defined themselves in relation to their mission as professionals, namely, treating patients. They did not consider the EPR implementation as an explicit way of improving the overall patient treatment.

## Discussion: the value of a combined theoretical perspective

To fully understand the EPR implementation in the clinical setting, we rely on both institutional and sensemaking theories. Institutional theory provides explanations of the outcomes of institutional pressures and logics on the EPR implementation by having its main focus on the macrolevel. Sensemaking theory directs our attention to the micro-level processes and how organisational actors’ cognition and situated actions are made collective and reified through social construction processes.

Although we argue that institutional theory and sensemaking theory are appropriate theories on their own, we stress the value of combining the two in order to alternate between levels of analysis, which again facilitates a much richer interpretation and analysis of the case study. The result of applying the two theories to our case study is presented in Table 2.

Institutional theory clearly offers strong explanations for addressing our case study at the organisational field level, providing constructs such as isomorphism created by institutional pressures and logics (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983), as well as rationalised myths (Meyer and Rowan, 1977). At this level, sensemaking offers very little explana tion; for example, the concept of frame that connotes past moments of experience with which present experiences are connected; however, the origin or the ‘substance’ of frames is not explained.

Both theories provide explanations at the organisational/ group level as is evident from the table. Institutional theory refers to the travel of the efficient EPR as an idea (Czarniawska and Joerges, 1996), typified actors with typified actions (Berger and Luckmann, 1966), and the technology inscribed with institutional logics (Gosain, 2004) challenging the organisational actors’ authority and work responsibilities, for example, the typified doctor. Sensemaking theory addresses how the EPR implementation implies uncertainty and ambiguity among the organisational members. It also shows the social structures and how actors may be perceived as being ‘fillers of roles and followers of rules’ (Weick, 1995: 71). This is what Weick, based on Wiley (Weick, 1995), describes as the level of generic subjectivity – what is known as typified actors and actions from the institutional perspective.

At the individual/socio-cognitive level, sensemaking offers strong explanations of the inter-subjective processes among actors (Weick, 1995; Henfridsson, 1999), for example, how individual doctors are active players in forming the course and outcome of the EPR implementation (i.e., enactment) and in bracketing and categorising the stream of cues that they experience. Similarly, it shows how the EPR challenges the identity and authority of the doctors, and also how their identity influences their actions. At this level, institutional theory offers very limited explanations, emphasising the importance of the human agency, where actors choose from different and sometimes conflicting institutional logics when making sense. Both theories are well-established and proven theories that offer valuable explanations of IS implementation. However, based on our analysis, we allege that by combining the theories, we attain even more valuable explanations of our research phenomenon.

## Conclusion

Earlier studies have not systematically examined the value of using institutional theory with sensemaking theory to study the phenomenon of IS implementation. This study shows that they are both highly informative when investigating IS implementation and their underlying ideas and assumptions are indeed compatible.

We concluded from the literature review that the two theories differ in their explanatory power of IS implementation with respect to theoretical foundations, levels of analysis, and main arguments. We explored the explanatory power of each theory with an illustrative case study of the implementation of an EPR system in an orthopaedic surgery ward. Our findings show the relationship between the expectations of politicians as to the value and purpose of EPRs, the doctors’ (as a professional group) expectations of the functionality and use of the system, as well as their individual interpretations of how to use or not use the system, and for what purposes.

Based on the case study, we show that institutional theory has its explanatory power at the organisational field and organisational/group levels of analysis, focusing on the role that larger institutional structures play in organisational actors’ sensemaking of IS implementation. Sensemaking theory has its explanatory power at the organisational/group and individual/socio-cognitive levels, focusing on organisational actors’ cognition and situated actions when introduced to a new technology. A combination of the two theories facilitates a much richer interpretation of IS implementation, as we are able to link macro-states that ‘frame’ the behaviour of individual actors with the situated actions and interpretations of the individual actors that (re)construct macro-states. This is important when studying the phenomenon of IS implementation, as it shows the need to address macro-level structures as well as individual interpretations and practical use situations in order to identify how and why IS are adopted by users.

The findings suggest several avenues for future research. First, the case study has served illustrative purposes by highlighting the explanatory power of each theoretical perspective and the value of combining the two. Further, exploration of the combination in more fine-grained and extensive empirical studies is needed to assess its value both as analytical construct and as identification of organisational and managerial implications for action. Second, we do not propose specific methodological guidelines for how to investigate the interrelationship between institutional structures/logics of IS implementation and how IS are enacted by organisational actors in practice. Future studies could investigate in greater detail a methodology for studying this in practice (in line with the study by Barley and Tolbert, 1997). Third, we have directed our attention solely to IS implementation as a phenomenon in this study; we therefore suggest that future researchers extend the combination of institutional and sensemaking theories to other phenomena within the IS field. Finally, the findings from this study pertain to a healthcare context. Future research studies could extend the combination of the two theoretical perspectives to other contexts.
