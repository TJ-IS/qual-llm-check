# An empirical study of mobile commerce in insurance industry: Task–technology fit and individual differences

Ching-Chang Lee <sup>a</sup>, Hsing Kenneth Cheng <sup>b,\*</sup>, Hui-Hsin Cheng <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Management, College of Management, National Kaohsiung First University of Science and Technology, Kaohsiung, Taiwan, ROC

<sup>b</sup> Department of Decision and Information Sciences, Warrington College of Business Administration, The University of Florida, Gainesville, FL 32611-7169, USA

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information Management, Shu-Te University, Kaohsiung, Taiwan, ROC

Available online 27 June 2005

## Abstract

Mobile commerce, generally defined as conducting information inquiries and/or business transactions by using mobile devices via wireless communications, is considered the next big wave of investment and application of information technology. Mobile commerce is estimated to account for a sizable portion of the US\$6.9 trillion e-commerce revenue by 2004. Although abundant research on the technology side of mobile commerce has been published, there exists <sup>b</sup>very limited research on the strategies and applications of M-commerce<sup>Q</sup> [A. Gunasekaran, E. Ngai, Call for papers: special issue on mobile commerce: strategies, technologies, and applications, Decision Support Systems 35 (2003) 187–188. [15]]. We propose and validate a modified task–technology fit model to explore the factors affecting the effective adoption of mobile commerce in the insurance industry. In particular, we study: (1) if the mobile commerce system based on personal digital assistant (PDA) technology is applicable for the insurance industry; (2) whether individual differences will impact the cognitive fit of insurance agents’ use of PDA mobile commerce system; (3) which of the three major insurance tasks is better suited for the PDA technology; and (4) which PDA technology characteristic is best for which type of insurance tasks.

Our research finds that the PDA mobile commerce system is indeed suitable for the insurance industry. In terms of the impact of individual differences, we discover that position experience, cognitive style, and computer self-efficacy are major factors that can predict the fit of applying PDA technology for insurance tasks. Counter conventional wisdom, other demographic variables such as gender and age are found to be non-significant. Among three major insurance tasks, we find that the PDA mobile technology provides the highest level of assistance in post-contract customer services, followed by recruiting new insurance contracts, and tax and legal information services. <sup>D</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Mobile commerce; Task–technology fit; Adoption strategy of mobile commerce for insurance industry

## 1. Introduction

Mobile commerce is generally defined as conducting information inquiries and/or business transactions by using mobile devices via wireless communications. Combining the portability of mobile devices and wireless communications, mobile commerce provides users the benefits of retrieving rich and current information via the Internet anywhere and anytime. Mobile commerce is considered by many businesses as the next major investment and application of information technology. The global investment in the mobile infrastructure will increase from US\$300 million in 2001 to US\$2.3 billion in 2005 (http://www.datamonitor.com, 2002). Mobile commerce is estimated to account for US\$200 billion of the US\$6.9 trillion e-commerce revenue by 2004 [2].

Because of small, lightweight, and portable mobile devices, mobile commerce is especially applicable for those industries consisting of highly mobile employees (e.g., financial and insurance, logistics, real estate, and automobile sales and services, to name a few). Noticing the potential competitive advantage and benefit enabled by mobile technology, many companies commence the planning and adoption of mobile commerce.

Although abundant research on the technology side of mobile commerce has been published, there exists <sup>b</sup>very limited research on the strategies and applications of M-commerce<sup>Q</sup> [15]. To fill the void identified in Gunasekaran and Ngai [15], this paper studies the adoption of mobile technology in the insurance industry from the task–technology fit perspective. How to adopt information technology effectively has been an active and important information systems research area replete with useful theories and findings. The task–technology fit (TTF) theory, developed by Goodhue and Thompson [14], is one recent model gaining popularity and acceptance among information systems researchers. For the adoption of a technology to be effective, the task–technology fit theory states that this technology must be willingly accepted and used by the users, and there needs to be a good fit between the technology and tasks, as well as a good fit between the technology and the users.

The task–technology fit (TTF) theory originated from the cognitive fit theory of Vessey [30]. Vessey proposed the cognitive fit model from the perspective of cognitive cost. The cognitive fit model postulates that a cognitive fit between the problem-solving aids and the problem-solving task reduces the complexity of the task at hand, thereby improving the problemsolving effectiveness. The task–technology fit theory believes that a fit among the task, the technology, and the users positively impacts the adoption of information technology [13,14]. When the users of technology feel that the technology is capable of supporting the task at hand, the users exhibit good performance. The ability of the technology to support the task means that the functionalities of the technology enable a smooth execution of the task, reduce the cost of performing the task, or make the task easily accomplished. The degree of the task and technology fit is affected by the interactions among the characteristics of the task, the functionalities of the technology, and the personalities of the users. The task–technology fit improves when the gap of these three aspects is narrowed. Moreover, different characteristics of the task will require support from different functionalities of the technology. For example, if the required information of the task comes from different organizational units of the company, the technology must have the capability of integrating various data sources.

Another complementary research stream on the linkage between information technology and individual performance focuses on the impact of utilization of information technology on performance (e.g., [8,9,28], among others). In this stream of research, user attitudes and beliefs are employed as the predictor of the utilization of information technology. To include utilization into our research model will require adding an arrow from task–technology fit to utilization and another arrow from utilization to task performance. It has been shown in [14] that there exists little support for the hypothesized relation from task–technology fit to utilization. One underlying assumption, stated or implicit, of the utilization of technology research is that more utilization of the information technology or systems leads to higher performance. However, higher utilization of a system with poor task–technology fit does not result in better performance. For example, Pentland [25] reports that IRS auditors’ positive attitude and extensive use of personal computers had little impact on their performance. For these reasons, this research applies the task–technology fit theory to explore the factors affecting the effective adoption of mobile commerce in the insurance industry, and the willingness of its employees to use the mobile technology. We conduct an empirical study to examine whether mobile commerce is suitable for the tasks of insurance industry. In particular, the objectives of this research include the following:

(1) to study if personal digital assistant (PDA) technology is applicable for insurance agents to perform their major tasks;

(2) to explore whether individual differences will impact the cognitive fit of insurance agents using the PDA technology to recruit new contracts due to the diverse background of insurance agents;

(3) to study which of three major tasks of insurance agents–recruiting new insurance contracts, postcontract services, and providing legal and taxrelated information and services–is better suited for PDA technology;

(4) to understand which PDA technology characteristic is best for which type of tasks of insurance agents.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 describes the three key tasks facing insurance agents, the background of the case company, and the functionalities of the PDA mobile commerce system. The research model is presented in Section 3. Section 4 describes the research samples, research hypotheses, and measurement methodology. Section 5 reports major research findings and analyses. Sec tion 6 provides the concluding remarks.

## 2. Tasks of insurance agents and the case company

According to the Occupational Outlook Handbook (2004–2005 edition) published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics of the U.S. Department of Labor, insurance agents <sup>b</sup>help individuals, families, and businesses select insurance policies that provide the best protection for their lives, health, and property. Insurance sales agents who work exclusively for one insurance company are referred to as captive agents. Independent insurance agents, or brokers, represent several companies and place insurance policies for their clients with the company that offers the best rate and coverage. In either case, agents prepare reports, maintain records, seek out new clients, and, in the event of a loss, help policyholders settle their insurance claims. Increasingly, some are also offering their clients financial analysis or advice on ways the clients can minimize risk<sup>Q</sup> (http://www.bls.gov/oco). In other words, insurance agents are those who recruit insurances on behalf of an insurance company, an insurance agency, or an insurance broker. Insurance agents, not brokers, are the focus of this study. Insurance agents are the first-line employees with direct contact with customers. The performance of insurance agents has a critical impact on the revenues of the insurance company they represent. As the society and the needs of customers diversify, insurance agents provide not only pure sales of insurance products, but also sophisticated insurance and financial planning services. The major tasks of insurance agents can therefore be categorized as follows:

(1) The recruitment of new insurances: The most important task of an insurance agent is to identify potential customers of insurance needs, introduce the best insurance products, and close the sales of the insurance.

(2) Post-contract customer services: The changes of payment method, beneficiaries, and other terms of an insurance contact require the assistance and services of insurance agents. The satisfaction and trust of the insured derived from such services will result in continuance of the existing contract and opportunity of new contracts in the future.

(3) Supply of other information and services: The competition in the insurance industry has become fierce. Providing basic legal, health, tax, and financial information, in addition to the insurance products, increases customers’ satisfaction and the insurance agent’s competitive advantage.

The subject company of this research, termed the insurance company N in this paper, is an international insurance company. The insurance company N belongs to the Asia Pacific group of an international conglomerate headquartered in the United States. The insurance company N offers numerous insurance products including individual life insurance, accidental insurance, group life insurance, etc. The insurance agents have to sift through many different portfolios of insurance products in order to devise a flexible insurance contract that fits the needs of the insured.

## 3.1. Individual differences of insurance agents

The impact of individual differences on the use of information systems has been a longstanding topic in information systems research. Zmud [32] categorizes individual difference variables as cognitive factors, demographics, and personality. The demographic variables that influence the use of information technology include age, gender, training, education, and exiting skills. The research on cognitive factors explores whether individual cognitive style impacts the success and the performance of using information systems [3,4,19,20].

Cognitive style refers to the individual’s consistent preference in information processing [21]. Most common types of cognitive styles include <sup>b</sup>intuitive<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>analytical<sup>Q</sup> types in information retrieval, and <sup>b</sup>fieldindependent<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>field-dependent<sup>Q</sup> types in information processing [31]. The most frequently cited personality factor in information systems literature is computer self-efficacy in [6]. Computer self-efficacy refers to the individual’s level of confidence or belief in his or her own ability to use computers. Research finds that the higher the self-efficacy one has, the more willing one is to adopt the new technology. Moreover, training and experience in using the computer enable the users to adopt the technology easily and successfully [14]. Hence, prior experience of using the computer will affect the cognitive fit of insurance agents in using the mobile PDA technology for their tasks.

## 3.2. The fit of applying PDA system in insurance tasks

The eight factors developed in [14], used to measure the fit between tasks and information technology, include the following:

(1) Quality

a. Currency: The data are current enough to meet the requirements of the task.

b. Right data: Data are used to store the necessary fields and elements for task.

c. Right level of detail: The stored data are maintained at the right level of details.

(2) Locatability

a. Locatability: The user can easily locate required data.

b. Meaning: Each item of the data has a clear definition and is easy to use for users.

(3) Authorization: The users are properly authorized to download data relevant to the task from corporate databases.

(4) Production timeliness: The system can provide relevant information to the task in a timely manner.

(5) Compatibility: The data are consistent with each other when they come from two or more different sources.

## 3.4. Literature review and research hypotheses

Conforming to our research objectives and model, we formulate the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1. The PDA technology is suitable for insurance tasks.

The PDA mobile commerce system stores all information needed to perform insurance tasks. Insurance agents can use the PDA to inquire product information and customer records, to help respond to customers’ needs, and to provide solutions to arising problems during the process of performing insurance tasks. Hence, we postulate that the PDA technology is suitable for insurance tasks.

Hypothesis 2. Insurance agents with different individual traits have different cognitive fits in using PDA technology to perform insurance tasks.

The individual traits examined in this study include demographic variables, computer experience, cognitive style, and computer self-efficacy. The demographic variables include gender, age, education, and position experience. Hypothesis 2 is refined into the following subhypotheses.

Hypothesis 2.1. Gender impacts the cognitive fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks.

Company N has more female than male insurance agents. It is of interest to determine whether gender impacts the cognitive fit of using PDA technology to perform insurance tasks as prior research showed that females have more computer anxiety than males [22]. Extant literature also reports that males have better computer aptitude than females [5,27] and that gender impacts the use of information technology [12]. Hence, we posit that male insurance agents have higher cognitive fit of using PDA for insurance tasks than female.

Hypothesis 2.2. Age impacts the cognitive fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks.

Insurance agents have a diverse distribution of different age groups. Older insurance agents are more experienced and are considered to have better judgment. However, they are less flexible and have lower tendency to accept and learn new technology. In contrast, younger employees have a better handle of using computers [22]. Zmud [32] believed that age will affect users’ computer usage behavior, and Todmand and Lawrenson [29] showed that older people have higher computer anxiety. Hence, we hypothesize that older insurance agents have lower cognitive fit of using PDA for insurance tasks.

Hypothesis 2.3. Education impacts the cognitive fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks.

Company $N \mathrm { { s } }$ insurance agents have diverse educational backgrounds, ranging from high school to master’s degree and above. Zmud [32] believed that education will affect users’ computer usage behavior. Mikkelsen et al. [22] found that people with higher education have less computer anxiety than those without. Howard and Smith [17] believed that there is correlation between higher education and positive preference for computers. Hence, we posit that employees with higher education have higher cognitive fit of using PDA in performing insurance tasks.

Hypothesis 2.4. Position experience impacts the cognitive fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks.

Position experience refers to the length of time one is working in the same area. The longer one works in the same area, the less dependent one is on the assistance of technology. Rowand [26] found that people with less position experience are more likely to use computer and network technologies to accomplish the tasks. Hence, we hypothesize that people with longer position experience have lower cognitive fit of using PDA in performing insurance tasks.

Hypothesis 2.5. Computer experience impacts the cognitive fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks.

The research on computer anxiety of employees by Mikkelsen et al. [22] found that training and computer anxiety are negatively correlated. That is, the more training received, the less anxiety. Relevant research also discovered that people with more computer experience have less computer anxiety [1], and that more computer experience and learning lead to positive attitude toward computers. Hence, we posit that people with longer computer experience have higher cognitive fit of using PDA technology in performing insurance tasks.

Hypothesis 2.6. Insurance agents’ cognitive style impacts the cognitive fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks.

Epstein et al. [11] believed that humans make decisions based on the combined function of intuition and analyses, where the inherent individual differences and the characteristics of the problem influence which decision mode prevails in the final decision. The inherent individual difference means that some people naturally rely on analyses to make decisions, while others tend to rely on intuition.

Those <sup>b</sup>intuitive<sup>Q</sup> type insurance agents will rely on intuition to promote insurance products, thereby reducing the need of assistance from PDA mobile commerce system. The <sup>b</sup>analytical<sup>Q</sup> type of insurance agents will depend more on the PDA system to arrive at final decisions. Hence, we posit that the analyticaltype insurance agents have higher cognitive fit of using PDA technology in performing insurance tasks.

Hypothesis 2.7. The computer self-efficacy of insurance agents impacts the cognitive fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks.

Computer self-efficacy refers to the level of confidence or belief of a user’s ability to use computers. It does not concern what one has accomplished in the past, but it rather predicts what one can accomplish in the future [6]. Murphy et al. [23] believed that computer self-efficacy can be used to judge an individual’s ability to use computer. Many scholars found that computer self-efficacy impacts the usage of computer and related technologies both directly and through other intermediate factors [6,7,16,23].

Delcourt and Kinzie [10] believed that the perceived self-efficacy will be reflected on computer usage behavior and behavior confidence. Levine [18] pointed out that perception influences behavior, and computer self-efficacy affects a user’s devotion to learning. A low computer self-efficacy may result in resistance to use computer and computer anxiety. In summary, a higher computer self-efficacy leads to a user’s higher willingness to use new computer technology. Hence, we hypothesize that insurance agents with higher computer self-efficacy have a higher cognitive fit of using PDA technology in performing insurance tasks.

Hypothesis 3. The PDA technology provides different degrees of assistance to different types of insurance tasks.

The major tasks of an insurance agent include recruiting new contracts, post-contract services, and legal and financial information services, each of which requires large amounts of data support. PDA can assist insurance agents by storing information relevant to the tasks at hand. Different types of tasks, however, require different data support and system functions. As a result, PDA may provide different levels of assistance to the different types of insurance tasks.

Hypothesis 4. The PDA provides different task–technology fit to different insurance tasks.

Goodhue and Thompson [14] measured the task– technology fit by eight factors, including data quality, data locatability, authorization, production timeliness, data compatibility, systems reliability, ease of use/ training, and relationship with users. As different types of insurance tasks have different characteristics and data requirements, the PDA technology may exhibit different degrees of task–technology fit to each type of task in these eight factors.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Research samples

The insurance company N in this research has 18 branch companies and 300 field offices. We took a random sampling of company N’s all insurance agents to examine the factors affecting their adoption of the PDA mobile commerce system.

We sent 450 questionnaires and received 274. Excluding incomplete and inconsistent questionnaires, we had 238 final useful samples. Out of the 238 useful samples, 42% was male and 58% was female. With respect to age groups, the highest percentage of the respondents was between 30 and 34 years old, while the lowest percentage was in the 20–24 and 50–54 age groups. In terms of education, majority of the respondents received associate degrees, and the fewest respondents received master’s degrees. More than half of the respondents had 3–10 years of experience in their positions, and the highest percentage of computer experience was between 3 and 5 years. The background of respondents is summarized in Table 2.

## 4.2.1. Cognitive style

The REI measurement requires only 10 questions to categorize the respondent’s cognitive style as either analytical or intuitive. The cognitive style instrument uses a fivepoint Likert scale.

## 4.2.2. Computer self-efficacy

We use the computer self-efficacy measurement tool of Compeau and Higgins [6] to determine a respondent’s computer self-efficacy, which refers to the user’s level of confidence in his or her ability to use computer technology. The computer self-efficacy instrument uses a five-point Likert scale.

## 4.2.3. Impact on task performance

The effect of PDA technology on the insurance agents’ performance is measured by the respondents self-assessment of how useful adopting PDA technology is to assisting them in performing three major types of insurance tasks, with one question for each type of insurance task. The user’s task performance is measured by a five-point Likert scale.

## 4.2.4. PDA task–technology fit

We developed a questionnaire to assess the task– technology fit of applying PDA technology for insurance tasks based on the eight factors of task–technology fit model of Goodhue and Thompson [14] described in Section 3.2. The research questionnaire was customized by using the precise acronym and name of the PDA mobile commerce system readily understandable by the correspondents. The task–technology fit instrument uses a five-point Likert scale.

## 4.2.5. Demographic variables

The demographic variables used in this research include gender, age, education, position experience, and computer experience.

## 4.3. Reliability and validity of research instrument

We use Cronbach’s a to measure the reliability of our research instrument. In practical application, the value of Cronbach’s a should exceed 0.5. It has been recommended that <sup>b</sup>the internal consistency, as measured by the Cronbach’s a, should be at least 0.60 for a self-report instrument to be reliable and at least 0.8 when used as a screening instrument<sup>Q</sup> [24]. The Cronbach’s a of our instrument ranges from 0.6347 to 0.9412, indicating a medium high to high reliability.

## 5.1. The fit between PDA technology and insurance tasks

Table 4 summarizes whether the PDA technology is suitable for insurance agents to perform their tasks using the eight task–technology factors of Goodhue and Thompson [14]. From this table, the fit between the PDA technology and insurance tasks is between 3.64 and 4.11—all above the median value of 3. The three factors with the highest fit are <sup>b</sup>authorization to retrieve data<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>production timeliness of data<sup>Q</sup>, and <sup>b</sup>locatability of data.<sup>Q</sup> This implies that most insurance agents recognize that they are given proper authorization to access the relevant data for their task, they can access timely information, and they can locate the needed data to perform their tasks. Hypothesis 1 of PDA technology being suitable for insurance tasks is supported as the average values of the eight task– technology fit factors are higher than 3.64, well above the median value of 3.

From Table 5, position experience has significant impact only on the <sup>b</sup>authorization<sup>Q</sup> factor of the task– technology fit, implying that insurance agents with longer position experience believe that they have sufficient authorization to access data. ## 5.2.5. The impact of computer experience on task– technology fit

According to Table 5, computer experience has no significant impact on any of the eight factors of task– technology fit. This implies that computer experience does not impact the cognitive fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks. Hence, Hypothesis 2.5 is not supported.

Our research shows that computer self-efficacy has significant impact on all eight factors of the task– technology fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks. This result implies that employees with higher computer self-efficacy have higher cognitive fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks. Hence, Hypothesis 2.7 is supported. Our research finds that computer self-efficacy is the major factor of impacting the task–technology fit of applying the PDA technology for insurance agents.

## 5.3. The application of mobile PDA technology in insurance industry

To explore whether the mobile PDA technology is suitable for the insurance industry, we analyze the task–technology fit of PDA in three major tasks of insurance agents.

## 5.3.1. The degree of PDA’s assistance in different insurance tasks

Table 6 reports the mean values and pairwise comparisons of PDA’s assistance in three major insurance tasks—recruiting new insurance contracts, post-contract customer services, and tax and legal information services. We find that the PDA mobile technology provides the highest level of assistance in post-contract customer services (mean = 3.95), followed by recruiting new insurance contracts (mean = 3.81) and tax and legal information services (mean = 3.60). The t-tests of all pairwise comparisons are all significant. Hence, Hypothesis 3 (that PDA technology provides different degrees of assistance in different insurance tasks) is supported.

The PDA mobile commerce system is most suitable for post-contract customer services because most insurance contracts are long-term contracts that require various services when the contracts are in force such as the modifications of payment method, beneficiaries, and face value of the contract, taking out loans against the contract, etc. This type of task requires the support of a large volume of documents and data. The PDA mobile commerce system provides the storage and access of these critical documents and data to enhance the efficiency of insurance agents in the field.

## 5.3.2. Task–technology fit of PDA in various insurance tasks

Using regression analyses, we aim to identify those factors affecting the task–technology fit of PDA in various insurance tasks. Table 7 reports the task– technology fit of applying PDA in recruiting new insurance contracts. Among the eight task–technology fit factors of Goodhue and Thompson [14], only the <sup>b</sup>data quality<sup>Q</sup> factor is positive and significant in the task of recruiting new insurance contracts. The <sup>b</sup>data quality<sup>Q</sup> factor refers to whether the data in PDA mobile commerce system are accurate and current with the right level of details to meet the requirement of the task. For example, insurance agents need accurate and current information regarding various insurance policies, their terms and conditions, and premium rates in recruiting new contracts. Table 7 shows that the higher the <sup>b</sup>cognitive fit<sup>Q</sup> of data quality, the higher the assistance the PDA system provides for recruiting new insurance contracts. In other words, the more the insurance agents feel that the PDA mobile commerce system provides the right data with the right level of details, the higher assistance of the PDA system to their performance in recruiting new insurance contracts they report.

## 6. Concluding remarks

Mobile commerce is generally defined as conducting information inquiries and/or business transactions by using mobile devices via wireless communications. Mobile commerce, estimated to account for a sizable portion of the US\$6.9 trillion e-commerce revenue by 2004, is considered the next big wave of investment and application of information technology. Although research articles have been published on the technology side of mobile commerce, there exists <sup>b</sup>very limited research on the strategies and applications of M-commerce<sup>Q</sup> [15]. The objective of this paper is to explore the factors affecting the effective adoption of mobile commerce in the insurance industry from the task–technology fit perspective. In particular, we study: (1) if the mobile commerce system based on personal digital assistant (PDA) technology is applicable for the insurance industry; (2) whether individual differences will impact the cognitive fit of insurance agents’ use of PDA mobile commerce system; (3) which of the three major tasks of insurance agents is better suited for the PDA technology; and (4) which PDA technology characteristic is best for which type of insurance tasks.

To address the aforementioned research questions, we conduct an empirical study of an insurance company belonging to the Asia Pacific group of an international conglomerate headquartered in the United States. As the tasks facing the insurance agents of the case company and the functionalities of the PDA mobile commerce system are practically the same, we focus our analyses on the impact of individual differences of insurance agents and the task–technology fit of the PDA technology in insurance tasks.

Our research finds that the PDA technology is indeed suitable for the insurance industry since it provides a good fit for the insurance tasks. The insurance agents in our study believe that they are given proper authorization to access relevant data for their tasks, they can access timely information, and they can locate the needed data to perform their tasks. Our hypothesis that PDA technology is suitable for insurance tasks is supported.

In terms of the effect of individual differences, we discover that, counter conventional wisdom, gender, age, and computer experience have no significant impact on the cognitive fit of using PDA technology for insurance tasks, while education, position experience, and cognitive style are found to impact certain factors of the task–technology fit. Specifically, insurance agents with higher education believe that the PDA mobile commerce system provides quality data that support their task. Insurance agents with longer position experience believe that they have sufficient authorization to access data. Further, analytical-type insurance agents have higher cognitive fit in the following five factors of task–technology fit: locatability, timeliness, compatibility, systems reliability, and relationship with users. This finding suggests that analytical-type insurance agents will use the PDA applications more often, leading to more familiarity with the system. Among all individual differences, computer self-efficacy is the only trait that shows significant impact on all eight task–technology fit factors. Computer self-efficacy is found to be the major factor of impacting the task–technology fit of applying the PDA technology for insurance tasks.

Among three major insurance tasks, we find that the PDA mobile technology provides the highest level of assistance to post-contract customer services, followed by recruiting new insurance contracts and tax and legal information services. Our research supports the hypothesis that PDA technology provides different degrees of assistance in different insurance tasks as the t-tests of all pairwise comparisons are significant.

Finally, only <sup>b</sup>data quality<sup>Q</sup> of the eight task–technology fit factors of applying PDA in recruiting new contracts has positive and significant effect. The <sup>b</sup>data quality<sup>Q</sup> factor refers to whether PDA mobile commerce system provides accurate and current data with the right level of details to support the insurance tasks. Our research finds a positive and significant correlation between the insurance agents’ belief of whether the PDA mobile commerce system provides quality data and the degree of the PDA system’s assistance to their performance in recruiting new insurance contracts. The analysis of post-contract customer services follows the same pattern as that in the task of recruiting new insurance contracts. For the task of tax and legal information services, <sup>b</sup>data quality<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>compatibility<sup>Q</sup>, and <sup>b</sup>relationship with users<sup>Q</sup> are the three factors that have positive and significant effects on applying the PDA technology. Among these three factors, <sup>b</sup>data quality<sup>Q</sup> has the highest impact.

Our research provides useful contributions to both the academe and the industry. The traditional task– technology fit literature focuses on the task and technology aspects. To account for the case where there exhibits no variations of task and technology characteristics under consideration, we propose and validate a modified task–technology fit model by introducing the impact of individual differences on the task–technology fit of adopting PDA technology in the insurance industry. Our research results indicate that position experience, cognitive style, and computer self-efficacy of insurance agents indeed impact the effective use of PDA mobile technology in insurance tasks. However, demographic variables such as gender and age do not have a significant impact on the effective use of PDA mobile technology. Additional research is needed to shed some light into this decades-old conventional wisdom regarding the impact of gender and age on the effective use of information technology, in general, and mobile technology, in particular. In terms of practical application of our research, we find that the PDA mobile technology is very suitable for the insurance industry. Further, our research identifies several significant factors the insurance industry should focus their resources on to ensure the effective adoption of mobile commerce.

Our research is a first step in studying how to effectively adopt PDA mobile commerce system in the insurance industry. However, our research is not without limitations. Since this research focuses on the insurance industry, our results may not be generalized to other industries that are also amenable to mobile commerce (e.g., logistics, real estate, and automobile sales and services). To gain a broader understanding of effective adoption strategies of mobile commerce, additional research in other industries is needed. Although we employ the task–technology fit model to examine the adoption of PDA technology in the insurance industry because of the characteristics of the industry, it is worthwhile to investigate the effect of other factors not considered in this research, such as organizational culture, environmental factors, and social norms on the adoption of mobile commerce.
