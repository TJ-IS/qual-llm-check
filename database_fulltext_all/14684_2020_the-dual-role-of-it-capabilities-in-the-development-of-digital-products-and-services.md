The Dual Role of IT Capabilities in the Development of Digital Products and Services

## Abstract

Past research on the business value of information technologies (IT) has demonstrated the positive contribution of IT capabilities (IT-C) to product innovation performance. However, with the advent of digital technologies, a new kind of product innovations gained importance: digital product and service innovations (DPI). As a consequence of their idiosyncratic characteristics, the usefulness and validity of existing product innovation management concepts cannot be transferred to the context of DPI without further ado. Accordingly, this study reinvestigates the role of IT-C in the specific context of DPI. Therefore, we begin with a differentiation of DPI from traditional, non-digital product innovation. Then, we propose that IT-C affect the performance of DPI projects via two paths. First, based on the notion that DPI rely on the use of innovative IT, we assume that IT-C directly impact DPI performance. Second, following prior research that has argued that the performance contribution of IT-C is mediated by other organizational intermediaries, we assume that IT-C also indirectly contribute to DPI performance through their positive influence on other organizational capabilities. One such organizational capability that mediates the influence of IT-C on DPI performance is a firm’s DPI capabilities (DPI-C). DPI-C allow firms to deploy their digital resources for DPI purposes and, thus, are an important antecedent for DPI performance. To test our assumptions, we use partial least squares–structural equation modeling technique and analyze survey data collected from DPI projects across different industries in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland.

Keywords: IT capabilities, IT business value, digital innovations, digital products and services, digital capabilities, digital transformation, resource-based view, new product development

## 1 Introduction

In times of increasingly dynamic market environments characterized by rapid technological change and steadily decreasing product life cycles, the development of new products and services<sup>1</sup> represents an essential determinant of a firm’s competitiveness (Damanpour, 1991; Slater et al., 2014). Recent organizational history is full of prominent examples where firms failed to reinvent their product portfolios in order to meet changing customer demands (Lucas and Goh, 2009). Product innovation is, now more than ever, paramount to organizational survival (Zhou and Wu, 2010). This is especially challenging for organizations since with the advent of digital technologies a particular new kind of product innovation emerged. Nowadays, competitive advantage is often based on a firms’ ability to leverage the potentials offered from digital product and service innovations (DPI; Amit and Zott, 2001; Lyytinen et al., 2016; Marion et al., 2015; Nambisan et al., 2017).

DPI are defined as the development of new products and services “that result from the use of digital technology” (Nambisan et al., 2017, p. 224). This includes the use of digital technologies

to either create fundamentally new digital products and services or enhance existing products and services through the addition or integration of digital components (Fichman et al., 2014; Lyytinen et al., 2016). Both kinds of DPI offer significant value potentials for organizations (Antonopoulou et al., 2016). The success stories of video and music streaming providers, such as Netflix or Spotify stand testament to the importance of DPI for organizational success. Based on cloud technology, streaming providers were able to scale their streaming platforms rapidly and, thus, revolutionized the way end-users consume content which induced a general transformation of the entire media industry. In the same spirit, the failure to provide end-users with state-of-the-art digital products and services has plunged even former industry giants into misery—think, for instance, of the bankruptcies of Blockbuster or Kodak (Gershon, 2013; Lucas and Goh, 2009).

Past research on the business value of information technologies (IT) has demonstrated the positive contribution of IT capabilities (IT-C) to product innovation performance (Chen et al., 2015b; Kohli and Grover, 2008; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017; Melville et al., 2004). IT-C enable firms to mobilize and deploy IT resources for innovation purposes (Bharadwaj, 2000). Thus, IT-C allow firms to deal with technological-intensive and turbulent business environments (Chen et al., 2015b; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006; Ravichandran, 2018).

While past research has thoroughly investigated the performance contribution of IT-C in the general context of product innovation, our understanding of the performance contribution of IT-C in the particular context of DPI remains limited. DPI, however, merit a deliberate investigation because they are fundamentally different from their traditional, non-digital counterparts (Kohli and Melville, 2018; Yoo et al., 2012). As Nambisan et al. 2017 (p. 223) put it, the “[r]apid and pervasive digitization of innovation processes and outcomes has upended extant theories on innovation management […]. There is a critical need for novel theorizing on digital innovation management”. Among other idiosyncratic characteristics<sup>2</sup>, DPI are strongly driven by the concepts of convergence (Lyytinen et al., 2016; Yoo et al., 2012) and generativity (Bygstad, 2017; Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013). Convergence implies that the emergence of DPI led to the separation of medium from content and form from function. This allows DPI to bring previously separate user experiences together (e.g., second and third screen offerings in the media industry) and offer affordances that previously required individual solutions (e.g., smartphones

combine numerous affordances on a single device). Generativity implies that DPI are dynamically changing and malleable, meaning that existing functionalities can be changed or additional functionalities can be added or removed ex-post.

As a consequence of the idiosyncrasies shared by DPI, the validity and usefulness of existing theories and empirical evidence on product innovation management has repeatedly been called into question and a dedicated research stream on digital innovation management has emerged (Barrett et al., 2015; Kohli and Melville, 2018; Nambisan et al., 2017; Nwankpa and Datta, 2017; Yoo et al., 2010). However, to the best of our knowledge, there exists only very limited empirical evidence that takes into account the idiosyncrasies of DPI and investigates the performance contribution of IT-C in the particular context of DPI. With this study, we want to address this research gap by asking the following research question:

## What is the role of IT-C in the specific context of DPI?

To answer our research question, we propose that IT-C affect DPI performance via two different paths. Prior research on the business value of IT has argued for the existence of both a direct (Tang et al., 2014; Tarafdar and Gordon, 2007) and an indirect (Chen et al., 2015a; Melville et al., 2004; Ravichandran, 2018) effect of IT-C on product innovation performance. Following their argumentation, we propose that (i) IT-C directly contribute to DPI performance and (ii) that this direct contribution of IT-C is in parts mediated by a firm’s DPI capabilities (DPI-C) which refer to “a firm’s ability to assemble, mobilize, and deploy digital resources” (Wiesböck, 2019, p. 4) for DPI purposes. To test our proposed model, we analyze quantitative data collected from DPI projects across different industries in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland. Using partial least squares–structural equation modeling (PLS–SEM) technique, we investigate the direct and indirect contribution of IT-C to DPI performance.

Our results find support the two theorized roles. First, IT-C directly contribute to DPI performance. Second, IT-C indirectly contribute to DPI performance through their beneficiary effect on a firm’s DPI-C which, in turn, positively affect DPI performance. In addition, our results show that even though both IT-C and DPI-C are important contributors to DPI performance, DPI-C depict the stronger direct predictor. Overall, this paper contributes to the literature on the business value of IT and the understanding of digital innovations and digital capabilities and advances managerial understanding in the context of digital innovations and technology-related capabilities.

## 2 Theoretical background

## 2.1 Digital product and service innovations

## 2.1.1 Conceptual foundations

The advent of digital technologies—which in our days comprising social, mobile, analytics, and cloud computing information technologies (SMACIT; Legner et al., 2017; Vial, 2019)—has led to the emergence of DPI, such as smartphones, cloud-based content streaming services, mobile applications, or voice control services (Kohli and Melville, 2018; Yoo et al., 2012). DPI are based on “new combinations of digital and/or physical components” (Yoo et al., 2010, p. 725) and refer to both “the use of digital technology during the process of innovating” and “the outcome of innovation” by itself (Nambisan et al., 2017, p. 223).

In general, DPI are characterized by the use of digital resources—such as digital technologies (Nwankpa and Datta, 2017)—for innovation purposes (Chan et al., 2018; Kohli and Melville, 2018; Nambisan, 2013; Nambisan et al., 2017). Digital resources can be defined as “[e]ntities that serve as building blocks in the creation and capture of value from information in digital innovation” (Henfridsson et al., 2018, p. 92). Prior research has shown the value of such ITenabled resources and IT-enabled capabilities for organizational success (Chen, 2012; Nevo and Wade, 2010). IT-enabled resources, and the capabilities to leverage them for innovation purposes, offer significant value creation potentials for firms (Mikalef and Pateli, 2016; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2010; Wang and Hsiao, 2014)—in particular in the digital age (Benitez et al., 2018).

Prior literature has identified the following characteristics that make the development of DPI something different than traditional, non-digital new product development. First, the emergence of digital technologies has led to the emergence of distributed innovation (Henfridsson et al., 2018). The use of digital tools (such as video conferencing or project management tools) allows DPI teams to work locally dispersed (Banker et al., 2006; Lyytinen et al., 2016) and facilitates open innovation practices (Choi, 2016). While past research has shown that this trend is true for innovation in general (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017), it is expected to have a stronger impact in the case of DPI (Kohli and Melville, 2018; Yoo et al., 2012). Moreover, it is accompanied by “a shift from predefined set of focal innovation agents to evolving, innovation collectives with diverse goals, motives, and capabilities” (Nambisan et al., 2017, p. 227). Third, DPI are characterized by generativity (Bygstad, 2017; Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013). Generativity means that DPI are dynamically changing and malleable. As a consequence of their reprogrammable nature (Yoo, 2010), DPI can be extended (or “updated”) after they have been initially developed. Ultimately, generativity fosters successive follow-up innovations that were not anticipated by the original innovators and that extend the affordances of the original DPI (Yoo et al., 2012). Fourth, the availability of increasing amounts of data has increased the importance of data-driven innovation approaches (Loebbecke and Picot, 2015; McAfee et al., 2012). Following the increasing penetration of our everyday life with digital technologies, customers become more and more transparent (Morey et al., 2015). Today, the value of data for a firm’s product and service offerings becomes more and more essential (Dinter and Krämer, 2018; Günther et al., 2017; Nylén and Holmström, 2015). Firms can use customer data (e.g., data on usage behavior or customer preferences) to design new products and services or to evaluate production data to raise the efficiency of business processes. Partly or entirely digital products and services can be embedded with sensors to accumulate and process data. What is more, digital tools allow firms to endow their products and services with increasing levels of personalization (Fichman et al., 2014). So does the use of digital design tools, for instance, allow customer to individualize originally standardized products (“mass customization”)—a value-added service which is commonly used in the retail industry (Hansen and Sia, 2015). Similar, E-Commerce or streaming providers use customer data to offer personalized product recommendations (Bobadilla et al., 2013).

Besides the differences in the development process, prior literature has identified idiosyncratic characteristics regarding the usage of DPI. First, the rapid evolution of what is technological and economically feasible with digital technologies leads to rapidly and continuously changing customer preferences and, in the end, decreasing product life cycles (Fichman et al., 2014; Lyytinen et al., 2016). Moreover, the advent of digital technologies has given birth to new customer classes (e.g., digital natives) with special demands, preferences, and behaviors that firms need to account for in their innovation activities (Ebermann et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2013a). Second, DPI let formerly separate user experiences converge – either across previously different media channels and formats, or across previously separate industries, or across formerly non-digital and originally digital products and services (Lyytinen et al., 2016; Yoo et al., 2012). This way digital products and services can be embedded in existing technological landscapes, which leads to the creation of “smart products and services” that extend previously non-digital products and services into the digital realm (Yoo et al., 2012). ## 2.2 IT-C and innovation

Resource-based view (RBV) theory makes resources and capabilities responsible for a firm’s competitive advantage (Barney, 1991). Resources can be understood as a firm’s tangible or intangible assets. In order to be a source for competitive advantage, resources have to be valuable, firm-specific, rare, non-substitutable, and hard to imitate by competitors (Barney, 1991; Grant, 1991). Capabilities, on the other hand, are a firm’s ability to utilize and deploy its resources to create value (Bharadwaj, 2000; Grant, 1991). Accordingly, resources and capabilities are a primary determinant of firm performance and an important predictor of innovation success (Sedera et al., 2016; Terziovski, 2010).

Over the last decades, the IS research discipline has recognized the RBV as a central theory to explain the business value of IT (Melville et al., 2004). The RBV has acted as a theoretical foundation for numerous theoretical and empirical studies on the role of IT in organizations (Bharadwaj, 2000; Chae et al., 2014). Thereby, a firm’s ability to use IT resources—i.e., its IT-C—has been of particular interest. IT-C enable firms to acquire, deploy, combine, and reconfigure their IT resources (Sambamurthy and Zmud, 1997) “in combination or copresent with other resources and capabilities” (Bharadwaj, 2000, p. 171).

Prior literature has conceptualized IT-C along three dimensions that refer to a firm’s IT resources (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011): the ability to manage IT infrastructures (IT infrastructure), the ability to envision and use IT resources to reach strategic goals (IT business spanning), and the ability to proactively look for innovative ways to use existing or new IT resources in order to meet novel business opportunities (IT proactive stance). IT infrastructure reflects a firm’s data management services, IT architecture, and IT application platforms. This empowers firms to create robust communication and integration systems within and across company boundaries. IT business spanning describes a firm’s ability to envision and apply IT resources to support strategic goals and business objectives. IT proactive stance measures a firm’s ability to search for novel and innovative ways to use IT resources and to identify and exploit new opportunities and ideas based on IT. Even though each of the three above-mentioned IT resources is complex to procure and hard to imitate, firms that want to achieve a competitive advantage based on their IT resources have also learned to effectively combine their IT resources in order to create overarching, firm-wide IT-C (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011).

Previous literature has extensively examined the relationship between IT-C and innovation performance. However, the prevailing literature is unclear about how IT-C contribute to innovation performance. On one hand, there is a rich body of literature that assumes a direct impact of IT-C on innovation performance (e.g., Tang et al., 2014; Tarafdar and Gordon, 2007). Another stream of research, however, has called the direct effect of IT-C into question and argues for an indirect performance contribution of IT (e.g., Chen et al., 2015b; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017; Nwankpa and Roumani, 2016; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006). The argumentation is that IT-C have a positive, enabling influence on other organizational capabilities which, in turn, directly influence innovation performance. Consequently, the contribution of IT-C to innovation performance is partly or fully mediated by intermediaries such as corporate entrepreneurship (Chen et al., 2015b), IT tool usage frequency (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017), or the degree to which firms invest in digital technologies (Nwankpa and Roumani, 2016).

Apparently, the mechanisms by which firms apply their IT resources for product innovation purposes are not yet fully understood and, in particular in the digital world, demand further investigation. ## 2.3 Digital product innovation capabilities

With the emergence of digital technologies, the business landscape for product innovation changed dramatically (Kohli and Melville, 2018). Even though IT resources and firm-wide IT-C remain important determinants of product innovation performance (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017; Wiesböck, 2018), product innovation nowadays also requires specific digital capabilities that enable firms to exploit digital resources (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Lyytinen et al., 2016; Nambisan, 2003; Sambamurthy et al., 2003).

A firm’s DPI-C—defined as the “ability to assemble, mobilize, and deploy digital resources for DPI purposes” (Wiesböck, 2019, p. 4)—presents one important digital capability in the context of DPI. DPI-C are conceptualized along three dimensions that refer to a firm’s digital resources: the ability to use digital technologies, the ability to develop and implement digital solutions, and the ability to develop and implement digital business concepts. First, the ability to use digital technologies for DPI purposes is a general prerequisite for DPI (Chan et al., 2018). Digital technologies provide the technological basis of DPI and are responsible for the many properties and functionalities that characterize DPI (Legner et al., 2017; Nambisan, 2013; Nambisan et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2010). Typical examples for digital technologies are analytics and big data applications, cloud applications, social media applications, mobile platform applications, or intelligent solutions, such as Internet-of-Things or smart technology applications (Nwankpa and Datta, 2017; Vial, 2019). To be a source for competitive advantage, firms have to be forerunners and leaders regarding the use of innovative digital technologies in their DPI projects (Nwankpa and Datta, 2017). Second, firms need to be able to create and implement digital solutions (e.g., social media platforms). Digital solutions are based on the innovative (re)use

of digital components and their (re)combination with physical components (Lyytinen et al., 2016; Yoo et al., 2012). Third, firms need to be able to create and implement digital business concepts. Digital business concepts are the innovative IS-based product solutions, business applications, and product concepts that are embodied in DPI (Kohli and Melville, 2018; Westermann et al., 2011). They complement innovative digital solutions and, thus, put digital solutions into specific use cases (e.g., social media marketing).

The three digital resources complement each other in order to enable higher levels of DPI performance. This means that firms need the ability to combine their digital resources if they want to achieve superior DPI results (Wiesböck, 2019). Digital solutions only create value if they are complemented by digital business concepts that specify possible business applications of the technological and economic opportunities offered from digital solutions (Kohli and Melville, 2018; Nwankpa and Datta, 2017). For instance, using cloud technologies and relational database technologies as underlying digital technologies, software companies, such as Oracle, SAP, or Salesforce were able to develop cloud-based customer-relationship-management applications that provide firms with more efficient ways to segment their customer data for marketing or sales purposes.

Despite their conceptual analogy, DPI-C are theoretically different from IT-C. While IT-C refer to the mobilization of a firm’s general IT resources and, thus, provide the basis for IT operations, business processing, and IT-related improvements of business performance (Chen et al., 2014; Melville et al., 2004; Nwankpa and Datta, 2017); DPI-C refer to a firm’s proficiency in the development of partly or entirely digital products or services through the mobilization of its digital resources (Wiesböck, 2019).

Finally, past research has argued for a positive link between IT-C and innovation capabilities (see Table 2). To begin with, IT-C have been shown to support two important general prerequisites for innovation: absorptive capacity (Liu et al., 2013) and organizational agility (Chakravarty et al., 2013). More in detail, a firm’s product innovation capabilities have been shown to depend strongly on its IT-leveraging competence (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006). Firms that more frequently and more effectively use IT tools in product innovation projects are better at the development of new products and services (Kawakami et al., 2015). Moreover, this effectiveness and frequency of IT tool usage in product innovation projects depend on a firm’s general ability to use IT resources, which is, its IT-C (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017). Furthermore, IT-C seem to shape multisided, digital platform capabilities that enable platform-based product innovation (Ravichandran, 2018; Tan et al., 2015). ## 3 Research model and hypotheses development

The literature discussed in the theoretical background indicates that IT-C may assume two different roles in the context of DPI. Accordingly, this study proposes that IT-C affect the success of DPI projects via two different paths. First, we propose that IT-C have a direct impact on DPI performance. Second, we propose that IT-C indirectly contribute to DPI performance through their positive influence on other organizational capabilities that positively influence DPI performance. ## 3.1 DPI performance

Given the importance of product innovation for a firm’s competitive success, product innovation performance has been generally recognized as a critical factor to evaluate organizational success (Calantone et al., 2002; Chen et al., 2015b; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017). In line with the traditional understanding of product innovation performance (Marion et al., 2015; Slater et al.,

2014), DPI performance refers to the successful conceptualization, development, and commercialization of new digital products and services. Firms that show superior DPI performance excel in the adoption and implementation of new ideas and the development of new digita products and services (Hurley and Hult, 1998). They are forerunners regarding the introduction of new digital products and services to the marketplace and the fulfillment of emerging customer demands (Slater et al., 2014). ## 3.2 The direct effect of IT-C on DPI performance

Following general RBV argumentation and the traditional second order nature of the IT-C construct (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011), IT-C enhance DPI performance through their resource subdimensions (Bharadwaj, 2000; Nwankpa and Datta, 2017). These are IT infrastructure, IT management, and IT proactive stance (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011).

First, superior IT infrastructure capabilities increase the superiority and quality of a firm’s overall IT landscape (Bharadwaj, 2000; Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017). Among other things, a superior and integrated IT infrastructure affords superior communication and information sharing and processing capacity (Bharadwaj, 2000; Chen et al., 2015b; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006). In the general context of product innovation—and especially so in the turbulent, fast-changing environments of DPI—excellent information acquisition, efficient and effective knowledge management, rapid information processing and agile decision-making skills are important prerequisites for the successful development of new product and services (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006; Zhang et al., 2013). Moreover, a superior IT infrastructure allows firms to speed up their innovation processes, launch innovative applications more rapidly than competitors, and reduce innovation costs (Bharadwaj, 2000). Additionally, firms with superior IT infrastructures are more likely to provide state-of-the-art and easy-to-use IT tools (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017), which, in turn, will be used more frequently and more effectively by employees (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017; Reid et al., 2016; Venkatesh and Davis, 2000). Better IT tools, then again, combined with their more frequent and more efficient usage, may increase the diversity of generated ideas because they allow the collection and development of ideas from a variety of sources. This, consequently, results in a larger number of new product concepts and prototypes (Reid et al., 2016).What is more, IT tools, such as simulation and visualization tools, will lead to shorter development time and lower launch costs, since such tools allow to create virtual mock-ups and reduce the need to develop

and test numerous product models (Joshi et al., 2010; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017). Also, in general, more frequent and more effective use of IT tools enhances a firm’s overall ability for idea generation, concept development, product design, and technical development (Kawakami et al., 2015). Furthermore, big data algorithms that are embedded in a digital service are supported by high-performance processors that are part of the basic IT infrastructure. Similarly, digital products and services often need to access data that are stored in traditional databases (Horlach et al., 2016), whereas, a superior IT infrastructure can improve data resources accessing of big data services (Zhu et al., 2018). Additionally, firms are required to develop a superior basic IT infrastructure in a way that supports and does not slow down or hinder the digital IT (Horlach et al., 2016), and to be able to run such infrastructure efficiently and reliably at low costs (Tarafdar and Gordon, 2007). Furthermore, a strong IT infrastructure can help bring together different stakeholders from different business units (Chen et al., 2015b) and foster cooperation among IT and business organization for DPI purposes.

Second, a firm’s IT proactive stance reflects its ability to envision and use IT resources to reach strategic goals. Firms with a strong proactive IT stance will seek to exploit existing and explore novel IT resources for DPI purposes (Lee et al., 2015; Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011). Such firms will avoid obsolete technologies and regularly implement state-of-the-art IT tools (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017). In turn, state-of-the-art IT tools likely increase the application effectiveness (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000), and can provide several opportunities for organizations to improve their innovation process (Melville et al., 2004). Moreover, a proactive IT stance facilitates continuous learning and organizational renewal to avoid rigidities that hinder innovation (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011; Sambamurthy et al., 2003).

Third, a firm’s IT business spanning capability allows management to envision how to exploit IT resources for strategic purposes and maximize firm value (Kim et al., 2011; Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011). Such a capability is beneficial to the use of IT for innovation purposes (Barrett et al., 2015; Ravichandran, 2018). This also refers to the integration of business and IT requirements and the alignment of IT and business objects (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011). DPI need to be developed and implemented in close cooperation of IT and business departments (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017). What is more, the ability for strategic alignment depicts an essential success factor in the digital age (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Yeow et al., 2017). Finally, firms with superior IT business spanning capability are more likely to deploy IT tools that are well aligned with particular business requirements (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017). Cross-functional DPI project teams might apply such well-aligned IT tools more frequently and more effectively, which, again, lead to better innovation results (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017).

## 3.3 The direct effect of DPI-C on DPI performance

In the theoretical background, we introduced DPI-C as a higher-order construct that enables firms to assemble, mobilize, and deploy three different digital resources (Wiesböck, 2019): digital technologies, digital solutions, and digital business concepts. Below, we will argue how each of the three digital resources contributes to DPI performance and, thus, argue for a positive direct contribution of DPI-C to DPI performance

First, DPI involve the use of innovative digital technologies (Nambisan et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2012). Digital technologies make DPI programmable, addressable, sensible, communicable, memorable, and traceable (Yoo, 2010). Thus, DPI can provide a much wider range of functionalities compared with non-digital products or services (Yoo et al., 2012). If a firm possesses superior knowledge of digital technologies and is able to understand different emerging technologies and to select the most promising digital technologies for their business requirements, it will likely experience positive impacts on its DPI initiatives (Lee et al., 2015; Leonhardt et al., 2017). Moreover, digital technologies facilitate the emergence of distributed and open innovation (Lyytinen et al., 2016; Nambisan et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2012). For instance, digital tools allow team members to work and collaborate locally dispersed independent from time zone or location (Lyytinen et al., 2016), permitting, for instance, the assembly of the most qualified employees for the project and the extension of daily working time to reduce product time-tomarket (Carmel and Agarwal, 2001). In addition, involving customers in innovation processes allows the customization of products and services according to customer needs (Nambisan, 2002) and has been found to improve innovation performance in terms of innovation speed, technical quality, competitive superiority, and sales performance (Carbonell et al., 2009), and increases overall customer satisfaction (Bendapudi and Leone, 2003). Thus, firms need to be able to employ digital tools and digital technology-based applications along the DPI process (Legner et al., 2017; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017). Furthermore, social media technologies allow firms to identify and target customer preferences and offer value-adding services to their products and services (Dong and Wu, 2015). Finally, digital technologies enable new sales chan-

nels, lower selling costs, and larger product demand (Fichman et al., 2014). For instance, Amazon’s connected dash buttons offer a new distribution channel, and social media platforms, such as Facebook allow data-based targeted marketing efforts.

Second, firms need to be able to create and implement digital solutions that translate abstract digital technologies to specific IS use cases (e.g., social media platforms; Wiesböck, 2018). Through digital solutions, firms can unleash the potential inherent to digital technologies (Chan et al., 2018). Digital solutions represent the innovative IS applications embodied in DPI that provide the technological functionalities to meet new business requirements with DPI (Aral and Weill, 2007; Lyytinen et al., 2016; Yoo et al., 2012). Superior digital solutions allow firms to enhance existing analog products and services with additional functionalities (e.g., smart-home applications) or to create entirely new digital products and services (e.g., music streaming services; Lyytinen et al., 2016). Firms with superior digital solutions have superior know-how and skills in the application of digital technologies (El Sawy et al., 2010; Matt et al., 2015). Thus, they are more likely to develop superior digital products and services.

Third, firms need to be able to create and implement digital business concepts in order to meet the new business requirements that have been induced by the advent of digital technologies (Aral and Weill, 2007; Repschlaeger et al., 2013). Superior digital business concepts allow firms to target customer preferences and create value through digital products and services (Amit and Zott, 2001; Aral and Weill, 2007; Kohli and Melville, 2018). Firms that are able to meet emerging customer preferences are more likely to develop marketable products and services (Durmuşoğlu and Barczak, 2011; Nambisan, 2010; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006) and, ultimately, are more likely to improve their customer offerings through increased customer ex perience, customer satisfaction, and customization (Mithas et al., 2005; Nwankpa and Datta, 2017; Vial, 2019). Moreover, digital business concepts allow firms to reshape their value propositions by creating new revenue streams (Berman and Bell, 2011; Karimi and Walter, 2015). Finally, superior digital business concepts allow firms to differentiate their product portfolios and open the door for business diversification—an important prerequisite for success in the digital era (Hess et al., 2016; Prahalad and Hamel, 1990).

In summary, firms with higher levels in each of the three digital resource dimensions will show higher levels of DPI performance. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2. A firm’s DPI-C are positively related to its DPI performance.

## 3.4 The direct effect of IT-C on DPI-C

In addition to the direct effects of IT-C and DPI-C on DPI performance, we also theorize on the interplay of the two capability constructs. Following Bharadwaj (2000), we argue that a firm’s IT-C are beneficial to other capabilities. And vice versa, IT-C are often theorized to need other organizational resources and capabilities as intermediaries (Chen, 2012; Chen et al., 2015b; Melville et al., 2004). Thus, we assume a positive contribution of a firm’s IT-C to its DPI-C and argue that firms with higher levels of IT-C will show higher levels of DPI-C. Similar to the above, we will build up our argumentation along the three resource dimensions of the IT-C construct.

First, firms that are able to adapt their IT infrastructure are able to create new digital infrastructures (Bygstad, 2017; Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013). Digital infrastructures allow firms to pursue distributed and open innovation practices and new ways of marketing and distribution and thus improve their DPI initiatives (Choi, 2016; Koch and Bierbamer, 2016; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017; Saldanha et al., 2017). Moreover, a superior IT infrastructure represents a firm’s ability for information processing and sharing, knowledge management, and communication (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011). These traits represent an important prerequisite to sense and seize newly emerging opportunities (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006; Ravichandran, 2018), and consequently, identify and develop digital solutions and business concepts (Wiesböck, 2019).

Second, prior research has shown that communication, information, and knowledge sharing between IT and business units in product innovation projects is increased by the ability to foster cooperation among IT and business units (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017). This allows knowledge on digital technologies to be integrated with knowledge on customer needs (Chen et al., 2015b), thus increasing a firm’s ability to meet new customer demands through the development of new digital products or services. In addition, superior IT business spanning enables firms to achieve effective joint decision-making in the shortest possible time (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011). Thus, the DPI process improves and leads to higher DPI-C. Likewise, a firm’s ability to identify and develop new ideas can be strengthened by a shared perspective and shared values on key innovation principles (Karimi and Walter, 2015), and a climate of trust and toleration of risks can draw out employees’ overall innovation capacity (Martín-de Castro et al., 2013). Finally, innovative digital product ideas are appropriately targeted and aligned with business requirements.

Third, IT proactive stance encompasses a firm’s opportunity orientation toward improving a firm’s traditional IT (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011). Being up-to-date with new IT innovations for businesses, finding ways to improve core systems and operations by experimenting with new

and existing business IT, and promoting an innovative culture reflects a proactive IT stance (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011). Firms continually experimenting with traditional IT to create business opportunities and seek new core IT applications may be better prepared to transfer this exploring mode of operation toward digital technologies (Nwankpa and Datta, 2017), and thus be better able to experiment with digital technologies and to seek, identify, select and realize digital business opportunities.

In summary, firms with higher levels in each of the three IT resource dimensions will show higher levels of DPI-C. Hence, we posit the following hypothesis:

H3. A firm’s IT-C are positively related to its DPI-C.

## 3.5 The mediating effect of DPI-C

Business value of IT literature argues that the impact of IT-C on firm-level outcomes, such as DPI performance, needs to be analyzed by taking into account other organizational resources and capabilities as intermediaries (Chen et al., 2015b; Chen et al., 2014; Melville et al., 2004; Nwankpa and Datta, 2017; Ravichandran, 2018). The combination of H1 and H3 implies that DPI-C act as an intermediary of the relationship between IT-C and DPI performance. Thus, we assume that DPI-C mediate the relationship between a firm’s IT-C and DPI performance. Accordingly, we hypothesize that:

H4. A firm’s DPI-C positively mediate the relationship between IT-C and DPI performance.

## 4.1 Operationalization of constructs

Multi-item scales were used to measure all constructs. Appendix A gives an overview of our survey instrument and its sources. To fit the survey’s target group, the questionnaire was initially developed in English and was then translated into German. To ensure equivalence between the two survey versions, we used the back-translation technique. We used existing measures for our latent variables as much as possible in order to establish content validity for our survey instrument. Existing scales were, where necessary, slightly adapted to increase clarity or fit the target context of DPI. New measures were developed following standard procedure for scale development (MacKenzie et al., 2011) and are based on a comprehensive investigation of the existing literature on digital innovations and NPD as well as discussions with experienced IS researchers<sup>3</sup>. Furthermore, in interviews with experienced IS researchers (3) and business experts (2), we tested all scales to secure content and face validity (Lawshe, 1975) and refined unclear items afterwards. Additionally, the face validity of our survey instrument is supported by a Q-Sort among nine IS researchers (Anderson and Gerbing, 1991). Finally, the entire survey instrument was pretested among senior management students and business professionals and refined afterwards.

## 4.1.1 IT capabilities

IS literature conceptualizes IT-C as a multi-dimensional latent variable with sub-dimensions that relate to the different types of IT resources (Bharadwaj, 2000; Chen et al., 2014; Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011). The general argumentation is that firms with high levels of IT-C are expected to also show high capability levels in each of the sub-dimensions. For this study, we chose the operationalization and scales introduced by Lu and Ramamurthy (2011). They suggest to measure IT-C on the firm level as a reflective–reflective (type I) second-order construct (Becker et al., 2012) with three first-order constructs: IT infrastructure capability, IT business spanning capability, and IT proactive stance. Moreover, following RBV theory in IS research, firm-level capabilities need to be evaluated relative to competitors (Bharadwaj, 2000; Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011; Nwankpa and Datta, 2017). Therefore, respondents were asked to compare their firms relative to other firms in their industry. Thus, the items were operationalized as 7-point Likert-type scales, anchored at 1 (“poorer than most”) and 7 (“superior to most”).

## 4.1.2 Digital product and service innovation capabilities

Prior IS literature suggests measuring DPI-C as a multi-dimensional latent variable with subdimensions that relate to a firm’s digital resources (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Nambisan, 2013; Nwankpa and Datta, 2017). The scales for DPI-C are borrowed from Wiesböck (2019) who conceptualized DPI-C along three dimensions<sup>4</sup>: digital technologies, digital solutions, and digital business concepts. Similar to the IT-C construct, the argumentation is that firms with high levels of DPI-C are expected to also show high capability levels in each of the sub-dimensions. DPI-C were measured as a reflective–reflective (type I) second-order construct (Becker et al., 2012) and, again, following RBV theory (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011), evaluated relative to competitors. Thus, the items were operationalized as 7-point Likert-type scales, anchored at 1 (“poorer than most”) and 7 (“superior to most”).

## 4.1.3 Digital product and service innovation performance

Prior literature suggests various measures for product innovation performance (Chen et al., 2015a; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017). Such measures either refer to a firm’s proficiency in product innovation in general (Montoya‐Weiss and Calantone, 1994) or evaluate product innovation performance based on the efficiency of the innovation process and the effectiveness of the innovation outcome (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006). With this study, we follow the former approach and evaluate a firm’s DPI performance based on its general proficiency in DPI. Therefore, drawing on IS development and innovation management literature, we developed a new measure for DPI performance. DPI performance was measured as a reflective first-order construct with three sub-dimensions (Becker et al., 2012). The items were operationalized as 7- point Likert-type scales, anchored at 1 (“strongly disagree”) and 7 (“strongly agree”).

## 4.1.4 Control variables

To control for confounding effects, our study included firm age, firm size, and type of industry as control variables (Nwankpa and Datta, 2017; Ravichandran, 2018; Stoel and Muhanna, 2009). Prior research suggests controlling for firm age because older firms may profit from experience-based advantages while at the same time may feel hindered in their DPI activities through legacy systems or organizational structures. Firm age was measured based on the number of years the firm existed. Survey respondents were asked to name their firm’s foundation year. Firm size was included because prior research argues that smaller firms may have fewer resources available to invest. We designed firm size as a categorical variable and asked the respondents to assign their firms to a respective category based on their firms’ yearly revenues. Finally, we controlled for different industry sub-types. Prior research argues that different industry sub-types may be characterized by different environmental dimensions, which may affect a firm’s innovation activities or performance in general. Similar to firm size respondents were asked to assign their firms to a respective industry category. Table 4 provides information on the control variables’ operationalization.

## 4.2 Data collection and sample

In order to validate the research model, we collected data via an online survey among business professionals in the DACH countries (Germany, Austria, and Switzerland). Participants had to prove sufficient experience with digital products and services and had to be recently involved in DPI projects (i.e., within the last 12 months). The survey link was distributed via mail and had been accessible from August to October 2018. Respondents were financially incentivized to participate in the survey. To control for common source bias, we advised the participants that the results will be treated completely anonymous. Furthermore, we followed Nunnally's (1994) recommendations for questionnaire design to control for common source bias. First, we avoided framing responses in a way that let some responses seem more acceptable than other responses. Second, all responses were designed for equal effort. Third, all responses were carefully worded, and the wording was iteratively refined. Finally, we tried to avoid socially desirable responses. Moreover, to ensure adequately informed and knowledgeable respondents (Huber and Power, 1985), we screened-out participants based on their level of digital expertise and functional allocation. This means we excluded participants from our survey data that did not show sufficient levels of digital expertise and/or a respective background in innovation, research and development, digital business, product development, or product-oriented IS development from the study. Furthermore, we defined industry quotas to avoid industry biases. Finally, we removed unfinished or unmatched cases and duplicate responses from the data set and controlled for suspicious response patterns (e.g., speedsters or click-throughs), missing data and response inconsistencies to improve the quality of our sample. ## 6 Discussion and implications

## 6.1 Discussion

Our study aimed to examine the role of IT-C in the context of DPI. We theorized that IT-C affect DPI performance in two ways. First, we assumed a direct contribution of IT-C to DPI performance. Our results show a positive direct association between IT-C and DPI performance. This implies that firms with superior IT infrastructure, superior IT business spanning, and a superior proactive IT stance are more likely to develop superior digital products and services and, thus, achieve superior DPI results. Accordingly, IT-C allow firms to create superior DPI. This view on the creating role of IT-C in the context of DPI extends prior research on the business value of IT (Buchwald et al., 2014; Melville et al., 2004) and the contribution of IT-C to product innovation performance (Chen et al., 2015b; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017).

Second, we assumed an indirect contribution of IT-C to DPI performance through the mediating variable DPI-C. Our data support this assumption. On one hand, IT-C are positively related to DPI-C. Firms with superior IT resources and the firm-level capability to assemble, integrate, and deploy them (Chae et al., 2014), are better prepared to use digital resources for DPI purposes. Accordingly, in their enabling role, IT-C empower firms to reach higher levels of DPI-C. On the other hand, firms DPI-C are positively related to its DPI performance. Firms that excel in the usage and combination of digital resources are more likely to reach superior DPI results. This extends prior research on IT-C in the context of digital business (Nwankpa and Datta, 2017; Sandberg et al., 2014) and on digital capabilities (Karimi and Walter, 2015; Li et al., 2018; Wiesböck, 2019).

Figure 4 summarizes our results on the role of IT-C in the context of DPI. Since the IT department generally depicts the locus of a firm’s IT-C (Chae et al., 2014), our findings on the role of IT-C can also be generalized to a firm’s overall IT function.

## 6.2 Implications for research

Our study contributes to previous literature in three different areas. First, by investigating the contribution of a firm’s IT-C to product innovation performance in the particular context of DPI, we add to the existing literature on the business value of IT (Kohli and Grover, 2008; Melville et al., 2004). Following recent research that has argued that the idiosyncratic characteristics of DPI call for new research on digital innovation management (Kohli and Melville, 2018; Nambisan et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2012), we reevaluate existing findings on the role of IT for product innovation. The question of how firms can build a competitive advantage based on their IT function has always been a key question for IS researchers and practitioners (Kohli and Devaraj, 2003). Past research has shown that firms that are better able to use their IT for product innovation purposes will show better product innovation results (Chen et al., 2015b; Mauerhoefer et al., 2017; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006). However, although prior studies have found IT to play a significant role in supporting product innovation, there is only limited research on the contribution of IT in the specific context of DPI (Nwankpa and Datta, 2017; Zhu et al., 2017). We contribute to this stream by building and testing a model of the IT–product innovation relationship that accounts for the idiosyncrasies of DPI. Our results show that IT-C plays a crucial role in the domain of DPI, too, and, thus, contributes to the business value of IT for organizations. In addition, prior research on the business value of IT has argued that the impact of IT-C on firm-level outcomes needs to be examined in combination with other organizational resources and capabilities as intermediaries (Chen et al., 2015b; Chen et al., 2014; Melville et al., 2004; Nwankpa and Datta, 2017; Ravichandran, 2018). By showing the mediating influence of DPI-C on the relationship between IT-C and DPI performance, our study contributes to this stream of research.

## 6.3 Implications for practice

Our study advances managerial understanding in three ways: First, our results show the positive contribution of investments in their firm’s IT-C on the development of new digital products and services. Firms that show superior IT-C and, thus, are better able to apply IT in DPI projects will experience higher pay-offs of their DPI projects (i.e., be better innovators in terms of digital products and services). This is of particular interest to managers because firms that are well equipped for DPI are in a position to pursue digital business models (Nambisan et al., 2017) and are more likely to achieve higher levels of firm performance in general (Calantone et al., 2002; Nwankpa and Roumani, 2016). As a consequence, managers should make sure that DPI project teams efficiently and effectively use the functionalities provided by their firms IT departments in their DPI initiatives (Mauerhoefer et al., 2017).

Second, our results indicate a dual role of IT in the context of DPI. This allows managers to evaluate the performance contribution of investments in their firms’ IT function—which is oftentimes reduced to a mere support function—and provides the basis for future decisions on IT budgets and IT investments. Moreover, this helps managers to steer the efforts of their IT departments and organize cross-functional cooperation in DPI projects.

Third, our study helps managers to deal with the increasing challenge of newly emerging digital resources and digital capabilities (Karimi and Walter, 2015; Li et al., 2018). Our results show that superior IT-C may be a necessary, but not sufficient condition for superior DPI performance. The latter demands the combination of superior digital resources with superior DPI-C, which allows firms to deploy their IT resources for DPI purposes. Management should, thus, look for opportunities to invest in digital resources and build up DPI-C. Firms can groom DPI-C either internally (e.g., through trainings) or externally (e.g., through recruiting, cooperation, or acquisitions). Proven concepts for this are, inter alia, the establishment of digital business units, or the cooperation with start-ups (Westermann et al., 2011). Ultimately, since superior DPI results demand the combination of IT-C and DPI-C, managers should look for ways to coordinate and align investments in their firms’ IT-C and DPI-C.

## 6.4 Limitations and future research

Our study has several limitations. First, our data are limited to the German-speaking area (Germany, Austria, and Switzerland). This regional focus could partly limit our results’ generalizability. Future research could investigate the role of IT-C in other regional areas. Second, we examined IT-C at the firm level. Taking into account that product innovation is a phenomenon that also occurs at the individual level (Nwankpa and Datta, 2017), we targeted a sample of respondents that have actively been involved in DPI projects. Nevertheless, future research could investigate the role of IT at the individual level. Third, our theoretical model assumes that IT-C have both a direct and an indirect performance contribution. This is contrary to a large body of research on the business value of IT that argues against a direct performance contribution of the IT function (Melville et al., 2004). Fourth, we used single key informants for each firm, which makes our results susceptible to common method bias (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006). However, we tried to ex-ante control for a potential common method bias through the use of an already established and validated survey instrument and a careful pre-testing of the survey instrument. Moreover, ex-post we found no significant biases in empirical tests (full collinearity assessment). Nevertheless, future research could collect data from multiple respondents to further validate our findings and minimize the risk of common method bias. Finally, we used

established constructs for IT-C (Lu and Ramamurthy, 2011) and DPI-C (Wiesböck, 2019) that have been conceptualized as reflective first-order, reflective second-order constructs. While such reflective–reflective second-order models are generally accepted and heavily used in IS literature (Hair Jr et al., 2017), the separation of seemingly similar indicators on the first-order and seemingly similar dimensions on the second-order level is discussable. Accordingly, future studies could investigate the role of IT-C in the context of DPI using reflective–formative second-order conceptualizations (such as Chen et al., 2015b).

This study argued that DPI show several idiosyncratic characteristics that justify a reevaluation of the role of IT-C in the context of product innovation. Thus, we examined the dual role of IT-C in the development of new digital products and services. Based on an online survey among 177 business professionals across Germany, Austria, and Switzerland and using PLS–SEM technique, we found support for the two theorized roles. First, in their creating role, IT-C directly contribute to DPI performance. Second, in their enabling role, IT-C indirectly contribute to DPI performance through their beneficiary effect on a firm’s DPI-C which, in turn, positively affect DPI performance. In addition, our data showed that even though both IT-C and DPI-C are important contributors to DPI performance, DPI-C depict the stronger direct predictor. Overall, this paper contributes to the literature on the business value of IT and the understanding of digital innovations and digital capabilities.

## Appendix B—scale development of the digital product and service innovation capabilities construct

The DPI-C construct was developed by Wiesböck (2019) following standard procedure for construct conceptualization and scale development (MacKenzie et al., 2011). The authors based their conceptualization of their DPI-C construct on a comprehensive investigation of the existing literature on digital innovations and NPD and multiple discussions with experienced IS researchers, product innovation managers, and digital industry experts. The scales for the three dimensions of the DPI-C construct were adapted from the work of Nwankpa and Datta (2017; in the case of digital technologies) and self-developed (in the case of digital solutions and digital business concepts, respectively). The theoretical domain for the digital solutions and digital business concepts scales was drawn from IS and innovation management literature. Existing scales served as reference where applicable. The scales were pretested in cognitive interviews with researchers and practitioners (n = 5) in order to secure content and face validity (Lawshe, 1975). Unclear items were refined afterwards. Moreover, a Q-Sort among IS scholars (n = 9) secured initial content validity (Anderson and Gerbing, 1991). Finally, the conceptualized construct and the respective measurement instrument for a firm’s DPI-C were validated in their nomological context through a representative survey using PLS–SEM technique. Statistical analysis showed satisfactory results regarding established validation approaches (Chin, 2010).

## Biographies

Florian Wiesböck (wiesboeck@bwl.lmu.de) is research assistant at the Institute for Information Systems and New Media at the University of Munich (LMU). He holds a bachelor’s degree in economics (LMU) and a master’s degree in business administration (LMU). Before joining the Institute for Information Systems and New Media, he has worked as a management consultant with a focus on digital mobility services. His research addresses the management of digital technologies in general and the contribution of IT-based capabilities to firm and innovation performance. His work has been published in internationa journals such as MIS Quarterly Executive and in the proceedings of international conferences such as the European Conference on Information Systems (ECIS) and the Americas Conference on Information Systems (AMCIS)

Thomas Hess (thess@bwl.lmu.de) is professor of information systems and management at the University of Munich (LMU), Germany, where he also serves as director of the Institute for Information Systems and New Media and as coordinator of the Center for Internet Research and Media Integration. He holds a Ph.D. from the University of St. Gallen, Switzerland. His research focuses on digital business models and digital management systems, digital transformation processes and -methods and the management of IT-, Internet- and Media Companies. His work has appeared in international journals as Journal of Management Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Electronic Markets, Decision Support Systems, and Communication of the ACM. Professor Hess has also published in the proceedings of conferences such as International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS), European Conference on Information Systems (ECIS), and Americas Conference on Information Systems (AMCIS).

Jelena Spanjol (spanjol@bwl.lmu.de) is professor of innovation management at the University of Munich (LMU), Germany, where she also serves as director of the Institute for Innovation Management. She received her Ph.D. from the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. Her research investigates individual- and team-based managerial decision-making, the role of strategic orientation in product innovation, how innovation creates value for firms, and shared market knowledge development in new product markets. Spanjol’s research has been published in Journal of Marketing, Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, Journal of Product Innovation Management, Health Psychology, Creativity and Innovation Management, Journal of Business Ethics, Journal of Business Research, and in various book chapters.
