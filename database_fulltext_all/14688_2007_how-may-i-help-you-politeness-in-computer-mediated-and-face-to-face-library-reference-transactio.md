# ‘‘How may I help you?’’ Politeness in computer-mediated and face-to-face library reference transactions

Jessica Luo Carlo <sup>a,\*</sup>, Youngjin Yoo <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH 44106, USA <sup>b</sup> Fox School of Business and Management, Temple, University, Philadelphia, PA 19122, USA

## Abstract

Drawing upon Wittgenstein’s theory of language games, we apply a pragmatic approach to orga nizational communication. The study extends current theories of computer-mediated communica tion, and explores how language actually gets used across media. We conduct a comparative study of face-to-face versus computer-mediated reference transactions in an academic library, and analyze people’s use of politeness strategies. The study observes that people use politeness strategies to play diferent language games across media, and that a dynamic interplay exists among the three constituents of language games: sense making, language use, and forms of life. In particular, we found that: First, people use significantly more negative politeness strategies and fewer positive politeness strategies online than face-to-face. Second, language use influences people’s understanding and precedes practical forms of life. Third, CMC is a diferent form of life than a face-to-face com munication. The new online form of life shapes people’s sense making and the way they use lan guage, resulting in emerging new grammars of CMC. Finally, our findings suggest the interplay among multiple forms of life. This study ofers important theoretical and managerial implications for organizational communication and the production and delivery of services in the rapidly expand ing digital economy.

## 1. Introduction

Communication has always been an important aspect of the manager’s job (Mintzberg, 1973). Businesses have recently begun to increase their investment in computer-mediated communication (CMC) technologies, such as e-mail, short message services (SMS) and web-enabled media to support managerial communications (Bernett, Masi, & Fischer, 2002). Furthermore, the recent increase in the outsourcing of back ofice functions and financial services creates unprecedented challenges to efectively communicate with customers or suppliers through computer-mediated networks.

Past research on CMC has focused on material characteristics of media (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Daft & Weick, 1984), social factors (Carlson & Zmud, 1999; Fulk, Steinfield, Schmitz, & Power, 1987; Markus, 1987; Yoo & Alavi, 2001), the sense making process (Lee, 1994; Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997) and the structural elements of text (Orlikowski & Yates, 1994). While these studies have made important contributions, they often ignore the significance of language use itself, per se. Furthermore, even those studies that looked at the language (Lee, 1994; Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997; Orlikowski & Yates, 1994) have not looked systematically at linguistic strategies that people use when engaged in communications. They typically emphasize only the receiver end of communications and focus on the semantics of language. In this paper, we extend these analyses by proposing a pragmatic perspective of CMC. Based on Wittgenstein’s theory of language games (Wittgenstein, 1968) and Brown and Levinson’s (1978, 1987) universal theory of politeness, we aim to analyze how diferences in language use can vary across diferent media.

In particular, we put forward an idea that language use in diferent media may constitute a diferent form of life, even if the underlying tasks that are performed by the communicating actors remain the same. We explore this idea by conducting a comparative study of face-to-face and computer-mediated reference transactions in an academic library and address the following research questions:

1. Do people engage in diferent language games across media?

2. How do these diferent language games afect the use of language, especially politeness strategies, in diferent media?

We begin with a brief literature review on media richness theory, sense making and structural theories. This is followed by a summary of Wittgenstein’s pragmatic perspective of language as an alternative theoretical perspective in studying CMC. Then Brown and Levinson’s (1978, 1987) universal theory of politeness is presented as an analytical tool for understanding language use. We then outline our research design and data analysis strategy, followed by results of our study. Finally, we discuss the implications for theory and practice, and conclude our paper by exploring possible topics for future research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Media richness theory and its extensions

Media richness theory (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Daft & Weick, 1984) defines information richness as ‘‘the ability of information to change understanding within a time interval’ (Daft & Lengel, 1986, p. 560). According to the theory, communication media vary in their ability to process rich information due to their inherent capacity for ‘‘immediate feedback, the number of cues and channels utilized, personalization, and language variety’’ (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Therefore, a synchronous text-based chat room is considered leaner than face-to-face interaction because it is limited to text as a single linguistic channel. But a chat room is richer than email due to its interactivity and immediacy. The theory has served as a normative theory arguing that managers will become more efective by choosing the right media for a specific task.

While some empirical studies support the media richness model, others provide conflicting evidence (Fulk & Boyd, 1991; Schmitz & Fulk, 1991). Various attempts have been made to extend media richness theory by noting that the perceptions of media are socially constructed. They argue that media perceptions and choice are influenced by norms, others’ attitudes, the need to send symbolic messages (Fulk et al., 1987; Markus, 1994; Trevino, Webster, & Stein, 2000), the critical mass of users (Markus, 1987, 1994), the user’s experience with the media and his/her communication partner, and organizational context (Carlson & Zmud, 1999; Yoo & Alavi, 2001; Zack & McKenney, 1995).

However, both media richness theory and most of its extensions often fail to ‘‘address actual channel efects on communication behavior past the point of selection’’ (Walther, 1996), and actual construction of the media richness in communications. Although the extension of media richness theory emphasizes the interpretative flexibility of media (Bijker, 1995) and numerous forces afecting the choice, they do not examine how the message itself is ‘‘consumed’’ across diferent media. Therefore, both the media richness theory and its extensions have failed to examine the importance of language use in communication. In a way, they are trapped in the image of communication as an exchange of well-packaged items (contents) among communicating parties through a condui (Shannon, 1949), and therefore unable to recognize how the meaning of language emerges and is created in its use (Rommetveit, 1974; Wittgenstein, 1968).

## 2.2. Sense making theories

Among media richness theory critics, some interpretative-symbolic theorists have gone beyond the issue of media choice and looked at how people make sense of the CMC text within the same media. Drawing upon Markus’ (1994) data, Lee (1994) and Ngwenyama and Lee (1997) analyzed a series of e-mail messages on a single issue in order to understand how individuals deal with the lack of shared context in CMC. They consider com munication interactants as active ‘‘intelligent being(s) in a shared social context who can transform whatever ‘lean’ words and cues he or she receives into an understanding of what the speaker or writer meant,’’ either through critical reflection (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997), or hermeneutic circle (Lee, 1994). However, by only emphasizing the ‘‘enactment of meaning’’ (Lee, 1994) at the receiver end of the communication without looking at how speakers intelligently create and maintain the communicative context, they do not examine how language actually gets used across media.

## 2.3. Structural theory

Another stream of research on CMC has focused on the structural elements of texts (Eklundh & MacDonald, 1994; Ferrara, Brunner, & Whittemore, 1991; Orlikowski & Yates, 1994; Reed, 2001). For instance, Ferrara et al.’s (1991) study finds CMC discourse as a hybrid language variety that demonstrates features of both spoken and written language, such as syntactic reductions and the use of initial lower case and parentheses in sentences. Orlikowski and Yates’s (1994) study investigates how people respond to community norms, task exigencies and media capabilities by drawing upon a rich ‘‘genre repertoire’’ consisting of memo, dialogue and ballot. They mainly rely upon the language structure to identify diferent types of genres. Likewise, when studying how the use of quoting mechanisms preserves conversational context (Eklundh & MacDonald, 1994) or achieves sequential order (Reed, 2001), both studies focus upon the turn-taking within and cross email messages. As a result, this stream of research has paid little attention to how linguistic strategies are dynamically deployed within a specific genre or dialogue.

In summary, current research on CMC does not take into account the significance of language itself, or fails to go beyond the semantic level of language. Yet, communications in business settings consist of linguistic acts through which people describe facts, negotiate meanings, create commitments, and perform transactions (Grice, 1975). Some IS scholars have pointed out that CMC systems are ‘‘linguistic communication systems only technically implemented’’ in the sense that they provide ‘‘a means and environment for human communication’’ (Hirschheim, Klein, & Lyytinen, 1995; Lyytinen, 1985). Therefore, in order to design and understand a CMC system, it is critical to understand not only the language and its meaning, but how it is actually used.

In the following section, we discuss how Wittgenstein’s pragmatic theory of language games can shed new light on CMC by capturing interactions among language use, media, and sense making.

## 3. Language games: a pragmatic perspective of communication

Wittgenstein’s later work on the pragmatic perspective of language rejects the idea of seeing language as a static reference to the external object world: language acting like an image of described objects laid over the actual world. Instead, words act like ‘‘tools’’ with diverse functions that we can use for diferent purposes in diferent language games (Wittgenstein, 1968). His concept of ‘‘language game’’ is synonymous with ‘‘way of operating with language in action’’ (Pitkin, 1972). A language game is ‘‘the whole, consisting of language and the actions in which it is woven’’ (Wittgenstein, 1968). As shown in Fig. 1, each language game has three aspects: (1) language use – in the case of library reference transactions, it refers to how the linguistic signals are used by the librarians and clients; (2) understanding of the world – it includes both the librarians and clients’ understanding of their roles, the meaning of reference transactions, and the current communicative context in which they find themselves; and (3) practical form of life (Apel, 1967) – a form of life refers to the coherent pattern of activities or reactions when human beings participate in daily practices, usually within a community with normative constraints (Kripke, 1982;

We also assume that online reference transactions can imply a diferent form of life than face-to-face reference transactions, because the members of the online reference community will have diferent expectations and physical and social constraints than the face-to-face reference community and they may act diferently in their ‘‘library’’ activities.

As shown in Fig. 1, Wittgenstein’s action-oriented view of language suggests a dialectical unity of the three elements which constitute the language game. Namely, any one of the three elements shapes, and is always shaped, by the other two (Apel, 1967). ‘‘The speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life’’ (Wittgenstein, 1968). How we talk is imbedded in what we do in daily life. We learn new words or the meaning of words by knowing how to do things, and by engaging in diferent language games. Language then is not a fixed symbol singularly pointing to an object in the world, but a tool by which we actively shape the world in which we live. For instance, a person who has never used a computer will have a hard time understanding what the expression ‘‘browsing the Internet’’ means. Likewise, diferent languages also imply diferent ways of understanding the world, and diferent forms of life. For instance, a person who regards an online chat room as a private salon will speak or act diferently than a person who regards it as a public square.

We assume that computer-mediated business communication is a very diferent form of life from face-to-face business communication. Online communication eliminates social cues (Sproull & Kiesler, 1986), and relies exclusively on the use of written words. Unlike face-to-face communication in which interactants can access the ‘‘partly shared social world’’ (Rommetveit, 1974) enclosed in the same time and space, CMC interactants are immersed in their own local context with the written messages as the main common resource between them. As a result, online interactants will display ‘‘diferent recurrent patterns, regularities, and characteristic ways of doing and of being, of feeling and acting, of speaking and interacting’’ (Pitkin, 1972). We suspect people may use language and make sense of the situated game diferently across media. Whereas ‘‘mouthing’’ the words difers from ‘‘mousing’’ the words, thinking with the mouth is diferent from thinking with the mouse. Moreover, since natural languages are ‘‘primarily designed. . . for use in faceto-face interaction’’ (Levinson, 1983), predictably new linguistic conventions and ‘‘grammars’’ will emerge in CMC. Speech that is inappropriate in face-to-face interactions may be considered normal online. At the same time, diferent uses of language afect interactants’ understanding of the world diferently, and implicate new conventions or regularities in people’s activities. Therefore, the perceived impersonal characteristics of CMC could be an outcome of alternative language uses. For example, a linguistic strategy, such as joking, that is appropriate in a face-to-face situation may strike people as absurd online.

One challenge of applying Wittgenstein’s theory is how to empirically analyze language use. In the following section, we introduce the theory of politeness as a way to approach language use empirically.

## 4. Politeness strategies: a way to understand language use

One way to understand human linguistic acts is to study diferent strategies people use during conversation. One such theory is Brown and Levinson’s (1978, 1987) theory of politeness. It has its roots in Grice’s Maxims (Brown & Levinson, 1987, p. 4), which are ‘‘guidelines for achieving maximally eficient communication.’’ According to the Gricean theory, people ideally say things that are truthful (Maxim of Quality), that are no more or no less than required (Maxim of Quantity), are contextually relevant (Maxim of Relevance), and are clear (Maxim of Manner) (Grice, 1975). For instance, the maximally eficient way of inviting someone to come in a house is to utter ‘‘Come in!,’’ which is what Brown and Levinson called Bold on Record without politeness redress. Usually it is a performative<sup>2</sup> or imperative<sup>3</sup> utterance.

However, according to Brown and Levinson, communication acts are face threatening acts and interactants have to communicate politely to maintain each other’s ‘‘face’’ (Gofman, 1967) – ‘‘the public self-image that every (competent adult) member (of a society) wants to claim for himself’’ (Brown & Levinson, 1987). For instance, a person being asked to come in a house may feel it annoying because he is in a hurry to go somewhere else. Therefore, there is a need to soften the potential face damage of such face-threatening acts. People often violate Grice’s Conversational Maxims (Brown & Levinson, 1987, p. 94) by using modifications or additions to indicate their desire to ‘‘give face’’ to the addressee (hence the term ‘‘redressive’’). There are two redressive super strategies:<sup>4</sup> positive politeness, which consists of three higher-order strategies and 13 output strategies,<sup>5</sup> and negative politeness, which consists of five higher-order strategies and 10 output strategies (see Table 1 for detailed categories). ‘‘Positive politeness’’ attends to the other’s positive face – his/her wish to be thought of as desirable. Positive-politeness utterances imply common ground or intimacy between the speaker and hearer. The speaker uses such strategies as ‘‘a social accelerator’’ to minimize social distance and indicates his/her desire to ‘‘come closer’’ to the hearer (Brown & Levinson, 1987, p. 103). For instance, instead of ‘‘Come in!,’’ the speaker could say ‘‘Come in, honey!’’ Here, the otherwise bold on record utterance is redressed by ‘‘honey’’ which implies an in-group membership (P4 in Table 1).

By contrast, ‘‘negative politeness’’ redresses to the other’s negative face – his/her freedom of action and freedom from imposition. Negative politeness utterances imply avoiding or minimizing the particular imposition that the face-threatening act unavoidably efects. The speaker uses such strategies as ‘‘a social brake’’ to increase social distance and indicates his/her desire not to impose upon the hearer (Brown & Levinson, 1987, p. 130). For instance, instead of ‘‘Come in!,’’ the speaker could say ‘‘Please come in, if you don’t mind.’’ Here, the otherwise bold on record utterance is redressed by ‘‘please,’’ a politeness marker indicating the speaker’s desire to minimize imposition (N4 in Table 1), and by the adver bial-clause hedge ‘‘if you don’t mind’’ (N2 in Table 1).

We choose to analyze language use through the lens of politeness strategies for three practical and methodological reasons. First, politeness is critical to business communica tion. Business communication usually involves either information seeking or goal achieving, both of which are potential face-threatening acts. Asking for information seeking may indicate an element of ignorance or being indebted to the other’s helping behavior, and it may sound intrusive and imply a desire to impose. At the same time, asserting the truthfulness of an utterance may imply the unwarranted assumption that the addressee agrees with the statement. Often the language used could convey the social distance and the relative power between the interactants. Therefore, the ability to recognize and respect the other’s face is central in business communications. Second, politeness is espe cially important for studying computer-mediated communication. Communicating over a restricted media such as the online chat room may impose special challenges for creating a context of politeness. Such unconstrained communication behaviors as ‘‘flaming’’ have been well-documented in CMC research (Kayany, 1998; Reinig, Briggs, & Nunamaker, 1998; Reinig & Mejias, 2004; Sproull & Kiesler, 1986; Watson, DeSanctis, & Poole, 1988). Third, politeness strategies are ‘‘like shells, no less integral parts of nature than are the substances they cover, but better addressed to the eye and more open to observation’’ (Santayana, 1922). Therefore, the analysis of politeness strategies could potentially achieve a broader purpose: to elucidate how people use language across media.

Recently, the universality of Levinson’s theory has been empirically challenged (Fraser, 1990; Mao, 1994). It has been suggested that Levinson’s theory needs to be examined in a specific type of discourse (Kurzon, 2001), in this case, reference transactions. Furthermore, to our knowledge, there exist no comparative studies concerning politeness strategies across diferent media to date, even though several authors have investigated written business correspondence (Pilegaard, 1997; Yeung, 1997), telephone conversation (Taleghani-Nikazm, 2002), or teleconferencing (Hiemstra, 1982), separately. By capturing the interaction between media and language, we hope to contribute to the existing body of knowledge in ‘‘media’’ pragmatics.

Drawing on Wittgenstein’s pragmatic perspective, we entered the field to examine whether individuals play language games diferently across media, and how their language games are actually being played turn-by-turn. In this study, we focus on politeness strategies as a way to play the game.

## 5. Method

We applied a field study method, where actual use of language was studied over an extended period of time across media. Such an approach is faithful to Wittgenstein’s method of grammatical investigation (Savickey, 2002), which elucidates the complexity and diversity of our use of language through concrete examples.

## 5.1. Research method and site

The data was gathered from direct observations of reference services at a major private university in the US (which is referred to as the Library thereafter) and complemented by interviews. To address the decreasing usage of the reference desk during the past few years, the management of the Library elected to adopt LivePerson, an interactive online chat software. The oficial rollout began in the fall of 2001, but usage of the tool did not actually pick up until the spring of 2002. Library usage was normally very low during the summer. As a result, we entered the field in the fall of 2002.

The software ofered several convenient features: (1) editable canned responses: librarians could use preprogrammed messages stored in the canned response database to reduce response time; (2) spell check and text box formatting: librarians could spell check and format their responses in real time; (3) audio alert: diferent sounds of a ringing bell would ring to notify the librarian that a client was either entering the site or actually requesting a chat; an automatic message appeared to ask the client to wait if the librarian was currently engaged with someone else; (4) multiple chats: librarians could conduct as many chats simultaneously as the administrator allowed; (5) site monitoring: librarians could view, in real-time, clients’ website activity and communication history; and (6) ofline email response: when librarians were of duty, the email mode allowed clients to send questions electronically.

While librarians performed their regular reference work at the reference desk in the lobby of the library, they conducted online reference from their own ofices, between 1:00 a.m. and 5:00 p.m. week days. During the of time, LivePerson allowed clients to email their questions, which were processed by a dedicated librarian during normal ofice hours.<sup>6</sup>

## 5.2. Data collection

Data were collected by the first author in the field over 3 months, normally 2 days a week, except for 2 weeks when only face-to-face transactions were observed, due to technical problems with LivePerson. Initial interviews with librarians explored their perceptions of and experience with LivePerson and their daily practice. The first author sat in the librarian’s ofice or behind the reference desk directly observing the transactions. After each face-to-face reference transaction, an immediate post-session semi-structured inter view was conducted with the librarian and the client. Since it was not possible to interview online clients immediately, a face-to-face interview was arranged within two days to eliminate the recall bias. Two clients were interviewed via telephone due to the dificulty of travel or scheduling. Topics included background information, specific aspects of the reference transaction, reasons why certain questions were asked or actions taken, suggestions for improving future transactions (See Appendix C for Interview guides). Interviews from both librarians and clients about the same transaction helped us to triangulate our interpretations. The first author also attended reference staf meetings.

Throughout the data collection period, the two authors met once a week to analyze the raw data through an iterative and progressive theme building and categorization. Data collection ended when new cases did not give substantive new information (Strauss & Corbin, 1990, 1998), which resulted in 15 chat observations, 14 face-to-face observations, 29 interviews with the clients, 31 interviews with the librarians and related documentations and reference staf meeting minutes. Twenty-nine clients and five librarians participated in the study (see Tables 2–4).

We analyzed politeness strategies at the level of move. Move refers to ‘‘any full stretch of talk or of its substitutes which has a distinctive unity bearing on some set or other of the circumstances in which participants find themselves (some ‘‘game’’ or other in the peculiar sense employed by Wittgenstein)’’ (Gofman, 1981). A move signals a shift in topics or emphasis. The coherence or order in conversation lies at the level of ‘‘interactional moves,’’ not at the level of sentences in isolation (Levinson, 1983). As a result, a move could be composed of a word, a phrase, a part of one sentence or several sentences (Tsui, 1994). For instance, in the following chat room scenario, the client made two moves within his first three turns. His first move provided the information regarding his personal background – his afiliation with the university and his new-comer status. His second move inquired about the semester-loan policy:

Client: (move 1) I am a grad student here at University.

Client: I came 3 weeks ago, (move 2) and one of the things I was told at the desk is that there is a way for grad students to keep books for a semester. . .

Client: if they need them for their research.

Joyce: (move 1) The circulation department handles that type of request. Their phone number is 2222506.

However, the shift of emphasis or topic could be often ambiguous and prone to subjective interpretation (Hiemstra, 1982). Moves needed to be only used as the analysis unit to provide a contextual environment for understanding the politeness strategies. Therefore, the moves were pre-established by one researcher and the inter-rater reliability was only considered for the politeness strategy categorization. We collected 1314 moves.

We developed the coding scheme<sup>7</sup> based on Brown and Levinson’s (1987) original hierarchical category and Holtgraves and Yang’s (1992) scheme. Unlike Holtgraves and Yang’s (1992) study which only concentrates on request strategies (i.e., asking for time, money), our coding scheme also included strategies for asserting the truthfulness of an utterance which is a face-threatening act common in library reference transactions. For instance, a librarian might have softened the force of his sentence when telling a client where to find a book, because there was always a chance such a book could not be found at suggested location, which could damage the librarian’s face of being a good source of information. At the same time, such a statement could also imply a presumption that the client agreed that the suggested book was the book he/she needed. We developed this portion of the scheme based on Brown and Levinson (1987) and Holmes (1984).

To ensure reliability, we tried to use as many linguistic markers to identify the strategies as possible. For instance, the politeness markers ‘‘thank you/thanks!’’ and ‘‘great!’’ indi cated positive politeness strategies 1 (‘‘P1: Notice, attend to it’’), while the politeness mark ers ‘‘please’’ and ‘‘just a moment’’ indicated negative politeness strategy 4 (‘‘N4: Minimize the imposition’’). Two coders followed the double-coding technique (Miles & Huberman, 1984) to examine the transcripts independently for politeness strategies. A reverse fourstep analysis (Hiemstra, 1982) was applied to each move: (1) identifying whether a facethreatening act exists; (2) if yes, identifying whether the move is polite or not; (3) if yes, identifying output-strategies; and (4) the politeness super strategies (positive vs. negative) and higher order strategies were decided by default according to the hierarchical category.

Krippendorf’s alpha was calculated for the inter-rater reliability. The initial alphas for the two super strategies were 0.83 (negative) and 0.76 (positive), and the alphas for the higher order strategies ranged from 0.72 to 0.84 with an average of 0.79, and the alphas for the output strategies ranged from 0.69 to 0.86 with an average of 0.78.<sup>8</sup> They all exceeded the ‘‘lowest conceivable limit $\mathrm { o f } ^ { \prime \prime } \ \alpha \geqslant 0 . 6 6 7$ for being conditionally reliable, and approach the customary level of $\alpha \geqslant 0 . 8 0$ for being unconditionally reliable (Krippendorf, 2004). The judges reached 100% agreement after reconciliation discussions. The relatively low inter-rater reliability for some strategies was due to the large number of choices for the output strategies (Hiemstra, 1982). Moreover, some of Brown and Levinson’s (1987) categories were overlapping. For instance, a person saying ‘‘I simply can’t figure out how to use this map’’ while asking for help could be considered as either adopting the negative politeness strategy 5 (‘‘N5: Give deference’’: humbling one’s capacities) or adopting the negative strategy 6 (‘‘N6: Apologize’’: giving overwhelming reasons). In the following section, our primary claims will focus on the super strategies, and the output strategies will be discussed to provide more richness to the analysis.

## 6. Results

In this section, we first report the general use of politeness strategies across media; we then look at how the three constituents of language games interact with each other. Finally we compare two episodes of reference transaction demonstrating the dynamic process where the participants play language games move by move.

## 6.1. Politeness strategies in general

We observed that face-to-face transactions were much longer than chat transactions, with an average of 64 moves vs. 25 moves per transaction. In the following analysis, we used the percentage of politeness strategies out of the total numbers of the moves by either librarians or clients within each medium in order to control the diferences in the length of the transactions between the two media.

Our results (Table 5) support Levinson’s view that interactants used both positive and negative strategies in both CMC and face-to-face communication. This could be explained by the fact that both information seeking and information providing are potentially facethreatening acts. While they had to refrain from sounding too imposing or demanding, the clients needed to be positively polite in order to create a common ground, which they hoped would help them get what they want. At the same time, while they had to show empathy and understanding of their client’s needs, the librarians also needed to use some negative strategies to avoid being intrusive when trying to clarify their clients’ request.

We observed that these two output strategies consisted of statements that ofer help such as, ‘‘I’ll check the online directory,’’ or statements ofering reason, such as ‘‘Can you hold, I need to get a reference from the desk.’’ Since participants were geographically dispersed, and there was a lack of visual cues online, it is not surprising that additional strategies of this variety were needed to serve as coordinating devices or simple activity reports.

Overall, librarians used significantly more positive strategies in face-to-face transactions and more negative strategies online, while clients used significantly more negative strategies online. Our interviews indicated that the librarians were fully aware of the limitations of the restricted medium and consciously decided to take the questions asked, in Robin’s words, ‘‘at face value’’ (librarian interview with Robin), and were less likely to engage in extensive rapport building positive strategies. This is also consistent with prior research, which has found CMC discourse to be a reduced ‘‘register’’ characterized by shorter sentences, contractions and informal spellings (Ferrara et al., 1991, p. 20). Our study suggests that the major reduction in CMC discourse is due to the decrease in positive politeness efort and the increase in negative politeness efort. At the same time, the increased use of negative politeness strategies as a ‘‘social brake’’ (Brown & Levinson, 1987) could cause the perceived impersonal, lean characteristics of CMC. The increase in online use of ‘‘Thank you’’ and ‘‘please’’ markers may be due to the fact that their meanings were clear and not prone to misinterpretation.

## 6.2. Language impacts understanding and precedes the practical form of life

First, we examined how the language that describes the communication media – online reference – afects individuals in their understanding of the world and, subsequently, their activities. Our data lend support to Wittgenstein’s view that language precedes forms of life. For example, the 11 diferent words for basic color enable an English speaker to see more shades of color in the world than New Guinea natives do, who have only ‘‘light’’ and ‘‘dark’’ for basic colors in their language (Clark, 1996). Likewise, the meaning of the phrase ‘‘online reference’’ helped interactants in making sense of the online transactions and dictates the patterns of their actions or reactions. In particular, our data suggests that the diferent meanings between the virtual world and the material world, and between a site operator and a professional librarian influenced peoples’ sense making and reactions as they participated in reference transactions.

## 6.2.1. Virtual world vs. material world

For the librarians, ‘‘online reference’’ meant fast answers, and they made a conscious decision earlier on that they should not answer involved research questions and should ask the client to come in or call if the questions were too complicated. Sometimes, abiding such rules could become frustrating. As shown in scenario 1 (Fig. 2), when the client requested some URLs for company websites, librarian Mary<sup>10</sup> tried searching Google first in vain. But she decided not to check print sources, because

‘‘To me, chat should be quick and I couldn’t do it quickly enough. I couldn’t go in and found what I wanted quickly. And I felt that I really should have gone to the reference desk to look that up. But that is not the purpose of the chat. So I thought that just by telling him there will be things in the library that would be ok.’’ (librarian interview with Mary, p. 2, italic added)

As a result, instead of ofering help (‘‘P9: Ofer, promise’’), she only ofered her reason for not being able to check print sources by saying ‘‘I am not at the reference desk at this time’’ (‘‘P11: Give/or ask for reason’’). Mary did not want to violate the rules of the new form of life which were delineated by the phrase ‘‘online reference.’’ In fact, out of the 15 chat sessions, the librarians pushed a web page to their clients six times, and checked physical print sources once and called a colleague twice. By contrast, out of the 14 face-to-face sessions, the librarians checked print resources once, called a colleague once, and gave their clients print-outs or written notes of relevant information 6 times. As a result, in the chat sessions that restrict opportunities to give out physical gifts such as print outs or written notes, we observed a pattern where librarians were giving out digital gifts, such as pushing a web page.

However, Mary was ‘‘frustrated’’ by the unsuccessful transaction, and wished she would have used her call-me-on-the-phone rule instead of ofering politely to do a Google search for the client.

The thing is that he is on the Internet. Maybe I was being too polite. I should have said ‘‘you are on the Internet, you could search himself by searching in Google to start with’’. (Why didn’t you tell him?) Well, I guess I thought I should do it for him. Maybe I thought just saying that you do it yourself is not right. I should have told him in a polite way that he should have done that himself or come to the library to look at the directory himself. It (the chat session) was not successful. . . ..Anyhow, I did not use my rule ‘‘please call me on the phone’’. (librarian interview with Mary, italic added)

Often online clients also expected the online transaction to be a quick and easy exchange, and they did not expect the librarian to check print sources. When the librarian did go to check print sources or consult another expert, the client was ‘‘almost astounded. . . beyond expectation’’ (client interview with David). In fact, when librarian Barbara conducted a pretty extensive search online, client Diana was a little annoyed that it took so long:

. . .Especially when she said ‘‘I’m getting closer to some answers,’’ that kind of said to me that we were almost finished with this, although at that moment I remember being a little annoyed that it was taking so long. I just figured this was going to be a quick interaction. Even when I first logged on, I figured this was going to be quick, yes or no. This is another venue to pursue, thanks. I did not realize this was going to be this in-depth and taking this amount of time. (client interview with Diana, italic added)

This finding complements Robey, Schwaig, and Jin’s (2003) view that material work and virtual work should be efectively intertwined in the forms of reinforcement, complementarity, synergy and reciprocity. Sometimes people may understand the diferent connotations the words ‘‘virtual’’ and ‘‘material’’ imply. Accordingly, they might try to arbitrarily separate the physical context from the virtual context and resist crossing the borders. Such separation can help them in making sense of the world and enable them to go on in their language games. However, a Wittgensteinian would not consider such separation fixed and permanent. Instead it is followed, justified, extended, or bent in a social practice of following a rule, in response to expectations of the community and people’s sense making in situated practices (Huen, 2000). It is out of such a dynamic rule building process (which could be painful, as Mary’s frustration demonstrates) that a coherent pattern of actions or responses – the form of life – emerges.

## 6.2.2. Site operator vs. professional librarian

We also found that the diferent meanings implied by the labels of ‘‘site operator’’ and ‘‘professional librarian’’ influenced people’s sense making and reactions during reference transactions.

Nine of the fifteen online clients said that they did not have an image of a professional librarian when they were chatting online. Instead, seven of them had the image of the online librarian as an operator at a call center or typing at a desk in a small cubicle, although the librarians typically held their online sessions in their own personal ofice. A possible reason may be that the canned greeting message – ‘‘Please wait for a site operator to respond’’ (italic added) – employed negative politeness strategy 7 (‘‘N7: Impersonalize S and H’’): instead of directly mentioning the librarian, a third person ‘‘a site operator’’ seemed to separate the librarian from the face-threatening act of requesting the client to wait. However, it inadvertently evoked an image of a person at the switchboard.

The image of an operator also made the clients more sensitive to the language used by librarians that might imply authority (or lack thereof). For example, sometimes librarians had to transfer a request to another librarian with special knowledge of the field. Each time they referred to the other librarian by full name in both face-to-face and online situations. However, the interviews revealed that only online clients showed sensitivity to this linguistic practice. It could be due to the jarring efect of trying to associate the mundane operator with an authority figure. Client David found it ‘‘kind of interesting’’ when librarian Joyce suggested that he contact another fellow librarian Jane Mattson.

‘‘It is kind of interesting that she gave me the full name of the lady, instead of saying it was this other lady, or according to one of my colleagues. . . She clearly believed that Jane Mattson is the authority. I think that is the reason that she gave the name, because she believed it. People drop names when they want to make a point. I know so and so, I know the president. If she did not think that this person isn’t important to contact, or she wasn’t knowledgeable, if this person is similar to her and maybe know one more piece of knowledge than her, she wouldn’t drop her name. By dropping her name, she means that this lady not only knows this information but she knows a lot more than I do.’’ (client interview with David)

A post-session interview with Joyce also confirmed that she regarded Jane Mattson as the authority.

If there’s a tough question that goes beyond what I know about the reference sources, I refer the user to the appropriate librarian. Yes, I have gotten tough questions, I just follow the reference policy to refer them to the appropriate librarian with subject knowledge to answer their questions. (librarian interview with Joyce)

During the interviews all the librarians emphasized their expertise in their own areas. They considered themselves as ‘‘information content managers’’, ‘‘facilitators,’’ ‘‘educators,’’ and ‘‘detectives.’’ At the time of interview, librarian Barbara was even lobbying the Special Library Association to change its name to ‘‘Association of Information Manager.’’

The word ‘‘librarian’’ has such a bad rap through literature, movies and everything. Compared to in the past, we do a very diferent type of service here today. We need a new name for what we do. The work of librarian was very labor- or process-oriented in the past, as far as handling information, which was essentially in the book. Now, it is a lot more management, more analytical. It is a little bit higher level, I think, the type of responsibility we have, the type of decisions we have to make, even the amount of the money in transaction. So for that reason, I think we should call ourselves information manager, and leaving the word ‘‘librarians’’. There is more status in the word ‘‘information manager’’ than there is for ‘librarian.’’

(librarian interview with Barbara)

We can safely extrapolate that none of the librarians would be very happy with the term ‘‘operator’’, which ‘‘has a bad connotation. It sounds like someone who just deals with phones at the switch board’’ (librarian interview with Mary). This echoes the long cherished sentiment that computers are both empowering and dehumanizing (Hiltz & Turof, 1993, p. 149). Our study indicates that an indiscriminant use of linguistic strategies could inadvertently contribute to such a dehumanizing side-efect.

## 6.3. Practical form of life shapes language use and understanding of the world

Second, we compared the online and face-to-face transcripts to examine how the new online form of life shapes the way people use language and make sense of the world. Wittgenstein would predict that we might have expressions such as we now do for temperature, like ‘‘it’s painful in here,’’ or ‘‘it’s painful today,’’ if the world were such that all people in a given region or time felt pain at once (Swanson in Pitkin, 1972). Likewise, we found a different organization of language use, what Wittgenstein calls ‘‘grammars,’’ emerge across the face-to-face and online context. The online interactants relied on diferent linguistic strategies to overcome the lack of social cues: new farewell etiquette; breaking sentences into small chunks; and using deictic statements.<sup>11</sup> We also observed that the form of referencing practice became embedded in an interrelated web of other forms of life.

## 6.3.1. Farewell etiquette

All the face-to-face transactions were concluded politely by the adjacent pair of grati tude expressions such as ‘‘Thank you’’ (‘‘P1: Notice, attend to it’’) and ‘‘You are welcome’’ (‘‘N10: Go on record as incurring a debt, or not indebting H’’). Such cliche´ formulae served to maintain face work, as librarian Joyce pointed out ‘‘when they say ‘thank you’, that’s a good feel’’ (interview with librarian Joyce). By contrast, 4 out of the 15 online sessions ended abruptly where the client either left for a meeting or went of to browse another website, or the librarian had to help other clients. Since all the online clients were multitasking while chatting, either at home or in their ofice, and librarians sometimes had to juggle multiple clients, it was predictable that this kind of disappearance would happen. During the two weeks of technical break down of the software, many times, clients were knocked out of the system without warning, leaving the librarian wondering where the clients had gone. Librarian Barbara did not seem to be bothered by her client’s sudden disappearance because, in her words, ‘‘this is the nature of being’’ – the nature of digital ‘‘being’’ (librarian interview with Barbara). However, for some other librarians, this heightened the sense of lack of closure in online transactions. As one librarian said:

‘‘I am not happy that he just got of. Because I didn’t get to find out whether Investext the report I recommended actually had that kind of data. I would like to have him say ‘oh, yeah, I see it’, or give me his name and email so I can follow up and say ‘‘is that what you wanted?’’. . . It is frustrating that you can’t control it all, they are just gone, [Laugh.] and you have no idea what happened.’’

(librarian interview with Brenda)

As a result, librarians and clients sent out presence inquiries such as ‘‘Are you still there?’’ or absence requests such as ‘‘I need to step away from my desk for just a moment. I will be right back this time (I promise), please do not end session. Thanks.’’

In a chat session when the librarian left without notice to help another client after a few out-of-sync exchanges with the client, the client Ann sent out the following as her departing statement: (Guess I responded too quickly before. ‘Bye.). Ann suggested that she deliberately put her statement within a parenthesis:

I thought somewhat we got rather out of synch. I left the conversation and went straight ahead to the homepage. But before I went, I did send this message in case she did come back and she would know what happened. . . [referring to the parenthesis] I was just trying to make it clear that I was not sending a message that needs response. I was just sharing a thought. You know, like I am leaving now, this is why I think this conversation is over because I think we lost each other. If this is not, when you get this message you will know why I disappeared.

(client interview with Ann)

Obviously, with the improvised use of parenthesis, Ann was bidding farewell to a person whom she was not sure had left the conversation or not. This finding complements with prior studies where parentheses were utilized in CMC discourse as strategies to compensate for the paralinguistic cues of spoken language (the written equivalent of lowered pitch and loudness) (Ferrara et al., 1991, p. 25). In our case, parentheses were used to leverage the storage capability of CMC as well as the discretionary nature of temporal commitments within asynchronous media (Walther, 1996, p. 24).

## 6.3.4. PhDs, professors, and poor man’s daughter

Later studies after Wittgenstein have pointed out the multiplicity and multilevel of forms of life and the interaction among them. For instance, diferent cultures and sub-cultures in the world have diferent ways of living, customs and values. Each of these cultures and sub-cultures are a form of life (Kripke, 1982; Savickey, 2002).<sup>12</sup> In our case, chat reference transactions could be influenced by face-to-face reference transactions, and both of them were embedded within more general practices such as research, taking classes, or even participants’ personal lives.

A detailed analysis of the negative politeness strategy N5 (‘‘N5: Give deference’’) revealed that the librarian’s use of language and sense making was influenced by the client’s educational level. On the surface, the librarians seemed to use the same amount of N5 across the medium: 17 times (3%) for face-to-face sessions and 7 times (3%) for online sessions. A closer look revealed that even though only one face-to-face client clearly identified herself as a PhD, and two online clients identified themselves as doctoral students, the librarians used N5 twice (0.4%) with each of the two self-identified online PhD clients, and four times (0.7%) with the self-identified PhD client face-to-face. Taking into consideration that there were 15 online clients and 14 face-to-face clients, theoretically librarians should have spent only 0.2% of their moves as N5 on each client if they were to occur equally among all the clients. Obviously they spent more than a fair share of the N5 strategies used on the PhDs.

As demonstrated in scenario 4 (Fig. 4), when client James requested some information on mutual funds, librarian Brenda felt intimidated even before he identified himself as a doctoral student. She felt that James ‘‘has a lot of background and he knew what he was doing.

## 6.4. Language games move by move

Wittgenstein’s action-oriented view of language suggests a dynamic nature of language games. Linguistic strategies are deployed, move by move, by reflexive interactants ‘‘on the fly’’ in multiple situated games (Boland, 1996). Our data revealed that the interactants were constantly attuning to the other’s way of speech and deploying linguistic moves to impact the other’s sense making and reactions.

## 6.4.1. Chat session

Let us take one exemplary online session where the librarian Joyce helped Jeremy with his question about the loan policy. (Fig. 5 is a simplified version. See Appendix A for a full transcript).

Joyce opened the conversation by using a mix of both positive (‘‘P1: Notice, attend to it.’’) and negative politeness strategies (‘‘N1: Be conventionally indirect.’’). In his first two moves, Jeremy properly explained his request, sending in small chunks. He hedged the force of his statement by stating it was what he heard, so it gave him a way out in case there was no such policy (N2). A post-session interview revealed that Jeremy’s hesitation about the truthfulness of this statement stemmed from his belief that such a loan policy might be a hush–hush insider privilege:

In response to the question, Joyce deployed the negative strategy N7 (impersonalize speaker) twice in her move 3: using ‘‘the circulation department’’ instead herself as the sub ject of the sentence, using the distant ‘‘that’’ instead of the proximal ‘‘this.’’ As a result, she disassociated herself from the potential infringement on Jeremy if she sounded as if she was demanding him to go to another department for help. However, this seemed to have an unfavorable impact on Jeremy’s sense making, who later disclosed that her lack of personal reference to him in such a matter-of-fact instruction, plus her properly capitalized and punctuated writing drastically increased his perception of social distance and formality. (All the canned messages were grammatically perfect.) In order to drop the formality level and raise common ground, he consciously used three positive strategies in his response: ‘‘thank you’’ marker (‘‘P1: Notice, attend to it.’’), an emotional icon (‘‘P13: giving gift for hearer.’’) in move 4, and small talk (N7) in move 5. Also he did not capitalize his first sentence deliberately:

‘‘I consciously did this: . . . an attempt to try to lower the formality of conversation. It was getting a little too formal for me. . . .It is a common act for me, the Internet things, to print a smiley face, or exclamation points or something extra, when I feel the conversation is too much about words not about your actual interaction. It makes it more human this way when I talk to people like that.’

(client interview with Jeremy, p. 6)

Shortly after Jeremy’s attempt, Joyce did not capitalize the first letter of her sentence in move 4, for the very first time and the only time during the whole transaction: a sign of informality. This is very interesting, because, as she admitted in the interview, she always checked her grammar and was the type who ‘‘dotted her i’s and crossed her t’s’’. She also commented on the smiley face during the interview that ‘‘it was a kind of nice little thing. It gave a little personality, a little feeling.’’

Later in move 6, Joyce’s use of the positive strategy P1 by ofering the compliment ‘‘great’’ seemed to backfire too, because of the formality level that had already been created by her proper writing. Jeremy felt that she overused ‘‘great’’ and the second ‘‘great’’ sounded ‘‘contrived.’

However, during the follow-up interviews, none of the face-to-face clients or librarians ever commented on the language they used during the reference transaction, except one client complaint that the librarian misspelled one word. This indicates that in CMC people’s awareness of language use is heightened and they are more conscious of the impact of language use than their face-to-face counterparts. They deploy the politeness strategies without knowing it in face-to-face situations. In Wittgenstein’s words, the strategies are so ‘‘trivial’’ because they are ‘‘in front of you.’’ By contrast, for online participants, language is ‘‘a most critical tool’’ that should not be ‘‘trashed’’ (client interview with Ann). They are like ‘‘barbarians’’ challenged by strange forms of life (Apel, 1967), which ofers the chance of looking at their ancient way of using language from a new perspective.

## 7. Discussion

In this study, we examined how the use of CMC changes language use. Departing from the current literature on CMC that builds on the conduit model of communication (Shannon, 1949), we focused on the use of language itself across diferent media, drawing on Wittgenstein’s theory of language game and Brown and Levinson’s (1987) theory of politeness. Our results indicate that people play language games diferently across media, and that there exists a dynamic interplay among the three constituent aspects of the language game: people’s sense making, the way they use language, and the practical form of life in which they engage themselves. Politeness strategies are important venues for playing such games.

In particular, this study yields four major findings: First, people use significantly more negative politeness strategies and fewer positive politeness strategies online than face-toface. This also corroborates prior research which has found CMC discourse to be a reduced ‘‘register’’ (Ferrara et al., 1991, p. 20). Our study suggests that the major reduction in CMC discourse is due to the conscious efort by the participants to limit their use of positive politeness strategies. Second, language use influences people’s understanding and precedes practical forms of life. In particular, we found the diferent meanings between the virtual world and the material world, and between ‘‘a site operator’’ and ‘‘a professional librarian’’ influence peoples’ sense making and communicative behaviors. Third, CMC is a diferent form of life than a face-to-face communication. The new online form of life impacts people’s sense making and shapes the way they use language, resulting in emerging new grammars of CMC. In response to the limited channel capabilities, individuals in CMC are more aware of the significance of language in communication and actively utilize linguistic signals to create a communicative context conducive to business transactions: new farewell etiquette; breaking sentences into small chunks; and using deictic statements. Our study challenges the prior work (Hiltz & Turof, 1993) which suggests that face-work is less important in the new digital form of life. Instead of ‘‘letting go of face’’(Hiltz & Turof, 1993), people simply apply alternative strategies to maintain the face. Finally, our findings suggest the interplay among multiple forms of life (Hiltz & Turof, 1993). In our case, chat reference transactions could be influenced by face-to-face reference transactions, and both of them are embedded within more general practices such as research, taking classes, or even participants’ personal lives.

## 7.1. Theoretical implications

There are four theoretical implications of our study. First, the pragmatic perspective extends current research on CMC by looking at how language actually gets used in prac tice. The practical forms of life implied by diferent media, language use and understanding of the world are dynamically intertwined. In response to media capabilities, people engaged in diferent language uses which in turn impact their sense making and actions. The increased use of negative politeness strategies as a ‘‘social brake’’ (Brown & Levinson, 1987) could cause the perceived impersonal, lean characteristics of CMC, whereas individ uals could consciously utilize positive politeness strategies as a ‘‘social accelerator’’ (Brown & Levinson, 1987) to make CMC more pleasant. Walther (1996) has hypothesized that ‘‘hyperpersonal’’ communication is possible with CMC, where seemingly impersonal media provide opportunities for selective self-presentation, idealization and reciprocation, thereby increasing levels of intimacy, solidarity and liking among people. Our study sug gests that a deliberate use of positive politeness strategies can contribute significantly to this hyperpersonalizing process.

Our study also extends the current research on flaming. Kayany’s (1998) study shows diferent levels of flaming across diferent social groups. Our study suggests that this is due to the fact that people from diferent forms of cultural life have diferent understandings of what constitutes civic life and utilize diferent politeness strategies accordingly. This pragmatic perspective departs from both media richness theory, which assumes that media capacity to convey meaning is technological predetermined (Daft & Lengel, 1984, 1986), and its critics who assume that communication contexts which are believed to expand the media’s capacity to convey complex message are given a priori (Carlson & Zmud, 1999; Yoo & Alavi, 2001; Zack & McKenney, 1995). Contrary to these views, we found that individuals actively construct communicative contexts through their use of language, and their politeness strategy is an integral part of this process. Traditional CMC models often assume that language use does not change across the media and attempts to compare the impact of diferent media assuming the language itself remains constant. Our results suggest that when individuals engage in a diferent form of life as they move from the face-to-face environment to an on-line virtual world, they change their use of language. Future research on computer-mediated communication should thus pay closer attention to the interplay among the media, forms of life and the language use.

Second, the pragmatic perspective is consistent with structuration theorists (Giddens, 1986) who argue that people actively enact the social structures which in turn constrain their acts. Linguistic strategies are both the medium and outcome of human interactions. Like Pentland’s (1992) ‘‘organizing moves,’’ they connect the actions and structures, and may help addressing the dificulty of operationalizing Gidden’s theory (Rose & Scheepers, 2001). Unlike extant sense making theories (Lee, 1994; Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997) and structural theories (Eklundh & MacDonald, 1994; Ferrara et al., 1991; Orlikowski & Yates, 1994; Reed, 2001), which focus on the semantic level of language, this perspective enables us to look at how people actually use language across media. We found that the linguistic strategies that people use contribute to the hermeneutical construction of the virtual world.

Third, the pragmatic perspective also sheds light on business communication as a facethreatening linguistic act. Brown and Levinson’s (1987) universal theory of politeness holds true only in the sense that both face-to-face and CMC interactants engage in both positive and negative strategies. However, Wittgenstein’s action-oriented view of language suggests that linguistic strategies are deployed, move by move, by reflexive interactants ‘‘on the fly’’ in situated language games (Boland, 1996). We found that the actual deployment of the strategies changes across diferent media, and that there is a dynamic interplay among the form of life, language use and people’s understanding of the world. When confronted with a new form of life brought about by the online media, people find their understanding of politeness conventions challenged and find themselves engaged in new linguistic strategies.

Finally, our study corroborates Robey et al.’s (2003) proposition that contemporary work often consists of a dual ontology: material reality ‘‘intertwining’’ with virtual reality. We suggest that such ontological diferences are partially achieved by the diferences in the language use. Wittgenstein (1921–1922) once noted that ‘‘the limits of my language means the limits of my world.’’ Engaging in diferent language use across medium, people are actively constructing the diferent worlds through their language games. At the same time, they have to face the challenge of making sense of the unfolding situation and maintaining language conventions in both worlds. This observation challenges the existing way we study virtualization. The IS literature often regards the virtual world as ‘‘an electronic copy or analogue of the material world’’ (Robey et al., 2003, p. 116). They often treat virtual organization as nothing more than a mere copy of traditional organizations, except with more spatial and temporal distribution (Robey et al., 2003). Instead, our study suggests that virtual world is ontologically and epistemologically diferent from material world, but yet just as real as the material world. Our study shows that some words from the material world move into the virtual world with minimum changes in their meanings. Some words take new meaning as they transfer into the virtual world. And, sometimes, we see the emergence of new words in the virtual world. It is these uses of language that intertwine virtual and material worlds. As our language use of the material world is often shaped by our existential experiences (Lakof & Johnson, 1980), the transition of language from the material world to the virtual one can be ironic and problematic. It is through the attempts to repair such problems in language use that new language games for the virtual world seem to emerge. More careful examination of the language use in virtual world, therefore, will help us to better theorize how virtual and real worlds are intertwined, and help us to examine the virtual world as real but novel experiences of our existence.

## 7.2. Managerial implications

Our findings have several implications for organization and systems design. First, managers must address the relationship between material and virtual representations of work practices (Robey et al., 2003). Organizations should not design their virtual work as an exact copy, or substitute, of the material work. As our study shows, online reference trans actions can not answer the same kinds of questions in the same way as live librarians can at the reference desk. Instead, organizations need to create a seamless integration of material and virtual work where a CMC system is embedded in the natural working environment. As in our case, chat reference is ‘‘complemented’’ (Robey et al., 2003) by phone, email, or live reference. At the same time, providing chat reference at the front desk where librarians could easily assess print reference materials might create ‘‘synergy’’ between material and virtual work. More importantly, a failure to translate the appreciation of the ‘‘dual ontology’’ (Robey et al., 2003) of work practices into their pragmatic consequences could prove detrimental. For instance, a national survey reports an ‘‘odd’’ and ‘‘questionable’’ practice where libraries are collecting a significant amount of unnecessary personal information through their online services, even though such information has little or no bearing on answering questions (White, 2001). Our study suggests that such missteps could be due to an attempt to overcome the restrictive nature of online media to create a more isomorphic representation of the material world without considering its pragmatic significance. It is easy to gather much personal information without asking in face-to-face situations, but it is problematic to ask about it online.

Second, when limited to a restricted electronic communication technology, managers should pay keener attention to linguistic strategies to create efective communication for business transactions. In today’s world, companies are increasingly outsourcing help desks, back ofice functions and financial services overseas, or using virtual communities to build informational and marketing relationships with their customers. Some companies are equipping their ofshore call-center operations with ‘‘accent neutralization’’ training (Hayes, 2003). However, our study shows that the efectiveness of computer-mediated communication, or trust in the virtual world, involves more complex issues than a good accent. It is a mindful merging of people’s understanding of the world, the form of life in which they engage themselves and an active deployment of linguistic strategies. At the same time, some software development companies have noticed that the application interfaces designed by their ofshore Indian programmers are not user friendly (Matlof 2004), while Western vendors’ penetration into the Chinese ERP market is sometimes marred with poor language translation, and awkward reporting format (Liang, Xue,

Boulton, & Byrd, 2004). Our theoretical framework indicates this may result from the fact that people from diferent cultures communicate with the application interfaces diferently when engaging in language games.

Third, this study also has some implications for systems design. New standards could be established to address the issue of ‘‘closure’’ within the CMC context, such as the goodbye function and the dynamic update message capability in MSN Messenger (i.e. Person X is typing a message); a picture of a person smiling could serve as a good social accelerator for CMC; and an appropriate canned message may not be the one properly capitalized and punctuated.

Finally, a Wittgensteinian approach suggest several ways in which diferent language use can help making encounters feel like relationships or ‘‘enhanced encounters’’ (Gutek, Groth, & Cherry, 2002). For example, it is critical to give service providers appropriate titles, since titles such as ‘‘operator’’ have diferent reputational connotation than titles such as ‘‘librarian’’. Messages rich in style and sensitive to the ongoing situation can also make an encounter more personalized. Finally, breaking sentences into small chunks gives conversation a more dynamic feel and shorten the perceived waiting time. These insights can be incorporated in corporate training in order to make their service communication with customers can be more efective in an on-line environment.

## 7.3. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the presence of the researcher inevitably altered the communicative contexts. It was no surprise that two of the chat room clients actually said hello to the ‘‘fly-on-the-wall’’ researcher during the reference transaction, while many of them admitted in the later interview that they had a mental image of the researcher sitting side-by-side with the librarian. Second, the study did not look at other contextual variables such as personal or task complexity. For instance, whereas the Chinese often speak indirectly trying to be polite, English-speaking Americans often assume positive (‘‘solidarity’’) politeness (Brown & Levinson, 1987). Third, the study was restricted to a one-time interaction between people with minimum group history. We believe that language games will change as people become more accustomed to the technology.

## 8. Conclusion

The pragmatic view of organizational communication could open up new venues for studying emerging challenges that companies face in the digital economy. We have studied a boundary spanning function in which providers deliver information and services to customers through a new digital medium. Our study suggests that the new digital form of service delivery changes the very nature of service work. Online transactions induce more of a ‘‘service encounter’’ between strangers, while face-to-face transactions induce more of a ‘‘service relationship’’ where a customer and provider know each other personally (Gutek, Bhappu, Liao-Troth, & Cherry, 1999, 2002). As more companies turn to electronic media to provide information and service both internally and externally, it becomes increasingly critical to understand how the use of new forms of digital tools to mediate our relationships fundamentally reshape the meaning of social relationships. A pragmatic perspective on language ofers three ways to approach this important problem. First, we need to understand how to efectively intertwine these two forms of service deliveries efectively, instead of making an isomorphic electronic copy of face-to-face service. We can look at how diferent language use help making encounters feel like relationships or ‘‘enhanced encounters’’ (Gutek et al., 2002). Second, we should not limit our focus on the commonly used output measures such as accessibility, availability, price, satisfaction, or use frequencies, nor should we solely focus on the provider strategies. Instead, we will need to treat services as a process where both providers and customers co-produce and co-deliver the service by reflexively engaging in language games on the fly. Third, in addition to focusing on the service transaction on hand, we need to study other practices in which it is nested. If we are to study the relationship between a Western ERP vendor and Chinese companies, for example, we will need to study China’s unique accounting and financial systems, and local purchasing practices.

Overall, the pragmatic view of organizational communication opens the door to a wealth of evidence and of ideas relating emerging organizational forms and individual behavior.

## Appendix C. Interview guides

## Background interview script (Librarian)

The following interview script will be administered to librarians in a semi-structured format.

1. Tell me about what brought you to XYZ Library?

2. How did you get involved with the LivePerson reference project?

3. What skills do you bring to the XYZ Library and the current project?

4. What is your role as a librarian in general? As a LivePerson librarian?

5. What do you see as the objective of the LivePerson reference project?

6. How do you see yourself working with other librarians on this project?

7. What is your best experience of working with a client face to face?

8. What is your best experience of working with a client online?

9. In your opinion, what are the characteristics of a successful reference interview with a client?

10. Tell me candidly, how you see yourself as a professional reference librarian?

## Interim questionnaire and interview script (Librarian)

The following questions will be asked in a semi-structured interview manner. These questions will only be asked occasionally in order to obtain the perspective of the subject on the progress of the LiverPerson reference project and their growth and performance. Interim questions will be kept to a minimum in order to reducing influencing the action of librarians. Sample questions include:

1. How do you think the LivePerson reference project is going?

2. What does this Æaspect of projectæ mean to you?

3. How and when did you find out about Æaspect of projectæ?

4. Why did you discuss this Æaspect of projectæ?

## Post-study project evaluation interview script (Librarian)

Following the project observation stage, the researcher will interview the participating librarians in an attempt to obtain consensus as to what was the lessons learned from their involvement with the LivePerson reference project. Sample semi-structured questions include:

1. What do you think was the most important lessons you’ve learned from your experience with LivePerson reference in the last few months?

2. What advice would do you give the designers of a digital reference system?

The following interview script will be administered to librarians in a semi-structured format. Sample questions include:
