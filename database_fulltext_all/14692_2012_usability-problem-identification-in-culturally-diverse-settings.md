# Usability problem identification in culturally diverse settings

Torkil Clemmensen

Department of Informatics, Copenhagen Business School, Howitzvej 60, Denmark, email: Tc.inf@cbs.dk

Abstract. There are indications that established methods for evaluating information system usability that have been developed for use in, e.g. Europe or the USA, fail to give reliable results in countries such as India, China or Malaysia. This paper presents the theoretical background, related work and a definition of culture that should be useful for studies of multiple-country usability testing. This includes a discussion of cultural fit and the consequences of cultural (in)consistencies between stakeholders in system development and use. As an illustrative example of the kind of academic research that needs to be done, a pilot study is described. The pilot study exemplifies themes to explore, who should be participants and where should the study be done, how to find examples of multiple-country usability testing, how to collect data and how to analyse that data and what kind of results and discussion of results that may be expected. The conclusion summarises the paper, discusses the limitations of the pilot study and gives detailed suggestions to future research.

Keywords: IS cultural models, usability problems, usability testing, usability pro fessionals, cross-cultural research

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Increasingly, the global production and use of technologically advanced information and communication applications need to be based on an improved understanding of the diversity of user groups around the world. In this paper, we explore the potential influence of national culture on the evaluation of information system (IS) usability, illustrate the discussion with a pilot study and suggest how we could further study this issue. The aim is to find valid and practical ways of evaluating design from a user perspective cross culturally. We argue that culture is important not only in the design of user interfaces but also for the user-oriented methods that we use for design.

Recent developments in cross-cultural psychology have shed light on the limited universality of basic cognitive processes and made it clear that diversity in intellectual traditions and cognitive styles introduces a hitherto unknown bias in basic cognitive processes across different regions of the world (Witkin & Berry, 1975; Bond, 1986; Nisbett, 2003).

In the area of human–computer interaction (HCI), cultural aspects of usability evaluation and usability have received increasing attention (see e.g. Yeo, 1998; Chavan et al., 2009; Dray & Siegel, 2005; Vatrapu & Pérez-Quiñones, 2006; Kurosu, 2008; Clemmensen, 2011; Smith, 2011). There is now a call for new paradigms for HCI such as ‘cultural computing’ (Rauterberg, 2006), ‘culturally sensitive IT’ (Zakaria & Stanton, 2003) and ‘cultural usability’ (Barber & Badre, 1998; Vöhringer-Kuhnt, 2002; Sun, 2003; Noiwan & Norcio, 2006).

Within IS research, studies on the influence of culture on HCI have appeared, e.g. Ford & Gelderblom’s (2003) study of cultural dimensions and human performance with IS. There is increased interest in the influence of culture on the development and use of information technology (IT). Existing IS models, such as the technology acceptance model, have been found to be less valid when tested in countries outside the USA and Europe (Al-Gahtani et al., 2007; Bandyopadhyay & Fraccastoro, 2007; McCoy et al., 2007). There are indications that this is also true for how IS design is conducted (Pauleen et al., 2006), including cultural differences in the readiness to report bad news in software development projects (Keil et al., 2007), behaviour misalignment evident in cross-cultural communications and team-interaction processes during requirement engineering such as user acceptance tests (Hanisch & Corbitt, 2007), and problems of mismatch in social status and cognitive style between evaluator and test user in cross-cultural usability testing (Clemmensen et al., 2008).

The influence of culture on development and use of IT has been discussed in three recent reviews of IS research. The first of these reviews was a citation analysis of the use of Hofstede’s cultural dimensions in 57 IS research papers by Ford et al. (2003) in which it was shown that compared with issues related to culture and managerial aspects of IS, issues related to culture and IS development and use remain relatively unexamined. The second review was by Leidner & Kayworth (2006) who carried out a review of 82 papers that together represented a diversity in the use of research methods. The results showed that the cultura values of system designers influence their position towards development methodologies and end users; furthermore, the results overwhelmingly showed that differences in culture result in differences in use and outcomes of IS.

When looking for explanations of the findings, Leidner & Kayworth (2006) noticed the popularity of the notion of cultural fit. The cultural fit hypothesis is that the level of congruence between a group’s values and values embedded in a system will determine the group’s perception and use of that system. Thus, a mismatched cultural fit will lead to a negative perception and use of the system, while a high degree of fit will lead to positive perceptions and use of the system.

The third review was by Kappos & Rivard (2008) in which the authors reviewed 56 papers. They found, first, that from an integration point of view of culture, a high consistency between developers’ values and values built into development methodologies, and similarly, a high consistency between users’ values and the values built into the IS, predicted appropriate cultural patterns of use. Second, they found that from a differentiation point of view on culture, cultural value inconsistencies generate tension between different groups of developers stakeholders in a project and with respect to users, generate unpredicted and sometimes culturally new patterns of IS use in order to resolve the inconsistencies.

In this paper, we take up the discussion of cultural fit and the consequences of cultural (in)consistencies between stakeholders in system development and use. With a pilot study, we begin the analysis and discuss the practice of usability testing of IS designs in multiple geographical locations. Inspired by studies of usability testing in practice (Dray & Siegel, 2005; Shi, 2008), we suggest to study whether it makes a qualitative difference in the evaluation results when the evaluator has a cultural background that is consistent with that of the test user compared with whether the evaluator has a cultural background that is inconsistent with that of the test user. This question has theoretical implications for established methods of evaluating IS design from a user perspective as to date, cultural aspects of usability evaluation methods have not been demonstrated in qualitative studies in the IS literature. An empirical demonstration of possible cultural influences in usability evaluation will be useful knowledge for IS practitioners. They are frequently faced with difficult choices in international usability testing between testing localised versions of software with target users or non-target users, testing with local evaluators or non-local, foreign evaluators, remote testing and testing in face-to-face situations in real contexts, etc. In some situations it might make sense to send foreign evaluators to a local country to carry out usability tests because that would ease the communication of the result to the foreign software developers who develop the application. Much of the software that is used by office workers in many countries has actually been developed and tested by foreign developers. This practice for evaluating the usability of IS appears to be worth investigating.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. First, I present the theoretical back ground, related work and the definition of culture that is suggested in this paper. Then, I present a pilot study to provide an example of the kind of questions that we may consider when we want to study the practice of multiple-country usability evaluation. This includes which themes to explore, who should be participants and where should the study be done, how to find examples of multiple-country usability testing to study, how to collect data and how to analyse data and what kind of results can we expect. In the conclusion, I summarise the paper, point out the many limitations of the pilot study and give detailed suggestions for future research studies.

## 1.1 Background, related work and theoretical framework

## 1.1.1 Culture defined as values

The background for many studies of cultural aspects of IT has been Hofstede’s national cultural dimensions, i.e. power distance, individualism–collectivism, masculinity–feminism and uncertainty avoidance (Hofstede, 1980; Vatrapu & Suthers, 2007). Particularly in IS research, dimensions of nationally shared cultural values have been the dominant concepts of cultures, which is evident from the three review studies discussed earlier. The first review by Ford et al. (2003) showed that Hofstede’s cultural value dimension framework was cited in 22% of global IS research papers. The second review by Leidner & Kayworth (2006) defined and developed a value-based approach to the study of IT and culture. The third review by Kappos & Rivard (2008) applied a definition of culture as patterns of meaning that link visible manifestations (artefacts and practices) to ideational manifestations (beliefs. assumptions and values) of the everyday functioning of a group of actors. The preference for seeing values as central to culture is not limited to IS research. Most culture and design theorists, many professiona designers across all disciplines and some users believe that national cultural value dimensions pervade every human activity and every artefact, including user interfaces (Marcus, 2002).

## 1.1.2 Culture defined as representations in situations

Opponents of the cultural value dimension approach argue that the current process for the design of universally useable systems is not appropriate because of its overdependence on guidelines, the difficulty of determining the user from the present cultural grounds, its tendency to build stereotypes, which later become design rules, and its treatment of different cultures with one specific language that does not take into account cultural heterogeneity. Instead, these researchers see culturally determined usability problems in interfaces as caused by the users’ (mis)understandings of the representations whose meaning lie in the culture-specific contexts (Bourges-Waldegg & Scrivener, 1998).

The culture-as-representation view of culture and usability is related to social psychologica approaches to culture that take into account the establishment of social facts and peoples sense of the reality of social groups due to their use of symbols to construct their social reality; these are processes that are firmly related to culture and communication (Kashima, 2004). Also related to culture-as-representation is the situation-specific approach to culture. Hong & Mallorie (2004) conceptualise culture as a loose network of domain-specific cognitive meaning systems (including theories, beliefs) and argue that individuals frequently hold more than one cultural meaning system even if the systems contain conflicting cultural knowledge. According to situation-specific theories, the meaning creation that will happen in a situation depends on which knowledge is accessible, available and appropriate in that situation. When combined, the culture-as-representation view and the situation-specific approaches explain cultura usability as a psychological outcome of applying cultural models of technology use in concrete situations. I call this the ‘cultural model approach to usability’ (Clemmensen, 2009).

## 1.1.3 The definition of culture in this study

In the ‘cultural model of use’ approach to usability that I use in this paper, the relation between the internal cultural models of technology use (cognitive, psychological: to write a letter, do so and so) and the external artefact cultural models (how to use this product) are considered to be mutually dependent (one makes no sense without the other). In contrast, the usability evaluation model (how to do a usability evaluation) functions more similar to a loosely coupled mediator that creates the perception of a specific set of usability problems. The evaluatormoderated, usability evaluation-enabled communication about the references between the user’s expectations of a technology and the specific artefact mediates the psychological sense of usability and produces the list of usability problems. Because this process is dynamic and varies with the situation, it can not simply be predicted from a list of cultural value variables or cultural models but needs to be explored to see what is produced in concrete situations. The list of usability problems produced when testing in a new or cross-cultural context may not necessarily be similar to the typical usability problems found by using established usability evaluation methods in known cultural contexts. Thus, there may be identified qualitatively different usability problems in usability tests in different countries.

## 1.1.4 Classical usability testing

From the debate on international usability tests, it can be concluded that to locally adapt user interfaces, we must use local user populations and classical usability engineering methods similar to those used in the development of the original user interface (Nielsen, 1990; Molich et al., 2004). The classical cognitive account of what it means to think aloud (Ericsson & Simon 1993) suggests that to give the evaluator the optimal data about the information used by the test user during the task performance, there should be very little interaction between a test user and an evaluator during the test, and after a task begins, the only interaction allowed is to ask the user to keep thinking aloud.

## 1.1.5 Classical usability test in cross-cultural settings

The classical usability test should be useful with local user populations worldwide, but there are empirical indications that the existing practice, derived from the West, of letting software from a source culture migrate to a target culture may work in the design and implementation phase but not in the usability evaluation phase (Yeo, 2001). For example, if a test user in Malaysia has a higher rank than that of the evaluator, it will result in more negative comments about the product than if the test user has a lower rank than the evaluator has (Yeo, 1998). In some countries, testing subjects individually should be avoided as little information will be retrieved (Herman, 1996). In a study of usability test interviews with Indian participants in the USA, the participants that had a similar socio-cultural background as that of the interviewer (India) pointed out more usability problems than did the participants interviewed by an interviewer with a different socio-cultural background (in this case, Anglo-American) (Vatrapu & Pérez-Quiñones, 2006). Finally, Clemmensen et al. (2008) used empirical results from experimental cultural psychology to discuss problems of mismatch in social status and cognitive style between evaluator and test user in cross-cultural testing of the usability of IS.

## 1.1.6 Cross-cultural usability testing in different countries

International usability tests often take place in more than one country and sometimes, in many countries, the results of which are usually presented in comparison with one other. An important unresolved issue in international usability tests is whether it is always the best choice to use local evaluators when testing with local users in each country or cultural setting. The problem is not only how to ensure a reliable, valid and comparable list of usability problems;

more recently, it has also become obvious that we do not know how to compare and analyse usability problem data across heterogeneous user groups and national cultural contexts. In a study of international usability testing with standard user test procedures, Law & Hvannberg (2004) studied 19 users of an e-learning system, four in England, seven in Germany, five in Iceland and three in Slovenia, each performing 10 task scenarios that covered the main functions of an e-learning platform. At each testing site, the test users in their native language interacted with local testers. The local testers were responsible for implementing the test, recording the data, transcribing and translating the think-aloud data and then forwarding the qualitative and quantitative performance data to the overall coordinator. The overall coordinator in collaboration with another evaluator analysed the data by extracting a list of usability problems for each site, merging the four lists into a master list and rating the severity of the problems. By using this procedure, Law & Hvannberg (2004) found that the heterogeneity of the cultural subgroups in the sample negatively influenced the power of the usability test. When the list of problems from all four sites was merged into one master list, the unique usability problems (those that were found only by users at one site) appeared to be less important than the shared usability problems (those that were found at more than one test site) simply because the unique problems were found by relatively fewer users. Thus, testing in multiple sites made it difficult to interpret the correlation between problem frequency and problem severity (Law & Hvannberg, 2004). This may be a serious issue, as the risk related to overlooking a severe usability problem is not well understood: ‘. . . [the usability communities] lack adequate means of analysing and expressing the comparatively large effect of fixing the few problems of high severity . . .’ (Hertzum, 2006, p. 125). Something that is really important may be missed by using current standard procedures in international usability testing.

## 1.1.7 User and evaluator effects in cross-cultural usability testing in different countries

Why this important ‘something’ may be missed in international usability testing is not clear. Law & Hvannberg (2004) explained it as a ‘user effect’, i.e. the varied capacity of individual users to identify usability problems in a usability test combined with a lack of sophisticated statistica procedures for combining and analysing usability problem lists. Given the complicated procedure in Law and Hvannberg’s study (four local testers and two central evaluators overlapping each other in data collection and analysis), it is thus also possible that the decreased power of standard usability tests in international usability testing is due to an ‘evaluator effect’ (Hertzum & Jacobsen, 2001), i.e. different evaluators evaluating the same system tend to identify different sets of usability problems. Exploring both user and evaluator effects is important when we want to know how to identify usability problems in culturally diverse settings.

## 2. A PILOT STUDY

The issues about culture and usability, which in the aforementioned was identified from the current literature, however, can not be resolved purely through a theoretical and conceptua analysis. Culture and usability need to be studied empirically. The question is how can we study these issues? In this section, I present a pilot study that my colleagues Xianghong Sun from Chinese Academy of Science, Beijing, and Pradeep Yammiyavar from Indian Institute of Technology, Guwahati, and I did in order to find a way to study usability problem identification in culturally diverse settings. Then at the end of the paper, I discuss the limitations of this pilot study and potential further research.

## 2.1 Themes to explore

We wanted to explore ways to study the current practice of using standard think-aloud usability tests in multiple geographical locations. In the industry, usability testing in multiple countries is currently done sometimes with usability test evaluators having the same cultural background as that of the test users and sometimes, with usability test evaluators having a foreign cultural background. Based on advice from our industry partners (e.g. Tom Plocher, staff scientist, personal communication, Honeywell, 2006), we expected that usability testing with local evaluators would provide unique and better described usability problems because the local evaluators and local test users, in principle, would share detailed models of technology use. This seemed a worthy topic for empirical study, but how to study it? We thought that this kind of study of the practice of usability testing initially could be explored in a multiple-country pilot study. Countries could be theoretically sampled to differ substantially in the users’ cultural background and the setting for the usability evaluation. Further, we could explore the themes related to the users’ use of common cultural knowledge, the priming of the users’ alternative meaning systems and the users’ application of appropriate knowledge in the test situation. From our reading of theory as presented earlier, we expected that users would apply common cultural knowledge (which they may not share with their evaluator) simply because this knowledge was accessible to them. However, being with a local evaluator could prime the user to give more detailed knowledge relevant to the artefact because the evaluator and user share this knowledge. Being with a foreign evaluator, in contrast, suggests to the user that he/she can give fewer details and can rather explain general cultural issues because more communication is needed to establish shared perception of the artefact and social reality. So we wanted to see if we in a pilot study could study themes such as:

1 Theme 1: does usability testing with foreign evaluators result in the same usability problem descriptions as the testing with local evaluators?

2 Theme 2: will a possible difference in usability problem description between tests with foreign evaluators and tests with local evaluators in the think-aloud test appear in all cultura settings?

3 Theme 3: in a usability test situation where the user is under time pressure, will he/she tend to look for readily and widely accessible knowledge (shared cultural knowledge) for solutions to a problem?

4 Theme 4: will both cultural content of application and appearance of the evaluator make one of the users’ cultural meaning systems most available in a given situation and indicate to the user what the appropriate knowledge is to apply in the situation?

## 2.2 Participants and place

For the pilot study, the participants and locations were students and staff at three universities. A total of 33 participated, 23 males and 10 female university students and teachers. They were aged between 20 and 39 (median = 22, standard deviation = 4.6) and from different nationalities (nine European, six Chinese, 18 Indian). Most were able to speak and think aloud in English, and if not, they were allowed to use their preferred language during the test; two Chinese users and one Danish user chose to do this.<sup>1</sup> All of the participants had experience of working with the test application environment (MS Word, Microsoft, Redmond, WA) and understood the study task (design your own wedding invitation). The participants in Denmark and India received course credit, while the participants in China received a small financia reward. according to local research practice. The places for the study were university office environments in Copenhagen (for the Danish users), Guwahati (for the Indian users) and Beijing (for the Chinese users).

## 2.3 Procedures and data collection

To explore the themes that we were interested in, we ran a simulated, multiple-country usability test. In each country, we introduced a systematic variation by splitting each local test user group into two: one half for the local evaluator test condition and the other half for the foreign evaluator test condition. The evaluator role included both finding usability problems and facilitating the test (thus, we here use the term evaluator as synonymous with moderator for the purpose of simplicity). Each test user participant only participated in tests at her or his university.

We asked each test user participant to take part in a usability test of an MS Word (Microsoft) clip art application that contained culturally specific icons and pictures. The participant was instructed to use the clipart to design his or her own wedding invitation and make it look traditional. The icons and pictures were collected and implemented in MS Word clip art in country-specific clip art collections by respective Danish, Indian and Chinese research assistants. Some clip arts were common across all three collections, e.g. wedding rings, and others were country-specific, e.g. the Indian elephant god icon.

Foreign and local evaluators were used to moderate and facilitate the simulated usability tests. The people that we used as foreign and local evaluators were selected and trained so that they could administer the usability test with competence. They had, on average, 5 years of experience with usability testing. Local evaluators were born and raised in the same country as the test user participant and belonged to the same ethnic group. Foreign evaluators were non-citizens of the country of the test user participant and also did not belong to the same ethnic group as the test user participant judging from physical and social appearance. At each site, we used two foreign evaluators with, on average, 4 years of experience with usability tests and from two to four different local evaluators with, on average, 6 years of experience with usability tests.

The people playing the role of the evaluators wrote down in a report template the potentia usability problems that they identified during each test. The tests and interviews were recorded in audio and video. After each usability test, the researchers (the author and colleagues at the university where the test was run) interviewed the evaluator and the test users individually and together about their observations from taking part in the usability test. The data were collected per country, and then, a combined analysis was made.

## 2.4 Analysis and interpretation

To explore what usability problems were identified in the usability tests, three researchers (the author and an Indian and a Chinese colleague) computed a usability problem list by creating a non-overlapping set of usability problems found across all usability test sessions for a similar procedure see (Law & Hvannberg, 2004). First, for each of the three geographical locations, separate lists of usability problems were created. In this process, the post-test interview transcripts were used to explore how the particular cultural contexts constituted the usability problems. To avoid misunderstandings due to language or cultural background issues, this list was prepared by the researcher, native to the country of the experimental setting. Second, one researcher (the author), in collaboration with the two other researchers, integrated the three lists of usability problems into a new list of non-overlapping usability problems. The non overlapping usability problems were then counted.

To further explore the details of the usability problem descriptions, one researcher (the author) carried out a qualitative analysis of a selected usability problem. First step was to pick, from the list of non-overlapping usability problems, the usability problems that occurred most frequently (so that there was material enough for analysis). The second step was to apply the grounded theory apparatus of open, axial and selective coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). In the open coding, the researcher developed categories of the usability problem by examining the data that were related to that problem until no further instances could be found. In the axia coding, the researcher organised the usability problem material into categories and subcategories. In the selective coding, the researcher connected the usability problem categories by identifying a single central category and explored how the other categories were related to the central category. Finally, a story was constructed to connect the usability problem categories and illustrate the theoretical themes that were explored in the pilot study. How to display the results was decided by applying the rules of authenticity (display data in their original form to force the reader to diagnose on the basis of the original situation), inclusion (displays should never show just examples but the data set itself) and transparency (displays should be explained, axes and dimensions should be clear to the reader and data sorting should be intuitive and easy to understand)(Dahler-Larsen. 2002)

## 2.5 Results

## 2.5.1 Identification, counting and comparing usability problems

Our analysis of the evaluators’ reports on usability problems from all three country locations resulted in a total of 534 problem instances that we summarised into a list of 35 nonoverlapping usability problems. The usability problem with the most instances was a problem that we called ‘the inappropriate image collection problem’ and which covered 101 instances of troubles that the participants in our simulated usability tests apparently had with the clip art collections. Most of the 35 usability problems occurred both in foreign evaluator and local evaluator usability test conditions (shared usability problem), but we found nine usability problems that only occurred in local evaluator test conditions (unique usability problems).

## 2.5.2 Country-specific usability problems

Among the usability problems that we in this pilot study found in the local evaluator conditions were a number of problems found in the Beijing tests. These problems were seemingly related to the use of background in MS Word documents (Microsoft) and included background edi problems (the user wanted to centre the Chinese character ‘xi’ on the background but it was, of course, fixed, or the user found the picture too clear and wanted it to be blurred similar to a background), the background collection range (the user wanted to have more backgrounds to choose from), no background category (the user wanted names on each background picture and/or sub-folders of the different kinds of backgrounds) and problems with print preview of backgrounds (the user wanted to see the colour of the background in print preview, which was not possible and/or the user was in doubt as to whether the printer would print the background colour because the colour was not shown in print preview).

The problem with the lack of colour in the print preview of backgrounds is illustrated in Figure 1. The figure illustrates how the Chinese users of an MS Word (Microsoft) English version are at a disadvantage compared with the Danish users of the same application. For the Chinese users, the default MS Word (Microsoft) print preview function did not show the background colour of the invitation, which was very important in the Chinese setting (but less important in the Danish setting). This was identified in a local evaluator, think-aloud tes condition in Beijing in the pilot study. Potentially, this kind of usability problem will be importan to the large group of ‘background-oriented’ people worldwide (see e.g. Nisbett & Masuda, 2003).

The local evaluator condition tests in the Beijing setting also gave us the idea that searching for clip arts might be an important problem for Chinese users, although we did not come up with any explanation for this. Also, the local evaluator condition tests in the other countries gave us ideas to what could be country-specific usability problems. In Copenhagen, the local evaluator tests helped identify a problem with the quality of the pre-formatted invitation text (the user found that the fonts and the texts did require more consideration, that the format of the invitation was unconventional and that the address and phone numbers used in the letter had wrong formats). In Guwahati, the local evaluator tests revealed that the participants felt it was necessary to have an overview of many different high-resolution icons and varieties of invita tion letter backgrounds and thus, got into troubles with having to go back and forth between their word processor window and several other windows.

## 2.6 Summarising the pilot study

## 2.6.1 Social communication and identification of usability problems in multiple-country usability tests

In our pilot study, we attempted to find a way to study the identification of usability problems in multiple-country usability testing of localised interactive products. The definition of culture that we sought to apply (see section 1.1.3) says that the communication and the specifics of the setting in local evaluator tests will allow the local test user to access, activate and apply his or her culturally specific knowledge in interacting with the product in ways that he or she can not do in a foreign evaluator test. This prompted us to do a qualitative analysis of a selected usability problem types and instances. By doing a grounded theory analysis of the most frequently occurring severe usability problem, we could illustrate how the local evaluator condition provided specific and rich grounding of the usability problems type in different subcategories and their associated instances. In contrast, the foreign evaluator condition in the pilot study provided poor grounding of the usability problems in vague and not detailed instances.

## 2.6.2 Usability problems or localisation problems?

Was the local evaluator test condition more suitable in order to identify and describe usability problems, compared to foreign evaluator test condition? Were the problems found by local evaluators country-specific usability problems in terms of the test artefact’s cultural model of use interfering with country user group’s cultural models of use? Or were the problems merely superficially bad localisations that did not really affect the usability of the software? From the pilot study, we can not know. What we saw in the Chinese setting was that the local evaluator tests identified a unique usability problem: The users had trouble with not being able to see the background colour of the document in the print preview. Research in cultural psychology suggests that people raised in China have a stronger tendency than people raised in Western countries to take a holistic view and take the background into account when perceiving a picture (Nisbett & Miyamoto, 2005). Because it interferes with the user’s basic cognitive processes, the problem of not being able to preview the document background may be regarded as a severe usability problem.

From a traditional view of usability, task completion is very important in deciding whether something is a usability problem or not. The ISO (International Organization for Standardization) 9241-11 defines usability as ‘. . . the extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use’ (9241-11, 1998). Therefore, what is found in local evaluator tests of unique usability problems may be considered from a traditional usability point of view as mere instances of bad localisations and not usability problems if they do not interfere with the users completion of the tasks. From a cultural usability perspective, however, the definition of usability may itself be culturally biased. Having a strong task focus in ways of working may be specific for Western countries and opposed to ways of working in other countries (Sanchez Burks & Lee, 2005). Local evaluators may not pay attention to certain problems that influence task performance simply because they perceive them as minor problems of low importance. Thus, the weight put on task completion by standard usability definitions may be an expression of a task-focused Western work culture, and more weight should perhaps be put on other aspects of usability, such as in the newer definition of user experience. The newer standard (ISO 9241-210, 2009) definition of user experience specifies three factors that influence user experience – system, user and context of use – and emphasises the subjective nature of user experience. We need to know better how different groups of people around the world perceive the quality of their interaction with computers.

## 3. CONCLUSION

This paper has presented the theoretical background, related work and a definition of culture that should be useful for studies of cultural fit in multiple-country usability testing. The background for many studies of cultural aspects of IT has been national cultural value dimensions. In contrast, this paper defines cultural usability as the psychological outcome of applying cultural models of technology use in concrete situations. I call this the ‘cultural model of use approach to cultural usability as it focuses on the different practices of use of IT that emerge over time in different geographical locations of the world. Within this approach, the produced list of usability problems is the result of the creation of a psychological sense of usability by an evaluator-moderated, usability evaluation-enabled communication about the references between the user’s expectations of a technology and the specific artefact. The (in)consistencies between the cultural models of use involved in a usability evaluation will have to be resolved through communication. Hence, the very little interaction between a test user and an evaluator during classical think-aloud usability testing is inappropriate with new user populations and evaluators with a variety of socio-cultural backgrounds and cognitive styles. Something really important may be missed by using the classical procedures in international usability testing. For example, there may be both user and evaluator effects in the current practice of cross-cultural usability testing in different countries, and we need to explore those. As an illustrative example of the kind of academic research that we ought to do, I presented a pilot study. The pilot study exemplified themes to explore. who should be participants and where should the study be done, how to find examples of multiple-country usability testing to study, how to collect data and how to analyse data and what kind of results and discussion of results may we expect. The pilot study, however, is only one example, and it suffered from numerous limitations. In the remaining part of the paper, I will discuss the limitations in the pilot study and suggest further research.

## 3.1 Limitations in the pilot study

## 3.1.1 Limitations in themes to explore

The pilot study was limited in the choice of themes to explore. It focused on themes related to within-test sessions in one kind of usability evaluation: the standard think-aloud usability test. It would have been relevant to explore more themes related to other parts of current practice of usability evaluation in multiple countries, such as planning (which and how many countries and user populations to select) and knowledge sharing with designers (Nørgaard & Høgh, 2008) and developer effects (Law, 2006). The use of other established usability evaluation methods in multiple countries, e.g. the use of heuristic evaluation methods across countries, would also have been relevant themes to explore.

## 3.1.2 Limitations in participants and place

Perhaps the most difficult part of the pilot study, and the biggest limitation, was related to the choice of participants and place. An obvious limitation of the pilot study was the low number of participants. The complex research design, with two evaluator conditions and several countries, calls for a much larger study. Also, usually, real-life industrial multiple-country usability tests are done with more participants (Nokia User-Experience manager, 2009, persona communication). The number of test users in the pilot study of approximately five users per usability test condition has been proven to be far too few by critics of ‘discount usability (Cockton & Woolrych, 2002).

Another limitation of the pilot study was the use of students and teachers as evaluators rathe than practitioners in work settings. There is a need to involve practitioners in a larger study. Furthermore, having students as test users was also a limitation because university settings may be too similar across countries compared with other settings in society. For example, had the pilot study used rural participants in rural settings in the three different countries, we might have found other between-country differences in usability problem identification.

Finally, the pilot study was limited in its inclusion of a small number of countries. On the one hand, having three countries was better than having two because it allowed us to avoid dichotomising our results, e.g. in East vs. West. On the other hand, in this type of comparative cross-cultural studies, as many countries as possible should be involved.

## 3.1.3 Limitations in procedures and tasks

The pilot study had several limitations related to procedures and tasks that were used to run and study simulated think-aloud usability testing in university labs in three countries. First, each test user had to use culturally specific MS Word (Microsoft) clip art to design his or her wedding invitation and make it look traditional. While we did ask the users in a pre-test questionnaire about their skills in MS Word (Microsoft) and in the use of clip art, the lack of clear measures of computer literacy levels among the three country groups of users was a limitation of the pilot study. Second, the pilot study did not clearly establish if the participants’ task of ‘designing a wedding invitation’ was reasonably familiar to all test users. Third, not only was the participants’ task perhaps culturally biased, but also, the material (clip arts) that the participants were asked to use to solve the task may also have been culturally biased in ways we do not fully understand, see (Nawaz et al., 2007; Qu et al., 2007).

Fourth, the people playing the role of the evaluators were expected to write down the potential usability problems that were identified during the tests. However, each evaluator ran less than five test sessions. This is known from industrial practice to be too few tests to get an adequate sense of the usability problems that a given user population has with an application. Though the exact number may vary according to task domain, each evaluator should run a series of test sessions of the application before reporting on the identified usability problems. Fifth, only one evaluator was present in each test session, and each test user was used in one test session only so no user was ever working with more than one evaluator. This means that individual evaluator differences, besides those related to culture and thus, of interest to the study, could have potentially played an important role in the evaluator’s style of questioning and prompting, and hence, the problems were identified. Sixth, the evaluator and the test user were both interviewed about their observations. Because these interviews was of a qualitative nature, it is a limitation of the pilot study that the interviews were not presented and discussed with all the participants in each country and also across all participating countries as a participant-check of the interview data and interpretations.

## 3.1.4 Limitations in coding, analysis and interpretation of data

Obviously, in the pilot study, a larger number of sessions were run with local evaluator conditions (22) than with foreign evaluator conditions (11) so it is not possible to decide whether any of the usability problems were best described as shared or as unique problems. Caulton (2001), however, argues that any usability problem is, to a degree, both shared and unique, and that problems can be placed on a range from shared to unique. We think that this makes sense to think similar to that in the context of usability problems in multiple-country usability evaluations but did not really explore this idea in the pilot study.

In the pilot study, a researcher (the author) in collaboration with research assistants coded the usability problem data from each usability test session with the aim to come up with a usability problem list. This coding may have been seriously flawed as coding usability problem reports and computing usability problem lists take much training and is difficult to do reliably. Ideally, there should be at least two coders per country involved in the study, and an assessment of their consistency in coding should be given. Another weakness related to coding of data in the pilot study was that the qualitative analysis of usability problems and interview data was done solely by the author of this paper. The researcher’s cultural background was Danish, and he may thus have missed important meanings in the translations of words and concepts from Chinese and Indian English.

Regarding interpretation of the data, a limitation of the pilot study was the lack of clarity in what was the topic of study: the practice of multiple-country, think-aloud usability testing. First, the concept of a ‘usability test’ itself is somewhat ambiguous. In fact, a think-aloud is no always part of a usability test, though it has long been established that understanding an individual’s underlying cognitive processes as they perform a task is important to uncover interface problems (Lewis, 1982). Second, a concrete usability test may be carried out in a range of ways, from a formative test with the aim of finding design problems so solve to a summative test that tests final product. A formative, explorative usability test is carried out with much communication between evaluator and user, while a summative, formal usability test is carried out with little communication between evaluator and user. It is thus a serious limitation of the pilot study that it was not specified clearly whether the simulated think-aloud usability test was formative or summative. The choice of research methodology to study these two versions of a think-aloud usability test may be quite different. In the study of a formative usability test, we should study the evaluator’s communication with the user as the evaluator will have an active role in creating, as well as in identifying, the usability problems. In the study of a summative test, on the other hand, it becomes very important to study how the evaluator uses the think-aloud data from the user. When interpreting the data from the pilot study, we should have known in what way the test was intended to be carried out by the evaluator. Finally, when interpreting the data from a study of multiple-country testing, a lot of emphasis should be put in explaining what additional knowledge we need in order to do multiple-country testing compared with single-country testing. The additional contribution to usability testing in relation to multiple-country testing should be clearly stated. The connection between concepts inherent in usability testing and the differences in culture and communication should be made.

## 3.1.5 Limitations in results and discussions

The pilot study aimed at coming up with results related to identification of usability problems in culturally diverse settings. However, the assumption about what is a usability problem may vary in multiple-country settings (Hertzum et al., 2011). It has been suggested that both usability evaluation methods and usability problems should be seen as region- or countryspecific, see e.g. (Winschiers & Fendler, 2007). The pilot study was an attempt to use the identification of usability problems to compare the outcomes of using local and foreign evalu ators in local evaluations. This comparison was severely limited by the lack of good examples and knowledge of country-specific usability problems. Thus, there was a construct bias in the pilot study related to the possibility that the concept of usability may not be the same across countries/cultural settings, and this may have biased the results.

Finally, the pilot study was limited to a discussion of a standard usability situation. The standard think-aloud usability evaluation was, at the time of the pilot study, a recently imported context of use in two of the countries in the study. Thus, setting up the pilot study was based on the unspoken and wrong assumption that the think-aloud usability evaluation method was established as a similar practice in all the countries in the study.

## 3.2 Further research

In this section, I suggest research ways in which a full study could be completed based on the proposed approach of ‘cultural models of use’ to usability problem identification in culturally diverse settings. Future research should range from validation of established usability and user experience evaluation methods in new cultural settings to research in regional or countryspecific concepts of usability and user experience.

## 3.2.1 Validation of existing usability and user experience evaluation methods cross-culturally

Exploratory observations of current practice of usability evaluation in multiple countries can be modelled on previous studies of the practice of usability testing in single countries. We have, by now, several field studies that explore the practice of usability and user experience evaluation within a single, non-Western country (e.g. Kurosu et al., 2004; Shi, 2008; Irani, 2010; Paterson et al., 2011). A full study could follow the approach used in these studies, which is some form of ethnographic study of local user groups’ cultural models of use, combined with proposals for change of design methods and critique of the current definitions of usability and user experience. However, a full study would need to include more countries, systematically explore usability testing practice in real companies across several countries and study both the companies individually and the companies as subcontractors to one large multiple-country usability evaluation. One example of this approach is a study of think-aloud usability testing in three countries (Clemmensen, 2011).

Second, existing usability evaluation methods could be validated by experimental studies. We know from anecdotes and results from basic psychology that think-aloud usability evaluation may be culturally biased (Clemmensen, et al., 2008). However, few have tried to directly test hypothesis related to the (lack of) cultural bias in usability evaluation methods. A full study could follow the approach by Oyugi et al. (2008). Lee & Lee (2007) did a similar study with students in Korea and students in the Netherlands. Another way to test hypotheses related to culture and communication in usability evaluation would be to follow the approach used by Sh & Clemmensen (2008). They chose a complex research design with participants from two countries living in their home country. In each country, both local and foreign (from the othe country) professional usability evaluators were used.

## 3.2.2 Exploring regional or country-specific concepts of usability problems and user experiences

In some countries, adapting existing usability evaluation methods to local settings will not be sufficient to identify usability problems simply because the concept of usability of software does not make sense or fit with how people relate to technology. Hence, it may not be possible right out to start studying the problems that people experience when they apply their cultural models of use in their interactions with computers as problems of usability. Instead, we may start with studying the values and meaning of the equivalent concepts to usability and user experience A way to do this is to do lexical studies that are focused on identifying the words that people use to describe their relation to computers.

If the HCI researchers in a country suspect that the concept of usability of software may not make sense to user groups in this country, an initial exploratory study could be done. Fo example, Winschiers & Fendler (2007) decided to investigate the local meaning of usability by running small group brainstorming with different Namibian user groups. In order to compare the equivalent with concepts of usability and user experience across several countries, the approach by Hertzum et al. (2011) can be used. They argued that the concept of usability is traditionally defined analytically (without users), but based on psychological theory, we need to study how people relate to systems through their personal constructs. Then they used repertory grid interviews to study users’ and developers’ personal usability constructs in three countries.

A direct approach to study the words that people use to describe their relations to computer systems was taken by Frandsen-Thorlacius et al. (2009). They did a large-scale questionnaire study of users in two countries. In an online questionnaire, self-selected participants were asked about their relations to the email and word processor programs. The questions had been translated and back-translated to minimise cultural bias. The questions were based on aspects of usability from the literature. They were designed to elicit attitudes about the importance of different aspects of usability and also to assess attitudes about the relative importance of aspects of usability (prioritise among aspects of usability).

More work is needed to develop the practice of usability problem identification in culturally diverse settings. One suggestion is to build a ‘comparative informatics’ approach that can help researchers and practitioners to become more sensitive towards culturally diverse settings for usability and user experience (Nardi et al., 2011). For example, Winschiers-Theophilus et al. (2010) suggested that humanness, the African ‘Ubuntu’ concept, indicates that seemingly irrelevant discussions and activities are essential to ensure collaboration between users and developers and I would add perhaps also for human–technology interaction. More theoretica work can help us to discuss how and if we should support national and ethnic cultural diversity in interactive and collaborative computing, i.e. indigenous HCI approaches. The overall goal of such studies should be to enable a satisfying IT experience for a much broader range of users.

## Biography

Torkil Clemmensen is Associate Professor at the Department of Informatics, Copenhagen Business School, Denmark. He has more than 20 years of academic and industrial experience with human-centred methods and techniques for analysis, design and evaluation of informa tion and communication technology and digital media and how these are designed and used in organisational, human, social and cultural contexts.
