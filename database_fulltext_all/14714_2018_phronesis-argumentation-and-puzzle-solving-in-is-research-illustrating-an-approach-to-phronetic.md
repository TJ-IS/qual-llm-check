# Phronesis, argumentation and puzzle solving in IS research: illustrating an approach to phronetic IS research practice

Ojelanki Ngwenyama<sup>a,b</sup> and Stefan Klein<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Ted Rogers School of Management, Ryerson University, Toronto, Canada; <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems, University of Cape Town Cape Town, South Africa; <sup>c</sup>Department for Information Systems, School of Business and Economics, University of Münster, Münster, Germany

## ABSTRACT

The embedding of information systems (IS) infrastructure into everyday social activity is creating intractable value conflicts for society. However, IS research largely focuses on: (1) episteme, explanatory and predictive theories; and (2) techné, theories of action for implementation and management of IS technologies. Consequently, we lack applicable knowledge for deliberating about IS value conflicts. The third type of knowledge, phronesis, which Aristotle proposed to inform ethical application of episteme and techné, has not been given much attention in our discipline. Presently, we are experiencing a critical limitation in our capabilities for generating ethically applicable IS knowledge in the face of increasing value conflicts arising from pervasive digitalisation. In this paper, we introduce phronesis, discuss its philosophical foundations and illustrate an approach to phronetic enquiry into the underlying value conflicts of a failed national IS infrastructure project. Our aim is to strengthen and expand IS research methods for systematic critical enquiry into social-values dilemmas resulting from continued embedding of IS technologies into our social world.

## KEYWORDS

Phronesis; value conflicts; IS infrastructure; digitalisation and ethical dilemmas; argumentation; retroduction; eHealth infrastructure; innovation

## 1. Introduction

The twentieth century has seen the embedding of information systems (IS) infrastructure into every aspect of social life (Anderson & Tracey, 2001; Van Den Eede, 2011), and the emergence of intractable challenges of value conflicts and digital disruptions (Riemer & Johnston, 2013). While IS infrastructure is seen as essential to social progress, it brings new challenges to democratic values and individual rights (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Kling, 1992; Ngwenyama, 1998). Social research into IS must grapple with fundamental issues of how these technologies can transform human existence in both positive and negative ways, and how we can reason about them and take informed deliberate actions to reduce the risks. Some scholars argue that the IS discipline lacks research approaches for addressing value conflicts and generating pragmatic knowledge for ethical deliberation and decision-making (Constantinides, Chiasson, & Introna, 2012; Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Mingers & Walsham, 2010). More recently, Schultze argues, “as IS researchers we need to examine our own practices – including the questions we ask, the methods we deploy and the theories we adopt – to understand and critically review our world-making” (2017, p. 65).

The IS discipline has advanced significantly using two of Aristotle’s types of knowledge: (1) episteme, explanatory social scientific knowledge; and (2) techné, knowledge for the design, construction and implementation of IS technologies. However, phronesis, Aristotle’s third type of knowledge concerned with ethical action, has not been equally enrolled in IS research despite increasing value conflicts emerging from the use of IS technologies (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Kling, 1992; Stahl, 2008). Researchers have long recognised that value conflicts are central to IS research and practice (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Kling, 1992). Public management decision-making on digitalisation is fraught with competing stakeholder interests, value conflicts, and social risks, which traditional IS research methods cannot adequately address (Klein & Hirschheim, 2001; Kling, 1992; Whitley & Hosein, 2008). Artefacts such as smartphones and social media are having profound social and psychological impacts on our identity and worldviews. The information we consume is influenced by factors like Wikipedia’s ideals, Google’s criteria for promoting digital content and Facebook’s policies for prioritising news and viewers’ interests. Algorithms for autonomous driving make decisions about who to hit in case an accident cannot be avoided. All these point to the importance of deliberate enquiry into the value rationale behind these social innovations.

The goal of this paper is to advance our understand ing of the relevance and applicability of phronesis (variously translated as practical wisdom, prudence, the art of judgement) to IS research. Specifically, we are interested in illustrating how Toulmin’s discursive approach to academic enquiry, which aims for plausible reasoning about particulars, can facilitate attention to methodological guidelines for phronetic research elaborated by Flyvbjerg (2008). Our approach emphasises critical evaluation of the role of power in value judgements about the adoption of digital infrastructures in public management situations where there are conflicting stakeholder interests. To illustrate the methodological commitments necessary for phronetic research we introduce a case of contested digitalisation in the German health care system, and use Toulmin’s model of argument (Toulmin, 1958) to interrogate widely accepted claims and make sense of the failure of the initiative. Our enquiry involves a critical analysis of public discourse of the stakeholders, judging their behaviours and unwillingness to engage in open consultation and dialogue, and the consequences of these in undermining the public good. Our work highlights the central importance of phronesis in addressing an often ignored but ever expanding class of IS research problems, the core of which are value conflicts and public accountability.

## 2. Conceptual foundations

Our discussion of phronetic IS research is primarily based on the works of Stephen E. Toulmin and Bent Flyvbjerg, and to a lesser extent on Hans-Georg Gadamer. These scholars ofer three distinctive perspectives on phronesis and make their own contributions to the project of phronetic social science. However, it must be noted that Toulmin’s interest is much broader than Flyvbjerg’s or Gadamer’s. His distinctive interest is history and philosophy of science. In Cosmopolis (Toulmin, 1992), he explicates how phronesis became marginalised in modern science and sought to reintegrate it as Aristotle had intended (applicable to guiding episteme and techné). He provides a critical account of how we moved from the sixteenth century humanist conception, to the modern acontextual and rationalist view of science. Toulmin states:

In choosing as the goals of Modernity an intellectual and practical agenda that set aside the tolerant, skeptical attitude of the 16th-century humanists, and focused on the 17th-century pursuit of mathematical exactitude and logical rigor, intellectual certainty and moral purity, Europe set itself on a cultural and political road that has led both to its most striking technical successes and to its deepest human failures. (Toulmin, 1992, p. x)

Arguing from a historical perspective, Toulmin explicates the importance of reinstating phronesis as “a pragmatic method appropriate in dealing with ethical questions in all fields of science” (Toulmin, 1992). As early as 1976, he drew attention to the limitations of episteme and techné in addressing the particularities of concrete human problems. He states:

When we are concerned with human conduct in reallife situations, … [t]he conditions that hold good in physics are suspended, and the goal of “universality” is no longer relevant to the nature of the case. … human conduct falls not in the realm of episteme, but in that of phronesis (“practical” understanding): it must be described and explained in terms of “concrete particulars”, not “abstract universals.” (Toulmin, 1976, p. 49)

Toulmin is also keen to point out that Aristotle’s objective was that phronesis would inform the wise use of episteme and techné in human afairs pros ton kairon, as the occasion requires (Toulmin, 1992). In The Abuse of Casuistry: A History of Moral Reasoning (Jonsen & Toulmin, 1988), and Return to Reason (2001), Toulmin was adamant that the exclusive emphasis on universal and invariable rules (theories) is impoverishing science and restricting scientific competences for ethical reasoning about value conflicts in all fields of science. In The Uses of Argument (1958) Toulmin developed an approach of “plausible reasoning” (further elaborated in Toulmin, Rieke and Janik, 1984) and interrogating the academic account of practical knowledge. A considerable legacy of Toulmin (1992, 2001, 1985, 1988, 1999, 2006) is focused on developing principles for “substantive argumentation” and dialogical reasoning, fundamental to phronetic enquiry and necessary for ethical issues in human sciences.

Flyvbjerg’s distinctive contribution is in outlining a programme for phronetic research in public management: “an approach to the study of social phenomena based on a contemporary interpretation of the classical Greek concept  phronesis …” (Flyvbjerg, 2016). His contribution is more pragmatic and specific, and lacks the reach of Toulmin’s philosophical exposition of phronesis. Furthermore, Flyvbjerg (2006b, p. 373), acknowledges Toulmin’s much earlier work on phronesis as informing his own eforts at establishing a programme for phronetic social science research in public management of mega projects. Given his interest in engaged studies of contemporary public management problems, Flyvbjerg has a distinct interest in clarifying the value and contribution of phronesis to case study research (Flyvbjerg, 2006a). The third scholar we reference is Gadamer, whose concept of hermeneutics builds on phronesis as “… the way in which knowledge is for the sake of acting, in other words, for the sake of living. … existence means existing with others, which requires dialogue born of humility and an openness for enquiry” (Barthold, n.d.). Finally, Gadamer (1977) links the role of the phronetic researcher and dialogic enquiry to the Heideggerian tradition and critique of rational ism. Gadamer, like Toulmin, tried to overcome narrow rationalistic traditions (e.g., Cartesian Dualism and logical empiricism). The distinctive perspectives of these three scholars are important foundations of a phronetic research approach to IS phenomena.

## 2.1. Characterising phronesis

Phronesis can be characterised by the relationships among phronimos, praxis and eudaimonia. The phronimos (person exercising phronesis) is concerned with virtuous praxis in particular contexts with the aim of enhancing eudaimonia, individual and social well-being. Aristotle argues that praxis (acting) is distinct from poiesis (producing). Poiesis results in some kind of artefact whose measure of success might be its usefulness or its formal accuracy. Praxis is a particular mode of engaging with the world, it is highly contingent and follows a distinct logic, and can produce good or bad outcomes. Phronesis is the capability for virtuous praxis where individual and social well-being is its measures of success. The fields of praxis are politics, public management, teaching, etc., which aim for neither artefact nor abstract knowledge, but the public good, individual, and social well-being. Virtuous praxis is “guided by a moral disposition to act truly and rightly; a concern to further human well-being and the good life” (Smith, 1999/2011); it is a distinct and complex phenomenon with varied aspects depending on particulars of the context. Although the idealisation of science and engi neering interventions in our world as value free rejects the distinctive and important contribution of phronesis, there is growing awareness that episteme and techné are not value neutral; they have profound implications for individual and societal wellbeing (Flyvbjerg, 2016; Toulmin, 1985). While episteme, techné, and phronesis are diferent forms of knowledge, they are all necessary for understanding and acting in our world. Phronesis is most relevant for informing and enabling practical ethical intervention and the application of episteme and techné in human afairs (Aristotle, 1941, pp. 1026–1032).

Eudaimonia is a primary objective of phronesis. The goal of the phronetic approach is “to add to society’s capacity for value-rational deliberation and action” (Flyvbjerg, 2016). Phronesis requires a mode of knowing attuned to the particulars of praxis and asks if and how actions contribute to well-being. Phronesis (practical wisdom, common sense, prudence) refers to the ability of practitioners to deal competently with practical issues characterised by uncertainty and value conflicts. The knowledge interest of phronesis is not universal abstracts, but plausible reasoning for ethical, practical, and deliberative action in particular circumstances of managerial and political afairs which are variable (Flyvbjerg, 2001). The aim of phronetic research is to contribute to societies’ well-being: “… to clarify values, interests, and power relations as a basis for praxis … not to develop theory (though it may be done), but to contribute to society’s practical rationality in elucidating where we are, where we want to go, and what is desirable according to diverse sets of values and interests” (Flyvbjerg, 2016). Thus, phronetic social science is neither value free nor value neutral, it makes an intellectual commitment to developing knowledge for reflective ethical (virtuous) action in human afairs. The result of phronetic research is an account of the possibilities, problems, and risks we face in specific domains of social action (Toulmin, 1976, p. 169).

## 2.2. The primacy of judgement and engagement

The phronimos is understood to be a skilful actor in conditions of uncertainty, a “virtuoso social actor” who contingently applies a distinctive set of competences related to the specific field (Flyvbjerg, 2016). This distinctive set of competences (practical wisdom, common sense, prudence) is earned from extensive experience of self-reflexive action that attend to value conflicts, as well the historical, social, and political complexities of the situation. In other words, phronetic knowledge is grounded in the empirical experience of practice. For Gadamer (1975), the phronimos “is radically undogmatic because of the many experiences he has had and the knowledge he has drawn from them, [and] … is particularly well equipped to learn … not in definite knowledge but in the openness to experience that is made possible by experience itself ” (Gadamer, 1975, p. 355). This implies the art of judgement, a critical reflexivity to one’s unfolding experience, and how it might relate to, and inform virtuous acting in the particular situation (Shotter & Tsoukas, 2014a). Gadamer (1977, p. 189) terms this “holding oneself open in a conversation”, openness, engagement and self-reflection in the dialogue that enables the phronimos to see beyond the immediate.

Engagement is a core principle of phronetic social science; the researcher enquires from “within” the empirical situation, she is not a scientist-as-spectator but a participant who has responsibility for the social consequences of her work. At the 1984 National Science Foundation roundtable on scientific progress, Toulmin argued for this perspective for scientific research:

Scientists must learn to see themselves not just as spectators looking on the world of nature from outside, but also as participants with roles to play from within that world. … the old positivist idea that all the sciences have to be based on a single set of methods is no longer viable. … the scientist-as-spectator option is no longer open to us, neither is the assumption that science is value-free or that scientist bear no responsibility for the social consequences of their work. (Toulmin, 1985, p. 29)

Flyvbjerg (2004) also argues that phronetic researchers must become immersed in the political dialogue and attentive to the history of the situation. In politics and public management, discourse is also practice; however, if separated from actions, the context of meaning is lost. This is a key point of Flyvbjerg (1998) and Habermas (1993), Gofman (1970) and others. In Rationality and Power (1998), Flyvbjerg is explicit about the need to interrogate discourse on the same level as actions or outcomes. In this regard Forester proclaims Flyvbjerg (1998) the best illustration of a study of systematically distorted communication (cf. Forester, 2001). Critical analysis of discourse and action requires that the researcher is socialised into the social and institutional context of the case situation if she is to obtain “insider knowledge” necessary for making sense of it (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997). Immersion is essential for understanding the history of the case situation, the “position” of each stakeholder, social norms and value rationale operant in the case situation; in other words, the particulars of the case. The empirical situation of phronesis is not just the case, but the actors, their worldviews, as well as historical and social context (norms and institutional structures) within which the case is situated. Thus, the researcher must focus on the actors and their practices in relation to social and institutional norms, which orient them. As Flyvbjerg explains:

Phronetic researchers are highly aware of the importance of context and perspective, and see no neutral ground, no “view from nowhere,” for their work. (Flyvbjerg, 2016)

Phronetic research is a form of engaged scholarship (van de Ven, 2007), which reflects the identity of the researcher and a genuine commitment to research in and for practice. The researcher must make substantial investment of time to learn the specifics of the field and the case for skilful analysis of the case situation and to gain the respect of the practitioners who are grounded in it. The critical self-reflexive and value oriented approach of phronetic social science requires that the researcher clarifies her role, experience and perspective in doing the enquiry. The phronetic researcher must not only be skilful at observing and interpreting details of case situation, but must engage in systematic interrogation and testing of his observations and interpretations if he is to render a valid explanation of it. As Long states:

The “self-reflective” dimension of phronesis suggests that the one who judges must be capable of reflecting on the various ways in which she or he is always already implicated in every act of judgment. This entails an ability not only to critically consider the concrete historico-ethico-political realities that condition each judgment, but also to recognize … the “enabling prejudices” that always function in human judgment. (Long, 2002, p. 45)

## 2.3. Methodological principles

Phronetic research acknowledges that its results are inevitably tentative, at times ambiguous, but nevertheless sound, important and appropriate for the study of practice. The measure of phronetic research is arguments that are reasonable, substantive, and reinforced by dialogue and critique (Toulmin, 1976, 2001). Flyvbjerg (2004) and Toulmin (1992) therefore embrace a narrative and dialogical, non-dualistic approach to phronetic enquiry and suggest guidelines for its accomplishment. The aim of such methods is reflective, hermeneutic, value laden understanding, which discloses “the concrete possibilities of being in a situation, as the starting point of meaningful action, processed with resolution, while facing the contingencies of life” (Jankelson, 2013, p. 55). Gadamer (1977), who rejects the theory – practice dualism of traditional social science, argues that phronesis is an academic practice rooted in hermeneutic philosophy in which the social scientist understands that both experience and dialogue play a central role in knowledge production. Dialogical reasoning within the social and historical context, case situation and stakeholders, value conflicts, social risks and decision options are essential to phronetic social science. In Cosmopolis (1992) and later in Return to Reason (2001), Toulmin discusses the importance of rhetoric to phronesis for the development of substantive arguments. While argumentation in episteme focuses on formal proofs, in phronesis its concern is plausible reasoning and substantive arguments. The arguments of episteme and phronesis are judged by diferent metrics, validity, and soundness, respectively: “validity of epistemic argumentation is judged by deductive rigour, … while soundness of phronetic argumentation is judged by reasonableness and power to carry conviction” (Toulmin, 2001, p. 15). Phronesis emphasises the particulars and phronetic reasoning manifests itself in substantive arguments – comparable to reasoning in court:

The substance of everyday experience refers always to a “where and when”: a “here and now” or a “there and then.” … Substantive arguments are historically situated and rely on the evidence of experience: the best they can claim to do is to put a conclusion “beyond a reasonable doubt” and establish the “strongest possible presumption” on its behalf. (Toulmin, 2001, p. 15, 19)

## 2.3.1. Methods of empirical analysis

The aim of phronetic explanation is to create a rich narrative account (Weick, 2007). Rigorous attention to the analysis of evidence and argumentation is a core feature of phronetic research and essential for constructing narrative explanations. Phronetic empirical analysis employs abductive and retroductive logic for interrogating interpretations, generating and testing evidence to strengthen the plausibility of explanations (Pedersen, 2009; Shotter & Tsoukas, 2014b; Thomas, 2010). Both approaches, which are not exclusive to phronetic research, use a dialogic strategy to interrogate takenfor-granted assumptions, implicit understandings and norms, in order to surface and test the situational-logic to build plausible explanations of the case under study.

The abductive approach is concerned with developing understandings and explanations of “the skilled accomplishments of competent social actors drawing on the mutual knowledge that the actors use to make sense of their activities” (Blaikie, 2010, pp. 89–90). While the retroductive approach is concerned with discovering and understanding the interlocking logics and conditions that are implicated in specific outcomes, decisions, and states-of-afairs (Blaikie, 2010; Harré, 1983). In phronetic research, the retroductive approach is concerned with uncovering taken-for-granted beliefs, socially relevant influences and social conditions that can explain events, decisions or states-of-afairs (cf. Durand & Vaara, 2009; Pawson, 2000). It is a process for reasoning backward from an outcome (event, decision or state-of-afairs) to discover the situational-logic embedded in socio-historic conditions, and/or institutional structures that can potentially give rise to the outcome.

## 2.3.2. The field of praxis

Phronetic IS research is concerned with the individual, organisational, social, and political impacts of IS. The fields of praxis are particular case situations within their specific historical and institutional contexts (Flyvbjerg, 2001; Toulmin, 1986). Decisions to build public infor mation infrastructure or implement certain information security or privacy policies fall into the class of problems of phronesis (Flyvbjerg, 2008; Toulmin, 1982). Phronetic IS research is concerned with interrogating politics, motives, and value rationale guiding our decisions to adopt (or not to adopt) digital innovations which could profoundly impact social well-being. For example, decision-making about mega infrastructure projects, such as the national roll-out of high speed Internet, are neither value free nor devoid of politics. They address concrete human problems, such as unhindered access to information or the protection of privacy. Decisions about regional availability, cost for the user, net neutrality, security regulation, etc., not only reflect political orientations and conflicting stakeholder interests, they have profound efects on the opportunities for millions of users and society at large (e.g., the digital divide). Phronetic IS research aims to contribute to the public discourse by illustrating mechanisms of power and sug gesting alternative paths to achieve societal well-being. As Flyvbjerg (2001) argues, the central concern of phro nesis is interrogating the underlying dynamics of decision-making that is consequential to society by posing four basic value questions: “(1) Where are we going? (2) Is this desirable? (3) What should be done? (4) Who wins and who loses; by which mechanism of power?” The epistemology of phronesis ofers flexibility that allows the phronimos, (after clarifying values, interests, power relations, and mechanisms) to make judgements about; (a) what should be done; (b) what could be done, or (c) give no advice on what is to be done. The phronimos opens to public discourse value conflicts and political dynamics, specific to the particulars of the case situation, which require resolution in the interest of the public good. The outcome of phronesis is not intended to be a general theory of action (as is the case with techné) but a critical explication of the risks and challenges we face and possibilities for their resolution (Flyvbjerg, 2016).

## 2.3.3. Historical and institutional context

Phronetic IS research is concerned with concrete human problems related to digital social transformation. As Flyvbjerg (1998) sees it, digital social transformation projects can be characterised by interest, opportunism, and power which distort rationality. Flyvbjerg argues that context is essential to interrogating the case given “that in the history of science, human action has shown itself to be irreducible to predefined elements and rules unconnected to interpretation” (2001, p. 136). Given the importance of Gadamerian hermeneutics to phronetic enquiry, the phronimos must focus on the actors and their practices in relation to social and institutional norms which orient them (Toulmin, 1976). Phronetic enquiry goes beyond the immediate, it aims to develop a wider horizon of possibilities and opportunities, and engages in processes of learning from and with others (Spinosa, Flores, & Dreyfus, 1997). Flyvbjerg explains the objective of phronetic research as follows:

It is a basic tenet of phronetic research that in so far as social and political situations become clear, they get clarified by detailed stories of who is doing what to whom. Such clarifications provide a main link to praxis. Hence a main task of phronetic research is to provide in-depth narratives of how power works and with what consequences, and to suggest how power might be changed and work with other consequences. The result of phronetic research is an account of the possibilities, problems, and risks we face in specific domains of social action. (Flyvbjerg, 2016)

The empirical investigation combines micro (the “particular” and the “local”) and macro analysis and embraces the complexity of the case. Bernstein (1986) argues that phronesis is exemplified by its integrative analysis of the particular and universal. Flyvbjerg (2004) also argues that phronetic analysis subsumes pre-given universals elevating the particulars of the case, and privileges “a form of reasoning which yields an ‘ethical know-how’ in which the universal and the particular are co-determined” (Flyvbjerg, 2004, p. 300). It is in critical examination of the history and politics of the case situation that power, motives, institutional constraints, and opportunities are clarified (Flyvbjerg, 2016). It is in discourse that issues are framed, controversies are articulated, positions are taken and collective momentum for action is achieved.

… In each case a few individuals or groups envisioned the need for some form of institutional change to address a problem or opportunity and then initiated actions consistent with their intentions. … change as emerging from a dialectical process in which opposing actors in the organizational field frame issues and construct networks in an attempt to introduce new institutional arrangements. (Hargrave & van de Ven, 2006, p. 865)

## 2.3.4. Values and well-being

Phronetic research shares a common orientation with other critical social theory approaches to IS research (cf. Klein, 2009; Myers & Klein, 2011; Ngwenyama, 1991; Rowe, 2009; Truex & Ngwenyama, 1998). It focuses on the praxis of using IS and the use of IS in and for praxis, and takes a particular interest in the intersection of technology and well-being, the social and the political. It looks at settings in which IS yield ambivalent or controversial outcomes. Yet it refrains from making grand, generalised claims, but tries to makes sense exploring the richness of particular situations and cases and their contexts. In this regard, phronetic enquiry aims at criti cally examining how discursive practices influence decision-making and action (cf. Jansson, 2014). Attention is given to the dialogue, words, and actions: what is done and what is said. Espoused values are examined for their efects on compromises and trade-ofs, as well as historical conditions and prevailing institutional arrangements. Winners and losers from the decisions and actions are identified, and the mechanisms of power employed to achieve the outcomes are critically examined. The empirical investigation is focused on critically examining the discourse and the structure of the arguments of the stakeholders, their backgrounds and worldviews. Actions are examined for fairness and openness or strategic and political orientation: Are pertinent issues ignored, taken of the agenda, suppressed or silenced?

The influence of stakeholders on collective action and outcomes are examined: Has the framing of the problem been contested? Have coalitions been formed to support the initiatives, and based on which values? Who takes responsibility and who accepts accountability? Is there a recognition that critique and inaction entails accountability? Arguments are examined for plausibility, consistence and credibility of evidence and backing. Phronetic enquiry has a strong background in dialogic and rhetorical reasoning and the ethics of dialogue (Gill, 1962). Thus, it looks at the substance of the discourse and the arguments presented as much as its tone and the related political or tactical manoeuvres.

## 3. Illustrating the research approach

## 3.1. The enquiry procedure

With the puzzle in focus and general principles of phronetic research discussed above, the enquiry unfolded as a multi-stage process using the five pointers in Table 1 (cf. Appendix A for warrants). Stage 1: We collected 10 years of data to enable a detailed understanding of the historical institutional context of the public discourse. Data were collected from media, published studies, discussion fora, and policy briefings (cf. Appendix B). Stage 2 and 3: We identified key stakeholders, critically analysed the discourse, and identified key historical events (cf. Appendix C) to understand their underlying motives and framing of issues. Stage 4: We analysed who has been winning and who has been losing as a way to make the power structures and motives more transparent. We investigated the role of collective action in erecting barriers to progress. We also examined whether stakeholders recognised and assumed accountability for their actions in the infrastructure failure. Moreover, we examined how the implementation of the infrastructure was enabled or constrained by existing institutional arrangements of the health care sector. Finally, using retroductive analysis we construct and test scenarios in order to posit the most plausible explanation for the infrastructure failure given the evidence. In line with principles of phronetic research, our interest is in identifying and providing plausible and substantive reasoning that exposes conditions and factors implicated in the failure. As in all critical social theory research, substantive argumentation is the device for unpacking and explicating the dynamics of power and rationality that is dysfunctional to progress and the achievement of the public good (Habermas, 1993; Rowe, 2009; Truex & Ngwenyama, 1998).

## 3.2. The puzzling case situation

By 2003 medication non-compliance among polyphar macy patients (individuals taking three or more diferent medications) in developed countries was recognised as a major public health problem in need of a solution (WHO, 2003). In Germany about 6.5 million individual are polypharmacy patients taking three or more medications (Stüve, Hildebrandt, Daul, & Salmon, 2008, p. 15), and noncompliance is estimated to cost 75 billion EUR annually (Weddige, 2013). Noncompliance varies from missing medication schedules, taking incorrect combi nations and compensatory behaviours like doubling for missed medications. A study by Palen and Aaløkke (2006) details the daily medication management struggles of elderly citizens, and their consequences. In this context Kohl Medical AG launched Assist Pharma in 2003 (later renamed 7x4 Pharma) and announced plans to build an automatic drug dispensing (ADD) infrastructure to produce medication blisters in support medication com pliance among polypharmacy patients. Medication blisters are individualised packs of medications (cf. Figure 1) that patients take at prescribed intervals; enabling them to easily follow their medication regimes. Kohl’s goal in manufacturing individualised blisters was: “to contribute to an ethical, human, and afordable life by ofering innovative solutions in the health sector” (Kohl Medical, n.d.). This innovation project was conceived as part of a comprehensive e-health strategy to improve health outcomes and reduce healthcare cost. The 7x4 Pharma project comprised facilities for manufacturing medication blisters and digital infrastructure to support: (a) Document Management for medication plans and prescriptions, (b) Decision Support for analyzing critical drug interactions, (c) Electronic Document Exchange among stakeholders (doctors, pharmacists, patients, etc.), and (d) Electronic Invoicing. The medication plan was a critical digital component enabling doctors and pharmacists to collaborate on providing ADD services to the poly-pharmacy patients (Ditzel, 2005).

In the following five years, 7x4 Pharma commissioned studies to substantiate the economic and safety related benefits of blistering and national ADD infrastructure (Ditzel, 2005; Lauterbach, Gerber, et al., 2004; Lauterbach, Gerber, et al., 2006; Lauterbach, Lüngen, et al., 2004; Lauterbach et al., 2007; Pharmafacts & Glaeske 2007). In 2009, 7x4 Pharma launched the ADD pilot project to prove the concept and provide evidence of its benefits to the healthcare system. The innovators tried to mobilize support from key stakeholders: pharmacists, physicians, statutory health insurance providers, and public oficials responsible for health care. They also tried to mobilize pharmacists and physicians to take responsibility for managing the medications of poly-pharmacy patients. Although the societal benefits were confirmed by several studies (Leker & Kehrel, 2011; Neubauer, 2011; Neubauer & Wick, 2011), strong opposition from the associations of general physicians (KBV) and national association of pharmacists (ABDA) resulted in the failure of this health innovation. This is a puzzling outcome given the strong evidence of its societal value, a national sensitivity to high quality patient care, and success of similar infrastructure projects in Denmark, Sweden, Norway and Finland (Sinnemäki et al., 2013).

## 4. The empirical analysis

## 4.1. Institutional context, stakeholders and governance structures

The German health care governance model is meant to provide expert participation and democratic decision making. The Federal Joint Committee (G-BA) is the self-governing body of the healthcare system.

Members of the G-BA are, states, the federal government, civil society organizations, representatives of healthcare providers, and health insurance providers who share decision-making power. The Federal Ministry of Health has mainly supervisory functions, it does not have direct influence on the health care stakeholders, but can make legislative proposals, e.g. health reform acts (Busse & Riesberg, 2004, p. 29 f.). In the public debate on the national ADD initiative, the National Association of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians (KBV) and the Federation of German Associations of Pharmacists (ABDA) and the Association of Research Active Pharmaceutical Manufacturers (VfA) and health insurance companies are the most powerful stakeholders. They oppose the national ADD infrastructure initiative as it will transform power relationships and accountable structures. The next set of stakeholders are the poly-pharmacy patients and care providers who are directly afected by medication noncompliance issue. These are proponents of blistering, and are supported by care homes and association of blister manufacturers (BVKA, BPAV, and DBU) cf. Appendix D for acronyms.

## 4.2. Critical analysis of the discourse

## 4.2.1. The opposition

Although blistering was done by regional centres and local pharmacies in Germany since 2000, the announcement of 7x4 Pharma pilot project triggered intense opposition from the VfA. They commissioned a study (Wille & Wolf, 2006) to assess the economic benefits of ADD. Using neo-liberal economic assumptions and modeling, Wille and Wolf (2006) argue that a national ADD infrastructure would likely lead to a monopoly and cause market distortions. They hypothesized a scenario in which: (1) pharmaceutical manufacturers lose revenue; (2) doctors lose freedom in prescribing medications for their patients; (3) pharmacists become dependent on the blister manufacturer; (4) existing regional blister centers are unsustainable against the monopolist; and (5) the health insurance and government regulator impose eficiencies on pharmaceutical manufacturers. They also argue that patients who demand dose administration aids show already a level of conscientiousness that providing ADD would not increase their compliance, hence ADD would not be efective (Wille & Wolf, 2006). A well-known lobbyist, Bellartz, echoed the monopoly charge claiming: “the only thing Kohl [CEO, Kohl Group] wants to achieve with industrial blistering is vertical integration of the pharmacy market controlled by himself ” (Bellartz, 2006). Subsequently, the ABDA took a stance that too few pharmacists would profit from ADD and its chief legal counsel claimed – counterfactually – no studies show benefits from blistering (Schumbach, 2013).

In 2009, the 7x4 Pharma pilot opened a production facility with a weekly capacity for 600,000 medication blisters. The monopoly claim was refuted when a second service provider, AvidiaMed launched a competing pilot. The successful operation of these pilots engendered further opposition. The association of statutory physicians and the association of pharmacies for the State of Brandenburg published a joint position paper (LLB, 2011) rejecting the national ADD initiative. This paper repeated the claims: the national ADD initiative was neither beneficial to polypharmacy patients nor economically viable. However, the association of individual blister companies (BPAV, 2011) published a rebuttal pointing out serious flaws in the LLB paper. In 2014, yet another position paper (LT, 2014) attacked the pilot projects repeating the discredited claims of Wille and Wolf (2006). Again the blister association issued a rebuttal pointing out the misrepresentation of facts (“Holdermann”, 2014).

In 2011, ignoring the success of the pilot projects, the ABDA and KBV made a new proposal for medication management (ABDA and KBV, 2011a, 2011b). They defined five principles: (1) a catalogue of recommended medicines for primary care; (2) prescription of active ingredients instead of branded medicine; (3) physician and pharmacist to be responsible for polypharmacy patients for one year; (4) comprehensive medication plans for polypharmacy patients to tackle critical drug interactions; and (5) joint reimbursement at the suggested level of 360 € per patient per year. The proposal however did not address the issue of ADD infrastructure development. In 2011, the 7x4 Pharma pilot infrastructure was shut down. Kohl cited resistance from the ABDA and KBV, and regulatory gaps inhibiting profitability as reasons for the termination. The next year, the German government granted the ABDA and KBV permission for regional pilots. In 2013, pilot project developments started in Saxonia and Thuringia. In 2013, AvidiaMed discontinued its pilot, citing resistance from the ABDA and KBV, and lack of regulatory support.

## 4.2.2. The style of the discourse

The discourse is combative: the more powerful opposition (VfA, ABDA, and KBV) used their considerable resources to undermine and delegitimise the 7x4 Pharma ADD infrastructure project. The supporters (blister manufacturers, BPAV, BVKA, DBU, health insurance providers, care homes) of blistering focused on solving medication non-compliance. Kohl, AvidiaMed, and AOK Insurance invested in empirical studies to ensure a viable ADD infrastructure. They also conducted and evaluated pilot studies using G-BA’s regulations for e-health infrastructures (G-BA, n.d.). The opponents ignored the pilot projects, as well as the patients’ needs and avoided debates on design criteria. Instead, they tried to redefine the reality and undermine the legitimacy of the initiative. They claimed the regulator was creating a monopoly situation with potential adverse efects for doctors and pharmaceutical manufacturers. Although empirical reality falsified this claim, it was repeated throughout debate. Necessary dialogue on the design of infrastructure never emerged, because by engaging in dialogue the opponents would signal acceptance of the value rational for the national ADD infrastructure initiative. Moreover, the proposed infrastructure would force changes in power relations among the stakeholders. Consequently, the opponents remained focused on obstructing debate and consensus formation.

The opposition’s strategy focused on misinformation and arguing for maintaining the status quo, despite its obvious societal costs. They claimed that if the polypharmacy would conscientiously manage their medication there would be no need for ADD services. They questioned the credibility of blister operators and suggested that the Kohl Group had a hidden agenda of market control. Third, they tried to undermine economic viability of the ADD infrastructure by arguing for user paid blister services. Then in 2011, after years of resistance, they announced a new concept for medication management (ABDA and KBV, 2011a). However, the concept lacked operational procedures and the related pilot project has been significantly delayed by uncertainties around reimbursement for services. In 2017, they again attempted to delay critical components of the digital infrastructure by claiming the software deployed in pilot projects is not yet reliable. Their strategy seems to fit Flyvbjerg’s (1998) observation that:

lies are more useful to its purposes than truth and rationality … when powerful groups stand to gain from propagating certain interpretations, rationalizations and lies about reality. (p. 230)

The self-governing health care model, enacted to ensure democratic consensus decision-making in the interest of society ofered no defence against manipulation of self-interested the stakeholders. It enabled the powerful stakeholders to inhibit progress, and the regulator showed a reluctance to intervene until the process failed.

## 4.3. Looking for motive

Kohl Medical is an innovative company involved in importing and re-packaging of medication. It identified an opportunity to leverage its competence to address the public health issue of medication noncompliance. However, developing a national ADD infrastructure puts them in competition with pharmacies involved in blistering and blister centres. The 7x4 Pharma initiative has the potential to provide the technical expertise to provide safe and afordable blisters as well as economies of scale. Kohl’s prior innovations of buying drugs at lower prices under the EU single market framework and importing them to Germany is supported by the regulator (Federal Ministry of Health) whose interest is in health care cost reduction. This has angered the research oriented pharmaceutical companies who want to charge higher prices in the German market. If Kohl’s latest innovation is successful it could convince the regulator to introduce blistering as a standard option for polypharmacy patients covered by the statutory health insurance. This could radically change the business model of a lucrative market segment dominated by the research-oriented pharmaceutical companies. They are alarmed by the prospect of losing revenue due to reductions in medication wastage (about 10%, Möhlmann et al., 2011, p. 71). Moreover, they are concerned the digital medication plan and a digital infrastructure would enable Pharma 7x4 to use imports and generic drugs in blisters. This could further hurt the revenues of the research-oriented pharmaceutical companies. The VfA commissioned 2006 study enabled them to stay in the background while exercising powerful influence in the debate.

The doctors’ and pharmacists’ associations fearing potential losses also used their vast resources and privi leged positions to defend the status quo and inhibit progress of a national ADD infrastructure. They too view digitalisation as disruptive to traditional practices of the medical and pharmacy professions. A digital medica tion plan would introduce a new business model with significant economic eficiencies in health care system. For doctors and pharmacists, a digital infrastructure implies unprecedented visibility over their interactions with patients. The doctors are also concerned about changes in their responsibilities for polypharmacy patients. The new medication management requires: compiling medication plans, analysing drug interactions and monitoring patients while reimbursement for such services are unclear. Both doctors and pharmacists, are also concerned about information quality and privacy in the digital infrastructure and its interoperability with their local IT systems. Digital functionality, such as med ication management, tracking of polypharmacy patients and decision support for analysing complex drug interactions, introduces an unprecedented level of visibility into health care practices (ABDA, 2014a, 2014b). This increased transparency exposes them to scrutiny and accountability from insurance companies and the government regulator (“Rabatte”, 2015). Furthermore, a digital medication plan could enable disintermediation; patients can interact directly with new digital entrants (online pharmacies, international chains, etc.) bypassing existing market structures. This is a potential “Pandora’s box”, which they don’t want to open. In resisting the digital infrastructure, ABDA and KVB prioritised the interests of powerful members over the interest of the polypharmacy patients. They erect barriers to high quality care for polypharmacy patients when their own interests are at stake.

## 4.4. Outcomes: winners and losers

Presently, we see positive and negative outcomes of the controversy and some winners and losers. The discussion of a comprehensive medication management is a positive outcome. There is substantial progress on public policy for medication therapy safety and medication management. Kohl had made it clear that he endorses this development, and sees himself as the originator of the concept of medication management (“7x4”, 2014). The 2011 ABDA and KBV position paper on medication review and medication management signalled acceptance of key elements of the eHealth initiative, proposed a pilot study and a timetable for implementing medication management. However, this initiative efectively delayed the difusion of medication management. The polypharmacy patients, care homes, regional blister operators, 7x4 Pharma, AvidiaMed, the statutory health insurance and the German people are the big losers. The resistance of ABDA and KBV has efectively slowed infrastructure development and the status quo prevails (Winnat, 2011). The polypharmacy patients still do not have a voice in the discourse, because they are mostly elderly and not individually powerful. Delays in the national ADD infrastructure denies support to care givers and care options for polypharmacy patients. The patients are still at risk of drug interactions and those who pay for blister services cannot claim reimbursement from the statutory health insurance (Sorensen et al., 2005).

Finally, in 2015 the German government felt compelled to pass a new eHealth law, to remove barriers to innovation. This law mandates medication planning for polypharmacy patients with financial penalties for non-compliance by the doctors’ association. In expressing his dismay at the continued resistance and lack of progress, the Minister of Health stated:

Losing time now is a costly endeavor and hurts everyone – in every respect.… in Germany, unfortunately, more and more people are dying through unwanted drug efects than in road trafic. A digital overview of prescription medicines can be a real advance here, especially for elderly people living alone. … I don’t understand why those who have been blocking in the past are coming back yet again and want to stop this great progress in the digital age of health care with specious arguments. …. Anyone who refuses to take this step because of self-interest hurts the common welfare. This is why the “e-health law” that we are now launching also contains a simple principle: whoever blocks, will have to pay. (Gröhe, 2015, translated from German)

## 5. Explaining the dynamics of failure

Detailed analysis of the historical and institutional context of case situation, stakeholders’ commitments, motives and actions, reveals a broader picture of the challenges to national ADD infrastructure innovation in general and the Kohl Medical AG pilot in particular. Figure 2 below is an influence diagram mapping the situational logic of positive and negative influencing dynamics implicated in the failure of the national ADD initiative. When viewed in this wider background context, the Kohl Medical AG pilot initiative aligns with public policy objectives for innovation in medication management to improve care for polypharmacy patients and introduce health care system eficiencies through digitalisation. However, the national ADD infrastructure initiative held far-reaching implications for existing professional practices and their regulation, as well as the establishment of wider eHealth infrastructure. Consequently, it engendered collective action and resistance from powerful stakeholders and failed. Our phronetic enquiry suggests that the following interconnected factors can plausibly explain the failure: (1) Institutional constraints, inadequacies of democratic self-governance and reluctance of the regulator to intervene and steer the consensus formation process; (2) collective resistance of key stakeholders in the health care system; and (3) socio-technical challenges of adopting a new infrastructure in the complex institutional structure of the health care system. These three factors will need to be carefully considered and appropriate solutions designed in order to unblock the health care innovation process.

## 5.1. Institutional constraints and inadequacies of self-governance

As in other developed countries, the German population is ageing and the cost of health care is escalating. The government must act to reduce the escalating cost of running the national health care system. The goal is to seek eficiencies in various aspects of the health care system while improving patient care. The strategy for this institutional innovation is focussed on facilitating entrepreneurial competition in creating new business models and infrastructure (Hargrave & van de Ven, 2006). The national ADD infrastructure initiative (specifically the 7x4 Pharma pilot) can be viewed as a vehicle for jumpstarting health care systems innovation which had stalled. The dedicated action plans on medication therapy safety starting in 2007 and 2013 (BG, 2013), the 2017 e-health law and the health minister’s comments (Gröhe, 2015) illustrate the central importance of health care innovation and eHealth infrastructure. It appears to be purposeful strategic action by the government to use medication safety as a generative mechanism for institutional change in the health care system. Moreover, public health concerns over medication non-compliance among polypharmacy patients, together with citizen commitment to high quality health care strengthens the government’s hand in pushing for innovation (B-BRD, 2015). However, institutional arrangements and the political dynamics of self-governance constrain the Ministry of Health’s plans for rapid progress.

The self-governing body (G-BA) of the health care system exhibited structural inadequacies for ensuring the public good. The G-BA failed in collaborative decision-making for innovation, health care improvements and accountability to patients and society. Selfgovernance requires a deliberate search for consensus among the stakeholders. This requirement of consensus decision-making can enable powerful members of the G-BA to slow and inhibit progress. While the medication compliance problems are well understood, the value rationale for digital innovation was well supported and there is willingness and commitment (by 7x4 Pharma and AvidiaMed) to deliver a national ADD infrastructure for the public good, consensus never emerged and the process was gridlocked by self-interest and politics (cf. Stüve et al., 2008). Dialogue is essential for launch ing a national ADD infrastructure, but by engaging in dialogue the opposition would signal acceptance of the value rational. The opposition’s fear of Kohl Group’s innovation capabilities and their market impacts led them to pursue an obstructionist strategy. However, the regulator was reluctant to intervene to facilitate dialogue among the stakeholders, unblock obstacles and steer the process to a successful outcome. The arm’s length principle and reluctance of the regulator to intervene served to embolden powerful stakeholders to pursue narrow self-interest. As Flyvbjerg (1998) points out, powerful business interests are substantially more skilled than public administrators in manipulating democratic processes to maintain the status quo and reproduce power relations. A related issue, not articulated directly in relation to blistering, is the widespread resistance against eHealth initiatives (e.g., Wessel et al., 2017). Proposals to introduce electronic prescriptions or digital patient medication records have faced profound resistance and have not yet been implemented in Germany. The successful difusion of ADD would require an underlying digital infrastructure such as a national e-prescription system, the achievement of which will require more forceful regulatory intervention. This interpretation is supported by the 2015 eHealth initiative of the Ministry of Health, which clearly conveys a sense of impatience with the blockade of the self-governing bodies of the health care providers and includes costly sanctions in case the timelines are not met (Gröhe, 2015).

## 5.2. Mobilised resistance of key stakeholders

The powerful resistance strategy of the key stakeholders can be characterised by three factors: (1) mobilising; (2) political opportunity; and (3) framing processes. Our analysis reveals that primary opposition came from research-active pharmaceutical companies, who mobilised their association, the VfA, to oppose blistering. For political reasons they targeted the 7x4 Pharma pilot and framed the issues for resistance to blistering around it. They declared blistering inefective and Kohl’s motive was monopoly control, detrimental to an eficient health care system. The Kohl Group was a fierce health care market competitor for whom they held deep distrust and resentment. The pharmaceutical companies, pharmacists and doctors regarded Kohl’s innovations in health care market with suspicion and antagonism. In 1979,

Kohl founded Kohlpharma and built it into a leading importer of pharmaceuticals. Kohlpharma imports products of these pharmaceutical manufacturers from other European countries at lower prices and sells them in Germany at a discount, saving the German health care system 300 million Euros annually. Moreover, the German regulator endorses this practice as it reduces medication cost of the statutory health insurance fund. In 2004 Kohl started Avie, a pharmacy franchise chain (now 200 members), focused on individual health management and medication therapy consulting. Consequently, the majority of pharmacists also view Kohl as a competitor, even though they are customers of Kohlpharma’s lower priced drugs, and are resistant to new entrants (Kohl or Körber) into their market. Pharmacists active in blistering and blister centres also view the 7x4 Pharma pilot as a potentially formidable competitor, but they are ambivalent as Kohl has pushed for polypharmacy patients blisters to be included in health insurance benefits. This would benefit all pharmacists and blister centres, but Kohl’s size, logistics and innovation capabilities would result in economies of scale and market domination.

The association of large research-active pharma companies (VfA) envisaged an overall loss of revenue in a lucrative market segment, if ADD would be successful and live up to its promise to reduce medication waste by up to 10% in the target segment. They also envisioned the ADD pilot initiative as an incremental step in the transition from using retail-size packages to less expensive bulk packages of medication as input for the blister production (cf. Wille & Wolf, 2006). Moreover, there was concern that ADD providers would obtain control over the choice of medication included in their blisters and for technical and economic reasons the ADD operator might shift to a small set of medication with the same active ingredients, rather than the full assortment of brands. Given the power and interests of pharmacies and physician associations, their collective action resulted in an asymmetric burden of proof. The ADD proponents had to follow specific procedures, collect evidence, etc., while the powerful opposition did not provide evidence, nor were they held accountable for their opinions. While many of their specific concerns with the ADD design were addressed in the second pilot project, the opposition did not revise their negative opinions and resistance.

## 5.3. Infrastructure adoption challenges

While political action is a major inhibitor of institutional innovation, there are also socio-technical challenges to adopting the new infrastructure. First, traditional institutional practices and routines of patient health management are deeply embedded, resist uprooting, and inhibit adoption/transition to the new infrastructure routines (Cho et al., 2007; Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010). As Aanestad et al. (2005) argue, historical processes reinforce practices and routines, institutionalising, and embedding them into the background context of action, making their uprooting very dificult (see also Aanestad & Jensen, 2011; Marsan et al., 2017). Successful introduction of ADD infrastructure necessitates a transformation and extension of patient medication management and information sharing practices. Transformation in these practices will create uncertainties as to who – physician or pharmacist – will be in charge, what will be the basis of reimbursement (if any) and how coordination between physician or/and pharmacist will be organised. Therefore, opposition from doctors and pharmacists is understandable, and can be explained by what Besson and Rowe (2012) term psychological inertia. Doctors fear further loss of control over their choice of medication, which has already started as a result of health insurance policies. And pharmacists fear losing revenue from new entrants due to business model disruption (Online pharmacies) and new ADD operators.

Second, there are dificulties in adopting a new medication management infrastructure before the professional institutions (ABDA, KBV) are prepared. The scale and complexity of the health care system with its varied professional practitioners requires the building of momentum for the transformation of professional norms and practices (Davidson & Chiasson, 2005; King, 2013). This highlights the temporal dynamics of infrastructure development and evolution. The installed base is a platform and background to which careful attention must be given if new specialised infrastructures will be built upon or transform it (Bygstad & Hanseth, 2010; Klein & Schellhammer, 2017). The government was hoping that success of the national ADD information infrastructure pilots (7x4 Pharma and AvidiaMed), would accelerate transition to a new digital health information infrastructure and adjustment and adaptation by the health care professional institutions. However, political momentum and regulatory steering mechanisms proved inadequate. Kohl also failed to influence the regulator to provide a legal framework, which would simplify the participation of patients, doctors, pharmacists, and insurance companies necessary to successfully up-scale the 7x4 Pharma pilot infrastructure. Insurance companies aided by academics acknowledged the benefits of the solution for geriatric care (Stüve et al., 2008, p. 10) but did not actively influence the outcome.

## 6. Discussion and implications for IS discipline

In this paper, we presented an approach to conducting phronetic social science enquiry based on the contemporary interpretations of Aristotle’s phronesis by Toulmin and Flyvbjerg, and illustrated its appropriateness to developing ethical actionable knowledge in IS. Our objective is to demonstrate phronetic enquiry as a pragmatic method, “appropriate in dealing with concrete human problems” (Toulmin, 1992, p. 193), and suitable for a critically reflexive social science practice accountable to the same standards it applies to research. In the above sections we outlined key principles of phronetic social science enquiry (Table 1 and Appendix A) and illustrated their application on a case study of an IS infrastructure failure. Specifically, we are interested in illustrating how the phronetic method can reveal the underlying situational logic influencing the failure to adopt an infrastructure which is generally believed to support the public good. Doing phronetic research entails an extensive engagement with the case to uncover and explore hidden worldviews of the stakeholders. In demonstrating the phronetic of enquiry, we tried to emphasise how the holistic approach and orientation of phronetic social science is especially applicable for IS research on information infrastructures and specifically digital transformation or digital disruption of societies. We are careful to recognise the role of the researcher as a phronimos and discuss some of the methodological implications of treating practitioners and phronetic researchers symmetrically. Phronetic social science enquiry requires the researcher to make judgements about individuals and institutions on the basis of their espoused and their enacted values. However, the researcher must be critically self-reflexive and open to deliberative discourse within the community of scientists (Flyvbjerg, 2000; Toulmin, 1999). Furthermore, as Gadamer (1977, p. 189) commenting on accountability in phronesis explains: “there is no higher principle than holding oneself open in a conversation”. We also highlight the rhetoric tradition of phronesis and therefore relate it to discourse analysis, argumentation theory (Toulmin, 1958) and the use of inferential techniques, abduction, and retroduction to analyse claims (Landman, 2012).

The complexity of the case required extensive engagement with multiple facets and forgoes the typical ana lytical approach, which focuses on isolable cause-efect explanations. Instead, we enrolled values-based reasoning and prudence in critically interrogating the public discourse and stakeholder actions. We critically examined what appeared as political inertia (Besson & Rowe, 2012) and strategic manoeuvring in the self-interests of key stakeholders, which was in contradiction to their stated and implied commitments to seeking the common good of the wider society. For over seven years, we have been collecting evidence, observing, and engaging with stakeholders in order to comprehend the discrepancy between words and deeds, and the entangled phenomena and narratives. We take a long term perspective critically reflecting on our earlier, preliminary assessment in light of recent events (“The benefit of the hindsight”). This also implies that our findings inevitably remain tentative, part of an ongoing learning process and an invitation for dialogue, clarification, extension, and possibly refinement. In conducting our enquiry, we used our own judgement in making sense of the situation and tried to understand what might be ethical and appropriate action under the circumstances. Personal insights into the situation of polypharmacy patients provided part of the horizon of understanding, without getting unduly emotionally involved. Rhetorical analysis was employed to disentangle claims and critically examine the public discourse. Retroduction was used to identify and test possible explanations for the failure of the national ADD infrastructure initiative. However, as we implemented Flyvbjerg’s advice to interrogate history, institutional context and particulars of the case situation we realised that while specific actions of the opponents can plausible explain the failure of the infrastructure initiative, they need to be historically located within a context of broader challenges to institutional innovation in the German health care system.

Our enquiry revealed that the ADD initiative was not only an attempt to build and extend a medication management information infrastructure, but to explore and propose alternative models of geriatric medication therapy. Such models would require professional collaboration among diferent health service providers, comprehensive documentation of medication regimes, decision support and information exchange. Our research also reveals a startling discrepancy between: (a) a broad consensus and common sense concerning medication safety and compliance and (b) severe resistance to an infrastructure, which could facilitate a solu tion to the problem. The resistance seems to fit a pattern of defending the current medication market structure and impeding innovation in the German health care system. Our research also raised questions concerning the government regulator’s apparent unwillingness to intervene and unblock the barriers to the infrastructure development even though it has the power and authority to do so. Does adherence to principles of self-govern ance of professional associations and private enterprises trump intervention in the defence of ordinary citizens? From the perspective of the immediate public good this seems to be a value conflict of public management of the G-BA that must be resolved. Furthermore, the deliberative processes of the G-BA should be open and include rules and procedures to exclude members from the decision-making discourse when their interests involve significant conflicts with the public good. In the absence of such rules, democratic self-governing processes are undermined by strategic discourses for the maintenance of stakeholder interests (Flyvbjerg, 1998; Habermas, 2006). As can be seen from our empirical findings, the outcomes of phronetic enquiry are not always tidy explanations as might be expected of tra ditional social science research. However, these are the types of practical situations that Aristotle envisioned appropriate to phronesis and the generation of ethically applicable knowledge, a narrative useful to scholars and practitioners which ofers not only better understanding of some of the intricacies of the case, but a basis for continued discourse, probing, and interrogation of paths for reconciling our value conflicts for the public good (Flyvbjerg, 2000, 2006; Toulmin, 1985).

## 7. Conclusion

Presently, our societies are facing new intractable value conflicts emerging from the rapid colonisation and transformation of our social world by digital technologies. As IS scientists we are deeply implicated in the ethical dilemmas of digitisation. We promote the instrumental value (eficiency and efectiveness) of IS technologies for increasing economic returns while espousing that IS science is value free. However, ethical dilemmas we now face challenge us to deliberate about the societal costs of IS technologies, and their impacts on individual and social wellbeing (eudaimonia). Some examples that illustrate these ethical dilemmas are: Facebook’s social experiment on 700,000 individuals who were neither informed nor had their consent solicited (Arthur, 2014; Flick, 2016). The circumventing of worker protection laws by Uber and other labour crowd sourcing tech nologies which lead to worker precarity (De Stefano, 2015; Horton, 2011; Isaac, 2014; Prassl & Risak, 2017). Attempts to manipulate the 2016 US presidential elections using bots and social media platforms to propagate fake ads and fake news (Bessi & Ferrara, 2016; Metaxas & Pruksachatkun, 2017). Plans for expansive and intensive automation of ordinary human activity, knowledge work and skilled labour (Dregger et al., 2016; Rajnai & Kocsis, 2017). Easy access to information, misinformation, and handheld digital devices that is undermining our cognitive capabilities, degrading our critical thinking, and endangering our psychological well-being as well as our personal privacy and security (Glanz et al., 2014; Mazmanian et al., 2013; Samaha & Hawi, 2016; Sparrow et al., 2011). The classes of problems that these ethical dilemmas present are beyond the scope of our methods for episteme and techné, thus requiring us to reach beyond them.

As IS scientists we are uniquely positioned, and have the scientific legitimacy to generate knowledge on the intersection of digital technologies and the social world. However, as some IS scholars argue, we are lacking research approaches for generating ethically applicable knowledge, even though IS research and practice involve value conflicts as a matter of course (Constantinides et al., 2012; Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Mingers & Walsham, 2010). For example, the dominant approaches to IS research focus narrowly on economic rationale, while ignoring the increasing and persisting challenges of social value conflicts in decision-making concerning the adoption and use of IS technologies in society (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Kling, 1992; Stahl, 2008; Whitley & Hosein, 2008). As social scientists developing knowledge about digitalisation of organisations and society, we cannot ignore the transforming efects and dualistic outcomes on the work, public and private lives of individuals (Schultze, 2017). It is time we take IS value conflicts seriously and take responsibility for the conse quences of our work, because as Toulmin (1985) argued, “the assumption that science is value-free or that scientist bear no responsibility for the social consequences of their work” is untenable. Recently, Hirschheim and Klein (2003), Klein and Rowe (2008) and Constantinides et al. (2012) have suggested that Aristotelian phronesis can enhance IS research. Although phronetic research is making increasing contributions to knowledge in other branches of social science (cf. Shotter & Tsoukas, 2014a; Tsoukas & Cummings, 1997), it is still not widely embraced in the IS discipline. We believe that there are four compelling reasons for adopting phronesis to extend the repertoire of existing IS research approaches: (1) Digital technologies are transforming our social world with profound consequences which must be critically investigated. (2) Most current IS research approaches (except critical theory) assume value neutrality, but digital technologies are not value neutral; there are winners and losers. (3) Presently, decision-making about societal digitalisation is left to few stakeholders (Google, Facebook, Microsoft, Uber, etc.) whose interest is profit maximisation. In an open democratic society IS scientists have a responsibility to generate knowledge that enhances the society’s capabilities to preserve and enrich the commonwealth. (4) The dilemmas we face are transdisciplinary, requiring researchers to engage with the concrete historical, ethical, and political realities that condition decision-making about embedding of digital technologies into the social fabric of our societies. Systematic research into these classes of problems will require methodological guidance exemplified by phronetic social science (Flyvbjerg, 2016; Long, 2002; Toulmin, 1992).

## Table A5. The researchers’ stance

Both authors have a deep interest in understanding how decision making about digital infrastructures impacts everyday life in democratic societies. One of the authors has been studying the development of e-health infrastructures for about 10 years and the case of ADD infrastructure for more than five years. He was surprised by the uncompromising opposition of the doctors and phar macists’ associations in light of international experience and indeed national use of blistering. We view fair and open discourse – including a discourse about technology – as an important democratic ideal. We also acknowledge the need for inter-professiona collaboration and “controlled experiments” as way of learning, disclosing and extending possibilities for social research on technology challenges facing society. We see personal and institutional responsibility and accountability as core societal values. We both play multiple roles in health care systems: as researchers, citizens covered by health insurance, patients, and one of the authors is a care-taker of a close relative who is a polypharmacy patient in the German health care system. One of the researchers has insider knowledge of the case situation from seven years of participation in the study of blistering in a national and international context. He had extensive informal conversations with key actors in the field and participated in the Blister Symposium 2015 as speaker on international trends in blistering. This insider knowledge was important in analysing and interpreting the data. We have studied medication compliance and blistering across numerous, mainly in European countries, to become sensitive to diferent rationale, concerns and practical issues. We both also study public processes of digital infrastructures adoption at the societal level.
