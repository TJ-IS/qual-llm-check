# The role of context in it assimilation: A multimethod study of a SaaS platform in the US nonprofit sector

Ryan T. Wright<sup>1</sup>, Nicholas Roberts<sup>2</sup> and David Wilson<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA, USA; <sup>2</sup>College of Business, Colorado State University, Fort Collins, CO, USA; <sup>3</sup>Price College of Business, University of Oklahoma, Norman, OK, USA

Correspondence: Ryan T. Wright, McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA, USA E-mail: rtwright@virginia.edu

## Abstract

Nonprofit organizations (NPOs) operate in environments characterized by growing competition for resources and greater stakeholder demands for accountability, which makes deploying and maintaining information systems a struggle. In this competitive, resource-constrained environment, enterprise Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) platforms offer NPOs a cost-effective way access reliable, low-maintenance information technology services. Thus, the extent to which NPOs assimilate SaaS is an important area of inquiry. Yet despite the wealth of research on organizational IT assimilation, we know little regarding whether, when, and how NPOs assimilate IT innovations. We further our understanding of NPO assimilation of SaaS by conducting a context-based study. To do so, we employ multiple methods with data collected from US-based NPOs executives. Our first study showed that organizational factors (e.g., readiness, top management participation) and environmental factors (e.g., social gains, industry) affect the degree to which NPOs assimilate SaaS. However, we also found that technological factors do not appear to play a significant role in NPO SaaS assimilation. We conducted a phenomenological analysis to shed further light on this unexpected finding. Our analysis found that readiness to adopt SaaS, perceptions about SaaS complexity, and the use of outside consultants all played a key role in NPOs’ assimilation process.

## Introduction

There is consensus among academics and practitioners regarding the complexity associated with the assimilation of new information technology (IT) within organizations. A recent industry survey revealed that over two-thirds of large-scale enterprise system implementation projects provided the responding organization with less than 50% of the expected benefits (Panorama Consulting, 2014). Prior research has found that organizations may not fully realize expected benefits from new technologies without these technologies’ deep assimilation (Cooper & Zmud, 1990) and has noted a troubling ‘‘assimilation gap’’ between rates of initial adoption and full assimilation (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997). Even in the face of these difficulties, organizations continue to invest significantly in information systems initiatives, with continued increases projected in the coming years (Columbus, 2015).

With the recent availability of enterprise Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) platforms, there is a new opportunity to apply and evaluate past assimilation research within a new context. Enterprise SaaS is a new information systems paradigm, encompassing software applications, data, and other services accessible over the Internet and available to multiple concurrent users. SaaS platform providers turn the traditional install-based computing paradigm on its head in at least three substantive ways – maintenance, cost, and location. Whereas few organizations possess the ability to maintain the infrastructure necessary to support software across many devices, most organizations can afford to use Internet-based services and applications found on remote hosts. Moreover, because SaaS providers charge only for actual use, rather than for access to the software, they generally offer lowercost services than traditional software providers. Finally, whereas traditional applications are tied to specific machines and locations, SaaS is location-agnostic, with users able to securely access data and services anywhere via the Internet. Consequently, SaaS has made enterprisequality platforms accessible to a broader range of organizations, with significant performance benefits attributed to their use (Battleson et al, 2015).

The maintenance, cost, and location advantages found in SaaS may cause organizations to follow different paths when innovating with SaaS-based applications versus non-SaaS-based applications. For example, past research has posited that organizational readiness plays a significant role in an organization’s decision to adopt EDI systems (Chwelos et al, 2001). EDI has higher levels of IT sophistication and needs more financial resources – which provide greater readiness – to facilitate smoother adoption and assimilation. SaaS does not necessarily depend upon a sophisticated IT infrastructure. Organizations may thus need to account for a distinct set of factors when innovating with SaaS. While organizations may still engage in high-level IT innovation processes in the SaaS arena (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004), there may also be unique technological and environmental challenges along the way.

Enterprise SaaS platform pricing models also offer unique opportunities for organizations other than large, for-profit corporations. Nonprofit organizations (NPOs) are one type of organization that can benefit greatly from the accessibility of the various forms of enterprise SaaS solutions. However, NPOs have customarily approached enterprise information technologies with caution (Burt & Taylor, 2003; Hackler & Saxton, 2007; Zorn et al, 2010). NPOs struggle to manage data because they lack the time, money, and abilities required to deploy enterprise-wide data management applications (West & Green, 2008). As SaaS requires minimal infrastructure, NPOs with a staff as small as five people and total revenue under one million USD can use the same enterprise software used by multibillion dollar organizations. Further, efficient economies of scale allow many cloud providers to offer deep discounts to NPOs. As a result, enterprise SaaS platforms offer NPOs a cost-effective means to secure services that enable collecting, managing, and analyzing data (Zhang et al, 2010).

While cloud computing technology holds great potential benefits for NPOs, scant research informs our understanding of why or how these organizations might assimilate such technologies. Research suggests that an array of factors including environmental context (Thatcher et al, 2006), external technical support (Hackler & Saxton, 2007), and revenue (Shi, 2013) discriminate among those NPOs that assimilate versus fail to assimilate new information technologies. However, because SaaS differs from traditional computing in important ways, we suspect that the traditional factors identified in the extant NPO assimilation literature may not apply to this context. Hence, we seek to examine IT assimilation through a contextualized lens, namely NPOs assimilating an enterprise SaaS platform.

Following recent calls for ‘‘examining the use of IT in its context’’ (Te’eni, 2015: p. 362) and for examining IT within the nonprofit context (Zhang et al, 2010), we have drawn on recent guidelines for context-specific theorizing in IS research to offer insights into diffusion of IT in the nonprofit space (Hong et al, 2013). Specifically, we draw on the rich tradition of assimilation research to examine ‘‘situational opportunities and constraints’’ (Johns, 2006: p. 386) for NPO assimilation of enterprise SaaS platforms. Context-specific theorizing provides a number of advantages when exploring a unique IS context. It allows us to examine the interplay between an IT artifact and other organizational factors (Hong et al, 2013), ideal for our investigation of SaaS in a nonprofit context. Context-specific theorizing also allows us to evaluate how context enhances or inhibits common phenomena described in a more general theory (Johns, 2006). Because this method allows us to forgo parsimony to capture the impact of contextual factors, we can also better assess the applicability of our findings (Hong et al, 2013). Taken together, these advantages offer a rich opportunity in which to pursue a context-specific research question.

We illuminate our study context by employing a multimethod approach to investigate factors that shape the assimilation of an enterprise SaaS platform among NPOs. First, we adopted a general theory (the technologyorganization-environment framework) to examine NPO SaaS assimilation. Our empirical results showed that organizational and environmental factors affect NPOs’ SaaS assimilation; however, technological factors do not appear to play a role in SaaS assimilation. Leveraging this analysis as a diagnostic tool, our second objective is to contextualize and refine the theory to the NPO SaaS domain. We addressed this objective by conducting a phenomenological analysis of qualitative data. We found that readiness to adopt SaaS, user perceptions about SaaS complexity, and the use of external consultants all played an important role in whether, when, and how NPOs assimilated SaaS. Finally, we investigated an alternative

NPO enterprise SaaS platform assimilation model. This investigation reveals context-specific findings that further our understanding of enterprise SaaS assimilation in the nonprofit context. In summary, our three objectives provide a more complete picture of why and how NPOs assimilate SaaS platforms in their activities and routines.

## Literature review

Comparing the public, private, and nonprofit sectors The public administration literature has typically distinguished between three types of organizations: public, private, and nonprofit. Whereas private and nonprofit organizations are independent of governmental support and administrative control, public organizations are managed and supported by a government (Sheh, 1989). Public organizations have a so-called public mission, are typically engaged in providing what economists refer to as public goods (e.g., law enforcement, public schooling, roads and transportation services), and are largely or wholly dependent on government support (i.e., allocations from taxes). In contrast, private and nonprofit organizations are self-managed entities, largely free from dependence on government subsidies, and thus enjoy comparatively little governmental oversight.

Private, for-profit organizations such as corporations, partnerships, or owner-managed businesses also differ substantially from NPOs such as charities, associations, or religious entities. These two sectors differ in terms of their purpose and goals (e.g., profit maximization vs. improving community welfare), sources of funding (sales revenue vs. donations), and other managerial or marketing practices (Nutt, 2006; Rangan et al, 1996; Sukel, 1978). These differences present a compelling opportunity to leverage the nonprofit context for greater insight since, as we shall see below, the vast majority of IT assimilation research has been developed and tested in the private, for-profit sector. We have summarized past contextual based findings in Table 1 below.

Studying IT assimilation in the nonprofit context should yield powerful insights into what barriers prevent IT assimilation and how organizations can overcome these barriers. NPOs differ substantially from for-profit organizations (Henderson et al, 2002; Mellon, 1998) and tend to adopt innovations differently (Damanpour, 1991; Morgan, 1995). NPOs tend to be cost-conscious compared to for-profit organizations (Henderson et al, 2002; Mellon, 1998), which has likely been a key factor preventing their investment in expensive IT innovations in the past. On the other hand, IT innovations provide tremendous value to organizations (Bharadwaj, 2000; Mata et al, 1995; Melville et al, 2004; Wade & Hulland, 2004). As such, IT innovations present attractive opportunities for NPOs that are concerned with maximizing the efficiency of their limited resources (Baruch & Ramalho, 2006; Henderson et al, 2002; Mellon, 1998). Thus, in examining assimilation of an attractive technology within the resource-constrained NPO context, we contribute valuable boundary conditions to the general IT assimilation literature.

## Diffusion and organizational assimilation of innovations

The literature on innovation addresses both the process of innovation (e.g., Kay, 1993) and the diffusion of innovations as they are adopted among individuals and organizations (Frambach, 1993; Rogers, 2003; Wejnert, 2002). One widely used theory is Rogers’ (2003) Diffusion of Innovations (DOI) theory. A central notion within the DOI literature is that a given innovation possesses certain characteristics that either facilitate or inhibit its adoption. Previous research has identified many such characteristics (Fichman, 2001; Fichman & Kemerer, 1999; Moore & Benbasat, 1991; Rogers, 2003). While the DOI framework has been widely applied in the IT adoption/ assimilation literature (e.g., Angst & Agarwal, 2009; Bradford & Florin, 2003; Fichman, 2001; Fichman & Kemerer, 1999; Grover & Goslar, 1993; Moore & Benbasat, 1991; Thong, 1999; Xue, 2009), its focus is on the diffusion of products among individuals and is primarily an individual-level theory. IS theorists have thus employed other theoretical frames – often in conjunction with DOI constructs – to account for the complex factors affecting organizational assimilation of innovations. Such studies have employed the technology-organization-environment framework (Kuan & Chau, 2001; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990; Zhu & Kraemer, 2005), structuration theory (Rai et al, 2009), institutional theory (Liang et al, 2007; Purvis et al, 2001) and the resource-based view of the firm (Mishra et al, 2007), among others.

One shortcoming of innovation research within, as well as outside of, the IS literature is its near-exclusive focus on the for-profit industry sector (Nugroho, 2011) with relatively little empirical attention afforded to the diffusion of innovations in the nonprofit sector (Bishop, 2010; Burt & Taylor, 2003; Corder, 2001; Hackler & Saxton, 2007; Nugroho, 2011; Pinho & Macedo, 2006, 2009; Thatcher et al, 2006; Zorn et al, 2010). Even fewer empirical studies within the IS discipline have examined these phenomena within NPOs, though exceptions exist (Boyle et al, 1993; Mackay et al, 2004; O’hanlon & Chang, 2007; Sabi et al, 2015; Ward & Never, 2012). Given the unique nature of NPOs regarding their resources, goals, and tendencies, a rich research opportunity exists to develop understanding around NPO assimilation of innovative technologies and the factors that facilitate or inhibit deep assimilation of those technologies within NPOs.

## IT assimilation stages

Although more granular stage models have been proposed (e.g., Cooper & Zmud, 1990), previous innovation diffusion research has depicted at least three stages of organizational IT assimilation: (1) initiation, during which a newly discovered innovation is evaluated for its usefulness, (2) adoption, in which the decision to adopt and initially use the innovation is made, and (3) assimilation, during which the innovation becomes widely used as an integral part of a firm’s business processes (Rogers, 2003; Zhu et al, 2006a). The third stage is a crucial link in the chain from initial adoption of a technology to its downstream effects on organizational performance (Chatterjee et al, 2002), and research has identified an ‘‘assimilation gap’’ where full assimilation into the organization typically lags behind initial adoption rates (Fichman & Kemerer, 1999; Zhu et al, 2000b). As mentioned, a majority of the innovation diffusion literature has focused on the first two stages. Our focus is on the crucial third stage of innovation diffusion – assimilation. Consistent with the literature, we adopt the definition of assimilation as ‘‘the extent to which the use of technology diffuses across the organizational projects or work processes and becomes routinized in the activities of those projects and processes’’ (Purvis et al, 2001: p. 21).

## Assimilation of SaaS-based technologies

Cloud computing technologies are disruptive innovations that provide organizations with attractive alternatives to many in-house IT services (Choudhary & Vithayathil, 2013). The technologies acquired the ‘‘cloud’’ label as a result of their wide availability and accessibility to any device connected to the Internet (Vouk, 2008). Cloud computing technologies are generally categorized into three types: Infrastructure-as-a-Service (IaaS), Platform-as-a-Service (PaaS), and Software-asa-Service (SaaS) (Mell & Grance, 2011). Offering these technologies ‘‘as a service,’’ cloud providers are able to support pay-as-you-use pricing and elastic scalability, allowing some businesses to forgo the expense and administrative costs of an internal data center (Armbrust et al, 2010).

We focus on SaaS solutions in this paper as they have the widest adoption compared to other cloud systems (Columbus, 2015). Further, cloud computing assimilation, and more specifically SaaS solutions. have received limited theoretical and empirical treatment in the IS literature. A large section of the literature on cloud computing, which includes SaaS, is dedicated to understanding the related technical and operational issues businesses need to consider (Lee et al, 2013; Oliveira et al, 2014). Several published articles investigate cloud computing innovation, but these tend to focus on preadoption or adoption processes (Lin & Chen, 2012; Low et al, 2011; Nkhoma & Dang, 2013; Oliveira et al, 2014; Trigueros-Preciado et al, 2013; Wu, 2011; Wu et al, 2013). Literature reviews of the cloud computing literature repeatedly show the adoption or pre-adoption stages to be the primary focus of cloud computing research (Adam & Musah, 2015; Karunakaran & Krishnaswamy, 2015; Yang & Tate, 2012). We could find very few published studies that examined the assimilation of any cloud computing systems beyond the adoption phase, the exception being a small subset that has considered actual usage behavior in addition to adoption (Adam & Musah, 2015; Lian et al, 2014; Sabi et al, 2015).

Assimilation has been the focus of several studies on cloud-like technologies before the terms ‘‘cloud computing’’ and ‘‘SaaS’’ became popular. Basu et al (2000) studied the diffusion of executive information systems, finding that traditional enterprise information systems were, at the time, being replaced by web-based solutions akin to modern cloud technologies. Using a qualitative analysis of 11 companies, the high-level conclusions offered by Basu et al do not provide much guidance on specific technological, organizational, or environmental factors that facilitate or inhibit assimilation. Ranganathan et al (2004) examined assimilation of web-based supply-chain management technologies, highlighting external environmental factors and internal organizational factors that predict diffusion throughout an organization. Lastly, Son and Benbasat (Son & Benbasat, 2007) compared the predictors of B2B e-marketplace adoption and assimilation. Their findings indicate that external pressures are less relevant in assimilation, while market volatility and product characteristics are much more relevant. Although these prior assimilation studies have targeted SaaS-like technologies, their findings may not generalize to modern cloud computing services. Moreover, each of these prior studies was contextualized within the forprofit sector.

In summary, while the organizational assimilation of IT innovations has received extensive treatment within the IS literature, the majority of this research has focused on the pre-adoption and adoption stages of diffusion, and, moreover, very few studies have focused on such assimilation in the nonprofit sector. Studies on the assimilation of SaaS-based enterprise systems are gaining traction, but to date they have almost exclusively focused on early-stage adoption. ‘‘Appendix A’’ summarizes the findings and areas of focus in the for-profit and nonprofit sectors for both traditional and all cloud-based enterprise systems. Having reviewed the relevant literature, we now focus our attention on the assimilation of SaaS-based enterprise systems within the nonprofit sector.

## Contextualizing NPO SaaS assimilation

Following recommended guidelines for context-specific theorizing in IS research (Hong et al, 2013), we aim to achieve three objectives in this section. We first adopt a general theory to examine NPO SaaS assimilation. Using the technology-organization-environment framework as our general theoretical foundation, we develop and test a research model that enables us to compare our results to theoretical predictions and prior IT assimilation research. Leveraging this analysis as a diagnostic tool, our second objective is to contextualize and refine the theory to the NPO SaaS domain. We address this objective by conducting a phenomenological analysis of qualitative data collected through open-ended survey questions. Building on the results of our first model, our phenomenological analysis provides deeper insight into why technology characteristics do not appear to play a role in NPO SaaS assimilation. Finally, our third objective is to investigate an alternative NPO SaaS assimilation model. This investigation reveals insights that further our understanding of SaaS assimilation in an NPO context.

Examining NPO SaaS assimilation in a general theory The first step to develop context-specific theory in IS research is to ground the effort in a general theory. Although there is no single theory of IT assimilation (Fichman, 2000), the technology-organization-environment (TOE) framework provides a helpful lens through which we can study the assimilation of SaaS within NPOs. The TOE framework identifies three aspects of an organization’s context that influence its assimilation of an IT innovation – technological, organizational, and environmental characteristics (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). This framework is consistent with Rogers’ (2003) popular Diffusion of Innovations theory in which he draws attention to technological characteristics, as well as both internal and external organizational characteristics, as drivers for technology diffusion. The TOE framework has proven useful in IS assimilation research in general (Chau & Tam, 1997; Iacovou et al, 1995; Mishra et al, 2007; Raymond & Uwizeyemungu, 2007; Wu & Subramaniam, 2011; Zhu et al, 2006b), and in SaaS adoption research in particular (Alshamaila et al, 2013; Gangwar et al, 2015; Gutierrez et al, 2015; Hsu et al, 2012; Lian et al, 2014; Safari et al, 2015). Thus, we use the TOE as a diagnostic tool to identify areas of concern for our specific context (Hong et al, 2013). Specifically, we develop and empirically test a TOE-informed research model that allows us to rigorously compare our results to TOE predictions and prior IT assimilation research.

Research model The TOE framework includes three broad factors that influence IT assimilation: (1) the technological context describes both the existing technologies in use and new technologies relevant to the organization; (2) the organizational context refers to descriptive factors regarding the organization (e.g., managerial structure); and (3) the environmental context includes the setting (e.g., industry, competitors, suppliers) in which an organization operates (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). Figure 1 illustrates our proposed research model, which is based on the TOE framework.

The ultimate dependent variable in the research model is SaaS Assimilation. We draw on (Liang et al, 2007) to develop this construct. The reflective items include percentage of: current assimilation, what the plans for assimilation are for the next year, and the plans for assimilation in the next 3 years. Also, included in the construct is an objective measure that was provided by Salesforce.com. This measure captures what features that are currently deployed in the organizations. For example, was Salesforce.com just a CRM, or were the organizations using the HR, accounting, finance (and so on) components (see ‘‘Appendix B’’ for more detail). Therefore, the SaaS assimilation construct captures both perception and actual assimilation.

Technology-related factors encompass the characteristics of IT innovations that tend to make them more (or less) prone to assimilation. The technological context refers to the various characteristics of potential innovations as proposed by Rogers (2003) and others, but also the technological capabilities of the organization’s current employees and systems (e.g., Zhu et al, 2006a; Zhu & Kraemer, 2005). Based on the for-profit organization literature, we would expect factors such as relative advantage (Rogers 2003), perceived complexity (Cho, 2006), perceived value (Ramamurthy & Premkumar, 1995), and trialability (Rogers, 2003) to influence SaaS assimilation. Relative advantage is defined as the organization’s perception of a technology’s advantage relative to other available technologies in the larger environment (Rogers 2003). Perceived complexity is a measure of how difficult the innovation is to both understand and use (Rogers 2003), and is closely related to the popular perceived ease of use construct from the technology acceptance literature (Davis, 1989). The cost of an innovation is assumed to be inversely related to its adoption and assimilation among organizations (Tornatzky & Klein, 1982), and some innovations will provide more value for a given cost than others. We term this concept perceived value and define it as the perception that an innovation provides appropriate value for its required cost. Trialability refers to the extent to which an innovation can be experimented with prior to adoption (Rogers, 2003), and is unique to the innovation adoption/diffusion literature. Particularly at the organization level, technological innovations have traditionally required substantial investment of time and resources (Liang et al, 2007).

TOE posits that certain organizational characteristics will influence the rate and extent of assimilation of an innovation (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). Such organizational traits as size (Rogers, 2003), centralization (Damanpour, 1991), and communication environment (Nilakanta & Scamell, 1990) have all been studied vis-a\`- vis innovation assimilation. Organizational characteristics also include the strategic behavior of, or support provided by, top management within the organization (Zhu et al, 2006b). Drawing from the seminal work that examined the role of the organization in enterprise IT assimilation (Liang et al, 2007), we focus our research on three key management factors: top management beliefs, top management participation, and top management satisfaction. Further, we also look beyond management and include two other important diffusion-oriented organizational factors: readiness to adopt IT innovation and organizational innovativeness.

Following prior work, we separate top management beliefs (TMB) regarding the IT innovation from top management participation (TMP) in facilitating and promoting the use of the IT innovation (Chatterjee et al, 2002; Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1991; Liang et al, 2007; Yigitbasioglu, 2015). TMB refers to top management’s subjective cognitive beliefs regarding the technology’s potential to provide significant benefits to the organization, while TMP refers to the actions taken by top management to promote and facilitate assimilation of the technology (Barki & Hartwick, 1989; Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1991; Liang et al, 2007). Given our interest in capturing multiple aspects of top management’s influence on assimilation within an organization, we also include the influence of top management’s satisfaction (TMS) with the SaaS platform. Satisfaction is typically captured as a success metric, but we draw on past research that posits IT assimilation as the ultimate dependent variable and TMS as an influence on assimilation (Liang et al, 2007).

We also include the organization’s level of organizational innovativeness, defined as the willingness of key organizational decision-makers to experiment with new technologies that are external to the organization (Kim & Pae, 2007). Finally, the IS literature has examined innovation compatibility of organizations (Ramamurthy et al, 2008). Innovations that are incompatible with an organization’s capabilities to support these innovations and existing technological infrastructure (Schultz & Slevin, 1975) will likely not have a high rate of adoption or assimilation. Due to the confusion that is caused by the term compatibility, we dropped the concept of compatibility and coined a new term that we believe is much more clear – readiness to adopt IT innovation or readiness.

The environmental context constitutes the third and final component of the TOE framework. The environmental context refers to the external ‘‘constraints or opportunities that a firm must take into account when developing its technology acquisition strategy’’ (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990; p. 171). We have taken a micro-environment view in that we draw on factors that measure the organizations position with the externa market. These external factors include the competitive characteristics of an organization’s industry and the relevant technology support infrastructure external to the organization. Further, we investigate the external environmental context in terms of the NPO’s awareness of, and willingness to experiment with, new solutions available in the marketplace. One important construct that influences this is social gains (Kennedy & Fiss, 2009). In addition, objective measures such as years in operation, the type of industry (e.g., NTEE code), and the size of the organization, measured in revenue, are included to measure the current competitive environment for these organizations. These objective variables are commonly utilized in the assimilation literature (Liang et al, 2007).

When adopting IT, there are many implications for organizations beyond changes in processes. Past research has identified socially driven factors that influence the successful assimilation of IT. Specifically, the concept of social gains, which comes from institutional theorists, was introduced to capture the impact of IT adoption on external partners. Kennedy & Fiss (2009) argue that adoption of IT creates economic gains, technical gains, and social gains. This concept is more granular than Roger’s observability, which captures how the innovation may be visible to others (Rogers 2003). (Liang et al, 2007) have also explored the external forces of diffusion using institutional theory. They found that mimetic pressures, or those pressures that limit uncertainty by modeling after a similar organization (Dimaggio & Powell, 1991), and normative pressures, those pressures which are customary and socially expected of organizations (Dimaggio & Powell, 1991), influenced assimilation. Further, role modeling has been found as a positive influence on diffusion (Swan & Newell, 1995). What was not understood is how the social gains of the diffusion can affect assimilation. By investigating social gains, we can understand how the technology is viewed both by internal actors and the external constituents.

Research method Salesforce.com is our focal innovation of interest. We selected Salesforce.com because it is the leader in the enterprise SaaS platform market (Columbus, 2015) and has been adopted by more than 15,000 NPOs. With the support of the Salesforce.com Foundation, we assembled usage data from a random sample of 600 USbased NPOs actively using Salesforce.com in their organizations. The Salesforce.com Foundation contacted these organizations and requested their participation in the study. Next, the research team distributed an online survey to these organizations. We initially received 353 responses. We removed 13 of these from the data set as they were from universities and colleges, which might bias the sample frame, and we also excluded incomplete survey responses. We were left with 246 complete, usable responses for a final response rate of 69.6%. Tables 2 and 3 report respondent titles and organization characteristics.

We obtained further information from tax returns published by the US Internal Revenue Service (IRS). First, we captured the revenue of each organization. In order to account for fluctuations in reported revenue, we averaged the last 3 years (or less if the organization was younger). Second, we captured the age of each organization from their tax status information. In addition, all NPOs in the USA are classified into one of 25 industries. We report the sample categorical breakdown for the US National Taxonomy of Exempt Entities (NTEE) in ‘‘Appendix C’’, and we also use these industry designations as a control in the model. Taken together, the descriptive information collected from the IRS (see Tables 2 and 3) suggests that our respondents were executives from a diverse set of USbased NPOs.

We used standard scale development procedures (Mackenzie et al, 2011) to adapt existing measures of constructs to our context and to enhance construct validity. ‘‘Appendix B’’ contains all survey items and their sources. Covariance-based structural equation modeling (SEM) techniques can evaluate differences in models using fit indices. However, issues related to identification of complex models, distribution of data, and model complexity precluded our use of covariance-based techniques to estimate our measurement and structural models (Chin, 2010; Tenenhaus et al, 2005). Specifically, top management participation related to only one endogenous variable, so covariance-based methods would potentially fail to identify this unique nomological network (Diamantopoulos et al, 2008; Maccallum & Browne, 1993). Accordingly, we used partial least squares (PLS), a component-based SEM technique, to evaluate our research model (Chin, 2010; Ringle et al, 2012), accomplished using SmartPLS 3.0 with 500 bootstrap iterations and resampling 100 cases each time.

We followed Ringle et al’s (2012) guidelines to validate our measurement model. This included calculating the average variance extracted (AVE), internal composite reliability (ICR), and Dillon-Goldstein’s rho for each dimension. All constructs met the appropriate heuristics (i.e., $\mathrm { A V E } > 0 . 5 0 ,$ , ICR [ 0.8, and d.h. rho [ 0.70; see ‘‘Appendix B’’) for discriminant and convergent validity, as well as reliability (Chin, 1998; Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

Further, as shown in Table 4, the square roots of the AVEs for latent variables exceeded all off-diagonal correlations, further supporting discriminant validity (Chin, 1998). We found all item loadings to be significant at $p < 0 . 0 1$ and above 0.6, except for Readiness Item 1 (load-$\mathrm { i n g } = 0 . 2 5 4 )$ , Perceived Complexity Item 3 (0.337), Trialability Item 4 (0.698), and Top Management Beliefs Item 3 (0.534). After removing offending items, all construct measures exhibited satisfactory discriminant and convergent validity (Hair et al, 2007; see ‘‘Appendix B’’).

Common method bias is often a problem for crosssectional survey researchers. By collecting data for the assimilation construct that included actual usage, provided by the Salesforce.com Foundation, we were able to mitigate the impact of common method bias. Specifically, Salesforce.com provided us with figures on the extent to which each NPO had used each feature (e.g., Sales Cloud, HR Module, Dashboards, Constituent App, etc.). A full list of these features is included in ‘‘Appendix B’’.

Results We analyzed a second-order model, as prescribed by Hong et al (2013), to test our structural model. Following recommended guidelines (Polites et al, 2012; Wright et al, 2012), we aggregated the first-order constructs within their respective higher-order constructs drawn from the TOE framework (see Figure 2). We modeled each higher-order construct (i.e., technology, organization, and environment) as aggregate, such that the relationships flow from the first-order dimensions to the higher-order construct. The first-order multi-item constructs are reflective.

Our results show that the organization $( \beta = 0 . 3 7 6 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and environment $( \beta = 0 . 2 7 6 , \ p = 0 . 0 0 8 )$ factors influence assimilation; however, the technology factor is not significantly related to assimilation (see Figure 2). Of the first-order factors, only industry type is not statistically related to its higher-order construct. In sum, our TOE-based model provides no evidence that technology forces impact the assimilation of enterprise SaaS platforms within NPOs, which is contrary to prior findings in the for-profit sector (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Purvis et al, 2001; Zhu et al, 2006a, 2010). Caution must be taken when interpreting nonsignificant results. We now turn to qualitative data to further refine our understanding of SaaS assimilation. Specifically, we used qualitative data for two reasons: (1) to garner further insights into this possible discrepancy between past findings and our results, and (2) the research partner (i.e., Salesforce) requested this analysis.

Research method Contextualized data are often difficult to code and quantify (Hong et al, 2013). Thus, we used a phenomenography approach to ‘‘quantisise’’ qualitative data from this same sample. Please note that although we quantised this data, there was a great deal of human intervention in this process. The goal of this additional analysis was to analyze why technology was not a significant influence for assimilation in this unique context. In doing so, we are able to compare the results and relationships between the quantitative and qualitative data. We developed the open-ended questions that looked at actual experience with Salesforce.com rather than perceptual items developed in the assimilation literature. First, we reviewed the theoretical constructs in the assimilation nomological network (see ‘‘Appendix A’’). We then interviewed the CIO of Salesforce Foundation along with three other key executives at the foundation to help refine these questions within the context of enterprise SaaS platforms and NPOs. Based on this feedback, we developed eight open-ended questions for the data collection in which we used six of these questions in our analysis. For example, survey items for perceived complexity were followed by an open-ended question directed at the experience with the complexity concept. Of the 353 responses, 258 respondents (43% of the random sample of 600) completed the qualitative component (as opposed to 246 that completed the quantitative portion). A total of 46,514 words were submitted for all eight questions, for an average of over 189 words per respondent (i.e., about a page) with a standard deviation of about 67 words. To clean the data for analysis, the research team examined the responses and corrected 92 spelling mistakes.

We used the Leximancer 4 software program to conduct our analysis. Output from Leximancer takes two forms: (1) a concept map and (2) a statistical likelihood model. Leximancer has been shown to be a capable tool for phenomenographic studies (Indulska et al, 2012). The concept map is derived by examining the ranked list of important lexical terms on the basis of frequency and co-occurrence (Smith & Humphreys, 2006). These terms then seed the bootstrap thesaurus builder, from which sets of terms are classified from the text by iteratively extending the seed definitions using a machine learning algorithm. The result is a set of weighted groups of terms referred to as concepts. Next, concepts are classified into a co-occurrence matrix by calculating the relative co-occurrence frequencies of the concepts and the asymmetric co-occurrence matrix. This matrix is used to produce a two-dimensional visualization via a clustering algorithm. The connections among the concepts are represented by a semantic network.

Specifically, relative frequency is the number of times the concept has cooccurred with another concept. For example, one category may have a term (e.g., training) that occurs 17 times in the corpus of the text being analyzed, with a relative likelihood of 42%. In summary, the Leximancer tool was used to: (1) derive the main concepts from the open-ended questions, (2) identify important concepts, (3) assess and link concepts with each other, (4) visualize the textual information for the related concepts, (5) reduce human bias (Rafferty et al, 2012; Watson et al, 2005), and (6) analyze sentiments in large, textual datasets.

Results: readiness to adopt IT innovation We assessed NPO enterprise SaaS platforms readiness to adopt IT innovation by asking participants two related questions: (1) ‘‘Did your Salesforce implementation result in a disruption in the workplace at first?’’ and (2) ‘‘Describe how your Salesforce implementation decreased productivity at first due to time to learn the platform.’’ These questions were drawn from similar readiness measurement items used in the structural model. In the initial concept model, as expected, Salesforce was the baseline relevant concept at 100%; data had connectivity of 65%, organization 51%, change 42%, implementation 17%, and user at 5%. The terms trying, difficult, and people are all below 5% connectivity. In general terms, the concept map points to difficulties within the organization. To expand on these relationships, we selected the concepts ‘‘change,’ ‘‘difficult,’’ and ‘‘disruption’’ as categories (i.e., dependent variables) for further examination. Table 5 shows the statistics calculated.

When evaluating the category of ‘‘change,’’ we see that norms and culture are the first and second concepts, respectively. One clear insight was that employees were causing the disruption and problems with assimilation, not the technology. Comments that supported this finding ranged from, ‘‘Employees at first reluctant to change,’’ to ‘‘To be honest, productivity didn’t really go down because most people have not adopted all of the SF features into their dayto-day processes.’’ Others comments included:

• ‘‘It changed some of the norms such as how we interact with members to find their contact information in the database and generate their membership cards, but I don’t feel it changed the overall values or culture of the organization much.’’

• ‘‘Getting this set up for our organization was the hardest part. Next came adoption by staff.’’

• ‘‘It did require those of us using the database to shift how we perceive data and how we interact with our constituents (in this case, animal adopters and animal farmers).’’

• ‘‘Employees still blame SF rather than our own data entry or customization errors.’’

When evaluating the ‘‘difficult’’ and ‘‘disruptive’’ categories, we see similar patterns in the results. ‘‘Information’’ was a top concept; ‘‘management’’ and ‘‘learning’’ were also statistically prominent for both categories. When we scrutinized the data, we obtained findings similar to the ‘‘change’’ concept. For example, one executive wrote,

Very difficult to get most employees to realize that they are responsible for day-to-day analysis and management of their data. Hard to break the 20th century notion that all data must be entered/updated/corrected by dedicated data entry clerks and database admins.

Another respondent answered, ‘‘Whenever there is a problem getting information from SF, employees still blame SF rather than our own data entry.’’ One theme stated by a managing director of a human services NPO was echoed over 10 times: ‘‘There was a learning curve to go through, not exactly a disruption. Work processes were a bit slower until staff became skilled in Salesforce.’ These findings suggest the following takeaway:

While enterprise Saas platforms did not typically create major disruptions, most NPOs did experience organizational change while assimilating SaaS. Specifically, organizational norms and culture influenced the extent to which NPO end users leveraged SaaS features in their daily activities. NPO management also faced difficult challenges regarding data management and responsibility.

## Investigating alternative NPO enterprise SaaS platforms models

Our third and final objective is to investigate an alternative, context-specific model. Consideration of different models can help us understand the potential indirect effects of context-specific factors on a phenomenon (Hong et al, 2013). We compare NPOs that have been successful with enterprise SaaS platform assimilation to

NPOs who were not successful in the assimilation process. Comparing successful and unsuccessful NPOs provides further insight into the technological, organizational, and environmental antecedents to assimilation in our context. Furthermore, investigating the two extreme groups should magnify any contextual differences in a way that can be statistically tested.

Research method We gathered data from two distinct NPO populations – NPOs in which Salesforce.com was highly assimilated and NPOs that used Salesforce.com for a year but then discontinued their use of the SaaSbased solution and went back to a non-SaaS system. Given the nature of our sample, we expected moderate to low differences in effect sizes in the group analysis, and our sample sizes were not extremely large, in which circumstances Qureshi & Compeau (2009) suggest the use of PLS. Thus, we again used SmartPLS 3.0 to estimate our models and provide a multi-group analysis.

Salesforce.com provided us with the contact information for 76 NPOs that were highly assimilated according to their actual usage data (i.e., Salesforce.com was used for 50% or more of their business processes). These organizations were not included in our first study. Of these, 51 responded and 47 completed the entire survey. Further, Salesforce.com provided us with a list of 200 NPOs that had used Salesforce.com for more than 1 year but no longer used the solution in any capacity. Of those, 81 responded and 42 completed the entire survey. As before, we collected the respondent information and the organizational descriptive information, as presented in Tables 8 and 9.

Results In order to examine the differences between the models, we conducted a permutation analysis (Chin, 2010) that allowed us to compare the path estimates for the two groups. A permutation analysis consists of a Monte Carlo simulation that produces an observed tstatistic for the sample. This observed statistic is then compared against a cutoff value to determine significance. The cutoff value for this study was p \ 0.05. ## Discussion

Our multi-method analysis reveals a number of interesting findings regarding enterprise SaaS platform assimilation in NPOs. Namely, our first study showed that organizational and environmental factors affect NPO enterprise SaaS platform assimilation. These measures were designed to capture perceptions of the focal constructs. Organizational factors include top management beliefs, participation, and satisfaction, as well as readiness and organizational innovativeness; environmental factors include social gains, revenue, age, and industry. These findings are consistent with much of the research on IT assimilation in for-profit organizations and non-SaaS technologies (Liang et al, 2007; Mishra et al, 2007; Rai et al, 2009; Zhu et al, 2006b, 2010). However, contrary to prior findings in the for-profit sector (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Purvis et al, 2001; Zhu et al, 2006b, 2010), we also found that technological factors do not appear to play a significant role in NPO SaaS assimilation.

We conducted a phenomenological analysis to shed further light on this unexpected finding. We used actual experiences, rather than perceptual items, to capture how certain constructs influenced assimilation. Our analysis resulted in a number of contextual takeaways. First, readiness between SaaS capabilities and NPOs’ needs and values was not clear at the beginning of the assimilation process. This may be due to the relatively low diffusion of IT in general in NPOs. However, once NPO end users modified their perceptions toward SaaS, specifically regarding how IT could help them accomplish their goals and daily tasks, the assimilation process appeared to go smoother. In contrast to enterprise-wide implementations like those of ERP systems, SaaS assimilation did not create major disruptions for most NPOs. Even so, NPO end users had to go through a learning curve during the assimilation process.

NPOs appeared to find SaaS to be fairly complex during the post-adoption stage in the assimilation life cycle. As a result, many NPOs used outside help either through consultants or volunteers to help with the challenges in assimilating SaaS. Leveraging and learning from consultants is common practice in enterprise-wide systems implementation efforts (Ko et al, 2005). Most of the NPOs in our sample did not have an understanding of the investment required to effectively assimilate SaaS. Further, there was evidence that many NPOs were not interested in investing in internal skills that can help them assimilate SaaS. Our alternative model revealed another curious finding regarding complexity. Namely, SaaS complexity appeared to play a critical role in an NPO’s decision to further assimilate or abandon SaaS. Thus, despite vendors’ claims regarding ease of use and flexibility of SaaS solutions, through their experience many NPOs still found SaaS to be so complex that they abandoned the technology altogether. Future research should examine potential antecedents of SaaS complexity.

NPOs that did overcome the complexity barrier found SaaS to be flexible and easy to customize for their needs. While contemporary information systems are arguably more flexible and customizable than those developed 20 years ago, the flexibility aspect of SaaS does stand in sharp contrast to the oft-perceived rigidity and inflexibility of traditional enterprise-wide solutions like ERP systems. Furthermore, IT innovations often impose substantial barriers for adopters in terms of the knowledge needed to effectively implement them (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997). However, many NPOs in our sample did not mention the concept of system complexity during implementation. These organizations may have possessed sufficient related knowledge to overcome the burden of organizational learning associated with SaaS assimilation. Another explanation may be that some NPOs have organizational learning mechanisms in place that facilitate IT implementation (e.g., decentralized structures; Robey et al, 1996). By overcoming the knowledge barriers associated with SaaS implementation, many NPOs were able to focus on broader organizational issues, such as user resistance and process change. Although prior knowledge plays an important role in IT assimilation (Roberts et al, 2012), future research could investigate how NPOs leverage organizational learning mechanisms to further assimilate and exploit the advantages of SaaS in their business processes.

Given their limited resources, NPOs tend to be costconscious compared to for-profit organizations. Thus, cost is a key reason why NPOs are attracted to SaaS solutions. However, SaaS options were also an important component of the value proposition. Due to the economies of scale that cloud computing offers, it is more economically viable for cloud-based organizations, including SaaS providers, to offer their services at discounted rates to NPOs. Salesforce.com is one of many companies that have built philanthropy into their business model. For example, Google and Microsoft have discounted and free offerings for their cloudbased email services. Cloud storage services (e.g., IaaS) such as Dropbox.com and Amazon Web Services also offer NPOs a variety of discounted and free services. In sum, NPOs have a cost-effective opportunity to assimilate enterprise-class services now more than ever.

The environmental factors play an interesting role in NPO SaaS assimilation. Both revenue and years in operation had a negative relationship with the environment variable. This is counter to past research that suggested powerful coercive forces may influence adoption (Teo et al, 2003). NPOs, in this context, may not necessarily face such coercive pressures from larger organizations. Similarly, NPOs may not face the same environmental pressures that for-profit organizations encounter when engaged in the assimilation of interorganizational business process standards (Bala & Venkatesh, 2007).

Finally, our results indicate that that top management beliefs and participation in a SaaS-based project can positively influence organization-level drivers for assimilation. This echoes the findings suggested by Liang et al (2007), who found that management support was a good predictor of assimilation. Therefore, we posit that these two organizational factors can significantly improve organization-level drivers for assimilation.

## Implications for research

Our study provides several implications for research. First, the effects of technological factors on assimilation appear to vary across both traditional/SaaS systems and for-profit/nonprofit organizations. Specifically, readiness to adopt IT innovation plays a unique role in NPO SaaS assimilation. This may be due to NPO’s general lack of familiarity with IT. Thus, even though SaaS does not necessarily require IT infrastructure or IT experience on the adopter’s part, some minimum level of IT experience may be necessary for adopters to recognize ways in which SaaS could be compatible with their work processes and activities. Given our results showing the significant role of top management in SaaS assimilation, future research should investigate NPO management’s efforts to integrate SaaS and complementary organizational resources to enhance readiness and potentially create lasting value (Nevo & Wade, 2010).

Second, our findings indicate that perceived complexity is a relevant, even pivotal factor predicting successful assimilation of cloud technologies in the nonprofit sector. SaaS service providers tout the simplicity of cloud computing as a key advantage over traditional enterprise systems (Babcock, 2011). SaaS-based systems may be less complex than traditional enterprise systems because SaaS-based systems are managed by the service provider. Even so, NPOs may find SaaS-based systems to be just as complex as traditional systems. Thus, even if the SaaS infrastructure is managed by the provider, organizations still undergo a SaaS computing learning curve commonly associated with most IT innovations. Our results imply that NPOs that do not survive this learning curve will most likely abandon SaaS technologies (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997). Theories seeking to explain the assimilation of SaaS technologies clearly need to account for perceived complexity and investigate ways in which the supposed simplicity of enterprise SaaS platforms can be better demonstrated to adopting organizations.

## Implications for practice

Our results generate a number of implications for practice. First, NPOs should take a closer look at the ways in which enterprise SaaS platforms might be perceived as too complex for their needs, processes, and values. While SaaS is certainly not a silver bullet for NPOs. there are likely numerous ways in which SaaS systems can increase NPO efficiency and effectiveness. Second, advice from trusted external sources may also be valuable during the initial stages of assimilation. We emphasize trusted sources of information because institutional pressures may not be in the focal NPO’s best interest when it comes to adopting and assimilating enterprise SaaS platforms. Another implication of our study is that NPOs should consider the use of consultants when trying to implement enterprise SaaS platforms. Consultants can provide a wealth of IT-based knowledge, and there may be opportunities for learning during consultant-client exchanges.

Furthermore, consultants may help NPOs overcome challenges associated with system customization and end-user resistance throughout the assimilation life cycle.

## Limitations

Several limitations bound the generalizability of our research. First, our sample was drawn from US-based NPOs. Although many NPOs have international outreach, we do recognize that there are changes in perceptions of IT for different countries and culture (for example see Yayla & Hu, 2012). Location and culture were not captured in our study, and we acknowledge this weakness as a limitation. Further research is necessary to compare and contrast IT assimilation in different countries and culture. Second, we examined enterprise SaaS platforms assimilation using a single technology, Salesforce.com. Although Salesforce.- com is a leader in the cloud computing industry, this limits our ability to generalize our findings to other systems. Triangulation of phenomena experienced by NPOs using different systems would further help NPOs understand the assimilation process. Third, this cross-sectional study examines NPO sentiments for each stage retrospectively. Specifically, NPO executives were asked to remember the initiation and adoption stages. The only present-moment data we collected was concerning the assimilation stage. This is a common problem with cross-sectional survey research, and future research could expand on our findings with a longitudinal approach.

Fourth, we the open-ended questions were co-created by the research team and Salesforce.org management. This limited the amount of questions and the type of questions we could ask. Ideally, we would have had one open-ended question for every technology factor. Finally, identifying environmental variables is difficult in a nonprofit context. Admittedly, we have utilized crude environmental variables to study diffusion in the TOE framework. To our knowledge, no study has empirically examined how environmental variables may be unique in this context. For this reason, we are calling for more research in the nonprofit area to help identify appropriate environmental variables in the nonprofit sector.

## Conclusion

We employed a context-specific, theory-driven, multimethod approach to investigate enterprise SaaS platforms assimilation in the nonprofit sector. In doing so, we expanded our understanding of SaaS assimilation and IT assimilation in NPOs. We suggest that future studies extending our analysis of differences across NPOs are necessary. For example, we believe it would be useful to conduct in-depth comparative case studies that examine whether for-profit organizations and NPOs share similar concerns about assimilating new technologies or extending their use of technologies. We hope that our study will be a stepping stone for future research on why and how NPOs engage with enterprise SaaS platforms and other information technologies.

## Appendix A: Literature review

We used a systematic approach in reviewing the contextual assimilation literature for this study. We first searched the publication histories of each of the journals in the AIS basket of eight (Saunders et al, 2007) for studies on assimilation of technologies, and we performed more general searches by keyword using Google Scholar. We used keywords related to assimilation of technology, as well as those relating to NPOs and cloud computing. To ensure that we fully examined the literature on NPO assimilation, we also searched through the top journals from public administration, including Public Administration Review, Administration & Society, the Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory, the American Review of Public Administration, the International Review of Public Administration, and Nonprofit and Voluntary Sector Quarterly.

For inclusion in our literature review, a retrieved article had to fulfill a number of criteria. The article had to be empirical (either quantitative or qualitative) and have technology assimilation as a key dependent variable under examination. In the for-profit sector, we limited our set of articles to only those addressing late-stage assimilation (as opposed to early-stage adoption).

As the articles addressing assimilation in NPOs and assimilation of cloud technologies were much less numerous, we relaxed this requirement for studies examining assimilation in the nonprofit sector or of cloud technologies. A study in the table focused on adoption rather than assimilation is marked with an asterisk under the ‘‘Sector’’ column. In order to be classified as a nonprofit organization study in our literature review, the paper had to examine true nonprofit organizations such as voluntary organizations, charities, and the like. Public organizations such as governments or utility companies were excluded. Additionally, NPOs in the education or healthcare industries (e.g., universities and hospitals) were excluded (Table 10).
