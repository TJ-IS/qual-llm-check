# Adoption of information systems innovations by individuals: A study of processes involving contextual, adopter, and influencer actions

Anand Jeyaraj <sup>a,\*</sup>, Rajiv Sabherwal <sup>b,1</sup>

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 2 October 2007 Received in revised form 1 April 2008 Accepted 1 April 2008

## a b s t r a c t

The extensive literature on the adoption of information systems (IS) or information technology (IT) innovations by individuals has primarily used a variance approach, focusing on individual, organizational, and technological factors that influence the level of adoption. Probably due to this focus on factors affecting adoption, little is known about the processes by which individuals adopt IS/IT innovations. Considering IS/IT adoption as an emergent process involving actions by the potential adopter, actions by individuals who might influence the adopter, and actions within the adopter’s context, we examined the ways in which these actions interacted with each other over time. We conducted optimal matching and cluster analyses using data from 27 interviews about the adoption of 30 IS/IT innovations.

The emergent taxonomy includes three distinct processes by which individuals adopt innovations: Conscious Quest, Requisite Compliance, and Asserted Trial. The Conscious Quest process, which results in ‘‘full adoption,” seems to be driven by the adopter, with no social influence and no mandate. In contrast, in Requisite Compliance and Asserted Trial processes, both of which resulted in ‘‘partial adoption,” the adopter encounters considerable pressure, either from a contextual mandate (Requisite Compliance) or from influencers (Asserted Trial). Implications for research and practice are examined.

## 1. Introduction

Individuals in organizations continue to be besieged by a variety of information systems (IS) and information technology (IT) innovations everyday. The adoption of IS/IT innovations has been of enduring interest to IS research and practice over a considerable period of time. There has been exten sive research conducted on the adoption of IS/IT innovations by individuals in organizations (DeLone & McLean, 1992; Fichman, 1992; Jeyaraj, Rottman, & Lacity, 2006; Prescott & Conger, 1995; Rogers, 1995; Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003). While prior research has generated valuable findings and insights, it has largely been concerned with the identification of factors (such as perceived useful ness and perceived ease of use of the system) that affect IS/IT adoption with much less attention on the behaviors associated with adoption. Moreover, the focus has primarily been on the individual adopt ing the IS/IT innovation, with the context of the adoption as well as the social influences being limited to at most a few explanatory or moderating variables.

This paper seeks to address this gap in our knowledge and contribute to our understanding of the adoption of IS/IT innovations by explicitly examining the behaviors associated with adoption, instead of studying factors that may affect adoption. Moreover, it views the adoption of IS/IT innovations as a complex process that not only involves actions by the adopter, but also actions within the context in which the innovation is adopted and actions performed by other individuals who may influence the adopter. Thus, we argue that it would be more appropriate to view IS/IT adoption as a process in which three kinds of actions – actions within the context, actions by the adopter, and actions by indi viduals who might influence the adopter – interact over time. We empirically utilize this perspective to view the IS/IT adoption process as a sequence of actions. Based on the processes involved in the adoption of 30 IS/IT innovations, we develop a taxonomy including three types of IS/IT adoption processes.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides a summary of the literature on technology adoption, following which Section 3 delineates this paper’s points of departure from the prior literature and presents the conceptual model underlying this paper. Sections 4 and 5 describe the research methods and present the taxonomy of adoption processes. The paper’s implications and lim itations are discussed in Section 6, followed by the conclusions.

## 2. Literature review

Several hundred studies on the adoption of IS/IT innovations by individuals have been conducted over the past 25 years (Jeyaraj et al., 2006; Sabherwal, Jeyaraj, & Chowa, 2006). For example, Jeyaraj et al. (2006) examined 45 studies of IT adoption, whereas Sabherwal et al. (2006) examined 121 empirical studies of technology adoption by individuals. Technology adoption studies typically ask the question: ‘‘What factors influence the adoption of IS/IT innovations by individuals?” Somewhat infrequently, these studies also pursue the question: ‘‘What factors hinder the adoption of IS/IT innovations by individuals?” As can be surmised from these questions, and concluded from the relevant literature, the technology adoption studies generally seek to understand the various factors that are instrumental or detrimental to the adoption of IS/IT innovations by individuals.

The unit of analysis in IS/IT adoption studies is typically the ‘‘individual” and the focus is on the adoption behavior of such individuals. Studies on innovation adoption by individuals have examined individuals in a variety of settings, including individuals as: members of an organization (Igbaria, 1993), members of communities of practice (DeSanctis, 2003), members of homes (Venkatesh & Brown, 2001), or patrons of an organization (Agarwal & Prasad, 1999; Gefen, Karahanna, & Straub, 2003). This research focuses on the adoption of IS/IT innovations by individuals situated in organizations.

A variety of dependent variables have been employed to understand innovation adoption by individ uals. These include intention to adopt (Chin & Gopal, 1995; Karahanna, Straub, & Chervany, 1999), adoption (Sultan & Chan, 2000), acceptance (Al-Gahtani, 2001; Chau, 1996), intention to use (Agarwal & Prasad, 2000; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), usage (Moon & Kim, 2001; Szajna, 1996), continued use (Kim & Malhotra, 2005), and post-adoption behavior (Parthasarathy & Bhattacherjee, 1998)

Several theoretical models have been proposed and empirically tested in the context of individual adoption of IS/IT innovations, including the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) (Fishbein & Ajzen 1975), Diffusion of Innovations (DoI) for individuals (Rogers, 1983), Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1989), Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991), Perceived Characteristics of Innovating (PCI) (Moore & Benbasat, 1991), Model of PC Utilization (Thompson, Higgins, & Howell, 1991), Motivational Model (Davis, Bagozzi, & Warshaw, 1992), Social Cognitive Theory (Compeau & Higgins, 1995), Task-Technology Fit (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995), and Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Table 1 summarizes the prominent theories used to examine the adoption of IS/IT innovations over the past 30 years. The table helps appreciate the cumulative research tradition prevalent in the arena of technology adoption. Early theories contributed greatly to the formulation of subsequent theories. For instance, the TPB draws heavily on the TRA but also incorporates the notion of perceived behavioral control (Ajzen, 1991; Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Similarly, the DoI model was elaborated by the PCI Model (Moore & Benbasat, 1991; Rogers, 1983). Moreover, it can be seen how the theories have gradually expanded the inquiry net surrounding adoption behavior. For instance, TAM proposed only two constructs: perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as influencing individual behavior; however UTAUT also proposed addi tional constructs: facilitating conditions and social influence as well as moderator variables.

The above theories and models have identified various antecedent factors that affect innovation adoption by individuals. The antecedents include: (a) innovation attributes, or characteristics of the innovation as perceived by the individual, such as usefulness, ease of use, complexity, and qualit (Adams, Nelson, & Todd, 1992; Davis, 1989; Rogers, 1995); (b) individual characteristics, or characteristics of the individual adopting the innovation, such as gender, experience, age, education, attitudes, self-efficacy, and innovativeness (Agarwal & Prasad, 1997; Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Igbaria, 1993); (c) task characteristics such as difficulty, variety, and newness (Guimaraes, Igbaria, & Lu, 1992; Igbaria, 1990; Sanders & Courtney, 1985); and (d) contextual factors such as top management support, facilitating conditions and social norms (Sabherwal et al., 2006; Thompson et al., 1991; Venkatesh et al., 2003). These antecedents have been typically examined from the perspective of the potential adopter. That is, data on the various antecedents – including individual characteristics and contextual factors – are obtained from the research participants, generally assuming the role of the potential adopters.

## 3. Theoretical development

This paper departs in several respects from the way in which IS/IT adoption has been investigated in prior literature. In the next five sub-sections, these points of departure are discussed and the asso ciated aspects of the theoretical model are presented. This section concludes by providing a summar of the conceptual model used in the paper.

## 3.1. Focus on behaviors

IS/IT adoption research generally deals with individuals’ beliefs (regarding contextual factors, for instance), attitudes (regarding the innovation), intentions (regarding future adoption or use of the innovation), and adoption or use. There is a consensus in the literature that beliefs affect attitudes, which in turn, affect intentions, which in turn, affect adoption and use. Thus, technology adoption research, for the most part, is about perceptions rather than actual behaviors. Even the adoption or use of IS/IT innovation has generally been examined in terms of the adopter’s response to whether or not the adopter uses the system and the adopter’s perceptions of the level of use, respectively.

Thus, prior literature on IS/IT adoption by individuals has largely adopted a variance approach, focusing on the relationships among a set of variables, including adoption, use, or behavioral intention as the dependent variable, and characteristics of the adopter, the organization, and the IS/IT innovation as the antecedent variables (Markus & Robey, 1988). In contrast, this paper employs a process approach, viewing IS/IT adoption as a series of actions (Sabherwal & Robey, 1995). According to the Webster dictionary, an ‘‘action” is an activity performed towards achieving some objective.

Although prior empirical research on IT adoption by individuals has not, to the best of our knowledge, viewed adoption as a sequence of actions, this approach has support in prior literature. For example, in a study of the changes in organizational routines as a result of the adoption of a new tech nology for cardiac surgery, Edmondson, Bohmer, and Pisano (2001) examined actions by leaders and task members. Moreover, Sabherwal and Robey (1993) adopted a similar approach at the project level, and examined 53 IS implementation projects in terms of sequences of events. Finally, two recent articles have focused on behaviors in the context of individual-level adoption of IT. In a conceptual article on post-adoptive behavior related to IS/IT adoption, Jasperson, Carter, and Zmud (2005) identified a number of intervention actions, performed by users, peers, experts, and managers. Barki, Titah, and Boffo (2007) incorporated a number of behaviors in an expended model of use-related behaviors although they used these behaviors as parts of measures and did not consider the sequence in which the behaviors occur.

## 3.2. Examination of both adoption and other actions by the adopter

Prior literature on IS/IT adoption has incorporated characteristics of the potential adopter, the adopter’s intention to adopt IS/IT innovations, and initial adoption and subsequent use of the IS/IT innovation (Adams et al., 1992; DeLone & McLean, 1992; Parthasarathy & Bhattacherjee, 1998; Rai, Lang, & Welker, 2002). However, two general patterns are evident in prior literature.

First, adoption outcomes have traditionally been captured using two approaches. One approach involves the binary measure of ‘‘adoption” which indicates whether or not the individual has adopted an innovation (Agarwal & Prasad, 1999; Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1990). The second approach involves the continuous measure of ‘‘system use” which captures the extent to which the individual works with th innovation on a regular basis (DeLone & McLean, 1992; Rai et al., 2002). However, system use only indicates the extent to which the individual uses the overall system but does not deal with when the individual starts using the system or the individual’s use of the different features of the system. Moreover, in both these approaches, the specific actions related to adoption of the IS/IT innovation have rarely been examined.

Second, other types of actions by the adopter have received little attention. Two notable exceptions are Jasperson et al. (2005) recent discussion of behaviors such as ‘‘self-orchestrated learning” (p. 537), and Barki et al. (2007) recent discussion of behaviors such as communication with other users or IS professionals regarding the IS/IT innovation and information search behaviors independently conducted by the adopter.

In this paper, we incorporate both actions related to adoption as well as other actions performed by the adopter. The following two sub-sections examine these types of actions

## 3.2.1. Adoption actions by the potential adopter

The actions performed by the potential adopter in adopting the innovation are shown in Table 2. These actions were conceptualized to reflect the adoption of the features of the innovation (Jasperson et al., 2005). Prior literature has generally not examined adoption outcomes in terms of the innovation features except in rare instances (Kay & Thomas, 1995) or in specific innovation contexts such as com puting technologies (Lee, 1986; Winter, Chudoba, & Gutek, 1998). Four possible outcomes can be visualized depending on the extent to which individuals exploit the features of the innovation they are authorized to use for their specific organizational roles (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995; Jasperson et al., 2005): (a) full adoption; (b) partial adoption; (c) experimentation; and (d) non-adoption.

The focus of this research is on an individual’s use of the innovation within an organizational setting, and various individuals could have access to these innovations to different extents. An individua can at most adopt only those features of the innovation that (s)he has access and authorization to use. Therefore, full adoption was defined in this study as the use of all the features of the innovation that are available to the potential adopter (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997). Non-adoption refers to the potential adopter rejecting or not using the innovation (Rogers, 1995). Thus, full adoption and non-adoption are at the two extremes in terms of adoption. Both partial adoption and experimentation lie between ful adoption and non-adoption. Partial adoption refers to the use of only a subset of features of the innovation or use of the innovation for only a restricted set of tasks (Jasperson et al., 2005). By contrast, experimentation refers to the potential adopter playing around with the innovation, attempting to understand its features and/or functions or using it for only a limited time.

## 3.3. Explicit incorporation of actions in the context

Although the context has received considerable attention in the prior literature on IS/IT innovation adoption, this has primarily been done in terms of how certain attributes of the context affect the leve of adoption. For example, the effects of top management support, facilitating conditions, training, user participation, user involvement have been examined (Barki & Hartwick, 1994; Leonard-Barton & Deschamps, 1988; Santhanam, Guimaraes, & George, 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Thus, there has been a separation between organizational and individual levels of innovation adoption, with research focusing on one aspect or the other. Gallivan (2001) examined both levels, with the organizational adoption being characterized as primary adoption and the subsequent adoption by individuals being characterized as secondary adoption. Jasperson et al. (2005) also distinguish among organizational adoption decision, individual adoption decision, and post-adoption behaviors by the individual. Their conceptual model of post-adoption behavior also incorporates organizational and individual levels, which af fect each other.

This paper explicitly incorporates the actions that occur within the context of the IS/IT innovation, and thereby seeks to achieve a broader, multi-level, view of innovation adoption, which has been recommended in prior literature but has not been used much (Gallivan, 2001; Jasperson et al., 2005). Th actions occurring within the organizational context are summarized in Table 3. These actions are the purview of the senior levels of the organizational hierarchy or other entities (e.g., the IS department) delegated with the responsibility to manage the innovation. In general, these actions affect the entir population of the organization. Prior literature has generally examined these actions as factors from the perspective of the potential adopter. Examples include facilitating conditions, user participation, and training (Jasperson et al., 2005; Venkatesh et al., 2003).

In formulating the actions in the organizational context, the ways in which an organization might attempt to facilitate innovation adoption were considered. Assuming that the eventual outcome of interest to the organization is the adoption of the innovation by its members, the organization can choose to do any or all of the following: create an awareness of the innovation among its members, design and develop or otherwise acquire the innovation, implement the innovation such that it is available for use, establish a clear mandate for the use of the innovation, and provide training to its members on the innovation (Davis, 1989; Jasperson et al., 2005; Rogers, 1995).

## 3.4. Explicit incorporation of actions by other actors

Individuals adopting IT or other innovations do so within their social contexts. Social influences on innovation adoption have been recognized in the literature on champions (e.g., Howell & Higgins, 1990). The importance of social influence is also implicit in the arguments that an individual’s adoption behavior may be influenced by the individual’s perception of his workgroup’s adoption behavior (Fulk, 1993), and that an individual would resist adoption if (s)he believes it would adversely affect his or her relationship with others (Rice & Anderson, 1994). The importance of others’ influence on an individual’s adoption behavior is also implied in the use of social norms as affecting the intention to adopt (Ajzen, 1991) or attitude toward adopting (Aydin & Rice, 1991, 1992; Karahanna et al., 1999; Rice & Anderson, 1994). Prior research has argued that the potential adopters are influenced by others in their personal networks (who have already adopted the innovation, i.e. influencers), and that the potential adopters model their own behavior on similar others in their social networks (i.e. influencers) (Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1990; Burkhardt & Brass, 1990; Rogers, 1995; Valente, 1995). The importance of other actors’ influences has also been recognized in social influence models (Rice & Aydin, 1991) and social information processing theory (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978).

Despite this recognition of the importance of other social actors, the role of others’ specific actions has not been directly incorporated in empirical research on IS/IT innovations (Mark & Poltrock, 2004) Instead, other actors’ influence has been either restricted to the influence champions and IS personne exert during the IS development process, or examined in the form of variables representing the adopter’s perceptions of these influences (e.g., social norms and social influence). For example, in on empirical study, respondents reported on their intentions to use innovations as well as on social influence (Venkatesh et al., 2003). The research employed measures such as ‘‘People who are important to me think that I should use the system” and ‘‘People who influence my behavior think that I should use the system” for social influence. These measures capture the individual’s beliefs regarding social influ ence, but do not incorporate the specific actions those other individuals may have performed to influ ence the adopter.

This paper explicitly incorporates actions performed by other actors. Table 4 summarizes influencer actions, which are actions aimed by the influencer at only the potential adopter. Both the influencer and the potential adopter, as pointed out earlier, are members of the same organizational context, and are thus subject to the same actions emanating from the organizational context. Henc these influencer actions are over and above the influences the potential adopter may experience from the organizational context.

Extant knowledge of the influencer’s role in innovation adoption is limited to indirect accounts provided by potential adopters. For instance, we know that potential adopters are influenced by other individuals in their personal networks (Valente, 1995), that they model themselves on similar others in their networks (Burt, 1997), and that they ascribe some significance to what important individuals in their networks think (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). However, such knowledge has typically been accumulated from potential adopters; we do not have direct accounts from the influencers themselves That is, prior studies have generally assumed the existence of influencers but have not explicitl examined their role in the adoption process.

Influencers, treated explicitly in this research, may be those individuals who are responsible for enforcing the mandate for the innovation, or those individuals who function as ‘‘champions” of the innovation, or those individuals designated as ‘‘change-agents” for the innovation, or those technically savvy individuals who possess the expertise to assist others with the innovation (Howell & Higgins, 1990; Rogers, 1995). Influencers are generally enthusiastic about the innovation and would attempt to influence others to adopt the innovation. Influencers are important in the innovation adoption pro cess since they reinforce the innovation and its adoption to specific potential adopters in the organi zation on a one-to-one basis over and above the communal actions in the context.

Treating the influencers as agents allows for explicitly examining the actions they perform in the adoption process. The influence tactics literature (Kipnis, Schmidt, & Wilkinson, 1980; Yukl, Falbe, & Youn, 1993) from the organization behavior area proved a promising avenue for the influencer perspective since it deals with interactions between two individuals engaged in an interpersonal relation ship, just like the influencer and the potential adopter. Moreover, the influence tactics literature accommodates the individual differences of the persons (e.g. different hierarchical positions) in the relationship and explicitly addresses the direction of influence (e.g. lateral, downward, upward) in a relationship (Yukl, Guinan, & Sottolano, 1995). Finally, the influence tactics literature sheds light on the objectives of using influence tactics including ‘‘changing behavior,” which is consistent with the influencer’s objective of changing the behavior of the potential adopter with regard to the innovation (Yukl et al., 1995).

Individuals, as influencers, influence other individuals to change their behaviors using a variety of influence tactics. Different inventories of influence tactics have been proposed and examined in prior literature (Keys, Miller, Case, Curran, & Jones, 1987; Kipnis et al., 1980; Lee & Sweeney, 2001; Yukl et al., 1993). A single inventory of influence tactics (Kipnis et al., 1980) was chosen for mod eling influencer behaviors. Two reasons guided the choice of this particular inventory: (a) this was one of the first inventories found in the influence tactics literature, which has been validated in subsequent research, and (b) this was the only inventory previously examined in a technological setting (Howell & Higgins, 1990). The specific influence tactics in this inventory were building coalitions, appealing to higher authority, bargaining, acting in a clandestine manner, presenting rational arguments, applying sanctions, using friendliness and ingratiation, and being assertive (Kipnis et al., 1980).

The eight influence tactics provide a range of behaviors for the influencers for influencing the po tential adopters. ‘‘Presenting rational arguments” and ‘‘bargaining,” for instance, allow the influencer to logically argue in support of the innovation as well as offer help to potential adopters as they deal with the innovation. Since they do not demand specific responses, these tactics present somewha more control and choice to the potential adopters. On the other hand, ‘‘being assertive,” ‘‘building coalitions,” ‘‘appealing to higher authority,” and ‘‘applying sanctions” permit the influencer to pressure the potential adopters. These tactics curtail the potential adopters to a greater extent than othe tactics since these involve authority, power, and norms.

## 3.5. Beyond technological or organizational determinism

Prior literature on IS/IT innovation adoption has primarily focused on identifying ways to enhance adoption. Consequently, attributes of the IS/IT innovation (e.g., perceived usefulness or ease of use) and attributes of the context (e.g., organizational structure) have been conceptualized as determining adoption. Such a deterministic view has its limitations, as noted, for example, by Orlikowski (1992, p. 423):

‘‘Even though causal associations may be postulated and investigated, the premises of the structurational model caution us against undue determinism. While expected relationships may hold empirically for certain organizations in certain historical and socio-economic conditions, the ever-present ability of actors to alter the cycle of development, appropriation, institutionalization, and reproduction of technology may undermine any causal expectation.”

Although we do not adopt a structuration approach in this paper, we do try to move beyond tech nological or organizational determinism. Toward this end, we adopt an emergent perspective (Markus & Robey, 1988), and view the adoption of IS/IT innovations to emerge through a series of actions performed within the context, by influencers, and by the adopter. No specific ordering of these actions is assumed. Instead, actions involving these three actors are intertwined in the IS/IT adoption process. Patterns emerging from the actual stories of IS/IT adoption processes are used to develop an empirica taxonomy of IS/IT adoption processes.

## 3.6. Conceptual model

In the context of innovation adoption, actions refer to activities aimed at adopting particular IS/IT innovations. Actors performing these actions may include top managers (who institute the policies related to the IS/IT innovation and its adoption), IS personnel (who oversee or manage the process of innovation adoption), the influencers (who are responsible for upholding organizational policies or are themselves early adopters of the innovation), and the potential adopters (who are the target users of IS/IT innovations).

Fig. 2 summarizes the conceptual model, including actions by three major actors: (a) the potential adopter, who considers the innovation for adoption; (b) the influencer, who is influencing the poten tial adopter to accept an innovation, and (c) the organizational context, in which the potential adopter and the influencer perform their activities. In this conceptual model, the ‘‘context” accounts for stakeholders such as top management and IS department whereas the ‘‘influencer” and the ‘‘adopter” represent the influencer and the potential adopter respectively.

## 4.2. Data analysis

The tape-recorded interviews were transcribed prior to data coding (Patton. 2oo2). To facilitate uniform coding across all interviews, unique codes were assigned for each a priori action identified in the theoretical development section (Miles & Huberman, 1984). When the coding began, there were 25 types of actions, including: four adoption actions, labeled A1 through A4, and six other actions, la beled A5 through A10, by the potential adopter (see Table 2), seven contextual actions, labeled C1 through C7 (see Table 3); and eight influencer actions labeled I1 through I8 (see Table 4). Three addi tional action-categories, one each for the contextual actions, influencer actions, and potential adopter actions, labeled OCA, OIA, and OAA respectively were set up to collect actions that may not be classi fied into any of the 25 a priori actions.

The coding of the interview data proceeded as follows.<sup>3</sup> For each interview, we identified the interviewee, his or her position in the organizational hierarchy, the specific IS/IT innovation, the adoption context, and the roles assumed by the interviewee in the adoption process. We also identified the different types of actions relevant for understanding the process of adoption including the actions aimed at the entire organization, the influencer actions aimed at the potential adopter only, the pre-adoption actions of the potential adopter, and the adoption actions<sup>4</sup> of the potential adopter. Table 6 shows illustrative examples of the data coding effort for the a priori actions that were reported multiple times in the interviews.

A total of 237 actions were coded from all interviews. Of these, 194 actions were classified into one of the 25 a priori actions described earlier. The remaining 43 actions were categorized into the three additional action categories (OCA, OIA, and OAA) that had been created for gathering the unclassifiable actions in contextual, influencer, and adopter actions respectively. The analysis of the 43 unclassifiable actions proceeded as follows. The interview texts denoting the actions in each category were examined separately for patterns, and actions that involved similar behaviors were aggregated to form new types of actions. Three actions emerged from the OIA category, for the influencer role: expertise (OI1), wherein the influencer provides responses or answers to specific questions regarding th innovation posed by the potential adopter; demonstration (OI2), wherein the influencer provides a walk-through of the innovation to the potential adopter; and knowledge sharing (OI3), wherein the influencer transfers special knowledge s/he possesses about the system to the potential adopter Two actions emerged from the OAA category for the potential adopter role: favorable response (OA1), wherein the potential adopter reacts positively to the innovation when s/he was introduced to it; and unfavorable response (OA2), wherein the potential adopter reacts negatively to the innovation when (s)he was introduced to it. Based on this analysis, 30 actions were recoded into five emergent categories. The remaining 13 actions (five contextual actions, six influencer actions, and two other adopter actions) remained unclassified and were excluded from further analysis.

The within-case analysis of the interview data entailed the identification of the chronological order of all actions that transpired within the context (in which the interviewee was situated) for each innovation, and building a description of innovation adoption (Miles & Huberman, 1984). This procedur yielded the adoption process reported in each interview. The cross-case analysis of the data was aimed at identifying families of similar adoption processes (Miles & Huberman, 1984). This involved the classification of the action sequences into different groups such that sequences were dissimilar across groups but similar within groups. The resultant groups of sequences would then indicate different processes of adoption in actual settings. A combination of two quantitative techniques, specifically optimal matching (Abbot & Hrycak, 1990; Sabherwal & Robey, 1993) and cluster analysis (Hair, Andersen, Tatham, & Black, 1999), was used for this purpose. In pursuing these analyses, we followed the approach previously used by Sabherwal and Robey (1993), with similar analytical procedures and tests being used during both optimal matching and cluster analysis, and in the same order.

Optimal matching is a technique that can be used to measure the resemblance of two given sequences. For optimal matching to work, the sequences must be represented by ‘‘a string of well defined elements” that are typically drawn from a ‘‘relatively small set” (Abbot & Hrycak, 1990). In this study, there are a total of 30 actions, including 25 a priori categories and five emergent types. A sequence can be defined using a string of actions from this set. For instance, consider the following sequence: c2, c6, a5, a7, oi2, a2 (referred to as SEQ1 hereafter). It represents the following actions: issuing a mandate (c2), training (c6), review (a5), inquiry (a7), demonstration (oi2), and partial adoption (a2). Another sequence may be composed of the following string of actions: c1, c4, c4, c6, a3, a7 a2 (referred to as SEQ2). It represents the following actions: awareness (c1), development (c4), development (c4), training (c6), experimentation (a3), inquiry (a7), and partial adoption (a2). The extent of resemblance between SEQ1 and SEQ2 is evaluated by computing the ‘‘distance” between these sequences (Abbott, 1990; Sabherwal & Robey, 1993). The distance is typically defined in terms of the number of substitutions, insertions, and deletions that would be needed to transform SEQ1 to SEQ2 Such transformations can usually be accomplished in numerous different ways. The goal is to determine the closest inter-sequence distance between the two sequences so that their resemblance can be measured. This requires the computing of all possible transformation paths and then assigning of the minimum cost as the distance between the two sequences.

The optimal matching program<sup>5</sup> provides an efficient way of identifying the various transformation paths and the minimum distances between all pairs of sequences in a given set (Abbot & Hrycak, 1990). In computing the inter-sequence distances using the Optimal Matching program, all substitution costs i.e. the cost of substituting one action with another action, were set as 1.0. That is, the substitution cost assume that all actions in the set are dissimilar to each other (Sabherwal & Robey, 1993). All indel (i.e. insertion and deletion) costs, i.e. the cost of either inserting an action into a sequence or deleting an action from a sequence, were set<sup>6</sup> as 0.50. Finally, the distances are affected by the length of the sequences to be transformed. That is, the distances tend to increase as the sequence lengths increase. To minimize the disturbances due to sequence lengths, we standardized the distances by the length of the longer sequence (Abbot & Hrycak, 1990; Sabherwal & Robey, 1993). The output of the Optimal Matching program was a matrix of distances that contained the minimum distances for all sequences from all other sequences.

Cluster analysis is a technique that can be used to classify observations into different categories (Hair et al., 1999). The use of cluster analysis is consistent with the objectives of the study, since the objective is to identify different types of adoption processes. The observations for the cluster analysis procedure were the inter-sequence distances obtained using the optimal matching procedure. We used SPSS 11.0 for conducting the cluster analysis. Cluster analysis procedures use a variety of linkage methods such as Ward’s linkage, within-group average linkage, and between-group average linkage to join different observations into groups (Hair et al., 1999; Punj & Stewart, 1983). We con ducted cluster analysis using the Ward’s linkage method.

Cluster analysis procedures provide the ‘‘fusion coefficients” at each agglomeration stage, which can be used to finalize the number of clusters for the final solution (Hair et al., 1999). In general, major jumps in fusion coefficients indicate significant differences between the clusters in that agglomerative stage. We examined the fusion coefficients and selected the three-cluster solution based on the extent of difference between two successive agglomeration stages. To verify the robustness of the clusters, we compared the within-group distances with the between-group dis tances for each cluster (Sabherwal & Robey, 1993). We first computed the mean distance of each sequence from other sequences in its cluster, and considered the sequence with the smallest mean distance as that cluster’s ‘‘centroid.” Subsequently, we conducted a t-test for the mean distance of each sequence from others in its cluster and the mean distance of each sequence from the centroid sequence of the other clusters. A significant t-test indicates that the sequences are more similar to others in their own clusters and more dissimilar to sequences in other clusters. In this study, the t tests were significant, thus indicating divergent clusters with similar sequences within each cluster.

We conducted several tests to interpret the emergent clusters. First, we inspected the centroid sequences of the three clusters to assess their dissimilarity with each other. Second, we inspected the sequences in each cluster to determine their similarity with each other (Abbot & Hrycak, 1990). Third, beginning with the centroid sequences and through an examination of the sequences, we constructed an ‘‘ideal sequence” for each cluster; i.e. a ‘‘hypothetical sequence” that best approximates the sequences in a cluster (Sabherwal & Robey, 1993). We rearranged, while preserving the chronolog ical order, the actions of the sequences in each cluster to be vertically aligned such that number of sequences that contain a particular action can be obtained. For membership in an ideal sequence, we employed a threshold level of 40%, i.e., an action should appear in at least 40% of the sequences to be included on the ideal sequence. Lastly, we evaluated the extent to which the sequences in each cluster were similar to the ideal sequences for that cluster (Sabherwal & Robey, 1993). This was done by computing the distances of each sequence from its ideal sequence and then performing a t-test on the distance of a sequence from its own ideal and the mean distance of the sequence from the other two ideals

## 5. Taxonomy of adoption processes

The adoption processes were determined based on the analysis of 224 actions for the 30 adoption sto ries. The analysis yielded three processes: Conscious Quest, Requisite Compliance, and Asserted Trial

## 5.1. Conscious quest

The ideal sequence for the Conscious Quest process includes five actions: awareness creation, training, experimentation, seeking assistance, and full adoption (see Fig. 3). This process was found in 26.7% of the adoption stories (eight stories out of 30). The eight stories that involved the Conscious Quest process contained 63 actions and the average sequence length was 7.88 actions (see Appendix B for action sequences).

The process typically begins with two contextual actions – awareness creation followed by train ing. The ‘‘awareness creation” effort introduces the innovation to potential adopters. Generally meant for the entire organization (or an entire sub-unit), this activity informs the potential adopters that the innovation would soon be available for use within the organization (or the sub-unit).

We were notified by communications by the project team through email and said, ‘‘It [Change-Point] was coming.” [Veronica]

Next, to aid the adoption of IS/IT innovations, the organization typically arranges formal training sessions for all potential adopters. Typically lasting a few hours (in the IS/IT innovations described in the field interviews), the training sessions generally enable the potential adopters to gain a basic understanding of the innovation such that they can adopt and start using the innovation without too much trouble.

We got a ChangePoint expert, someone employed by that company, who came to our site and we sat in a room over there for one week, who showed us, demonstrated how to do this. [Karen]

Following the above contextual action, the adopters typically experiment with the IS/IT innovation, apparently in an attempt to understand whether the system would be beneficial to them, and possibly to get the most out of it. This experimentation helps them to identify new features of the system, to understand different ways in which the system can be used, and to find better ways to accomplish the same tasks.

You get the basics and then when you want to run one and say, ‘‘Oh you can use those fields,” and then next time you know they are there. [Jennifer]

Following experimentation, the potential adopters typically seek assistance from others, possibly to find out how they can address any issues encountered while experimenting with the IS/IT innovation. For this purpose, the potential adopters may interact with one or more individuals, such as members of development teams, help desk personnel, technical support staff, or liaison personnel.

They had a [company] liaison with our ChangePoint team. . . So everything we needed was funneled to our [company] liaison, like from our team lead to the liaison to the ChangePoint team to get answers. [Karen]

This process generally concludes with full adoption. The potential adopters usually adopt and use all features of the innovation available to them.

I think I’m using pretty much all features that are available. [Paul]

I’d say . . . we probably utilized 80% of the features of the tool. There are probably another 20% available that either don’t pertain to us, we don’t want to use yet, or we have no need for. [Karen]

## 5.2. Requisite compliance

The ideal sequence for the Requisite Compliance process includes six actions: awareness creation, issuing of mandate, presenting rational arguments, training, experimentation, and partial adoption (see Fig. 4). It was seen in 40% of the adoption stories (12 stories out of 30). The 12 stories tha involved the Requisite Compliance process contained 85 actions and the average length of these sequences was 7.08 actions (see Appendix B for the action sequences).

Like ‘‘Conscious Quest,” the ‘‘Requisite Compliance” process also begins with two contextual actions. The first action typically is again an ‘‘awareness creation” effort by the organization, intended to introduce the innovation to potential adopters.

They sent out an email that it [Task Tracker] was going to be available on this date. [Neil]

However, the process then differs significantly from ‘‘Conscious Quest,” with a mandate, requiring the adoption of the IS/IT innovation, being typically issued. Thus, unlike the other two processes, this process reveals that the potential adopters are generally required to adopt the innovations to accom plish the everyday activities on their jobs. The organization typically ‘‘issues a mandate” that the inno vation must be accepted by all potential adopters.

We were told this is what we were going to do and this is the way we’re going to do it. [Hilda]

Next, the potential adopters get to hear more about the innovation and how they would indeed be bet ter off adopting it. The potential adopters are subject to the ‘‘rational arguments” made by other individ uals, such as their managers, for instance, who explicate the advantages of using the innovation.

[My boss said,] ‘‘There’s this great new application and it’s really going to be helpful to keep more accurate time. . .” [Wanda]

The potential adopters are then provided formal training sessions typically arranged by the organization.

The group that developed the application . . . sent down a couple of people. . . for the training sessions. [Jake]

Following formal training, the adopters typically experiment with the innovation, as was the case with the ‘‘Requisite Compliance” process.

After I received the basic training. . . I just start nosing around in the program. I start clicking on different areas and say, ‘‘Gee I wonder what this does” and at that point a lot of my understanding is self-taught. [Brian]

This process typically concludes with partial adoption, with adopters only using a subset of the features of the innovation.

I don’t use the calendar function. . . I am also not as frequent in task reminders as some of my other colleagues. [Brad]

I’m probably working on 25% of it [the Bentech system]. . . on a daily basis. [Raymond]

## 5.3. Asserted tria

The ideal sequence for the ‘‘Asserted Trial” process includes seven actions: being assertive, demonstration, inquiry, experimentation, inquiry, experimentation, and partial adoption (see Fig. 5) and was found in 33.3% of the adoption stories (10 stories out of 30). The 10 stories that reported an Asserted Trial process comprised 76 actions and the average length of the sequence was 7.66 actions (see Appendix B for the action sequences).

The process typically begins with two influence actions: assertion and demonstration. The first action involves someone asserting that the potential adopter should adopt the innovation. Several different types of influencers, including the potential adopter’s manager or supervisor, co-worker, team member, etc., performed these assertive behaviors.

![](/api/attachments/DCQ3NSTQ/fulltext/images/a7a91b9aa0690ee1722bf6a46d145ac62a0d81b2cfa96af6019d13a30b58a749.jpg)  
Fig. 5. The ‘‘Asserted Trial” process.

Because it [the Bloomberg System] was considered on the job as well. . . It wasn’t until I actually got on the desk that someone take my hand and say, ‘‘You need this system,” and that’s about how it was initially introduced to me. And I said, ‘‘Okay.” [Katelin]

Following the assertive behavior, the influencers generally provide ‘‘demonstrations” or ‘‘walk throughs” of the innovation. These were intended to help the adopters to understand the ways in which the innovation could best be exploited.

[My boss] explained it was a flowcharting tool and then he showed me. . . This is how I got trained. ‘‘You take your mouse, you go to this and it goes like that. . . You just take it and you drag it over here.” [Cheryl]

The potential adopters then engage in ‘‘inquiry” – querying other individuals they come in contact with, such as their peers or managers. Such inquiry typically helps the adopters to understand aspects of the innovation that would help them in making better use of the innovation

I had to call our help desk. I called them on a number of occasions. . . I’m pretty sure the help desk wasn’t trained in XP. . . So, when I called up with an XP question they automatically routed me to a specialist. [Tim]

Following inquiry, the adopters typically experiment with the innovation. This enables them to be come familiar with the features of the innovation, and how they can be exploited for their specific needs.

Once I had the user ID and password, I just started poking around. . . I tried to figure out as much as I could on my own. [Teresa]

The adopters next conduct further ‘‘inquiry,” typically with other individuals they interact with in their networks such as their peers or managers. This allows them to obtain specific information abou the innovation or the features of the innovation or using the innovation.

To figure out how to add a task. . . a question or two about it but not very much. [Nancy]

The potential adopters subsequently conduct more experimentation with the innovation, thereby learning more about the innovation. During this experimentation, the adopters sometimes discover aspects of the innovation or new ways by which to exploit the innovation or more efficient ways to use the innovation.

Then you figure out. . . It’s just one of those things where it really is outside of the box and the book doesn’t tell you. [Helen]

Like ‘‘Requisite Compliance,” this process typically concludes with partial adoption, with the adopt ers only using a subset of the features of the innovation.

[I use] a reasonable amount of the operating system [Windows XP] features. [Tyler]

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Differing dynamics of the three processes

At first glance, it might seem that the three processes have some commonalities. For instance, ‘‘experimentation” was seen in all three processes; and ‘‘awareness,” ‘‘training,” and ‘‘partial adoption” were seen in two of the three processes. However, the three adoption processes were considerably dif ferent in the ways in which the actions coalesced over time to drive innovation adoption.

Both Conscious Quest and Requisite Compliance processes began with an ‘‘awareness creation” action and contained ‘‘training” and ‘‘experimentation” actions. Moreover, those three actions happened in the same temporal order in both processes albeit with some interspersion of other actions. However, the two processes differed in important respects. First, individuals who reported the Requisite Compliance process required both mandate and rational arguments before they experienced training, but this was not the case for individuals who reported the Conscious Quest process, as they moved directly from awareness creation to training. Moreover, those who reported Conscious Quest sought further assistance with the system and reached full adoption while those who reported Requisite Compliance did not take that additional step and stayed at partial adoption.

The remaining process, Asserted Trial, was typically seen in voluntary use contexts involving office or productivity tools. Unlike the other two processes, Asserted Trial seems to be a somewhat localized process that generally begins with the ‘‘being assertive” and ‘‘demonstration” actions. It did not contain any contextual actions, i.e. there were no actions aimed at the entire organization; there was no awareness or mandate. Moreover, unlike the other two processes, both of which involved ‘‘training,” Asserted Trial did not. That is, the organization did not arrange training sessions, which is not surprising consid ering how this process began to unfold. Subsequent to ‘‘being assertive” and ‘‘demonstration” actions individuals engaged in ‘‘inquiry” and ‘‘experimentation” actions followed by ‘‘inquiry” and ‘‘experimentation” actions again. Unlike Requisite Compliance, individuals were typically not required to use these systems in their regular jobs, and unlike Conscious Quest, they did not seem to themselves seek information and assistance related to the innovation. Instead, in Asserted Trial, potential adopters were influenced to adopt such systems by another individual in their personal networks, who typically provided a ‘‘demonstration” or a ‘‘walk-through” of the system as well. And, unlike Conscious Quest but much like Requisite Compliance, Asserted Trial resulted in ‘‘partial adoption” as well.

Thus, the three processes are distinct in terms of the types of actions that guide them, and provide valuable new insights into the processes that may unfold during the adoption of IS/IT innovations. One fundamental difference – which stands out when comparing Figs. 3–5 – is in terms of the types of actions that are missing from the typical sequence for each process. Whereas the Conscious Quest process involves no influencer actions, Requisite Compliance involves no actions by the adopter other than adoption itself, and Asserted Trial involves no actions within the context. The Conscious Quest process seems to be driven by the adopter, with no social influence and no mandate. In contrast, in both Requisite Compliance and Asserted Trial processes, the adopter encounters considerable pres sure – from a contextual mandate in the case of Requisite Compliance and from influencers in the case of Asserted Trial.

## 6.2. Culmination in partial or full adoption

Conscious Quest resulted in ‘‘full adoption” whereas Requisite Compliance and Asserted Trial only resulted in ‘‘partial adoption.” This may be related to the above differences among the three processes. Perhaps, the fact that the Conscious Quest process driven by the adopter, which involves the adopte seeking the IS/IT solution, learning about it, and so on, explains the full adoption in this process. In contrast, the adopter encounters pressure in both Requisite Compliance and Asserted Trial processes and perhaps this is why both these processes culminate in partial adoption.

The partial or full adoption could also be explained in other ways. The full adoption in Conscious Quest process could be associated in some instances to the systems being fairly stable over time, so that individuals did not have to deal with changes or upgrades as they got used to the system.

The more you learn, the more you know how to get in and out of things, but it’s [Personal Manager’s] basic structure has stayed the same. There’s been no upgrade. . . What’s there is there. It has its own little glitches. . . but no, since I’ve been here it’s never changed. When you learn the system . . . you’ve learned it. [Melissa]

By contrast, the partial adoption in Requisite Compliance was associated in a few cases to instabil ity of the system. Individuals did not adopt full features of the system because they believed the system would undergo changes, and upgrades to the system would require them to learn new features.

Some of them are upgraded to new releases, new pieces to get new functionality. And sometimes we know about them and sometimes we don’t. Sometimes we open up the machine on Monday morning and have a completely different look to it, and we’ve lost functionality, we don’t know why. [Hilda]

The full adoption in Conscious Quest process could be associated in some instances to the adopters being in charge of certain responsibilities, such as providing technical support for executives, which required them to have a good understanding of the system and the knowledge to work with it.

I had a team that was responsible for doing executive support and we wanted to know as much about this system [Travel Manager] as we could before the executive customers had to experience it. [Tim]

In contrast to this enthusiastic attitude in Conscious Quest, in both Requisite Compliance and Asserted Trial processes, a few adopters complained about time constraints, and attributed partial adop tion to this reason. In the Requisite Compliance process, individuals indicated that due to time constraints they only used those features of the system they needed the most to accomplish their tasks

‘‘I found the ones [features] I thought were useful and I use them. There may be others out there that are useful too. But you get to a point where you get too busy. It’s like, ‘‘Yeah, this kind of does what I need it to do and I don’t have time to figure out the rest of it.” [Wanda]

The partial adoption of IS/IT innovations at the end of the Requisite Compliance process may also have been due to the negative attitudes of the individuals toward the system for different reasons. These reasons may be attributed to the system itself, such as the ease of using the system, or to wha the system implied, such as issues related to control.

It was, in my mind, the most distasteful thing I’ve ever experienced. It [the Travel Manager system] was awful to use. [Elizabeth]

I didn’t have any concerns about the actual system itself. I think I had concerns like, ‘‘I’m a salaried executive and you’re wanting me to keep track of my time like I’m an hourly paid [employee].” [Jake]

In a similar vein, one of the causes for partial adoption in Asserted Trial is the lack of time for work ing with and discovering more features of the system.

If I had time to sit down with it [Microsoft Visio], you know with some down time, I think I could probably figure out most of the features. [Sue]

In Asserted Trial. several individuals used the systems only on an as-needed basis. Thus. if individuals did not face particular needs or tasks or requirements, then there was a good chance that aspects of the system never got used.

It was mostly need. . . When I needed to do something, I had to go figure out how to do it. . . So I never really set out to learn the system; I just did it on an as needed basis. [Tyler]

Further, personal dispositions of individuals played a role in partial adoption. Individuals who possessed more playfulness or innovativeness probably would have breached the stage of partial adoption and gone beyond.

As a user, I am not the leading edge person who is going out and looking around what the product can completely do. I kind of learn through trial and error and then I am introduced to new features as people kind of make them available to me. [Keith]

Finally, it is possible that individuals have access to other systems that they can access for accomplishing their regular tasks. In such a case, the new system would most likely be used for only those situations in which the old systems were insufficient

It [Stanford Chart] will do all kinds of charting. . . [However, Microsoft] Excel does charting very well also. I know how to use that [Stanford Chart for creating bubble charts] and use Excel fo the other kinds. I go to this [Stanford Chart] for the bubble ones, because Excel makes it difficult to do a bubble [chart]. [Janet]

## 6.3. Limitations

The findings should be interpreted in the light of the limitations of the research, from company access to data collection.

Single point of reference. First, the organizations that served as research sites were selected from a single point of reference, i.e. their representation on the advisory board of a large university. It is conceivable that the individuals representing these organizations participated on the advisory board because of their emphasis or their realization of the importance of information systems on the everyday operations of their organizations. Thus, the research findings can be considered more reflective of organizations that are pro-active in their approach to information systems. However, this particular bias was somewhat mitigated by the fact that the individuals on the advisory board served as ‘‘sponsors” (i.e. they referred us to other individuals within their organizations) rather than as ‘‘participants.” Thus, the participants need not necessarily share the same opinions or beliefs as the sponsors.

Retrospective accounts. The research relied on retrospective accounts of the adoption stories recounted by the participants. Further, the individuals reported their perceptions of the innovatio adoption process. Thus, retrospective accounts are based almost entirely on perceptions and may suffer from recall bias (Collopy, 1996). Consequently, the findings of the study are dependent on the extent to which the participants were able to accurately explicate their experiences related to adoption. We took some precautions to somewhat mitigate these potential biases in data collection. For instance, we allowed the participants to tell their stories in their own words, with minimal interruptions to maintain the storyline, and with appropriate probes to obtain riche descriptions.

Social desirability bias. The research may have suffered from bias associated to considerations b participants for social desirability (Collopy, 1996). The interviews allowed the participants to recount ‘‘stories” of their adoption behaviors related to IS/IT innovations. It is quite possible that the participants consciously excluded descriptions of their experiences that may have negative connotations or be perceived as negative by other individuals (such as the researchers). For instance, three of the influencer actions were not reported by participants: ‘‘appealing to higher authority,” ‘‘acting in a clandestine manner,” and ‘‘using friendliness and ingratiation.” Further, ‘‘applying sanctions” was reported only once across all interviews. While it may be that these actions were not employed for adoption, nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Linear sequencing of actions. The optimal matching analysis employed in the research requires a linear arrangement of the actions in a sequence. The linear arrangement of actions may not entirely capture the variety of actions experienced by individuals. For instance, it is quite possible that individuals are subject to multiple actions at the same time; the multiple actions then signify concurrent actions. Thus, an individual may experience an action from an influencer (say, rational arguments, I5) and an action from the context (say, awareness creation, C1) at the same time. For the optimal matching algorithm to work, the two actions would have been coded as C1, I5 or I5, C1, neither of which may be entirely accurate. Further, there is always the possibility of an error in identifying the sequence despite best efforts. However, optimal matching methods are found to be robust to such perturbations in the action sequences (Abbot & Tsay, 2000; Forrest & Abbot, 1990).

Common method bias. The data on each adoption process was collected using a field interview. Thus, only a single informant’s view of each process was obtained. Our emergent findings may have been limited by the use of a single method (interviews) and single informant on each process.

## 6.4. Directions for future research

Despite the above limitations, this paper has some implications for future research. First, the paper has adopted a comprehensive view of the IS/IT adoption process, incorporating not only actions by the potential adopter, but also actions by other individuals and actions within the context of the IS/IT innovation. Moreover, rather than assuming technological or organizational determinism, it has adopted an emergent perspective. Instead of viewing IS/IT innovations in terms of level of adoption, behavioral intentions, and so on, and relating such dependent variables to certain organizational or technological factors that might affect them, this approach examines the adoption process as one that includes three interlocking, and mutually influencing, sets of actions. This perspective on IS/IT innovation adoption, which has enabled us to develop a taxonomy of IS/IT adoption processes, should be useful in future research in this area.

Moreover, the emergent taxonomy has the potential to be useful for future research. It includes three processes that seem distinct, with one driven by the adopter (Conscious Quest), another driven by influencers (Asserted Trial), and the third driven by the context (Requisite Compliance). Further research is needed to examine whether these processes are supported with other samples.

Further research is also needed to examine the circumstances under which these three processes might occur. One possible explanation for the different processes may be whether the adopter is an early adopter or a late adopter. Whereas early adopters usually make the adoption decision based on rational evaluation of the innovation, late adopters more commonly make the decision based on the opinions of others (Parthasarathy & Bhattacherjee, 1998). Consequently, earlier adopters are more likely to fully adopt the innovation, whereas later adopters are more likely to partially adopt the innovation (Rogers, 1995). In the light of our findings and these conclusions from prior research, we expec early adopters to be involved in Conscious Quest, and later adopters to be more involved in the other two processes.

Finally, further research is needed to examine the reasons due to which the three processes cul minate differently, with partial adoption in Requisite Compliance and Asserted Trial, and full adop tion in Conscious Quest. Some initial insights have been provided in this paper, including the stability of the system, the time available to the potential adopter, and the presence of reasons for learning the system’s features. Additional research is needed to further develop these and other possible explanations.

## 6.5. Implications for practice

The paper also has some potentially useful implications for practice. It has identified three distinct processes, with Requisite Compliance being a top-down (i.e., management-driven) process, Conscious Quest being a bottom-up (i.e., adopter-driven) process, and Asserted Trial being somewhat in between, as influencer-driven. The paper has also examined the differences among them in terms of how they might culminate, and identified some possible reasons these different processes might arise. These findings should provide insights that might be useful for individuals who participate in IS/IT innovation diffusion processes, including managers, users, and IS developers.

One finding that should be beneficial to managers as well as users is that adopters of IS/IT innovations usually do not perform any actions other than adoption (such as inquiry or seeking assistance) when faced with a mandate for adopting the system. The mandate apparently makes the adopters re spond in a passive fashion. In contrast, in the absence of a mandate for adopting the system, potentia adopters are more proactive, and actively seek information and assistance! Moreover, mandates for the adoption of an IS/IT innovation lead to the use of Requisite Compliance, which culminates in partial adoption. Managers are also likely to benefit from the conclusion that stable systems typically involve the Conscious Quest process and full adoption, whereas systems that are more dynamic usually involve one of the other two processes and partial adoption.

Another useful finding for individuals managing IS/IT innovations is that early adopters are more likely to be involved in the Conscious Quest process, whereas late adopters are more likely to be involved in the other two processes. When individuals are involved early in the process (such as by training them or assigning them responsibilities), they seem to follow the Conscious Quest process, being enthusiastic and active during the adoption process and eventually reaching full adoption. The importance of involving users during initial stages of the IS/IT innovation process is consistent with prior literature on user involvement (e.g., Franz & Robey, 1984; Ives & Olson, 1984; Newman & Noble, 1990). On the other hand, individuals who are not involved early in the process follow either Requisite Compliance or Asserted Trial process, complain about time constraints, and only achieve partial adoption.

Finally, a useful finding for IS personnel and other influencers is that assertions and demonstrations lead to Asserted Trial process, while rational arguments lead to Requisite Compliance process, with both processes culminating in partial adoption. Indeed, IS personnel influencers and other influencers could conclude that full adoption is more likely when they make potential adopters aware of the inno vation and provide them some initial training about it

## 7. Conclusions

This paper set out to examine the processes by which individuals within organizations adopted IS/ IT innovations. Using data obtained from 27 interviews in actual work settings, and treating the IS/IT adoption phenomenon as a series of actions, we found three intertwined sets of actions, i.e., contextual, influencer, and adopter’s actions, that together provide a useful way of understanding the inno vation adoption process. Furthermore, we found that the three types of actions combine in three very different ways, i.e., Conscious Quest, Requisite Compliance, and Asserted Trial, when influencing the adoption behavior of individuals. Conscious Quest process, which results in ‘‘full adoption,” seems to be driven by the adopter, with no social influence and no mandate. In contrast, in Requisite Compliance and Asserted Trial processes, both of which resulted in ‘‘partial adoption,” the adopter encounters considerable pressure, either from a contextual mandate (Requisite Compliance) or from influencers (Asserted Trial).

Thus, this study shows the value of adopting a process approach to understanding IS/IT adoption, focusing on actions and the sequences in which they occur, in contrast to the predominant use of variance-based approach in prior literature. This study also demonstrates that individuals within organizations do not respond to IS/IT innovations in universalistic ways but rather follow distinctive paths to adoption, typically contingent on the larger context representing the organization, their immediate context comprising the network of individuals, and their own preferences and styles. Thus, when formalizing policies for the implementation of IS/IT innovations. organizations should take into account the distinct processes that may unfold during the adoption of these innovations.
