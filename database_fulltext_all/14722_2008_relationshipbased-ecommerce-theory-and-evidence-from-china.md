# Relationship-based e-commerce: theory and evidence from China

Maris G. Martinsons

\*Department of Management, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon, Hong Kong SAR, China, email: mgmaris@cityu.edu.hk

Abstract. Electronic commerce models and prescriptions from rule-based marke economies like the United States have limited applicability in emerging markets. This paper adopts a strategic management perspective to examine the distinctive challenges facing e-commerce in China. A theory is developed to explain how the lack of dependable rules encourages guanxi and relationship-based commerce. It suggests that personal trust, contextual and informal information, and blurred boundaries between business and government have shaped e-commerce in main land China. Case studies of online retailers in Beijing and Shanghai and a business-to-business (B2B) marketspace reveal how dynamic business relationships with complementary service providers and state agents can overcome institutional deficiencies. Short message service (SMS)-based mobile commerce (m-commerce) and other leapfrogging information technology (IT) applications could transform Chinese consumer behaviour and improve economic efficiency. The evidence from China helps to explain the influence of culture and institutions on different types of IT applications. Implications for e-commerce research and practice in China and other emerging markets are discussed.

Keywords: e-commerce, strategic management, institutional theory, interpersona trust, rule-based governance, developing countries

## INTRODUCTION

Despite a nearly global desire to benefit from electronic commerce, national progress has differed greatly. This paper explains why e-commerce in emerging markets like China is less developed and more challenging than in established market economies like the United States.

E-commerce in the United States has become very popular and beneficial to both consumers and businesses. Even after the dot-com bubble burst, Americans have remained overwhelmingly optimistic about the business potential of the internet (according to assorted market research). Online ventures such as Amazon, eBay and Google have flourished while corporate America (as exemplified by Dell. General Electric and Wal-Mart) has increased its productivity by embracing e-business. In 2006, the total value of e-commerce in the United States exceeded \$3000 billion while online retailing alone topped \$100 billion, up 20% from 2005 (US Census Bureau, 2007). A study comparing 10 countries found that US-based companies make the most intensive use of the internet as a business tool (Kraemer et al., 2006).

The situation is very different in mainland China. Transplanting e-business models from America to China has been difficult, as evident from the experiences of Yahoo and eBay. The frustrations of Fortune 500 firms led one chief executive officer to assert that e-commerce in China was ‘hopeless’ (Wong, 2001). Such despair has moderated over time and e-commerce is undeniably progressing across China, but it still faces substantial and distinctive challenges.

Business in China remains comparatively traditional, with levels of information technology (IT) and networked readiness being ‘remarkably low’ and ‘losing ground’, respectively (Quan et al., 2005; WEF, 2007). Martinsons (2005) optimistically suggests that IT is ‘transforming China’, but Chiu (2006, p. 9) reports that ‘the internet has limited benefit to Chinese enterprises’. Ma et al. (2003, p. 466) conclude that China will require decades ‘to reach the current stage (of e-commerce development) in Europe or America . . . (since the) transformation of doing business and consumption habits . . . will take a rather long period of time’. Less than 5% of commerce in China was conducted online in 2006 (CNNIC, 2000–2007), whereas e-commerce is approaching 15% in the much larger US economy (US Census Bureau, 2007).

The existing literature on e-commerce in China in both English (as revealed by searches of Google Scholar and the ProQuest databases) and Chinese (as evidenced by a search of the China Journal Fulltext Database) is predominantly anecdotal and case-based.

This paper aims to provide a holistic understanding of e-commerce in China that is also applicable to other emerging markets by adopting a strategic management perspective. It first considers why mainstream e-commerce models and prescriptions, derived primarily from the US context, are poorly suited to contemporary China. This leads to an explanatory theory, which (a) identifies key variables; (b) models relationships between those variables; and (c) aims primarily to explain observed phenomena, but may also help to predict the future. Revelatory cases (Yin, 1994) of three online pioneers in China are used to illustrate the nature of relationship-based e-commerce. Finally, implications are discussed for both the research and practice of e-commerce in China and other emerging market economies.

## E-COMMERCE IN ESTABLISHED MARKETS

Conditions in well-established markets like the United States foster e-commerce. Consumers have convenient, reliable and inexpensive access to the internet. They can search the Web to compare products offered by many rival vendors. Online purchases can be made with easily available and widely accepted credit cards, and they often benefit from sales tax savings. Meanwhile, online security systems effectively verify identities, authenticate transactions, protect consumer privacy and help to prevent contract repudiation.

Purchased products arrive quickly because of extensive transportation networks and keen competition among delivery services. Dissatisfied buyers can usually get a refund or credit towards another purchase. In the worst case, dependable recourses are available based on formal laws, regulations and commercial agreements. Americans enjoy convenient and costefficient e-commerce with minimal risk since information is widely available, contracts are enforceable and the underlying regulatory system is trustworthy.

E-businesses and marketspace providers can also leverage (or easily replicate) many services from traditional marketplaces. For example, creating a secure online payment system is easier because there is already a well-developed infrastructure to authorize credit card purchases and bill transactions. Highly formalized business processes and standardized data formats enable supply chain partners to coordinate (if not seamlessly integrate) their activities and to share information about customers and products, respectively.

E-commerce has proliferated in the United States due largely to comprehensive and reliable telecommunications; competition between suppliers and a wealth of information about them; secure and convenient payment systems; fast and dependable delivery services; and most importantly, a comprehensive and effective regulatory foundation (Markus & Soh, 2002; Xu et al., 2004). Not surprisingly, US-based firms perceive ‘that there are no significant difficulties or barriers to doing business on the internet’ (Kraemer et al., 2006).

The mainstream models and prescriptions for e-business (cf. Weill & Vitale, 2001) implicitly assume the conditions listed in Table 1. However, these conditions are not valid universally, and remain rare in mainland China. Assorted reports (Bin et al., 2003; CNNIC, 2000–2007; Kraemer et al., 2006; Logan et al., 2004, Tan & Wu, 2004; Xu et al., 2004) indicate that e-commerce in China has been stymied by inefficient telecommunications, inconvenient payment mechanisms, poor quality products, unreliable delivery, and fundamental concerns about security and trust due to a poor legal/regulatory system.

## RULES AND RELATIONSHIPS

Commercial activities in market-based economies are governed by a universal set of forma rules and supported by reliable public information. Codified agreements and transparent transactions between relatively independent businesses (and consumers) are enforceable, largely because they can be adjudicated by third parties (Li & Li, 2000). The professiona (impersonal) management of enterprises and competition between providers of telecommunications, financing, transportation and other services promotes efficient flows of money, people, goods and information. The state makes and enforces the rules governing business behaviour but severely limits its own participation in a market-based economy.

Developing a trustworthy rule-based system to govern commercial activities requires a huge commitment. Enormous investment is needed to draft, enforce and interpret the rules. After the institutions or ‘rules of the game in a society’ (North, 1990, p. 3) and systemic trust are established, the marginal costs of fine-tuning the rule-based system and enforcing each contract are low. The low marginal costs encourage large-scale market development.

A rule-based system with stringent policing underpins commerce in the United States. The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (passed by the US Congress in 1977, http://www.usdoj.gov criminal/fraud/fcpa/) also covers many international firms, but rule-based systems remain rare globally. In contrast to the rules-based US Securities and Exchange Commission, the United Kingdom’s Financial Services Authority is a principles-based regulator. In the European Union, rule-based market competition is officially subordinate to economic and social progress, employment and sustainable development.

Rule-based systems remain conspicuously absent in mainland China despite contrary rhetoric from the national government in Beijing (cf. Holliday & Yep, 2005). Imperial China emphasized the emperor’s discretionary authority and family-based social hierarchy (Martinsons & Hempel, 1995). After a failed experiment with state-led centralized planning and resource allocation (until the early 1980s), China has yet to develop institutions to suppor adequately a market-based economy. For example, the commissions regulating both banking and securities in China have severely compromised their institutional legitimacy with marke interventions and policy U-turns (cf. Kynge, 2006).

The inability to rely on universal rules and impartial courts, especially when state agencies are involved, has forced the Chinese to rely greatly on guanxi (Xin & Pearce, 1996; Seligman, 1999). Guanxi are relationships or connections between two or more people (or organizations) in which each can prevail on the other(s) for help. The Chinese ‘do not (particularly) love guanxi’ (Li & Li, 2000), but they must depend on it in the absence of systematic rules. Institutional deficiencies encourage people in China to do business with those that they know and trust – family, friends, classmates and others in their in-group or guanxiwang (network of connections).

Guanxi lubricates the early stages of business and provides psychological comfort in dynamic and uncertain environments. New business partnerships commonly result from informal recommendations by existing in-group members. Those making recommendations have strong incentives to be reliable and accurate because their personal reputation is at risk. Trusted partners can rely on handshake agreements' in lieu of formal contracts. Since those with the right guanxi can break or at least bend written rules (Seligman, 1999, p. 35), business conduct in China is often judged in terms of being reasonable, heli, rather than strictly legal – hefa. Personal reputations and abilities to get assistance within a guanxiwang thus depend on reasonable conduct (Tang & Ward, 2003, pp. 118–120).

In-group members tend to share key information selectively in order to maintain both thei competitive advantage and flexibility (Burrows et al., 2005). Information is exchanged through personal communications that are difficult to codify (Martinsons & Westwood, 1997). Publishing or broadcasting sensitive data is discouraged in China; it can even lead to criminal prosecution. The Chinese legal system tends to penalize those revealing sensitive information (especially if it involves state agencies) harsher than those misappropriating intellectual property or personal data (Chiu, 2006). This has hindered the development of public information networks. Even foreign firms searching for suppliers in China must develop guanxi because public information is scarce and unreliable (Millington et al., 2006).

In China, the Communist Party is virtually inseparable from the state. It enjoys a lot of discretionary authority in the absence of a trustworthy rule-based system. The state and/or its agents are also active in many spheres of commerce rather than merely being rule makers and enforcers. This has blurred the boundary between commercial actors and those regulating their behaviour. The lack of separation between business and government combined with the scarcity of reliable information in the public domain hinders transparency and encourages business people to rely on personal arrangements rather than systemic rules.

Amidst China’s economic reforms, it is apt to recall the Chinese saying ‘heaven is high and the Emperor is far away’. National authorities have gradually reduced their intervention in the economy since the 1970s, partly by delegating many governance responsibilities to provincial and local counterparts (Li et al., 1998). Since China’s commercial and contractual laws remain incomplete and poorly institutionalized, enforcement is subject to discretion. Regional and local authorities have taken up the central government’s former role of providing political protection and access to scarce resources in exchange for a profit ‘share (Boisot & Child, 1996, p. 612).

Business risk in emerging markets like China is heightened by an incomplete set of com mercial rules and arbitrary interpretation and/or enforcement of those rules. Many Chinese enterprises have diversified their businesses, partly because they wish to reduce their depen dence on those who regulate a particular industry (cf. Li et al., 1998).

Relationship-based commerce requires minimal infrastructure. It is attractive if the geographic area and number of participants are both small. However, the incremental costs escalate prohibitively as the market enlarges. The manageable number of relationships i limited severely by the time and effort needed to develop and maintain each one.

Relationship-based commerce restricts market scale and scope. Since business people share information very selectively and assist each other informally, supply chains and markets remain highly fragmented and poorly coordinated (Lovett et al., 1999). This inefficiency has discouraged the development of business services that benefit from economies of scale. Consequently, telecommunication and transport networks as well as financial and logistics services in relationship-based economies tend to be underdeveloped (cf. Li, 2005).

## IMPLICATIONS FOR E-COMMERCE

The underdeveloped infrastructure and institutions in China hinder the development of the capabilities needed for online competitiveness (Wong et al., 2004). For example, reliable credi payment and product delivery services in the United States predate e-commerce, but they remain immature in China (Stylianou et al., 2003; Li, 2005). Chinese businesses also hesitate to extend commercial credit primarily because creditworthiness is difficult to evaluate without reliable public information. Thus, business scope is determined more by personal discretion and social needs rather than economic forces.

Many firms in the United States and Europe have increased productivity by formalizing and redesigning their core business processes while outsourcing peripheral activities (Croom, 2005; Kraemer et al., 2006). In contrast, formalized business processes and standardized data formats remain rare in China. Enterprise-wide information systems (IS) are commonly resisted because they reduce operational flexibility and managerial discretion (Martinsons, 2004). Informal collaboration is preferred to formal outsourcing contracts while ‘transparency is anathema to most Chinese companies . . . (they) not only remain reluctant to share operational and financial details, but also lack the modern technologies, such as ERP systems’ (Chesse, 2006). Chinese managers rely much less on IT applications than Western (and especially US) managers (Martinsons & Westwood, 1997; Quan et al., 2005). E-commerce is difficult without IS to plan production and track orders, payments, inventories and deliveries.

E-commerce also lacks the social richness that Chinese consumers value highly (Efendioglu & Yip, 2004; Richards & Shen, 2006). They prefer to interact personally with vendors, inspect ing products physically before purchasing them. Consumers in China make more shopping trips and visit more stores per trip than their American counterparts, but they buy significantly less (Li et al., 2004). They enjoy the social and entertainment aspects of shopping, including the ability to haggle on prices and ask for special deals. Li et al. (2004) studied shopping mall behaviour and found that ‘persuading a Chinese shopper to commit to a purchase is more challenging that it would be with an American shopper’.

The transparency of e-commerce endangers the privileged status of political actors in emerging markets like China. State bureaus and government officials have traditionally used their discretionary power to authorize/support or forbid/hinder commerce. Consequently, busi ness people in economies like China cultivate government patrons in order to access statecontrolled resources and protect themselves from bureaucratic interference.

Digital databases and audit trails encourage economic efficiency. For example, a Chinawide Web database combats foreign exchange fraud that relies on forged customs declaration forms. E-commerce also makes it more difficult for state officials to restrict the business activities of ‘untrustworthy’ people (based upon social origin or status) arbitrarily or to manipu late government records. Already, the Chinese National Statistics Bureau is using the internet to collect data directly from companies in order to reduce distortion by local governments. Based on Francis Bacon’s assertion that knowledge is power, the internet literati threaten the paramount role of the Communist Party.

The central government has reduced its direct control of China’s economy since the 1970s. However, various ministries and regulatory agencies still greatly influence commerce. A common expression – gong gong shao le, popo hai hen duo (there are now fewer fathers-inlaw, but still too many mothers-in-law) reflects the ongoing turf battles between them (Tang & Ward, 2003, pp. 49–50). Meanwhile, sub-national authorities are using various mechanisms to support some commercial activities while inhibiting or even forbidding others. Therefore, online ventures seeking to achieve profitable economies of scale must typically satisfy regulators in numerous jurisdictions (provinces, municipalities, etc.) across China.

As discussed in the next section, e-commerce in China faces additional challenges because the country is very heterogeneous.

## CHINA-SPECIFIC CHALLENGES FOR E-COMMERCE

China is a rapidly developing country with 1.3 billion people and a strict birth control policy. A would-be online retailer may thus envision attracting hundreds of millions of Chinese parents, each with a treasured single child. However, China is a poor country. The median household income is about \$3500 per year. Even after adjusting for purchasing power, only 15% of households earned \$30 000 in 2005 (United Nations, 2006). China has growing numbers of millionaires and even billionaires, but the vast majority of Chinese still have meager earnings and savings. The economic disparity in China, reflected in its very high Gini coefficient (United Nations, 2006), creates additional difficulties for e-commerce.

China may be bisected conceptually, creating an oversimplified but useful dichotomy between its have and have-not regions (Zhao & Tong, 2000). The southern and eastern coastal regions are heavily urbanized, industrialized (or post-industrialized) and comparatively wealthy. The densely populated financial and trading hub of Hong Kong epitomizes modern development while cities such as Beijing, Guangzhou and Shanghai have emerged as major commercial and/or industrial centres. Transportation and telecommunication networks within and between these major centres are among the best in the world.

However, only one-third of China’s people live in urban areas compared to two-thirds in the United States, Canada and Japan. Most Chinese still work in agriculture or primitive industries. Despite some modernization efforts (cf. Davison et al., 2005), the Chinese countryside and even its western cities typically lack the basic infrastructure and services needed for e-commerce (Tan & Wu, 2004).

China is the world’s biggest telecommunications market with 370 million fixed-line subscribers and 460 million cellphone users by year-end 2006 (Kwong, 2007). While telecom services are increasingly affordable and reliable, less than half of all small businesses and 5% of households in China had a personal computer (PC) by 2005 (Dell, 2005, corporate information provided by William Amelio [Managing Director of Asia Pacific]). Although 150 million Chinese (overwhelmingly urban dwellers) used the internet regularly in 2006 (CNNIC, 2000–2007), up 50% from 2004 (Zhu and Wang, 2005), relating internet usage to e-commerce is naive.

Chinese internet users average 50% more time online than their American counterparts, but less than 20% of them made an online purchase in 2006, compared to 75% in the United States (CMRG, 2007). Chinese consumers save much of their modest incomes and nearly all are ‘very concerned’ or ‘concerned’ about internet security issues (e.g. the reliability of encryption technologies) and legal issues, such as the enforcement of online commitments (see Efendioglu & Yip, 2004; Fang & Yen, 2006). E-commerce also increases worries about counterfeiting since sellers can remain anonymous or provide false identities and goods cannot be inspected physically.

Even as online access and trust increase, delivery concerns linger beyond major cities and coastal provinces (Li, 2005). The infrastructure for moving physical goods across China is improving, but heavy regulations and/or competitive restrictions remain, especially for postal services and transport by both rail and water.

China also lacks many financial services that are very common in the United States (Tan & Wu, 2004). Deficiencies in payment systems and commercial insurance have retarded market place development. Chinese banks had issued over a billion debit cards and 50 million credit cards by early 2007 (CMRG, 2007), but their use was commonly restricted to specific stores o geographic regions. Cash was still used for 83% of all payments in China compared to 21% in the United States (Ip & Yip, 2007). Currency restrictions also limit foreign remittances. E-commerce in China thus depends heavily upon cash-on-delivery and post office transfers.

In terms of the technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989), e-commerce in China has not been easy to use (because of poor infrastructure and services) or particularly useful (because of questionable value propositions).

Online ventures in China have commonly adopted e-business models from the United States. Some portals (e.g. SINA and Sohu), search engines (e.g. Baidu) and online game providers (e.g. Netease and Shanda Interactive) have achieved lofty stock market valuations after growing quickly and profitably. In contrast, online retailers (e.g. Dangdang and Joyo) and name-your price web sites (e.g. Lalasho) emulating Amazon and Priceline, respectively, have commonly struggled. Their modest revenues and poor profits have delayed and perhaps precluded initial public offerings (IPOs). Many online transplants from the United States, including eBay and Yahoo, have also faltered in China. These failures have been attributed primarily to ‘unfamiliar business practices and restrictive government policies’ (Grau, 2007).

However, some e-commerce ventures have succeeded in China. Consistent with the emerg ing nature of the phenomenon and an interest in understanding the ‘hows’ and the ‘whys’, one study used a multiple case design to report on three early success stories (Martinsons, 2002). Those cases support the relationship-based theory of e-commerce.

The key success factor for e-commerce in China has been the development of relationships to overcome the three challenges shown in Figure 1 – opposition from privileged interests (or restrictions imposed by them); weak supporting infrastructure and services; and subsequently, expansion to achieve a profitable scale/scope of operations.

Shanghai-based Mecox Lane took its corporate name from a posh street in Long Island, New York. It started as a mail order business in 1996, selling a wide range of household goods as well as health and beauty products. Mecox Lane modernized its distance-sales channel by launching an online shopping site (http://www.m18.com) in April 2000, after conducting tria runs in 1999.

Both ventures initially had to legitimize business-to-consumer (B2C) e-commerce in China. Each went through a long and difficult process to get government approval, something that Mecox Lane had obtained earlier for its mail order business. Each eventually convinced its respective municipal government that their online venture would benefit both the economy and society. In one case, an evening visit to a top official’s home was critical. He provided critical support after being ‘pleased, but not surprised’ to be visited by the venture’s managers, who brought along several expensive gifts. The value of this relationship building became more apparent after government officials shut down several online rivals. Those rivals unsuccessfully asked to be forgiven after starting up without getting all the necessary permissions beforehand.

Even after benefiting from the clampdown on unauthorized rivals, both 8848.net and Mecox Lane faced two large financial challenges – funding their rapid development and collecting money from customers.

The state-dominated banking industry in China prefers lending to state enterprises rather than private firms, and tends to politicize loan decisions (Tsai, 2004). Both ventures obtained much-needed loans after nurturing government support.

As a mail order firm, Mecox Lane had already solved many payment and distribution problems. The online venture established relationships with local banks, to enable both credit payments and bank remittances, and the national postal service, to enable cash-on-delivery and postal remittances. 8848.net undertook similar relationship building. Both typically use cash-on-delivery for larger items. Customers pay for, and pick up their purchases at the local post office. Cash-on-delivery orders averaged 500 RMB (nearly \$65). Smaller items are bough with debit/credit cards or remittances and delivered by mail. They represent a majority of orders, but a minority of revenues, averaging only 200 RMB (about \$25).

In contrast to the prevalence of specialization and outsourcing in the United States and Europe (Croom, 2005; Kraemer et al., 2006), 8848.net and Mecox Lane undertook a full range of value-chain activities. Each adopted proprietary e-commerce software and applications. Significantly, their online ordering platforms were developed locally after products from US vendors were deemed unsuitable for political as well as functional reasons. One venture contracted a close associate of the Chinese Academy of Sciences to build its platform, largely to enhance its political standing. However, they did not try to significantly automate their back-end operations. The extensive reliance on manual labour reflects not only its low cost in China, but also implicit promises made to government patrons regarding employment.

8848.net and Mecox Lane deliberately chose Beijing and Shanghai, respectively. By 1999, each metropolis had a modern telecommunications and transportation infrastructure, a million middle class consumers with internet access and a credit or debit card, and high costs for labour and land (which disadvantage traditional stores).

Both ventures still had the challenge of getting consumers to augment their traditional marketplace behaviour with marketspace buying. They had to generate business by quickly creating market awareness, gaining consumer trust and overcoming concerns about internet security. Without a better business bureau or similarly ‘independent’ consumer protection agency, relationships with well-known local firms and state agents again proved helpful.

8848.net partnered with Sohu.com, a leading portal. It used a multi-faceted marketing campaign to attract its target segment – highly educated and influential young adults. The campaign included a privileged listing on Sohu’s pioneering Chinese language search engine, targeted advertisements and speeches with live demos at leading universities in and around Beijing. Employees of many state-controlled enterprises were also encouraged (but not coerced) to shop at 8848.net. These employees were offered price discounts and other incentives that were publicized widely by local Communist Party representatives.

Mecox Lane migrated many mail order customers to online retailing with a direct mail campaign and word-of-mouth advertising. It attracted additional customers with a mass media advertising campaign that highlighted its guanxi, most notably its relationships with influentia public agencies. Employees in these agencies and several large Shanghai-area state enterprises were encouraged to shop at Mecox Lane and treated preferentially. Special passwords gave them exclusive access to price discounts and the newest products.

Their initial marketing efforts were successful, partly due to relationships developed with the mass media that ensured positive news coverage. 8848.net registered more than one million customers by January 2001 while Mecox Lane signed up 600 000 users by April 2001.

Both ventures monitored online retailing developments in other geographic markets. They learned from and avoided the strategic mistakes of startups that initiated unsustainable price wars against entrenched rivals. Neither AdMart in Hong Kong nor Webvan in the United States achieved the economies of scale needed to sustain their cost-leadership strategies despite their use of predatory pricing. Mecox Lane and 8848.net maintained healthy profit margins. They trained staff intensively and achieved differentiation with superior order fulfilment and high-touch customer service ‘so that virtual shopping carts were rarely abandoned’.

While Amazon faced keen competition from large US chain stores, China’s online retai pioneers enjoyed fragmented markets. Most domestic rivals were poorly financed, single-store operators, representing the final link in an inefficient, multi-tier distribution chain (Li, 2005). However, the development of China-wide retailing networks by multinationals such as Carrefour and Wal-Mart will undoubtedly intensify competition.

The theory of relationship-based commerce suggests that the lack of a rule-based system to govern commercial activities will constrain online retailing in China. Significantly, Andrew Tsuei, the chief executive of Mecox Lane, insisted that ‘the Amazon.com business model wil not work in China’ due to deficiencies in both the physical and regulatory infrastructure. Meanwhile, Monita Mo, the chief financial officer of 8848.net, asserted that ‘vertical portals may be feasible, and even popular in the US market, but not here (in China)’. Another executive remarked that ‘building guanxiwang was essential, but now it limits our flexibility, we must consider the interest of our guanxihu (specially connected organizations)’. Their statements suggest that online business in China will deviate from mainstream US models, and thus reflect e-commerce with Chinese characteristics.

Chinese consumers made about \$6 billion in online purchases in 2006 (CMRG, 2007; DCCI, 2007). This total represents less than 1% of the retail economy in China versus 3% of consumer sales that were made online in the United States (US Census Bureau, 2007). In both China and the United States, B2B e-commerce greatly exceeds B2C and consumer-toconsumer (C2C) activity. The vast majority of B2B e-commerce in China, which amounted to about \$100 billion in 2006 (CNNIC, 2000–2007; DCCI, 2007), was conducted by foreign invested enterprises. The next section profiles one such online B2B pioneer.

## MARKETSPACE FOR METALS

B2B intermediaries play vital roles in China. They manage commercial complexity and integrate the activities of many small-sized enterprises.

Asia Aluminum Holdings (AAH) operates a large aluminium extrusion and fabrication busi ness in Guangdong province (which neighbours Hong Kong) and also provides industry specific services to other aluminium firms in mainland China. Its headquarters and stock exchange listing are in Hong Kong. AAH expanded its intermediary role and improved its services by relying on e-commerce. The development of i-Metal, an online marketspace, represented a logical extension of its business scope.

AAH initially saw the internet as a new marketing and sales channel. Its first web site offered some industry-specific information and primitive communications capabilities. AAH subsequently invested \$2 million to develop a one-stop virtual meeting place for aluminium traders. At the height of the dot-com hyperbole, the parent firm even temporarily changed its corporate name to Global Applied Technologies Holdings Limited.

i-Metal offered a wide range of bundled services, including a neutral online trading platform for aluminium futures contracts on the Shanghai Futures Exchange (SHFE); the physical metal using a B2B exchange model; and standardized finished products, including extrusions, using a B2B catalogue model. Users can also access real-time market data from the SHFE and the London Metals Exchange. Most of i-Metal’s services are offered in three different scripts – traditional Chinese (used by the overseas Chinese, including Hong Kong and Taiwan), sim plified Chinese (used in mainland China) and English.

Relationships were essential to i-Metal. Its development as a flagship intermediary between traders, brokers and the SHFE would have been impossible without the guanxi of AAH’s founder. Support from the City of Nanhai (now Nanhai District in Foshan City) gave i-Metal visibility and credibility.

Alliances with several banks enabled real-time electronic payment and settlement. Similar to 8848.net and Mecox Lane, i-Metal prodded local banks to upgrade their systems so that traders could transfer funds electronically. It collaborated with Industrial and Commercial Bank of China (ICBC), one of the Big Four state banks in China, to create a brand new product called the Internet Commerce Debit Card. Even then, 3 months of intensive and often frustrating efforts were needed to integrate i-Metal with the legacy systems of banks, brokerages and the SHFE.

i-Metal entered a regulatory grey area of economic activity – trading commodities and futures. It overcame potential resistance by using guakao – collaborating with the Nanha government and a state agency. The venture did not get official licenses for all of its activities, but tacit agreements were secured with relevant regulators. As one manager said, ‘If we had waited for every regulatory approval, we would never have done anything.

Unlike the well-developed rules and policies for trading commodities in market economies, the online process for trading aluminium in China was built from scratch. The integrity of the trading process was ensured by requiring both buyers and sellers to pay a 10% deposit. Here again, several banks were persuaded to accept collateral loans for commodities trading while the primary banking partner was coaxed to develop a new capability – freezing funds in their accounts pending the conclusion of the transaction.

i-Metal subsequently increased trading transparency and reliability by asserting the identity and track record of potential counterparties. Yet another relationship was established, this time with an independent third party, which authorized and verified key information about i-Metal’s customers. The web site temporarily posted a blacklist of problem users. However, it was removed after several well-connected customers threatened to stop using i-Metal if such sensitive information remained in its public domain.

Another critical concern was online security. External security threats were addressed primarily by collaborating with China Telecom (a state-controlled enterprise with relevant expertise) to introduce digital certification. However, Ying-Ki Kwong, the chief technology officer of i-Metal, mentioned that potential customers were still ‘very concerned’ about internal security threats – ‘their own employees may be tempted when trading huge amounts’. In response, i-Metal incorporated multi-level security mechanisms with checks and balances so that combinations of authorized staff were needed to both execute trades and transfer funds.

Relationships with logistics and warehousing providers enabled i-Metal to offer transport services as well as both non-bonded and bonded warehouses. The latter increased marke liquidity by enabling domestic buyers to purchase small metal quantities physically located in Guangdong province. With a bonded warehouse, foreign suppliers do not pay duty until the metal ingots are sold.

AAH envisioned i-Metal as a comprehensive online services provider to various metals industries (as reflected by its brand name). Rather than disintermediating or consolidating the market, it sought to improve efficiency by integrating a wide range of services. Based on this vision, i-Metal facilitated trading from primary materials (metal ingots and futures contracts) to finished extrusions, and provided banking, brokerage, logistics and warehousing services. Consistent with the Chinese business management emphasis on personal relationships and flexibility (Martinsons & Hempel, 1995), i-Metal offered a single contact point for assorted services, but avoided higher-level business process integration.

In the highly fragmented aluminium industry (where the combined market share of the top 10 firms was only 30%), i-Metal established standards for both the technology platform and data exchange protocols. It also spurred banks and business service providers to expand and modernize their operations. This expansion and modernization now benefits much of China’s economy. The B2B marketspace also improved the corporate image of AAH; it made Forbes’ list of small-sized international companies in both 2002 and 2003.

i-Metal educated many potential customers about the value of futures trading for risk management. It also overcame the Chinese preference for personalistic trust to create an online platform handling over \$100 million in futures contracts per month. Due to the lack of well-established business services in China, i-Metal pioneered a total business ‘system’ fo trading aluminium. It also established a dynamic network that includes both local government interests and providers of complementary business providers. Although the system can be leveraged to trade other metals and commodities, a senior executive of i-Metal suggested that ‘the quality of the system was less important to long-term performance than the quality of the network’, since ‘relationships will ultimately determine our success with aluminium and othe metals. That’s how business gets done in China’.

## DISCUSSION

The e-commerce literature in market economies identifies business models (cf. Weill & Vitale, 2001) and supply chain management (cf. Johnson & Whang, 2002) as key factors to profitably attract and satisfy buyers. Meanwhile, e-commerce studies in China highlight the challenges arising from weakness in the physical infrastructure, financial services and socio-economic institutions (Hempel & Kwong, 2001; Martinsons, 2002; Li, 2005; Chiu, 2006).

## Key success factors for e-commerce in China

The successful online pioneers in China provide many instructive insights. They relied on e-business models and practices that are distinctive from an American/Western perspective. They developed much of their own infrastructure and services, often partnering with established providers of financial, telecommunication and transport services. Consequently, their business models resemble hierarchies, with dynamic networks including complementary service providers, rather than markets with independent actors governed primarily by impersonal price mechanisms (Malone et al., 1987).

The successful online ventures in China also developed and sustained relationships with privileged interests, primarily state agencies and government officials, to ensure that they were operating legally and to access critical resources. An executive in one case stated that, ‘Good relations with the government are critical. For almost everything, we need official permission. We can get it quickly, and also lose it quickly.’ Many e-business failures in China have been attributed, at least partly, to poor relationships with the authorities.

eBay’s recent closure of its online site in China highlights the ongoing importance of connections (Bradsher, 2006). eBay lacked the political influence to fully implement its payment service. It also could not overcome the prevailing distrust of outsiders (unconnected people) in Chinese society. Taobao rapidly gained C2C market share from eBay by enabling potential buyers and sellers to communicate directly and establish personal trust with an embedded instant-messaging service (Ou & Davison, 2008).

In late 2006, eBay decided to rely on Tom Online, a company with good guanxi. The chief executive of Tom Online, Wang Lei Lei, is the well-connected grandson of a People’s Liberation Army general while its controlling shareholder, Li Ka Shing, ‘has cultivated a close relationship with top Communist leaders on the mainland for decades’ (Bradsher, 2006). Remarkably, Tom Online is struggling with value-added cellphone services. This suggests that guanxi is necessary but not sufficient for business success in China.

eBay was not the first multinational e-business to belatedly realize the importance of guanxi in mainland China. In August 2005, Yahoo handed over its China operations to Alibaba and paid an additional \$1 billion in cash for a 40% stake in the well-connected Chinese e-commerce company (Techweb Technology News, 2005). Many other ecommerce ventures operating in emerging markets have developed legitimacy and gained consumer trust by touting their relationships with influential state-controlled enterprises and public agencies.

The e-business strategies adopted by 8848.net, Mecox Lane and i-Metal leveraged their capabilities and suited their environment. Their geographic focus was deliberately limited to areas with well-developed infrastructure. The online retailers chose to do business in and around a modernized metropolis, where traditional stores had high labour and land costs. Meanwhile, i-Metal used servers in Hong Kong to link aluminium traders in neighbouring Guangdong to commodity markets in Shanghai and London. All three ventures developed, improved and/or integrated value-added services rather than displacing existing intermediaries. Again, relationships with well-established enterprises and agencies were vital.

The e-commerce pioneers deliberately followed international standards in order to benefit further as China fulfils its World Trade Organization commitments. Beyond those fundamenta standards, their systems were customized to fit China’s distinctive business conditions. The online retailers adapted their ordering systems to accommodate credit-scarce consumers while i-Metal’s trading system was developed together with banks and regulators. Significantly, their customers and business partners were not required to formalize or redesign their business processes or to share sensitive information.

Traditional commerce in relationship-based economies is not very efficient. Thus, the comparative advantage of e-commerce is greater in emerging markets like China than in the United States and other rule-based economies. Online retailers such as Mecox Lane and 8848.net have helped to rationalize the supply chains for consumer goods while B2B ventures such as i-Metal and Alibaba have enabled Chinese business networks to expand beyond their traditional limitations. As a result, the internet is increasingly used instead of trade shows and industry magazines to source products and find buyers.

E-commerce will undoubtedly continue to develop, and thus increase the scale and efficiency of China’s economy. The sub-sections that follow consider the infrastructure and institutions needed to support this development

## Improving infrastructure

The infrastructure and services needed for e-commerce are improving constantly, admittedly at different rates in different economies. Declining prices for PCs and internet access, together with more comprehensive and affordable telecom and transport services, are also steadily making e-commerce more attractive.

In a huge country like China, enormous investments are required to modernize the physical infrastructure. Mobile phone services as well as both road and air transport have improved greatly because of very large investments. Relaxed restrictions on foreign participation in logistics, transport and warehousing services are also having a positive effect. However, online payment issues remain a big bottleneck for e-commerce in China.

Nearly 50 million Chinese possess at least one credit card, but trust, security and liability issues limit their use for e-commerce (CMRG, 2007; DCCI, 2007). For example, Dell receives only 20% of its China orders online, mostly from credit card buyers in Beijing, Shanghai or Guangzhou, compared to over 60% globally (Dell, 2005, corporate information provided by Michael Dell [Chairman] and William Amelio [Managing Director Asia Pacific]). The emergence of online payment systems like Alibaba’s Alipay and its cooperation with China Post to provide e-remittance services at 30 000 points across China are promising developments.

A non-cash payment system spanning China will encourage e-commerce to spread. By stimulating rural spending and helping firms to overcome geographic obstacles, it should significantly increase inter-provincial trade and cross-regional competition while giving Chinese consumers access to assorted goods that are unavailable in local stores. Nationwide e-commerce would also promote transparency, and thus fight graft. Dramatically increasing point-of-sale and automatic teller machines from one per 1000 people in China (vs. 10.5 per 1000 people in the United States) is an option, but this would be very expensive (Ip & Yip, 2007).

The high penetration rate of mobile telephones in China and other relationship-based economies suggests other alternatives. The 3:1 ratio of mobile phone users to internet users in China (vs. a 1:1 ratio in the United States) reflects the importance of personal communica tions in a relationship-based economy. With nearly 500 million cellphone users (CMRG, 2007), China is an attractive market for developing mobile e-commerce (m-commerce) and other personalized forms of IT-enabled commerce.

Parts of East Asia are ahead of the United States in adopting personal communication tools and interactive media technologies (WEF, 2007). For example, telephone handsets are emerging as a substitute for credit cards in Japan and Korea. Contactless mobile payments (using Near Field Communications) are used widely to buy beverages and pay for public transport services. However, this requires enhanced handsets and specialized in-store readers (Ip & Yip, 2007).

A mobile wallet system, like that pioneered by NTT-DoCoMo in Japan and already popula in the Philippines (Grau, 2007), would be more feasible across China. China’s mobile phone networks already handle billions of short messaging service (SMS) communications each month. McKinsey consultants estimate that network upgrades to provide SMS-based mobile payment services nationwide would cost less than \$100 million (Ip & Yip, 2007). As 15% of the rural population in China are already mobile phone subscribers and 75% of them use SMS (CMRG, 2007), the physical infrastructure for this type of m-commerce could be implemented fairly easily.

SMS-based m-commerce could change Chinese consumer behaviour much as mobile phones have changed Chinese communications behaviour. The widespread adoption of mobile wallets could genuinely transform China (Martinsons, 2005). However, this form of m-commerce faces some large challenges. Banks, merchants and telecom service providers must collaborate to handle payments. Various regulators across China must approve and support this new form of commerce while both consumers and merchants must develop trust in the system. These institutional obstacles may delay or even derail SMS-based m-commerce.

## Institutional issues in China

Improvements in the physical and financial infrastructure will encourage online business growth, but privileged interests may impede the transition to rule-based e-commerce.

E-commerce (and commerce in general) benefits from predictable and transparent governance with honest. efficient and accountable bureaucrats. Ambiguous laws make it difficult to authenticate users, eradicate the sales of counterfeit goods and avoid repudiated transactions. In relationship-based economies, discretionary and opaque enforcement as well as other forms of interference by the state and its officials further hinder e-commerce.

In China, many online activities, including securities, commodities and futures trading, remain in a regulatory grey area. Would-be counterparts to Boom.com and eTrade await clarified rules and ‘official’ approval. Remarkably, the ‘rules of the game’ for many online service providers in China changed dramatically in 2006 when the state unexpectedly imposed substantial new online fees/taxes and a moratorium on new internet cafes (Grau, 2007).

The overlapping or ambiguous authority of state agents and inconsistent regulatory enforcement are a source of great uncertainty for private-sector e-commerce. State-controlled enterprises can also become frustrated. This has been evident as telecom service providers in China await approvals for third-generation (3G) telephony (Kwong, 2007) so that they can capitalize on the advantage over foreign rivals that they gained when the domestic Time Division-Synchronous Code Division Multiple Access (TD-SCDMA) standard was authorized. This uncertainty and frustration may encourage business models that involve the state directly (e.g. B2G2B) instead of streamlined forms of B2B and B2C e-commerce.

In China, the internet has already undermined the Communist Party’s monopoly on ‘public information (Holliday & Yep, 2005). Nevertheless, public information remains scarce, unreliable and subject to state controls. The state security apparatus expends vast resources to monitor and control the internet. The authorities apparently fear that Chinese citizens will find foreign ideas more attractive than those generated domestically.

However, governments typically benefit when their citizens prosper. The Chinese Communist Party needs sustained economic growth to maintain social stability, its own legitimacy, and thus its political monopoly. Consistent with this logic, numerous national leaders have acknowledged that IT is essential for economic progress.

The faceless world of e-commerce should stimulate market-based competition and private enterprise development in China. However, the transition to rule-based commerce (and reduced corruption) hinges on the formalization, standardization, and diffusion of reliable business information and the acquiescence of powerful and privileged interests. Thus, the future of e-commerce in economies like China depends critically on government officials and state agents, including authorities at sub-national levels.

Corruption is a universal concern. The United States is not immune from corruption scandals while the European Union has yet to eradicate corruption within its borders, mostly notably among its newest members. Nevertheless, corruption is typically a bigger problem in emerging markets. Corruption in China is a ‘lethal threat to economic development’ according to the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace (Pei, 2007). It was classified as a ‘serious concern’ by Transparency International (2006) after China scored only 3.3 out of 10 on its Corruption Perceptions Index in 2006, a decline from 3.5 in 2001. On 19 January 2007, the Chinese Communist Party’s newspaper People’s Daily stated that many officials ‘had become morally degenerate’ and were ‘sinking into the morass of crime and disorder’. Premier Wen Jiabao subsequently admitted that corruption affects every echelon of the Party. At the National People’s Congress in March 2007, Wen said that ‘overconcentration of power without effective and proper restraint and oversight’ is the root cause of corruption and results in ‘collusion between unscrupulous officials and business people’.

The situation in China differs starkly from the separation of government from business and checks and balances within government that are espoused and commonly enforced in rulebased economies. For example, the United States clearly divides political power between the executive, legislative and judicial branches, and between federal, state and local jurisdictions. Abuses of authority, even by the US President, are subject to investigations and sanctions as prescribed by the law. China currently lacks both the rule of law and ‘the checks and balances necessary to supervise and regulate a capitalist economy in an efficient manner’ (Kynge, 2006, p. 186).

If acknowledgements of the governance problems in China are followed by concrete actions, the Communist Party could move further from its founding principles and reinvent itself as the custodian of a rule-based market-driven economy. Harnessing the internet to increase the transparency and efficiency of the public sector and fight corruption would help the Party retain political power (even as it gives up economic control). Reliable rules and enforceable contracts would also enhance economic efficiency by enabling the development of larger-scale markets and enterprises. This, together with reduced environmental uncer tainty, will likely encourage both grass-roots entrepreneurship and strategic management practices.

The transition to rule-based commerce will be a difficult and disruptive process for China and its people. Similar transitions took about 8 decades in both Europe and the United States (cf Zucker, 1996). It will not be easy to establish and enforce a comprehensive rule-based system to govern commercial activities in China. However, it will be even harder to get 1.3 billion people to trust the system and abide by the rule of law when doing business; ‘the entrenched nature of the guanxi system (is) a powerful obstacle’ (Seligman, 1999, p. 35).

Alternatively, the internet may be used to reinforce the existing relationship-based economy by improving communications between personally trusted parties. The popularity o online role-playing games (offered by Netease and Shanda Interactive), online discussions (hosted by SINA, Sohu and Tencent’s qq.com) and online communities (such as 51.com and YouKu) clearly demonstrates the internet’s social role in China. Online communications between businesses and their customers and among user communities are already common. Business-oriented Alibaba and consumer-oriented TaoBao use bipolar coloured icons to inform potential buyers whether a seller is online (blue) or not (grey). Both have embedded an instant messaging tool called WangWang in their trading platforms. Traders can use this facility to communicate fairly tacit knowledge, and thus develop personal relationships.

Sharing such information online is risky since internet activities in China, especially those with foreign involvement, are monitored closely by the state. This oversight and a need to provide national identification card numbers for many online transactions raise acute privacy concerns. Even sensitive and proprietary information collected and warehoused by state agencies seems to be accessible to those with good guanxi. Consequently, private information is actively traded and frequently misused in China.

Authorities across China may manipulate e-commerce development so as to maintain their privileged positions. Meanwhile, the outright refusal of district courts to consider several cases brought by online ventures against state agencies/enterprises were ‘troubling precedents’ (Li, 2006). China moved towards a rule-based system with passage of a long-awaited property law in 2007. Remarkably, it focuses more on protecting state property (from below-value appropriation by private interests) than private or intellectual property.

## Implications beyond China

The theory developed here suggests that e-commerce depends critically on supporting infrastructure and institutions. Thus, online business faces greater challenges in markets where the following prevail: personal (rather than systemic) relationships and trust, informal and privately shared (rather than formal and public) information, and blurred boundaries between politica and economic actors. Without a comprehensive and trustworthy rule-based system, it will be difficult to displace traditional marketplaces that offer enjoyable social experiences with rituals such as haggling and customized deals.

Economic rationality suggests that business models and practices throughout the world wil converge. However, e-commerce in the United States and similar economies depends on reliable financial, telecommunication and transportation systems, affluent and educated people and, most importantly, a universally applicable set of rules. The theory developed here suggests that e-commerce models and prescriptions from the United States cannot be transferred readily or universally to emerging markets like China.

This paper also advances our understanding of how environmental factors influence different types of IT applications. Cultural factors (based on shared values, norms and belief systems) greatly influence IS at the individual, group and organizational levels (Martinsons &

Westwood, 1997; Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). Individuals can exercise significant discretion when they personally collect, organize, process, communicate, retrieve and use data/ information. Meanwhile, business leaders typically set policies or serve as role models fo IT application in their organizations. Their discretionary choices are based largely on the attitudes, values and beliefs that they share with other members of their culture. For example, the high power distance prevailing in the Chinese business culture encourages centralized decision-making, and thus limits the need for systems to formalize information (Martinsons & Westwood, 1997). Efforts to impose IT-based solutions that do not fit the culture can produce negative outcomes such as enslavement rather than empowerment (cf. Davison & Martinsons, 2002). Socio-economic institutions have less effect on IS within organizations because they primarily govern interactions between economic actors.

Our theory and evidence from China suggest that institutional factors have a greater influence on the larger scale, inter-organizational IS that are at the heart of e-commerce (see Figure 2). These institutional factors include the physical infrastructure for transferring infor mation (communicating), funds (financial services) and physical goods (transport and logistics) as well as the formal rules and informal conventions that reduce uncertainty by providing a structure to political, social and economic interactions (North, 1990, p. 3).

The Chinese rely on relationships to do business primarily because they can not rely on a rule-based system. This is a forced behaviour due to deficiencies in institutions or social structures rather than a preferred behaviour based on cultural values. Deficiencies in the physical infrastructure have also hindered the development of e-commerce in mainland China.

However, the infrastructural obstacles (represented by the left prong in Figure 1) are being alleviated steadily, often by mutually beneficial relationships between complementary service providers. This has enabled e-commerce in China to grow recently at annualized rates of

## CONCLUSIONS

E-commerce ultimately makes business less personal. It faces large and more complex challenges in emerging markets like China, where commerce is based on personal relationships, than in well-established markets like the United States, where rule-based commerce prevails. Online ventures in countries like China must overcome opposition and/or resistance from privileged interests, a comparative lack of supporting infrastructure and services, and greater difficulties in scaling up their operations profitably.

Deficiencies in the physical infrastructure and socio-economic institutions across mainland China have discouraged online venturing, especially beyond its first-tier cities. Better educational and financial services, together with more extensive telecommunication and transportation networks, should encourage e-commerce to expand geographically as China continues to gradually urbanize. More generally, improvements in the physical and financial infrastructure will help online ventures to achieve operational scales that are profitable.

Deficiencies in socio-economic institutions represent a more durable obstacle. The deeply embedded reliance on guanxi in China and, more generally, relationship-based commerce in emerging markets will likely influence e-commerce for many decades. The key role of government relations in both traditional and online commerce deviates from technical–economic rationality and suggests two other rationalities based on socio-political interaction and trustbased collaboration (Kumar et al., 1998).

The successful e-commerce pioneers in China provide lessons applicable across emerging markets. They created or extended a competitive advantage by using the internet to leverage their existing capabilities and connections. For example, Mecox Lane leveraged the distance selling capabilities (marketing, order taking, payment, distribution) from its successful mai order business while AAH leveraged its market position and founder’s guanxi within the aluminium industry to develop the i-Metal marketspace.

The fragmented supply chains and prevalence of relationship-based commerce in emerging markets represent both challenges and opportunities. E-commerce can significantly enhance the efficiency of individual enterprises, business networks, industry supply chains and even entire economies. Its comparative advantage is enhanced when the invisible hand of the market has yet to raise the efficiency of traditional (offline) businesses. For example, the mutually beneficial relationships developed by the online pioneers in China overcame the scarcity of business services, and thus modernized the supply chains of thei industries.

E-commerce in China has already stimulated the development of some infrastructure that is critical for larger scale commerce. It could potentially spur the enactment and consistent enforcement of comprehensive commercial rules. This would help to separate political actors (government agencies and officials) from economic actors (business people), and thus reduce market distortions, including corruption. The internet can also help to educate people about a rule-based system, support checks and balances on power, detect violations of the rules and even publicize the punishments given to violators.

Further study is needed to better understand how IT-enabled business development, includ ing e-commerce, is related to the tension between personal relationships and systemic rules. The evidence from China suggests that a state can simultaneously foster IT-enabled economic development while restricting or controlling sensitive IT application, such as online political dialogue. The outcomes of this selective use of the internet merit further research. Another important issue is whether e-commerce in developing countries like China will serve as a catalyst for a transformation to a rule-based market economy or used primarily to improve the existing relationship-based economy. Remarkably, relationships between banks, merchants, mobile phone operators and regulators may enable the development of SMS-based m-commerce, and thus transform Chinese consumer behaviour.

The theory and evidence presented here suggest that institutional factors (including both the physical infrastructure and socio-economic institutions) will influence e-commerce more than cultural factors. This supplements previous findings that cultural factors shape IT application at individual, group and organizational levels. Further study in various contexts, including emerging markets, is needed to confirm and elaborate a proposition that the primary influence shifts from cultural to institutional factors as the scope of IT application increases.

Our understanding of how cultural factors influence IS has been advanced by special journal issues (cf. February 2003 issue of IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management), literature reviews (cf. Leidner & Kayworth, 2006) and explicit research agendas (cf. Martinsons & Davison, 2007). It may now be timely to focus on how institutional factors, particularly relationships between political and economic actors, influence IS.

Ambitious studies are also needed to examine how different types of IS are affected by the complex mix of cultural and institutional factors that constitute real-world contexts. Given its distinctive business culture and institutional environment, China is an attractive place to pursue this holistic and globally significant research stream. Intensive, theory-driven studies in China and other ‘emerging’ contexts are also advocated to advance our understanding of how e-commerce is affected by environmental factors and how it may affect them.
