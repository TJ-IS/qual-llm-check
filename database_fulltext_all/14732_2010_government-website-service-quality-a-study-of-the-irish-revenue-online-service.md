# Government website service quality: a study of the Irish revenue online service

Regina Connolly<sup>1</sup>, Frank Bannister<sup>2</sup> and Aideen Kearney<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Dublin City University Business School, Ireland; <sup>2</sup>Department of Statistics, Trinity College, Ireland

Correspondence: Regina Connolly, Management & Information Systems, Dublin City University Business School, Glasnevin, Dublin 9, Ireland. Tel.: þ 01 700 5893; Fax: þ 01 700 5446; E-mail: regina.connolly@dcu.ie

## Abstract

Online taxation systems have been among the most successful of e-government applications both in terms of citizen take-up and savings to the taxpayer. Understanding the factors that lead to high take-up is of potential interest to other public sector online service providers. This paper examines the quality of the online service provided by the Irish Revenue Commissioners’ tax filing and collection system, Revenue Online Service (ROS). A modified version of the recently operationalized E-S-QUAL instrument is used to examine online service quality from the point of view of the citizens and tax practitioners who use this eGovernment system. The findings show that efficiency and ease of completion are the dimensions of website service quality that most influence ROS users’ perceptions of value and convenience as well as their intentions to use and recommend the website to their peers. The practical implications of these findings include the fact that providers of public sector e-services should concentrate on communicating the functionality of their e-services. In addition, they should focus on reducing citizen concerns regarding misuse or mismanagement of personal data.

Keywords: e-government; service quality; online taxation; E-S-QUAL; E-PUBLIC-SECTOR-QUAL

## Introduction

Online service quality has attracted considerable attention from the academic research community in recent years (Loiacono et al., 2000; Szymanski & Hise, 2000; Yoo & Donthu, 2001; Aladwani, 2002; Cai & Jun, 2003; Wolfinbarger & Gilly, 2003; Yang, 2004; Parasuraman et al., 2005; Park & Baek, 2007). This research has led to a growing understanding of the factors that affect user perception of online service quality. To date, most of this research has focused on business services provided by the private sector such as online banking and shopping. Relatively little attention has been paid to the question of online service quality in the public sector although some scholars, for example Grimsley & Meehan (2007), have drawn attention to the particular characteristics of public value delivered by government e-services such as trust and social inclusion. This paper examines the perception of service quality in the Irish government’s online tax filing system, Revenue Online Services (ROS). The Irish Revenue Commissioners (hereafter simply referred to as (the) Revenue) launched the ROS project in 2000. The objectives of ROS were to enable all taxpayers to file their taxes online. The initial target market was the business sector, the self-employed and certain taxes such as value-added tax returns. This system has been highly successful, with 75% of self-employed taxpayers using the service in 2008 (Revenue Commissioners, 2009). The system was extended to cover the much larger pay-as-you-earn sector in 2006. To date, the ROS system has been remarkably trouble-free. Unlike the U.K. system, which has suffered a number of problems ranging from software errors to unavailability (Nugent, 2003; Coleman, 2007), the only major incident to data was when the system shut down due to overload just before the tax deadline in its first year of operations.

A number of authors have proposed stage models of e-government maturity (Baum & Di Maio, 2000; Hiller & Belanger, 2001; Layne & Lee, 2001; Ronaghan, 2001; Wescott, 2001). In many of these models, the highest level of maturity is some form of two-way online interaction. One form of two-way interaction has been online taxation systems, which have been among the most successful of e-government applications. For example, in the U.K., in 2008, 5.8 million taxpayers filed their returns online, leading to an estimated annual saving of d20 million (Kable, 2009). Similarly, in the U.S., the Internal Revenue Service projected that approximately 86 million (63%) of the anticipated 138 million returns for the year 2009 would be filed electronically (IRS, 2008) and the U.S. Department of the Treasury reports annual savings of \$78 million from the move to e-filing (United States Treasury, 2008). Her Majesty’s Revenue and Customs estimate that each additional taxpayer who switches to online filing, results in a saving of d10. A 10% increase in the number of U.K. taxpayers filing online would save a further d6 million each year. In the U.S., the comparable figure is approximately \$17 million per annum.

For the state, e-taxation has numerous benefits apart from cost and manpower savings. Other benefits include reduced error rates, greater compliance and the release of resources with which to tackle more complex tax cases and serious fraud. However, for both legal and logistical reasons, governments cannot force individual citizens to pay tax online. If such a system is to be successful it has to be both attractive and trustworthy. In line with stakeholder theory, due regard must be given to the interests of those user groups who are critical to its success (Irani et al., 2007). Other factors which affect user behaviour include integrative communications and good two-way communications (Tan & Pan, 2003), information richness (Jahng et al., 2007) and factors such as convenience and cost savings (Khalifa & Liu, 2007). Understanding those aspects of website design and operation which create a perception of service quality is therefore important as these are critical determinants of website success and service take-up (see below). This research critically examines the response of users to the ROS website. The objective of this paper is to determine the dimensions of website service quality that users of the ROS system value. It asks what new insights can be obtained from studying the success of this service and what might be the lessons for the design of other public online services. The paper is structured as follows: The next section discusses the literature on website service quality. Then the research methodology is presented and the measurement instrument is discussed in detail. The research findings are outlined and the implications of these findings are then explored.

## Website service quality

Service quality has been defined as the difference between customers’ expectations for service performance prior to the service encounter and their perceptions of the service received (Asubonteng et al., 1996). When performance does not meet expectations, quality is judged as low and when performance exceeds expectations, the quality is judged to be high. Thus, in any evaluation of service quality, customer expectation is key. Moreover, Asubonteng et al. (1996) suggest that as service quality increases, satisfaction with the service and intentions to reuse the service increase. Meeting customer service requirements is therefore both a performance issue (whether the service satisfies customer requirement) and an issue of conformity to measurable standards. Swartz & Brown (1989) distinguish between the consumer’s post-performance evaluation of what the service delivers and the consumer’s evaluation of the service during delivery. The former evaluation has been termed ‘outcome quality’ (Parasuraman et al., 1985), ‘technical quality’ (Gronro¨os, 1983) and ‘physical quality’ (Lehtinen & Lehtinen, 1982). The latter has been termed ‘process quality’ by Parasuraman et al. (1985), ‘functional quality’ by Gronro¨os (1983) and ‘interaction quality’ by Lehtinen & Lehtinen (1982).

Given the importance of service quality, it is not surprising that a number of tools have been developed with which to measure it. Probably the best known of these is SERVQUAL, an empirically derived instrument developed by Parasuraman et al. (1985, 1988) that can be used for performing a gap analysis of an organization’s service quality performance measured against customers’ perception of the relative importance of service attributes, information which is then used to improve the organisation’s service quality. While SERVQUAL has been used to measure online service quality (Devaraj et al., 2002; Kim & Lee, 2002; Li et al., 2002; Kuo, 2003; Negash et al., 2003), some researchers (e.g., Riel et al., 2001) have cautioned against the use of this instrument for the purpose of assessing the quality of websites. This caution derives mainly from the fact that it has been shown (Gefen, 2002) that SERVQUAL scales in an unmodified form do not serve to capture fully all the dimensions of service quality as they relate to e-commerce. Nonetheless, there have been problems in developing a more suitable instrument. For example, the focus of the 12 dimension WebQual scale (Loiacono et al., 2000) is on providing website designers with information regarding the website (e.g., informational fit to task) rather than on providing specific service quality measures from a customer perspective. Thus it is not suitable for measuring specific service quality measures in a public sector website. Other scales such as Barnes & Vidgen’s (2002) WebQual (confusingly originally given the same name as that of Loiacono et al.) provide a transaction-specific assessment rather than a detailed service quality assessment of a website and would therefore be equally unsuited to measure website service quality in the public sector. The SITEQUAL scale proposed by Yoo & Donthu (2001) excludes dimensions considered central to the evaluation of website service quality as does Szymanski & Hise’s (2000) study, while other scholars (Parasuraman et al., 2005) have expressed caution regarding the consistency and appropriateness of dimensions used in the eTailQ scale proposed by Wolfinbarger & Gilly (2003). In summary, the position with regard to instruments for measuring website service quality has, until recently, been less than satisfactory. It is not surprising therefore that criticism has been levelled (Zeithaml et al., 2002) at a number of researchers’ attempts (Rice, 1997; Liu & Arnett, 2000; and Yang et al., 2001) to measure e-service quality. Among these criticisms are that the scales and dimensions employed by these researchers have not been empirically validated and some of the measures have either been taken from studies on service quality in the physical offline domain or were derived from human– computer interface literature. The limitations of the scales that have been used to measure e-service quality are summarised in Table 1.

## Research methodology

In 2005, the original authors of SERVQUAL (Parasuraman et al., 1988, 1985) developed and operationalized a measuring instrument designed to address the problems discussed in the preceding section. This measuring instrument, called E-S-QUAL, consists of a four-dimensional 22-item scale that endeavours to capture the critical dimensions of service quality outlined in the extant literature. The four dimensions are derived from the work of Zeithaml et al. (2002) who identified a number of website features at the perceptual-attribute level and categorized them into 11 e-service quality dimensions (reliability, responsiveness, access, flexibility, ease of navigation, efficiency, assurance/trust, security/ privacy, price knowledge, site aesthetics and customisation/personalization). These dimensions were subsequently empirically tested and validated and based on this testing four main dimensions emerged. The four dimensions were:

1. Efficiency: the ease and speed of accessing and using the site.

2. Fulfilment: the extent to which the site’s promises about order delivery and item availability are fulfilled.

3. System availability: the correct technical functioning of the site.

4. Privacy: the degree to which the site is safe and protects customer information.

E-S-QUAL has an accompanying subscale called e-RecS-Qual which contains items focused on handling service problems. This is relevant to customers who have had non-routine (i.e., abnormal) recovery service encounters with the website. e-RecS-Qual consists of a threedimensional, 11-item scale. The three dimensions are:

1. Responsiveness: effective handling of problems and returns through the site.

2. Compensation: the degree to which the site compensates customers for problems.

3. Contact: the availability of assistance through telephone or online representatives.

The authors of the E-S-QUAL instrument have modelled e-service quality as an exogenous construct that influences the higher order constructs of perceived value and loyalty intentions. They justify this on the grounds that the items that represent perceived value are consistent with the conceptualisation of perceived value as customer trade-off between benefits and costs (Zeithaml, 1988). The loyalty intentions construct was measured through a 5-item behavioural loyalty scale developed by Zeithaml et al. (1996).

Both the E-S-QUAL and e-RecS-Qual scales, whose specific purpose is the measurement of website service quality, have been subjected to reliability and validity tests and demonstrate good psychometric properties. For example, during their development the scales were subject to both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyzes and the results obtained demonstrated high internal consistency and hence reliability of each dimension in the scale. The values obtained in these analyses together with the strong loadings of the scale items on their corresponding factors (in both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses) support the convergent validity of each scales’ component dimensions. Collectively, these findings provide good support for the soundness of both scales’ factor structure. The authors tested the nomological validity of E-S-QUAL using structural equation modelling and the overall goodnessof-fit statistics imply that the data from the samples used fit the proposed model well. These results offer further confirmation of the psychometric soundness of E-S-QUAL.

Moreover, in addition to their psychometric soundness, these scales provide an important step forward in the conceptualisation of e-service quality as they address and resolve many of the concerns about previous scales. For example, the E-S-QUAL and e-RecS-QUAL scales provide a comprehensive and empirically validated means of measuring website service quality from a customer perspective, thus overcoming the shortcomings of the scales proposed by Loiacono et al. (2000) and Barnes & Vidgen (2002). They also include all of the service quality dimensions which were found to be central to the evaluation of website service quality, thus overcoming the deficiencies in the scales of Szymanski & Hise (2000) and Yoo & Donthu (2001). Moreover, as previously noted, the internal consistency of the measures used in the E-S-QUAL scale has been empirically validated and published.

For the above reasons, it was decided that E-S-QUAL was the most suitable instrument for this study. (Both the main E-S-QUAL scale and its accompanying e-RecS-QUAL subscale are employed in this study. All further reference to E-S-QUAL should therefore be read to include the subscale.) However, as has happened in comparable studies, modifications had to be made to this instrument, in this case for two reasons. First, although this was an independent study and not conducted at the behest of ROS, the Revenue, who would be sending out the survey, requested a number of changes that would reflect practical aspects of online tax filing. Second, some modifications were required because of the nature of the public sector. The changes requested by the Revenue included the following:

1. The Fulfilment dimension of the scale was removed, as it was not relevant for a tax filing website.

2. Revenue requested that all items relating to Compensation be removed.

3. As this survey was to be sent to their entire client base, Revenue wanted to use it to gather some additional information. They therefore specified a number of additional items that they wished to be included in the Efficiency section of the survey. The latter related to ease of completion and ROS online demonstrations.

4. Revenue also requested that three of the 5 items comprising the Responsiveness construct and one of the 4 items comprising Perceived Value be removed from the survey and that 1 item be added to the statements measuring Loyalty Intentions. This latter item related to the likelihood of using ROS for future payments and was considered to be consistent with the other items that comprised this construct.

5. As the E-S-QUAL scale models website service quality in terms of two higher order constructs (Perceived Value and Loyalty intentions), rather than directly, an additional 4 items which directly articulated the website service quality construct were included. The purpose of this addition was to determine whether any of the dimensions of e-service quality mapped more strongly to this directly articulated construct rather than to the higher order constructs.

The above changes improve the validity and relevance of the instrument as a measure of service quality for a public sector website by removing a number of unnecessary questions and by enhancing the survey to encompass public sector attributes. This modified instrument is hereafter referred to as E-PS-QUAL (E-Public Sector QUAL). Table 2 shows the number of items measured under each dimension. In total, the E-PS-QUAL survey consisted of 35 questions each measured using a Likert scale.

The survey instrument was divided into two sections. Section 1 related to the specific dimensions of online service quality as outlined in Table 2 whereas section 2 contained a number of demographic questions.

As the modifications made to the measurement instrument did not change it in any radical way, it was not considered necessary to conduct pilot testing. For example, no modifications other than trivially necessary ones, were made to the original instrument such as the insertion of the word ‘ROS’ instead of the more generic term ‘this website’. As previously outlined, some questions were deleted from the instrument because they were not relevant to online tax filing, for example questions on fulfilment. While a number of questions were added to the instrument, it can be seen that these questions were either of a factual nature or were similar to existing questions and consequently could not be considered to affect the validity of the instrument. In summary, no questions were changed in a way that could be considered to change the meaning of the question or the overall survey even slightly.

To execute the survey, in November 2007, ROSs emailed 22,000 citizens who file their tax returns online informing them of the study and inviting their participation. The email contained an HTML link that directed citizens to an online questionnaire. A total of 6,661 returns were received giving a high overall response rate of 30.3%.

## Findings

Of the respondents, 50.1% were male and 49.9% were female. The majority were in the 31–50 years age group and the most frequent use of ROS was in a business rather than personal capacity. More than 73% of the respondents had a third-level qualification, the majority of which was in a business discipline. As there is only limited information available on the underlying population, it is not possible to assess how representative the proportions in the respondent group are, although available data suggests that the breakdown between business and personal users is approximately consistent with returns filed (Revenue Commissioners, 2008). (There are no figures available on the breakdown of male and female registered tax advisors in Ireland. In a discussion with the Institute of Taxation in Ireland, they indicated that they believed that the male/female breakdown of registered tax practitioners in Ireland was not far from 50%. A large number of new members in recent years have been female.) Owing to the size of the response, there were no categories for which response numbers were small, so non-response error is not a concern when comparing groups. However, as is frequently the case with surveys of this type, certain statements about the characteristics of the population (such as gender or qualification) cannot be made. This study seeks to examine the service quality dimensions that influence users of a public sector website and to determine the predictive value of each dimension. In order to ensure that this can be achieved, the scale must be examined more closely to determine its reliability as a measure of website service quality. That examination includes the four stages of (1) reliability testing of the scale, (2) factor analysis to examine the degree of association between the questions that comprise the scale, (3) correlation testing to examine the relationships between the constructs, and (4) regression analysis to determine the predictive value of each of the service quality dimensions of the ROS website.

The first step in the data analysis, the reliability test, was conducted in order to establish the consistency of the scale being used to measure the service quality dimensions that are being examined in this study. One of the most popular reliability statistics in use today is Cronbach’s alpha (Cronbach, 1951). It is used in this study to determine the internal consistency or average correlation of items in the survey instrument to gauge its reliability. Values of 0.7 or higher are regarded as a satisfactory indicator of the reliability of a construct. When reliability measures for the constructs in this study were calculated it was clear that the four constructs of Efficiency, Perceived Value, Loyalty Intentions and Website Service Quality worked well with this sample, each showing a Cronbach’s alpha score of 0.90 or higher. The measures for System Availability, Privacy and Contact were also strong at 0.85. The Responsiveness construct provided the weakest (although still strong) measure at 0.79. Despite the fact that all of these measures appear satisfactory, as this is a new measurement instrument and as a number of new items had been included while others were adapted, added or omitted at the request of the Revenue, it was decided that the nature of the variables, that is their relationships with each other and their relationship with the response variables, should be examined more closely. In order to test construct validity, it was therefore decided to conduct a factor analysis. For this data, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure is 0.961 pointing to sample adequacy for a factor analysis and, as a value close to 1 indicates that patterns of correlations between constructs are relatively compact, the factor analysis should therefore reveal distinct and reliable factors. The Bartlett’s test is significant (Po0.001).

The factor analysis showed that the 9 items that comprise the dependent variables Perceived Value and Loyalty Intentions had a strong association with each other and with the 4 items that had been introduced into the study to measure Website Service Quality. This is the consistent view of the service quality literature. For example, Parasuraman et al. (2005) and Zeithaml et al. (2002), argue that the perceived value is a dependent construct and is a predictor of purchase/repurchase behaviour. They also note that the links from perceived website service quality to perceived value and from the perceived value to purchase/repurchase behaviour are consistent with the extensive conceptual and empirical work on the quality-value-loyalty chain.

Three of the constructs in the survey instrument are Website Service Quality, Perceived Value and Loyalty Intentions. These three similar constructs, each of which represents website service quality, showed a high degree of association and it was therefore considered appropriate to combine them to form a new Perceived Public Value construct. This new construct contains 13 items (see Table 5).

It was also observed that three of the items in the Efficiency construct relate to the ease of completion of tax returns and making payments. These are: ‘ROS enables me to complete my tax returns easily’, ‘ROS enables me to file my tax returns quickly’ and ‘ROS enables me to pay my tax easily’. The latter two were introduced at the request of the Revenue. It is not surprising that these show a strong association. Consequently, a separate construct comprising these 3 items was formed and named Ease of Completion.

Three of the constructs remained unchanged from the original E-S-QUAL. These are the System Availability, Privacy and Contact constructs. As the Responsiveness construct showed multiple loadings across the factors it was eliminated. The fact that three of the items that comprised the original responsiveness construct were deleted at the request of the Revenue, leaving only two representative items, almost certainly weakened the strength of this construct and thus contributed to this outcome.

In summary:

 Three dependent variables were combined to form a new construct, Perceived Public Value.

 Three items from the Efficiency construct were combined to form a new construct named Ease of Completion.

 Three constructs (System Availability, Privacy and Contact) remain unchanged.

 One construct was eliminated as it showed strong similarity across several constructs.

 The resulting modified model comprises the five independent variables of Efficiency, Ease of Completion, System Availability, Privacy and Contact and a newly formed dependent variable termed Perceived Public Value.

As a number of changes had resulted from the factor analysis, the three constructs containing new item associations were now subjected to a further reliability test. This reliability analysis showed that the three constructs containing new item associations, that is, Ease of Completion, Efficiency and the newly combined Perceived Public Value worked particularly well with this sample. Cronbach’s alpha values for each of these modified constructs are shown in Table 6.

As previously noted, several modifications to the survey instrument, involving both addition and removal of items, had to be made at the request of Revenue. Such adjustments are not unknown in studies of this nature. While it would have been preferable to retain some of the removed items, these changes were accepted as a condition of Revenue’s cooperation in providing access to their customer base. These changes had the effect of complicating certain aspects of the analysis, but as noted in the literature review, several researchers contend that instruments such as E-S-QUAL have to be contextualised. The experience of this study is in line with this observation. However, such contextualisation can cause problems for validity. It is argued that these problems are not material in this case as this is, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the first time this instrument has been applied to an online taxation filing website. Further studies will be necessary to confirm the general validity of the instrument. An alternative would have been to use an unmodified instrument and a much smaller direct sample, bypassing Revenue. On balance, it was considered that the cooperation and input of Revenue outweighed the disadvantages of having to modify the instrument. Finally, arising from additional items added by Revenue, a new construct, Ease of Completion emerged from this study. This is consistent with Babakus & Mangold’s (1992) argument that new factors will emerge in different contexts.

When considering the quality characteristics of e-government websites, there are several differences from those of business websites that need to be taken into consideration. These include the monopoly nature of most state services, the legal requirement to engage in certain transactions, the obligation of the state to provide alternative, off-line channels, the nature of the services being provided and the nature of the data required to access certain services. These differences suggest that there are likely to be material differences in the relative importance of the various dimensions that comprise the perceived quality of government web services from those of commercial websites.

One obvious difference relates to Loyalty Intentions, which is a critical factor in the commercial sector, but has generally no equivalent meaning in the public sector Inasmuch as Loyalty Intentions has a meaning in this study, it is loyalty to channel. Inasmuch as the government is interested in citizens using online services as opposed to more expensive paper or physical location based services, creating and maintaining loyalty to online services is important. It is argued in this paper that loyalty to a channel will occur if the user perceives that the channel delivers better value than alternative channels, in this case paper filing. It should be noted that it is not self evident, a priori, that online filing is better value for the citizen. For example, users may feel that the slower paper based system may allow them to delay finalizing their tax payments. Revenue has taken a number of steps, including guaranteeing faster refunds with ROS, to try to disincentivize paper filing.

Second, Perceived Value, which in the commercial world is a complex construct incorporating several factors such as price, delivery time and guarantees, has a different meaning in the public sector where value is perceived in terms of government efficiency (value for money) and of personal efficiency for the citizen using the website. Another difference between the dimensions of online service quality in the commercial and public sectors is the complexity of transactions and the consequent risk of errors. This is particularly the case with filing of tax returns. One would therefore expect Ease of Use to be an important dimension and indeed this emerges from this study.

Finally, in the public sector, user concerns are less about financial risk than about misuse or mismanagement of personal data. The e-government literature suggests that the public have high levels of concern about government misuse of personal data (West, 2007). West has shown that some people are reluctant to give personal information to the state because they are concerned that such information may be used for purposes other than that for which it was collected. He suggests that as many as two thirds of users feel ‘unsafe’ using the Internet to conduct government transactions online. There is also much empirical evidence to suggest poor management of security and controls of personal data by public agencies (BBC, 2007; Digital Rights Ireland, 2007; Guardian, 2006).

The results show that the all of the service quality dimensions examined in this study of a public sector website are significant. Efficiency is the dimension that proves most valuable in terms of its power to explain variation in ROS users’ perception of value. Ease of Completion is the variable with the second strongest predictive power. System Availability and Privacy are also important, but to a lesser degree. The Contact construct provides the weakest contribution. This may be a reflection of the fact that tax practitioners and others using ROS are computer literate and expect to interact with a government website that can anticipate their concerns. However, in order to validate this finding, the construct should be included in future public sector website service quality instruments. For users of ROS, their use of the system is for functional work-related purposes and it is therefore not surprising that their main emphasis should be on efficiency and ease of completion.

## Limitations

There are a number of limitations of this study, several of which have already been mentioned or discussed. In addition, there are limitations related to the Website Service Quality construct itself. Four items were originally introduced into this study with the intention of using these to form a website service quality construct that would operate as a dependent variable and enable testing of the predictive influence of each of the e-service quality dimensions. However, in the factor analysis, these 4 items combined strongly with the larger Perceived Value and Loyalty Intentions constructs resulting in the formation of a new construct termed Perceived Public Value. Consequently, it was not possible to measure the e-service quality dimensions specifically in relation to the degree to which they each individually and directly influence the perception of website service quality.

As already noted, the weakness of the Responsiveness construct does not necessarily indicate that it should be omitted from future studies as the original 5 items testing this construct were reduced to two at the request of Revenue officials. Future studies using the full construct may show it as having a greater predictive influence. In a similar vein, the 3 items that comprise the Ease of Completion construct were separated from the original Efficiency construct. On the basis of the strength of association between these items and the fact that this construct provided the strongest predictive influence on Perceived Public Value, researchers may find it more useful to test the items that comprise the Ease of Completion construct separately from the items that comprise the Efficiency construct in future studies. Finally, another aspect that might have been studied is the impact of computer literacy/experience. This was not measured in this study (nor is it included in the E-S-QUAL instrument) and should be included in future studies.

## Conclusion

There are two important and related contributions that emerge from this research. First, there is a clear difference between the factors that are of importance to users of online taxation services and those that are of primary importance to users of commercial online services. Second, a modified version of the E-S-QUAL instrument, adapted for the different characteristics of the public sector, is proposed.

Both these matter because of the importance of website service quality for online taxation and potentially for other government online transaction services. ROS has been a considerable success. In 2008 ROS recorded over 650,000 transactions, bringing in a total of nearly h22 billion in tax revenues (Revenue Commissioners, 2009). The ROS site has also won several awards for design quality and ease of use. Not surprisingly, therefore, this research shows a high level of user satisfaction with the service and a high level of intention to re-use it. (It is worth noting that other Irish government online taxation sites, including new vehicle registration and corporation tax, have been comparably successful.) It was noted above that there are several benefits from widespread take-up on online tax filing. The same can be said of many other types of citizen-government interaction, particularly in high transaction volume services such as social welfare. As noted in the literature review, while several authors have proposed stage models of e-government maturity, in several of these models the highest form of maturity is some form of two-way interaction. To date, ROS is one of the few e-government services in Ireland to offer an effective online and real-time service. As such, it provides a model for other e-government services, and other departments and agencies can copy the factors that have made it successful. The sizable savings arising from the shift by citizens from paper or direct contact to online services means that there is a considerable payback to be realized from improvements to the quality of government websites as each new user represents a direct and continuing saving to the public purse. With such large potential returns from small improvements, a good understanding of the factors that create user satisfaction is valuable.

This research suggests that two new constructs, Perceived Public Value and Ease of Completion are the most important factors influencing users’ perceptions of public sector service quality and are good predictors of continued use. This contrasts strongly with the findings for commercial websites that suggest that the most important factors for those websites are security, reliability, responsiveness, navigability and features such as a good search facility (Cao et al., 2005; Lee & Kozar, 2005; Lee & Lin, 2005; Kuo et al., 2009) though usability is also important (Flavia´n et al., 2006) as well as good post purchase delivery and service (Otim & Grover, 2006). A possible reason for this difference is that since tax filing is not a voluntary task, users place particular value on being able to do it quickly and with minimum effort. The same is likely to be true of other government websites involving transactions (such as motor taxation or paying local charges). However, the specific nature of taxation and its user base is such that further research, including research in other jurisdictions, is needed to confirm the extent to which this finding can be generalized for other types of public sector online services.

This research also proposes a new tool, E-PS-QUAL, that is designed for the particular circumstances of the public sector. These features include the unimportance or even irrelevance of loyalty intentions and compensation as well as the importance of ease of completion.
