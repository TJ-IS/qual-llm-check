# Hospitality and hostility in hospitals: a case study of an EPR adoption among surgeons

Tina Blegind Jensen<sup>1</sup> and Margunn Aanestad<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

The purpose of this paper is to discuss the adoption of healthcare information systems (HIS) from a user perspective. Our case study concerns how a group of orthopaedic surgeons experienced and reacted to the adoption and mandatory use of an Electronic Patient Record system in a Danish hospital. We propose to use the concepts of hospitality and hostility to turn our attention to the interaction between the host (the surgeons) and the guest (the information system) and consider how the boundaries between them evolved in the everyday work practices. As an alternative to previous studies on technology adoption, these concepts help us appreciate and put special emphasis on particular aspects of the adoption process: the mutual and co-constitutive relationship between the users and the technology and the continued coexistence of both positive and negative attitudes among the users. The findings suggest an alternative way of thinking about an adoption process that is considered relevant to managers who strive to ensure successful adoption of HIS.

## Introduction

The role of users when adopting information systems (IS) into an organisational context has been an important topic in the IS research field. There is a large body of literature on how to involve users in both the design and adoption phases, and it is generally accepted that a successful implementation hinges on the ability to ensure user acceptance. For instance, current research addresses topics such as user satisfaction (Bondarouk, 2006; Scheepers et al., 2006), user acceptance (Davis, 1989; Brown et al., 2002; Wilson & Lankton, 2004; Lim et al., 2005), user perceptions and attitudes (Van der Heijden et al., 2003; Lin & Silva, 2005; Doherty et al., 2006), user engagement and user resistance (Irani et al., 2001; Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006). A majority of these studies conclude that the acceptance and subsequent use of the IS are determined by those actors in the organisation who are actually going to use the system. The role of users is equally important when it comes to the adoption of healthcare information systems (HIS) (Mantzana et al., 2007). The healthcare professionals, who are the primary users of such system may enjoy considerable autonomy in the organisation (Berg, 1999, 2001) and it is important for managers to understand their views and roles in the adoption process. Mantzana et al. conclude that it is of high importance to recognise ‘ythe actors’ views since their actions can have a great impact on IS adoption’ (Mantzana et al., 2007, p. 97).

This paper reports data from a study of an Electronic Patient Record (EPR) implementation project in a Danish hospital, where both the nurses’ and doctors’ perceptions of the EPR adoption were examined (Jensen, 2007). Using the EPR system was mandatory and this may have implications for the users’ reactions towards the system (Gill, 1996; Sharp, 2006). According to Kohli & Kettinger (2004), mandatory IS use among physicians is far more critical compared to other professional groups (e.g. nurses) because of their traditional autonomy, legitimacy and status. Thus the focus in this paper is narrowed down to one of the groups, the orthopaedic surgeons, and their perceptions of the mandatory EPR adoption.

Analytically, the paper proposes an alternative approach compared to previous studies on technology adoption by drawing on the concepts of hospitality and hostility (Ciborra, 1996, 1999). The two concepts facilitate a focus on the ‘social, existential and mundane elements’ (Saccol & Reinhard, 2006, p. 154) of an IS adoption. We turn our attention to the interaction between the surgeons as hosts and the EPR system as a guest and consider how the boundaries between them evolved in the everyday work practices. The concepts help us put special emphasis on the co-constitutive relationship between the users and the technology as well as the co-existence of both positive and negative attitudes among the users.

In the next section, we present a brief review of the literature on user perceptions and attitudes in IS adoptions before we introduce the concepts of hospitality and hostility. We then present the case setting and the research methods. By using the lens of hospitality and hostility we describe how the surgeons experienced the EPR adoption. Finally, we discuss the findings and conclude on the relevance of the study.

## Understanding user perceptions and attitudes in IS adoptions

Previous studies within IS suggest that more focus should be given to issues such as user perceptions and attitudes in relation to IS adoption and use. It is argued that IS adoption processes are influenced by those actors in the organisations who are going to use the technology (Barley, 1986; Jasperson et al., 2005; Vaast & Walsham, 2005). Issues such as users’ acceptance, perceptions and attitudes therefore become crucially important to successful management of IS. Based on a literature review of studies on IS adoptions from a user perspective, we discuss two well-established approaches to how such phenomena could be studied. We then present hospitality and hostility as an alternative way of thinking about IS adoptions.

## Technology acceptance literature

The dominant models used within IS research for discussing user perceptions and attitudes are the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1989), its predecessors such as the Theory of Reasoned Action (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1980) and Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen, 1985) and its descendants such as TAM2 (Venkatesh & Morris, 2000) and the Unified Theory for the Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al., 2003). In general, these models account for the variance in individual users behaviour by presenting beliefs that are formed by the users in relation to accepting technology. For instance, in the TAM literature two determinants of user acceptance are presented. The first is perceived usefulness, which relates to the extent to which users experience that the use of an IT system will enhance their job performance, and the second is perceived ease of use, which relates to the extent to which the use of an IT system will require much effort (Davis, 1989).

The technology acceptance approach is widely used and extensions are numerous (see a review of the use of TAM in Sharp, 2006). For instance, there have been attempts to apply these models in a healthcare context. Chismar & Wiley-Patton (2003) examine physicians’ intention to adopt Internet-based healthcare applications and find that perceived usefulness (the primary predictor variable) is a stronger determinant of intention to use than perceived ease of use. A study by Schaper & Pervan (2004) applying TAM in a healthcare setting supports this finding and emphasises the importance of performance expectancy, that is, the degree to which healthcare workers believe that using IS helps them obtain gains in job performance.

The studies drawing on TAM and related models have been criticised on a number of points. For instance, one limitation is that the models are developed and verified using empirical data relating to simple, stand-alone and individual technologies as opposed to complex organisational technologies. Analytically, a majority of the studies have compared perceptions between subjects or crosssectional perceptions rather than following longitudinal processes. Moreover, the sample population during the experiments has often been students rather than actual users. Most of the studies have been conducted in a context of voluntary usage, which is not always relevant for organisational IS. Attempts have been made to validate TAM in mandatory use situations, and it has been found that ‘the results show a somewhat different pattern of relationships in the mandated environment than we have seen in the prior research on voluntary adoption contexts’ (Brown et al., 2002, p. 289). For the mandated use situations it has been argued that user satisfaction (rather than system usage) is a more accurate reflection of contentment (Scheepers et al., 2006).

Generally speaking, the aim of the studies on TAM and related models is to account for variance in users behaviour rather than to explain the sources of the perceptions that the users harbour. The main focus is on explaining the orientation of individual users, which may be helpful to managers in building more or less realistic expectations to behaviour linked to the individual characteristics of the adopter (such as age or gender). It is, however, of less value in explaining the phenomena at an organisational level, which is significant in the context of healthcare IS.

## Organisational implementation literature

Studies of organisational implementation seek to understand how and why users react to technology adoption the way they do and how they perceive the use of the technology from a social organisational perspective. Their aim is to study the meanings that users ascribe to technology and how users’ attitudes and perceptions relating to new technology are socially shaped. Orlikowski & Gash (1994) introduce the concept of technological frames to study the assumptions and knowledge of technology shared by the members of different groups. The technological frames represent the expectations, assumptions and knowledge that organisational members use in their understanding of technology in organisations: ‘y they include not only knowledge about the particular technology but also local understanding of specific uses in a given setting’ (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994, 178). This concept has been used in various IS adoption studies (e.g. Davidson, 2002; Lin & Silva, 2005) and shows how different groups of users may perceive a technology adoption in rather diverse ways.

Also the notion of sensemaking, introduced by Weick (1995), has been used to examine how the constructions or interpretations related to IS are created and enacted through everyday practices (Henfridsson, 1999; Bansler & Havn, 2006; Jensen & Aanestad, 2007). The same recognition of the social nature of users’ attitudes we find in studies of problematic implementation experience, for instance lack of user involvement in the selection process (Barki & Hartwick, 1994), resistance to changes in work practices (Gill, 1996) and power structures (Keen, 1981; Markus, 1983; Howcroft & Mitev, 2000; Puri & Sahay, 2004). Lamb & Kling (2003) suggest that by reconceptualising the user as a social actor, we will not only be able to study users’ perceptions and attitudes towards IS but also take into consideration the organisational context that shapes the adoption.

An understanding of both the organisational and social context is no less significant for managers concerned with HIS adoptions. The healthcare professionals who are the primary users of the IT system may enjoy a high degree of autonomy and their views and actions will influence the IS adoption. The context is crucially important to take into consideration as the role and impact of EPR systems in healthcare organisations is undefined. It is not given at the outset of the implementation in what way concrete work procedures will have to be redesigned, and it is difficult to foresee changes in the roles and responsibilities among various professional groups.

## Hospitality and hostility

The fundamental cognitive orientation of TAM and related approaches seems to overlook the complex relationships that develop between the technology, the users and the organisational setting. With this study we move beyond TAM, which has primarily focused on the individual user as unit of analysis. Furthermore, we attempt to keep our focus on the individual user while we address issues discussed in IS implementation studies. Here issues such as user resistance, lack of user involvement and power structures are often examined using the group or organisation as the unit of analysis, rather than the individual.

In order to build another discourse to frame the relationship between technology and organisation, one which is ‘closer to human existence and its basic institutions’ (Ciborra, 1999, p. 199), Ciborra proposes the concept of hospitality, originally suggested by Derrida (Ciborra, 1996; Derrida, 1996; Derrida & Dufourmantelle, 2000). Hospitality is an ancient human practice that allows relationships between strangers to be established. The host takes on an obligation to accommodate and protect the stranger seeking shelter. Hospitality is:

y an institutional device to cut down the time needed to merge cultures, and to integrate alien mindsets and costumes. Hospitality can precipitate the turning of an ephemeral contact into a relationship that ‘looks (and feels) like’ a long acquaintance (Ciborra, 1999, p. 193).

In other words, hospitality mediates the relationship between the host and the guest. The etymology of hospitality shows conflicting origins and meanings, where ‘host’ in Latin may both relate to a friend and an enemy (Ciborra, 1996; Dikec, 2002). Accepting a stranger is a risky endeavour, and Ciborra argues that hospitality may easily turn into hostility. For instance, users of a new technology may suddenly realise the transparency it offers for management surveillance, and the technology again becomes an ambiguous stranger (Ciborra & Patriotta, 1996). Drawing on this metaphor for studying an adoption process, Brigham & Introna (2006) argue that actors want to honour the technology as a guest but at the same time they try to keep it at a distance. This underlines the ambiguous meaning of the concept that we bring forward with our empirical case. When new technology is adopted into an organisational setting, it will thus most often be perceived as: ‘ya ‘stranger’ rather than as a familiar servant’ (Brigham & Introna, 2006, p. 142). The members in the organisation (i.e. the host) will try to enforce their needs and culture upon the technology. In return, the technology (i.e. the guest) will impose its functionality and standards on its users (Orlikowski, 1992; Saccol & Reinhard, 2006). Thus, rather than seeing technology as an unequivocal and fixed application, this approach highlights the risk and ambiguity of the stranger at the doorstep. The adoption process is unpredictable and not necessarily successful; the technology may drift, and it may disappoint its users and generate anxiety.

In discussing properties of hospitality, Saccol & Reinhard (2006) relate hospitality to matters of identity. A host and a guest do not have substantial a priori knowledge of each other; their identities must be revealed and disclosed in a process over time. Having to present oneself anew to a stranger may unsettle taken-forgranted self-perceptions. When we need to redefine socio-technical arrangements, responsibilities and commitments, we also rethink and change our identities; this may be a difficult and challenging process.

The concept of hospitality also emphasises the obligations of the host. It may take a lot of effort to host a guest. The host’s daily routines must be adapted to accommodate the guest, and the guest must accommodate to life in the host’s home. The introduction of new technology requires conscious work in securing acceptance as well as commitment among users. This may not be as straightforward as initially assumed. Concepts such as improvisations, bricolage, care, appropriation and cultivation become central in understanding technology adoption in organisations (Ciborra, 1996). By using the concepts of hospitality and hostility, we approach the HIS adoption as an open and undetermined process where emphasis is on the interaction between the EPR system as the guest and the surgeons as the hosts. It is not a simple matter of either acceptance or rejection of a technology. On the contrary, the two concepts highlight the possibility for ambivalent and non-stabilised relations between the host and the guest.

## Case setting and research methods

As mentioned, this paper reports on a case study conducted as part of a larger research project (Jensen, 2007; Jensen & Aanestad, 2007). The focus here is on how a group of surgeons perceived and reacted to an EPR adoption. The setting is an orthopaedic surgery department situated in a medium-sized Danish hospital. The department has an average of 3000 admissions (both emergency and planned) per year and it consists of a standard ward, an outpatient clinic and a secretary office. Ten consultant surgeons and one managing consultant surgeon are employed, specialised in shoulder, knee and hip alloplastics along with foot surgery.

The first author collected data between January and December 2005 in immediate continuation of the EPR adoption. A combination of data collection techniques was used. First, observation studies took place in the surgical ward and in the outpatient clinic. The observation served primarily as background information in relation to the interview situation but it also served as validation of what was said during the interviews.

The richest source of empirical data stems from 10 semi-structured interviews with representatives from the group of consultant surgeons. Each interview lasted from 60 to 90 min where the surgeons talked about their experiences with the EPR adoption. All interviews were taped and transcribed verbatim. The initial findings were reported back in writing to the surgeons, and a focus group interview was conducted with four surgeons. These activities served to enhance the completeness of the data from the single interviews. Finally, written material and documentation such as project plans, organisation charts, information on the websites, user manuals and newsletters completed the data material and served as background information. Our analysis mainly builds on the transcripts from the interviews and to some extent on the field notes and the written material. The concepts of hospitality and hostility were used as a sensitising device (Walsham, 1995) for analysing the empirical findings. We categorised the data into a set of themes that reflect the interaction between the surgeons and the EPR system and how aspects of both hospitality and hostility seemed to co-exist. These themes are presented in the analysis section below and represent the surgeons’ reactions in relation to hosting a new technology.

## The surgeons’ reactions to the EPR adoption

At a general level, the findings show that the surgeons welcomed the EPR system as long as it provided direct clinical benefit to their work and eased their work practices. At the same time, they harboured negative reactions towards the EPR system since it implied new mechanisms for administrative control of their work and introduced new work tasks previously performed by others. The surgeons’ perceptions of the EPR system indicated an ongoing shift between honouring the technology as a guest and at the same time trying to keep it at a distance. Below we examine how the orthopaedic surgeons expressed these reactions.

## Official reasons for hosting the EPR system

The EPR system introduced was a standard off-the-shelf system in which only minor changes were possible. It was a shared and interdisciplinary electronic version of what is known as the patient’s paper record and it was mandatory for the surgeons to use it. It comprised information in the form of patient notes (nursing notes, progress notes and physiotherapists’ notes), diagnosis, medicine scheme, history data, temperature, blood pressure, X-rays and laboratory data. According to the project plan and website, the benefits of implementing the EPR system were: the patient will be treated on the basis of one set of information; the record will always be accessible and brought up to date; and the risk of making mistakes is reduced because of a unified documentation system.

Based on these benefits, the EPR system seemed to be worth inviting in and the surgeons seemed rather positive to hosting this new technology. They considered the EPR system as a tool that supported and facilitated documentation procedures: ‘We need a record which functions as a tool for documentation as well as a daily work tool’ (surgeon 5). The paper record served both these tasks, and the surgeons thereby naturally expected the EPR system to preserve the same aspects.

## The EPR system facilitates the surgeons’ main work tasks

In addition to serving as a tool for documentation, the surgeons considered the EPR system as a means to facilitate the medicine prescription procedures in ways not previously possible:

We have some standard prescription procedures in the EPR, and these are much easier to use when prescribing medicine. Then you do not need to prescribe each drug separately, having to indicate the number of pills, how much and when (surgeon 2).

The medication prescription routines were also expected to become more secure:

Right from the beginning, we wanted to make sure that the medicine is only prescribed in the record and nowhere else. We didn’t want anything like medicine prescriptions in Kardex [the nurses’ note system] or any misunderstandings between secretaries and surgeons (surgeon 6).

Moreover, the EPR system allowed the surgeons to access patient information continuously, whereby no important information was missing as might be the case before with the paper record:

It is of great help that I can sit here and look up patient information. We save time (surgeon 1).

The surgeons welcomed the EPR technology as long as it provided prospects for better performance and direct clinical benefits to their professional work (Dikec, 2002). However, it was not only positive perceptions that were formed about the use of the EPR technology. The surgeons experienced that their opinions about the implementation and use of the EPR system were neglected in the decision-making processes.

## The surgeons were not consulted in the decisionmaking process

It was the hospital management who, together with representatives from other wards, decided what system to implement. The surgeons in the orthopaedic surgery were not consulted in the selection and decision-making process:

We would have liked to have a say in the selection process y the EPR system is something that highly influences our practice, and it is a central tool in our work procedures y Of course it is not possible for everybody to participate in this decision making process, but it would have been nice to have the impression that you were involved somehow and that it was not something that was implemented without our approval (surgeon 5).

## Another surgeon adds:

They have not even asked an old-timer like me for advice. In the old days, we decided everything. Now we’re left out in the cold (surgeon 1).

The surgeons here expressed rather negative attitudes because they felt neglected in the decision process. The organisational procedures around the EPR adoption did not take into consideration the surgeons’ points of views in relation to system selection and planning of the implementation. This influenced how the surgeons received the system and to what degree they integrated it into their work practices and participated in the adoption process.

## More control of the work practices

While most surgeons emphasised the way in which the system supported work, some of the surgeons however referred to the system as a control mechanism:

There is too much control of our work. I have to control every single blood sample, even though it is normal y [Before] we just received the test results on a piece of paper. Now we have to document that we have checked the results (surgeon 10).

It must be built around an American model because we have to control and control (surgeon 1).

The control aspects were considered as an external influence on the surgeons’ work practices, which previously were highly autonomous in nature. The surgeons experienced that these control mechanisms challenged their professional autonomy, expertise and clinical freedom. The way this criticism was raised, however, was related to how the control approach led to unnecessary and time-consuming work tasks:

It is a misuse of my time y we use so much time on controlling blood sample results and laboratory tests that we cannot focus on the most important aspects of our job (surgeon 1).

A majority of the surgeons believed that the EPR system would hinder them in investing their energy on the primary part of their work practices; namely to treat patients.

## The surgeons need to take on new tasks

The majority of the surgeons experienced that the EPR system introduced new tasks to their profession that were not considered their field of responsibility:

When patients need an appointment with an occupational therapist or a physiotherapist, then the surgeons now have to refer the patient by typing it into the system themselves. Before, the secretaries did thaty it means an extra task (surgeon 5).

A new X-ray system has been introduced in our ward. This means extra time in the outpatient clinic. Previously, the nurses were the ones to hang up X-ray pictures. Now, I need to enter the system and find the pictures myself. I may easily spend three minutes on this task (surgeon 6).

In this way, the surgeons’ work tasks changed with the adoption of the EPR system. They believed that they had to use their valuable time on tasks that they were not trained for and that they did not consider as part of their profession:

Before the EPR we were dictating the medicine prescription and the secretaries typed it. Now we have to do this ourselves y If you imagine a patient who gets 20 different types of medicine, then you have to check whether it is regular or one time medication. Then you need to find the name, the dose, the form – is it daily or every second day, and then how many times a day. Every single hour has to be typed into the system, the dose has to be written. You can easily spend 5 minutes on every drug yWe do not consider this to be the doctor’s task to sit around typing (surgeon 3).

One of the surgeons mentioned with an ironical undertone: ‘They should send us on a course in typewriting y or they’d better hire some secretaries’ (surgeon 3). The surgeons feared that the changes to their work practices introduced by the EPR system would devalue their status and authority in the organisation vis-a\`-vis the other healthcare actors. As discussed in the next section, this meant that the traditional and social relations to other groups of actors were questioned and subject to change.

## The identity of the surgeons is challenged

When receiving the EPR system, the surgeons sensed that their professional and personal identities became challenged and had to be re-thought and negotiated. As expressed by one surgeon:

Sometimes I recall that the reason why I chose the profession and job was because I wanted to be a craftsman – you know, to take care of hip replacements. Everybody in my profession is characterised by being a carpenter, a bricklayer, a butcher, a seamstress at the same time. Being placed in front of a computer is not natural to me (surgeon 4).

We are craftsmen. We need to have a hammer and a chisel to do our job. This [i.e. using a computer] is not really of interest to us. If it was, we would have studied computer science for our minor or something like that (surgeon 5).

The surgeons preferred to represent their identity as something very different from a computer user. However, when becoming familiar with the possibilities of the technology, this tendency to dichotomise the competencies seemed to give way to a more integrated view:

I know that some people still carry out their tasks in an awkward and difficult manner when there is an easier way to do it. I may have forgotten some of the short cuts and I know I have some habits that are not that practical y it gives you more time to do other things if you carry out your tasks the simplest way. And it also means that you get this ‘aha’ feeling it when you know that you can actually do like that and that is quite simple. Then you get a more positive attitude towards the whole project (surgeon 6).

The major reason behind such willingness and effort to accept the computer was the shift to see it as a helpful ally, not an obstacle: ‘I love everything that can help facilitate my job’ (surgeon 7). We end our presentation of the empirical material with a quote that demonstrates how sceptical attitudes co-exist with a wish to continue to use the technology: ‘I would like to repeat that now that we have it, we use it. I am in favour of the system and I would rather not work without it’ (surgeon 1).

## Analysis and discussion

A first reading of the findings confirms many of the established insights from IS research both within the technology acceptance stream and within organisational implementation studies. For instance, the case can be analysed from the perspective of TAMs as the surgeons’ appraisal of the EPR system can be linked to ‘perceived usefulness’ (a TAM construct) or ‘performance expectancy’ (a UTAUT construct). Similarly, the case exhibits traits that IS implementation studies have addressed of lack of user involvement, resistance to change and turbulence arising when organisational power structures are challenged (Dikec, 2002).

Rather than reiterating these points, we use the concepts of hospitality and hostility to illuminate other aspects of the EPR adoption process. The installation was the starting point of an ongoing process where the relations between the surgeons (the host) and the EPR system (the guest) were continuously evolving. Our findings show how the surgeons at first welcomed the technology and honoured it as a guest. Brigham and Introna argue that as a guest to be welcomed, a technology ‘ymust present itself as something worth inviting in’ (Brigham & Introna, 2006, p. 145). The EPR technology seemed worthwhile to invite in. The surgeons considered it as a tool to assist them in their documentation and as a way to facilitate their work procedures in relation to medicine prescription. After the implementation, however, the surgeons started to perceive the EPR system as challenging their authority and the previous division of work and responsibilities, since the system came with inbuilt controls and it took extra time for the surgeons to perform certain work tasks. The surgeons also blamed the managers for not consulting them in the decision-making process. But at the same time, they emphasised that they wished to continue using the system.

This ambivalence is a striking feature of our study. While the technology acceptance literature has primarily focused on issues that we may classify as hospitality aspects (i.e. perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness), and the organisational implementation literature describes either hostility (i.e. user resistance, lack of user involvement, power struggles) or hospitality (i.e. user acceptance, satisfaction, and organisational learning), our findings show that the same individual can harbour both hostile and hospitable attitudes simultaneously. Moreover, we have shown that a person’s attitudes are not just based on assessing the tool’s functional characteristics per se, but on perceptions gained from actual interaction with the new technology in an organisational setting. The surgeons try to enforce their immediate needs upon the EPR system by considering it as a documentation tool and as a tool to facilitate medicine prescriptions. In return, the EPR system imposes its functionality and standards on the surgeons by introducing what is perceived by the surgeons as control and time constraints.

The ambivalence towards the technology is to a certain degree related to the technology’s ‘openness’. This ‘openness’ means that the EPR system is open to many different interpretations and use patterns (i.e. what Orlikowski (1992) terms interpretive flexibility). There is flexibility in how the surgeons interpret and use the technology on the basis of their respective interests and it becomes difficult to predict how a technology will be used. The effect of the technology’s functionality is not uniform, but specifically related to the organisation of work procedures and the functionality of the EPR system in this particular case. For instance, the medication procedures in the EPR system are intended to improve quality by introducing standard procedures. It saves time by allowing the prescription of pre-defined standardised packages, but the detailed typing of non-standard prescriptions by the surgeons takes a lot more time than before. The system is thus not perceived as only positive or only negative. The different elements of its functionality are interpreted according to how the tool is linked to the organisational work routines. The openness also explains the continued existence of ambivalence as long as the work routines and the technology are still not fully aligned. The surgeons focus their attention on the demands of the present work situation and ask whether the EPR system may help improve the work procedures, rather than look for future gains. This strong emphasis on the practical and immediate usability of the system is in line with the findings of Chismar & Wiley-Patton’s (2003)

study applying TAM2 in a healthcare setting, suggesting that in such a context ‘perceived usefulness’ outweighs the role of ‘perceived ease of use’. This emphasis on the EPR’s affordances (Norman, 1993; Hutchby, 2001) reflects a practical-evaluative emphasis (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998), which can also be seen in the controversies over work tasks. The old work practices had a clear division of roles and responsibilities among the various groups of professions. The surgeons’ main argument is that changes in the work practices may result in less time to perform what they perceive as the core of their work, namely to treat patients. We see this clearly with respect to the perception that the system increases the (potential) external control over the work practices. The EPR system requires more precision when typing information into it, more standardised procedures and new routines of documenting. According to Doolin (2004), doctors prefer to rely on peer-monitoring procedures rather than external control, and this might explain the resistance to the EPR system’s control features. However, the reasons given by the surgeons are related to the inordinate workload generated by these control mechanisms.

The study shows that it is clearly an inadequate strategy (both analytically and practically) to treat the EPR system as just one application with given characteristics. The surgeons’ opinions towards it are shaped by the way it is implemented and accommodated, not only by the application in itself, detached from the usage situation. The routines related to the EPR system may have to be renegotiated after the initial exposure to the effects of implementing the system. Hosting a new technology requires continuous care, attention and commitment to adapting and revising the routines and structures around it.

## Conclusions and implications

In summing up the study, we have learnt that the surgeons welcomed the EPR system as long as it provided direct clinical benefit to their work and eased their work practices. At the same time, hostile reactions towards the EPR system were evident since it implied control mechanisms of their work and introduced new work tasks previously performed by others. The great emphasis that surgeons put on the practical and immediate benefits of the system and similarly the resistance that the difficulties provoke are findings that are relevant to managers when it comes to planning and managing specific change initiatives.

We introduced the notions of hospitality and hostility as a sensitising device for studying and understanding adoption processes. We proposed this approach as an alternative to the traditional models of technology acceptance and IS implementation studies. Let us draw attention to the kind of phenomena that this approach allows us to identify. We have shown that an understanding of how HIS (and IS in general) become meaningful among users needs to be grounded in the day-to-day work practices. Our case study shows how users and technology shape and influence each other (Brigham & Introna, 2006). An analysis of a technology as a stand-alone and pre-determined artefact (as implied by the TAMs) would not have captured these aspects. Moreover, the state of a user’s attitudes in an adoption process is not single or static, but multi-faceted and dynamic; there are many aspects of the technology towards which the attitudes are different; positive and negative attitudes may co-exist; and the attitudes may change over time.

The findings from this study pertain to a healthcare context and further research is needed to discuss in more detail how the relationship between users and technology evolves in the everyday work practices in other settings where organisational and professional dynamics are different.
