## a b s t r a c t

Most online auction studies focus on the buyer side of the marketplace. To bridge the imbalance gap, we investigate seller behavior in this research. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally emphasized on seller heterogeneity, an area that has not been well studied in the electronic marketplace literature, this study classi<sup>fi</sup>es sellers into new and experienced seller groups and compares their auction performance based on three outcome measures: success rates, ending prices, and feedback ratings. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the performance of new sellers is worse than that of experienced sellers in all three measures, namely, lower auction success rates, lower auction ending prices, less likely to receive positive feedback ratings and more likely to receive negative feedback ratings. We also show the dynamics of these auction performances while new sellers evolve into experienced ones. Our empirical analysis reveals structural differences in auction success and price determinants between new and experienced sellers. That is, the determining factors for auction success and ending prices are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different between the two seller groups. We also identify several key factors for non-positive (i.e. neutral or negative) feedback ratings received by the two groups and <sup>fi</sup>nd no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between these factors for both new and experienced sellers. We believe our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications to the online auction house and sellers, and contribute towards building more effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient electronic marketplaces.

## 1. Introduction

Electronic marketplaces have become vital platforms for many to conduct business transactions due to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant reduction of search costs [9]. While electronic marketplaces serve to facilitate trade, transactions in cyberspace also involve greater uncertainty and more opportunities for fraud than traditional exchanges. It is dif-<sup>fi</sup>cult for online buyers to really know the sellers in a virtual environment. So, sellers' endeavor to earn trust from potential buyers as well as sellers' marketing strategies are essential to the success of online transactions.

Supply and demand jointly create a marketplace and determine a market price. For continuous success, electronic marketplaces need to appeal to both buyers and sellers. The foci of existing studies on electronic marketplaces, in particular, online auctions, however, are skewed towards the buyer side which include buyer behavior and bidding strategies [2,11,17,35] and buyer heterogeneity [12]. In the traditional auction setting, it was reasonable to assume that the sellers belonged to a homogenous, symmetric, risk-neutral group.

However, online auctions construct a dynamic marketplace and a virtual environment which attract different pro<sup>fi</sup>les of sellers. More sophisticated auction listing options in this environment might induce sellers to behave differently than in a traditional auction setting. Moreover, the virtual environment of the Internet is beyond the reach of existing auction theory. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in market structure resulting from the online environment challenge some of the existing auction theory's key assumptions. For example, one seller facing many bidders is replaced by many sellers facing many bidders in online auctions. Traditional auction theory does not stress trust and reputation in the analytical framework, but these two constructs are critical in online auctions. The existing literature on online auctions shows that there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gap when it comes to the study of seller behavior, selling strategies, and seller heterogeneity in electronic marketplaces.

The focus of this study is seller heterogeneity in electronic marketplaces. We attempt to investigate whether seller experience plays a role, and if so, to what extent, in affecting the performance of an auction. We classify sellers into new and experienced seller groups and compare their auction performance based on three outcome measures: success rates, ending prices, and feedback ratings. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the performance of new sellers is worse than that of experienced sellers in all three measures, namely, lower auction success rates, lower auction ending prices, lower probability of receiving positive feedback ratings and higher chances to receive negative feedback ratings. We also show the dynamics of these auction performances while new sellers evolve into experienced ones. Our further analysis reveals structural differences in auction success and price determinants between new and experienced sellers. That is, the determining factors for auction success and ending prices are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different between the two seller groups. We also identify several key factors for non-positive (i.e. neutral or negative) feedback ratings received by both groups and <sup>fi</sup>nd no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between these factors for new and experienced sellers.

We believe that better understanding seller heterogeneity in electronic markets is critical for continuous and healthy growth of these markets. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications to the online auction house and sellers. First, the knowledge about the structural differences in auction success and price determinants between new and experienced sellers enables the online auction house to customize its service to different seller groups. Equally important, the insights derived from negative and neutral feedback analysis help sellers better understand what buyers need and likely lead to better customer relationship and smoother future transactions. These implications can lead to the building of useful decision support systems by the auction house along with market participants to bene<sup>fi</sup>t both buyers and sellers. Such DSS's will in turn invite more businesses from existing and potential buyers and sellers, and <sup>fi</sup>nally bene<sup>fi</sup>t the auction house as well.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Literature review is provided in Section 2. The conceptual model, research hypotheses, and data used in the analysis are presented in Section 3. Section 4 compares auction performances between new and experienced sellers and their dynamics. The explorations of the determinants of auction success and selling price are presented in Section 5, followed by an analysis of critical factors for negative and neutral feedback ratings in Section 6. We <sup>fi</sup>nally conclude the study in Section 7.

## 2. Literature review

Existing theoretical research about auction sellers primarily focus on optimal auction design. These studies investigate, under speci<sup>fi</sup>c auction mechanisms, relationships between seller revenue and bidder's bidding strategies [38,43,46]. Generally, these optimal auction design studies are for sellers in traditional auctions rather than online auctions. Research on online auction design has emerged at a fast pace [12,13]. Some of these studies explore optimal selling strategies for online auctions. However, these theoretical studies explicitly or implicitly assume seller homogeneity.

Empirical studies pertaining to online auction sellers can be roughly grouped into the following four categories:

1) Investigating the role of seller reputation and trust in online auctions [4–6,18,19,20,21,28,33,36,39,45,59,60];

2) Evaluating the effectiveness of different selling options and exploring seller's choices in online auctions [1,11,14,32,34,37,48,52,53,55,58];

3) Studying the determinants of attracted bids, auction success and prices [1,8,24,30,37,51,61]; and

4) Examining shilling, fraud, learning dynamics and herding in online auctions [26,32,49,54,57].

All the above studies, however, except for [4,19,54], treat sellers as one homogenous market participant without any distinction among them. This empirical work focuses on seller heterogeneity in online auctions, an area that has not been well explored in the literature. It differs from previous studies in that, 1) we distinguish and compare auction performance between two groups of sellers: new and experienced sellers, and demonstrate the dynamics of online auction performances as sellers accrue experience; 2) we explore the structural differences in the determinants for auction success and <sup>fi</sup>nal prices between new and experienced sellers, and 3) we inspect the critical factors for negative and neutral feedback ratings for new as well as experienced sellers.

## 3. Conceptual model, research hypotheses, and data collection

## 3.1. Conceptual model and research hypotheses

Despite abundant guidelines provided by eBay for potential sellers, the process of listing and selling items is more sophisticated than buying items at eBay [1]. These complications particularly present challenges to new sellers due to the lack of experience. In online auctions, feedback scores are generally considered a fairly good proxy for experience. Ba and Pavlou [5] show that sellers with higher positive feedback scores can win a price premium in their auctions due to their reputation and selling experience. However, to establish reputation, sellers need time to initiate and complete auctions in order to accumulate their positive feedback scores from buyers.

There are many other strategies that can be used by sellers to attempt to achieve the goal of successful auctions and high ending prices. Each strategy here refers to a set of auction options adopted by a seller when she decides to list a product for sale. These auction options can be categorized into three categories:

1) Auction parameters such as reserve price, starting price and Buy-It-Now options. Sellers can choose these options to protect them or attract potential buyers. For example, Stani<sup>fi</sup>rd et al. [50] <sup>fi</sup>nd that Buy-It-Now prices are ignored by buyers even if they are set below retail prices. But, Popkowski et al. [44] <sup>fi</sup>nd that Buy-It-Now prices can be used as the price signaling for some products. Existing studies also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the following auction parameters signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect online auction success and prices: starting price [16,37,51,52,56,61], reserve price [24,37,56], payment methods [15], auction ending time [22], auction length [24,37], shipping cost, shipping insurance and international shipment and trade [24,30,37].

2) Product assurance options such as the number of product pictures and product descriptions. Sellers can use these options to reduce the information asymmetry about products. Existing studies show that the number of product pictures signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects auction success and prices [40.51]. The detailed descriptions or file sizes about the sellers, items, and transaction conditions are also signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for online auction success and prices [24]. Granados et al. [25] suggest that sellers may also choose to offer more useful product information by releasing product pictures, descriptions, samples, and detailed speci<sup>fi</sup>cations in auction listings. Hinz and Span [27] study the impact of information sharing and information diffusion in digital networks on bidding behavior. Bhargava et al. [7] address how sellers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from information asymmetry when endowing buyers with private information.

3) Trust enhancement such as product refund guarantee. Even though Roberts [47] <sup>fi</sup>nds that a guaranteed or refund promise does not substitute for reputation in online auctions, sellers still can use these options to boost trust from potential buyers. Bruce et al. [15] study how seller ratings with the presence of insurance affect consumer behavior. A third-party trust mechanism such as an escrow service provider can be used to boost online transactions [29,41]. Pavlou and Gefen [41] also investigate the role of credit card guarantee in building effective online marketplaces. Other trust enhancement strategies include offering a warranty or refund guarantee [3,23], and obtaining quality certi<sup>fi</sup>cation from a trusted third party (TTP) [1].

Effective use of these strategies enables sellers to provide a better image of themselves and their items to prospect buyers, ease buyers skeptics and boost trust on the sellers, reduce information asymmetry problems, protect their auction from not being completed, and increase the auction ending prices. Table A-1 in the Appendix A lists the auction options used in this study.

Based on the existing literature, our conceptual model for this study is depicted in Fig. 1. Sellers (classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into new sellers and experienced sellers) use selling options in three categories, i.e., auction parameters, product assurance and trust enhancement to build auction strategies for their auction listings. Buyers enter into electronic marketplaces and bid for these listed items. After a certain period, an auction listing might succeed with a winning ending price, or fail without a winning ending price. A successful auction might receive a positive, negative or neutral feedback rating from a buyer. We conduct comparison analysis in auction performance in three measures (i.e., auction success or failure, ending prices and feedback ratings) between new sellers and experienced sellers (Hypothesis 1). Then, to explain possible differences in auction performance, we investigate structural differences in auction success determinants (Hypothesis 2-1), ending price determinants (Hypothesis 2-2) and negative and neutral feedback determinants (Hypotheses 3-1 and 3-2). Based on the proposed conceptual model depicted in Fig. 1, we propose the following research hypotheses for this study:

Hypothesis 1. New sellers and experienced sellers have the same auction performance.

Hypothesis 2-1. New sellers and experienced sellers have the same auction success determinants.

Hypothesis 2-2. New sellers and experienced sellers have the same auction price determinants.

Hypothesis 3-1. New sellers and experienced sellers have the same factor ranking in receiving negative feedback ratings.

Hypothesis 3-2. New sellers and experienced sellers have the same factor ranking in receiving neutral feedback ratings.

The main purpose of this study is to demonstrate and explain the differences, in both static and dynamic fashion, between new and experienced sellers through the testing of these hypotheses and derive useful implications from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 3.2. Data collection

We chose to collect data from eBay because it is currently the largest online auction site with over 80% of the online auction market share [59]. We chose Sony PlayStation 2 game console for this study because Sony PlayStation was one of major players in the video game console market and had a reasonable market thickness measured by the number of auction listings and attracted bids every day. A total of 23,355 auctions were recorded in our data set over three and a half months.

Firstly, we are interested in the distribution of sellers in terms of their experience. To begin this analysis, we need to operationally de-<sup>fi</sup>ne “new sellers” and “experienced sellers”. Dellarocas et al. [19] show that the probability that sellers receive feedback ratings from buyers for successful auctions is on average at about 70% at eBay. This implies that a more experienced seller will tend to have higher feedback scores than a relatively newer seller. Dellarocas et al. and Ba et al.[4,19] use feedback scores as a proxy for a seller's experience in online auctions. Following their approach for seller classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, we use the seller feedback scores to measure a seller's online auction experience. In addition, from the viewpoint of buyers, it is appropriate to use this approach because this is what buyers usually perceive and judge sellers as new or experienced sellers. To get a robust result for our analyses, we use several cut-off feedback scores in de<sup>fi</sup>ning new and experienced sellers. Table 1 shows <sup>fi</sup>ve such de<sup>fi</sup>nitions.

From the dataset, sellers with 0 feedback scores occupy 4.6% of total population, half of sellers have 70 or less feedback scores, and sellers with more than 500 feedback scores take only 25% of the population.

## 4. Auction performance comparison: Test of Hypothesis 1

We assess sellers' auction performance based on three measures: 1) auction success rate; 2) auction ending price; and 3) positive feedback rate. These three variables do not necessarily correlate with each other but all three possess a common characteristic for sellers, i.e., the higher the value, the better. We therefore compare individually each of the three measures for both new and experienced sellers.

## 4.1. Auction success rate

Auction success is one of the primary goals for a seller. Our dataset shows a general increasing pattern, indicating sellers with low feedback scores tend to have lower auction success rates than those with higher feedback scores. A simple z-test con<sup>fi</sup>rms this observation. For example, using De<sup>fi</sup>nition 5 of new (feedback scores ≤ 9) and experienced (feedback scores > 9) sellers, the statistical test shows that the auction success rate for new sellers, which is 74.8% (the overall average success rate for all sellers is 80.9%), is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower than that of the experienced sellers in our data sample (z-value = −8.837, p-value b 0.001). Using other de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of new and experienced sellers all reveal similar results.

## 4.2. Auction ending price

Another important goal that sellers want to achieve is a higher auction ending price. Since the quality of used items varies upon product conditions and many pre-owned consoles are listed with accessories and games in bundle, we only use the auction listings of new items, i.e., homogenous products, for the purpose of auction ending price comparison. Our data shows that the average price for a new item is US\$140.12. Using De<sup>fi</sup>nition 5 from Table 1, a simple t-test indicates that new sellers (feedback scores ≤ 9) have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower auction ending price (US\$131.9) than that of the experienced sellers (t-value = −3.366, p-value b 0.001). Tests of other de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of new and experienced sellers all yield similar results.

## 4.3. Feedback ratings

The third goal for sellers with successful auctions is to receive positive feedback ratings instead of negative or neutral feedback from buyers. Receiving positive feedback is particularly important to new sellers. This is due to their relatively low overall feedback scores and each extra neutral or negative feedback received will signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the positive feedback percentage, resulting in poor seller image to buyers.

In eBay, the buyer has up to 90 days after an auction is completed to leave a feedback rating to the seller and vice versa. We found that 83% of feedback ratings received by sellers happen within the <sup>fi</sup>rst three weeks. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne positive (negative) feedback rate as the ratio of the number of positive (negative) feedback ratings to the total number of feedback ratings received by sellers. Our datasets show the general trend patterns of going upwards for positive feed back rate and downwards for negative feedback rate with seller experience. This indicates that sellers with lower feedback scores have a lower average positive feedback rate and higher average negative feedback rate. Using our de<sup>fi</sup>nitions from Table 1, z-tests conclude that new sellers have lower positive feedback rates and higher negative feedback rates than experienced sellers.

Besides positive and negative feedback ratings, buyers can also give neutral feedback ratings to sellers. We found that new sellers have a higher likelihood than experienced sellers to receive neutral feedback ratings from buyers. As discussed earlier, it is extremely important that new sellers avoid receiving either negative or neutral feedback ratings since this will considerably lower their positive feedback percentage and leave a bad impression to potential future buyers. In Section 6 we identify critical factors leading to the receiving of neutral and negative feedback ratings so that sellers, especially new ones, can pay close attention and try to shun away from triggering these factors.

From the above analyses we reject Hypothesis 1 and summarize auction performance differences between new and experienced sellers as follows:

• New sellers have a lower auction success rate.

• New sellers have a lower auction ending price.

• New sellers have a lower positive feedback rate. Correspondingly, new sellers have a higher negative feedback rate.

## 5.3. Explanation and conjecture on the structure difference

Based on the above analysis, we explain and conjecture that the structural differences in auction success and ending prices between new sellers and experienced sellers might result from two sources (see Fig. 3): 1) The same selling options used by new sellers and experienced sellers play a different role in auction listings because buyers have different perceptions on these options under different user types. For example, releasing more pictures by experienced users can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase auction success rate than by new sellers because buyers trust more in what experienced users release than new sellers. 2) New sellers might use different selling options from experienced sellers. New sellers are typically not expert in using some of the selling options, or they are restricted to use certain selling options. For example, new sellers seldom use Gift Wrap service. Another example, eBay only allows sellers with 10 feedback scores or more (or sellers are “ID Veri<sup>fi</sup>ed”), or sellers with a minimum feedback score of 5 accepting PayPal as a payment method, to use Buy-It-Now option. The implications of structural differences are as follows: 1) while new sellers need to learn from experienced users for auction listings, they need to realize that adopting selling strategies by simply cat-copying experienced users might not work. New sellers might formulate their own effective selling strategies. 2) New sellers or experienced sellers need to construct effective selling strategies for both auction success and ending prices. Because one selling option might play a positive role for auction success but negative role in auction prices, sellers should consider the overall effects of these options on their auction objectives. Regression analysis in this study provides a preliminary insight into relevant factors for auction success and ending prices. Data mining might help offer new and experienced sellers explicitly effective selling strategies, which is an extended study for this research.

## 6. Negative and neutral feedback ratings: Test of Hypotheses 3-1 and 3-2

In eBay, a seller's reputation is represented by the total number of feedback ratings she received during the life time of her membership. This reputation is dynamic in nature. That is, the number of feedback ratings will keep increasing as more auctions run by the same seller are completed and the buyers choose to give feedback ratings with regard to each completed transaction.

There are three kinds of feedback ratings a buyer can give to a seller: positive, negative, and neutral. Based on the ratio of all positive to the total number of feedback ratings, eBay also reveals the positive feedback percentage for a seller. This percentage directly tells how well a seller is perceived by all her buyers in past transactions. Receiving positive feedback from the buyer is deemed by a seller as a norm after the auction is ended. Only when the buyer is not completely satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the transaction will a neutral or negative feedback be given. A neutral or negative feedback will reduce the positive feedback percentage for the seller and this impact can be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant if the seller is relatively new with low overall feedback scores. In this section, we analyze critical factors contributing to the receiving of negative and neutral feedback ratings for sellers. This helps sellers better serve buyers and build better customer relationship.

## 6.1. Analysis of negative feedback

After carefully checking the comments for each negative feedback, we identify the top 15 reasons that buyers give negative feedback ratings to sellers. These 15 factors are ranked based on the frequency they appeared in the comments and are presented in Table 8 (for a complete list of all factors and their detailed explanations, see Table A-2 in the Appendix A). For example, late shipment is ranked 8th for overall and experienced sellers in receiving negative feedback ratings but ranked 11th for new sellers.

The order of the factor ranking essentially reveals, from serious to less so, the mistakes sellers made that caused the dissatisfaction of the buyers. This might help inexperienced new sellers to prioritize issues when dealing with buyers. We use two non-parametric tests: Mann–Whitney test and Wilcoxon test. ## 7. Summary and conclusions

This study compares auction performance, identi<sup>fi</sup>es auction success and price determinants, and discovers critical factors for negative and neutral feedback ratings between new and experienced sellers. The analysis shows that new sellers are in a disadvantageous situation compared to experienced sellers. They have lower auction success rates, lower auction ending prices, lower positive feedback rates, and higher negative feedback ratings, which imply that new sellers face more dif<sup>fi</sup>culties to establish themselves in the current online auction settings. We show the dynamics of these auction performances while new sellers evolve into experienced ones. That is, how their auction performances change with what pattern when new sellers evolve into experienced ones. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd structural differences in determinants of auction success and prices between new and experienced sellers. This means effective selling strategies for new sellers versus that for experienced sellers are not the same. Based on different auction success and price determinants, the online auction house can design speci<sup>fi</sup>c tools for each seller group. This is important because personalized services can better satisfy each group of sellers and make their auction listing more effective. Only when sellers can achieve better performance will they stay in the marketplace hence leading to continuous growth of the markets.

Our research also has important implications to sellers. Performance comparisons clearly show that new sellers are in disadvantage in the initial stage. As they gain more experience and establish reputation, performance tend to improve. The seller segmentation analysis reveals that different seller groups have different determinants on auction success and prices. This suggests that sellers need to correctly position themselves in electronic markets and dynamically adopt the most suitable and effective selling strategies. The feedback analysis sheds light on how the sellers can avoid negative or neutral feedback ratings to improve their seller image to prospect buyers. Based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this research, auction houses can also provide useful decision support systems for both buyers and sellers to achieve better customer satisfaction. Such DSS's will in turn invite more businesses from existing and potential buyers and sellers, and <sup>fi</sup>nally bene<sup>fi</sup>t the auction house as well.

As a pioneer research in seller heterogeneity, there are some limitations in this study. First, despite insights gained from the determinants of auction success and prices, and factors for negative and neutral feedback ratings, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant research efforts are still needed for effective seller recommendations in electronic marketplaces. Second, we only consider revenues (auction prices) but not costs associated with different selling strategies. For example, from the analysis we know that more photos (see Tables 3 and 5) will help auction succeed as well as increase auction prices. However, more photos implies higher costs for sellers since eBay only offers one free picture, and charges for additional ones. Finally, there are research extension opportunities from this study. We only use one product, Sony PlayStation 2 console, in this research. More items can be used to further validate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Incorporating revenues with costs and providing effective selling recommendations to sellers are also possible future research directions.
