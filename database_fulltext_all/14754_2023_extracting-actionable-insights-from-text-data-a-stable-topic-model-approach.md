# EXTRACTING ACTIONABLE INSIGHTS FROM TEXT DATA: A STABLE TOPIC MODEL APPROACH<sup>1</sup>

Yi Yang

Department of Information Systems, Business Statistics and Operations Management, School of Business and Management Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Sai Kung, HONG KONG {imyiyang@ust.hk}

Ramanath Subramanyam

Department of Business Administration, Gies College of Business University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, Champaign, IL, U.S.A. {rsubrama@illinois.edu}

Topic models are becoming a frequently employed tool in the empirical methods repertoire of information systems and management scholars. Given textual corpora, such as consumer reviews and online discussion forums, researchers and business practitioners often use topic modeling to either explore data in an unsupervised fashion or generate variables of interest for subsequent econometric analysis. However, one important concern stems from the fact that topic models can be notorious for their instability, i.e., the generated results could be inconsistent and irreproducible at different times, even on the same dataset. Therefore, researchers might arrive at potentially unreliable results regarding the theoretical relationships that they are testing or developing. In this paper, we attempt to highlight this problem and suggest a potential approach to addressing it. First, we empirically define and evaluate the stability problem of topic models using four textual datasets. Next, to alleviate the problem and with the goal of extracting actionable insights from textual data, we propose a new method, Stable LDA, which incorporates topical word clusters into the topic model to steer the model inference toward consistent results. We show that the proposed Stable LDA approach can significantly improve model stability while maintaining or even improving the topic model quality. Further, employing two case studies related to an online knowledge community and online consumer reviews, we demonstrate that the variables generated from Stable LDA can lead to more consistent estimations in econometric analyses. We believe that our work can further enhance management scholars’ collective toolkit to analyze ever-growing textual data.

Keywords: Topic modeling, stability, Stable LDA, text analysis, empirical analysis

## Introduction

Massive amounts of unstructured textual data generated across various business processes offer firms a valuable opportunity to gain business insights in a bottom-up manner. For instance, high volumes of consumer reviews on online platforms provide an unprecedented opportunity for firms to understand consumer word-of-mouth, forecast product sales, and monitor product defects. With the proliferation of such text data, machine learning techniques that can automatically uncover relevant themes, topics, and semantic relationships from such unstructured textual data have immediate relevance to business practitioners and management scholars alike. Among them, topic analysis using statistical topic modeling techniques has become popular among researchers due to its simplicity and unsupervised nature.

Topic modeling techniques, such as latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) (Blei et al., 2003), automatically extract latent topics from a set of documents. Such techniques assume that each document consists of a mixture of topics and that each topic is represented as a mixture of words. For example, a hotel review may talk about the “price” topic and “location” topic. We would expect to see the topic of price to be associated with words such as “affordable” and “overpriced” and the topic of location topic to be associated with words such as “station” and “downtown.”

There is currently an upward trend in applications of LDAbased topic modeling to uncover topics in text corpora (Rai, 2016). In a nutshell, academic scholars typically use LDA in two ways. First, they use it as a data exploration tool to quickly identify the distribution of topics in a document or text corpus. For example, Mankad et al. (2016) used LDA to discover the topics in a collection of consumer reviews from a hotel platform. Second, LDA is synergistically combined with qualitative or econometric methods to generate theory (Rai, 2016). For example, Khern-am-nuai et al. (2018) applied LDA to consumer reviews and analyzed differences in review topics when the online platform began offering monetary incentives for writing reviews. After performing topic modeling, they set up a regression model, where the dependent variable is the probability that a review is about video game topics. In such a context, LDA is used to derive independent or dependent variables of interest, which adds both theoretical and practical value to IS research (Shmueli & Koppius, 2011).

With the burgeoning use of such techniques, researchers and practitioners must address the problem that topic models are not stable and the results are often challenging to replicate. One reason for this is that LDA may produce different results, often even on the same dataset at different times, largely due to multiple possible local optima and the approximate inference techniques employed in model fitting. As mentioned earlier, topic models learn a mixture of words that can represent a topic and they represent each document as a mixture of topics. Hence, when LDA is applied to the same dataset at different times, the topic model may be unstable when viewed from two complementary perspectives. Outwardly, the top representative words for the same topic and the document’s topic label assignment may change across different runs. This extrinsic instability can directly affect the utility of LDA as a data exploration tool due to its unstable face outcomes. From an internal perspective, the underlying topic-word and document-topic probability mixtures are also likely to be different. This intrinsic instability can be more problematic because the instability can propagate to the dependent or independent variables that are generated from the probability mixtures. As a result, further analyses, including parameter estimation through subsequent regression analysis, will be unreliable, leading to high variations in the effect size, directionality, and statistical significance.

This instability problem of LDA can have severe consequences for researchers. First, the instability of topic models can undermine their usability and potentially decrease scholars’ trust in modeling outcomes. While there are several empirical standards that need to be met by empirical methods, the reproducibility criterion is considered essential for the repeatability of study outcomes and the progression of scholarly work (Nosek et al., 2015; Stodden et al., 2016). Second, scholars often employ topic modeling to derive dependent or independent variables of interest prior to econometric modeling. The presence of model instability implies that the generated variables may be unstable and result in measurement errors. As pointed out in Yang et al. (2018), the imperfect construction of independent or dependent variables will introduce systematic biases into coefficient estimates and result in spurious or incorrect estimates.

There are detrimental consequences for practitioners stemming from the instability problem of LDA. As text analysis and topic modeling techniques become increasingly popular in the field, stakeholders who use such tools will expect reproducible and consistent model results for actionable insights. Consider a service management scenario where a hospitality manager applies topic modeling on consumer reviews to examine the relationship between consumer satisfaction and various topics mentioned in the reviews. If she runs the topic model and finds that consumers tend to write about “experience” when they are satisfied, but then runs it again and finds that consumers tend to write about “experience” when they are dissatisfied, it will obviously be difficult to obtain actionable insights.

We believe that information systems (IS), marketing, management scholars, and practitioners are likely to have experienced and/or observed similar inconsistency problems when using LDA to analyze textual documents. Prior topic modeling literature has noted the instability issues of LDA and several mitigation approaches have been proposed (Belford et al., 2018; Koltcov et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2016). To our knowledge, there are two gaps that our work addresses. First, none of the prior work has demonstrated how the potential concern of unstable LDA can adversely affect the reproducibility and robustness of subsequent econometric analyses. Second, we demonstrate through our study that prior mitigation approaches might not be very effective due to their focus on extrinsic stability at the expense of intrinsic stability. Our work proposes an effective instability mitigation approach for topic modeling with demonstrated improvements in performance in terms of topic exploration and variable generation.

We submit that the solution to alleviate the instability problem is to provide some guidance on the LDA model so that the model inference can be better controlled. We propose the concept of topical word clusters, in which words that often cooccur in the same context belong to the same cluster and have similar topic distributions. Specifically, we obtain the topical word clusters by clustering word embeddings (Mikolov et al.,

2013). Word embeddings represent words as continuous vectors and capture the semantic and syntactic relationships among words. Further, we convert topical word clusters into a tree in which each cluster forms a subtree, and then incorporate this tree into the standard LDA model under a Dirichlet tree prior. The Dirichlet tree prior allows us to treat the topical word clusters as soft priors, meaning that we can still rely on the Bayesian learning framework to estimate the posterior distributions. The inference using Gibbs sampling with topical word initialization is efficient and effective. We name our proposed approach Stable LDA.

We evaluate the proposed Stable LDA using several realworld textual datasets and find that it can significantly improve stability, as captured by different stability metrics. Moreover, we show that this improved stability does not come at the cost of model performance, as measured by model fit and topic coherence. We further validate the effectiveness of Stable LDA in generating variables that result in more consistent econometric estimations than those generated from a standard LDA model. Specifically, we employ two case studies related to an online knowledge community and online consumer reviews and show that Stable LDA can mitigate inconsistent estimation issues. Even though the instability problem cannot be completely solved due to the stochastic nature of the topic model, we believe that our work can provide a strong foundation for robust data analytics. Our work has direct relevance and practical utility for IS and management researchers and practitioners, with our main contributions being twofold: First, we make a methodological contribution by proposing a new method, Stable LDA, to mitigate the topic model instability concern. This method can significantly improve model stability, while maintaining or even improving the topic model quality. Second, we raise awareness that variables derived from unstable topic model outcomes may result in inconsistent estimations in subsequent econometric analyses. We use two highly IS-relevant case studies to show that the proposed Stable LDA approach can mitigate such issues and provide robustness to subsequent econometric analyses. Further, we make our Stable LDA implementation publicly available in order to enable other scholars to replicate and improve our research and to encourage further research.<sup>2</sup>

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. First, we review related literature. Following the review, we conduct empirical analyses to examine the topic model instability issue. Next, we propose Stable LDA to mitigate this instability problem. We benchmark our method with other innovative LDA instability mitigation methods. We further conduct two empirical studies to demonstrate the utility of

Stable LDA in subsequent analysis. We conclude the paper with discussions and implications.

## Literature Review

In this section, we first review existing research in information systems, marketing, and management science that employs LDA topic modeling techniques. Second, we review prior efforts related to the assessment and mitigation of topic model instability. Finally, we review how the stability problem is studied in machine learning and econometric modeling literature.

## Topic Modeling in IS And Management Research

We have witnessed growing popularity in the usage of topic modeling techniques such as LDA to mine textual data across different areas of management research (Rai, 2016). For an overview of statistical topic modeling research, please see Blei (2012). In IS and marketing literature, the LDA model has been extensively used in the analysis of consumer reviews to extract topics and generate variables of interest (Abrahams et al., 2015; Büschken & Allenby, 2016; Khern-am-nuai et al., 2018; Lappas et al., 2016; Mankad et al., 2016; Puranam et al., 2017; Tirunillai & Tellis, 2014; Wang & Chaudhry, 2018). These studies have used LDA to either discover topics in consumer reviews or to derive variables of interest for further econometric analyses.

LDA-based topic models have also been adopted in management research and practice to analyze other forms of unstructured textual data, such as online forum discussions (Yue et al., 2019), search queries (Gong et al., 2018), information on mobile apps (He et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018), literature reviews (Larsen & Bong, 2016), research papers (Hankammer et al., 2016), patents (Kaplan & Vakili, 2015), tweets (Geva et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2016), company descriptions (Shi et al., 2016), organizational blogs (Guo et al., 2017), corporate reports (Bao & Datta, 2014; Dyer et al., 2017), corporate tax disclosures (Bozanic et al., 2017), and analyst reports (Huang et al., 2017), etc. Hannigan et al. (2019) provide an overview of applications of topic modeling in management research. Overall, LDA modeling has become integral to data analytics research, including for the generation and validation of theory, which adds both theoretical and practical value to research in information systems and management science.

## Topic Model Stability

The statistical inference process of a probabilistic topic model LDA can introduce instability. Prior work has studied the instability issue of topic models. Since LDA generates document-topic distributions and topic-word distributions, the instability issue of LDA manifests at the topic level and at the document level. Mantyla et al. (2018) studied topic level instability, Waal and Barnard (2008) studied document level instability respectively, and Chuang et al. (2015) developed a visualization tool to diagnose topic model instability. Miller and McCoy (2017) studied the alignment of unstable hierarchical topic models, while Su et al. (2015) examined how noise in the text corpus can affect the stability of topic models. However, studies in this line of work have not proposed strategies to mitigate instability. Furthermore, they have primarily focused on extrinsic instability, i.e., stability with respect to document topic labels or the topic’s most representative words. Additionally, they have not considered intrinsic instability, i.e., the document-topic distributions and topic-word distributions, which are essential properties that drive extrinsic instability.

Several recent studies have aimed to alleviate the instability issues via innovative LDA extensions. Yang et al. (2015) and Yang et al. (2016) proposed improving document label stability using document labels, wherein documents with similar probability distributions would be assigned with the same labels. However, document labels are less effective in the context of topic modeling since, by default, LDA assumes a mixture of topics in a document. Koltcov et al. (2016) proposed a regularization procedure to encourage words in a local context window to have similar probabilities. However, imposing similar probabilities is a strong intervention and may introduce noise (e.g., two words that are not supposed to be similar are forced to have similar topic probabilities simply because they co-occur in a local context window). Belford et al. (2018) proposed an ensemble approach to aggregate topic models and then applied matrix factorization to obtain the final model. While the ensemble of models can reduce uncertainty, the additional matrix factorization step in the ensemble model can introduce new instability concerns (Gabbolini et al., 2021).

In a departure from prior studies on mitigation of topic model instability, we propose incorporating topical word clusters generated from a word-embedding clustering procedure with a Dirichlet tree prior to stabilize the inference process of topic modeling. There are related studies that incorporate pairwise word associations into LDA with the intent to improve topicword coherence (Gao et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2015). However, these studies do not address the topic model instability issue. Both of these studies incorporate pairwise word associations, which can be problematic for model stability because it may introduce undesirable word associations due to pairwise transitivity (e.g., “cellphone” is correlated with “battery,” “battery” is correlated with “crime,” but “cellphone” may not necessarily be correlated with “crime”). In sum, while prior studies have incorporated information from word embeddings, they do not address topic model stability.

In summary, prior research investigating topic model instability does not account for intrinsic instability. Furthermore, this prior body of work does not examine the consequences of the stability of the LDA-derived variables for subsequent econometric analyses. We believe that this link is very important to the topic-modeling stream of work in IS and management research. Moreover, we propose an effective instability mitigation method and validate its effectiveness in topic exploration and variable generation. We summarize related work in Table 1 and highlight how our work differentiates from prior research.

## Model Stability in Machine Learning and Econometrics

In machine learning literature, the stability of a predictive model (e.g., a classification model such as a decision tree) is a measure of how robust the model is in detecting underlying patterns from different subsamples of the original dataset. According to computational learning theory (Angluin, 1992), it is generally expected that a stable learning algorithm can produce consistent outcomes and reliably generalize to unobserved data. Prior work has studied the stability of supervised models such as support vector machines (SVM) (Bousquet & Elisseef, 2002) and unsupervised models such as K-means clustering (Rakhlin & Caponnetto, 2007).

In econometrics, it has been noted that model stability is of crucial importance for inference and the interpretation of regression results (Hansen, 1992). In this domain, model stability is based on the extent to which estimated parameters remain stable across samples. Among other reasons, the rationale for this notion is that it is important in policy analysis to know whether econometric models are invariant to nominal shifts in possible policy interventions. Statistical techniques (e.g., Brown et al., 1975; Garbade, 1977; McCabe & Harrison, 1980; Page, 1955) have been developed to detect shifts (or change points) in a regression relationship. In such a context, the premise is that the underlying data generation process may shift and, as a result, techniques are developed to detect such change. In contrast, in machine learning and in our work, stability is studied with the assumption that the underlying data generation process remains constant, and it is the intrinsic property of learning algorithms that causes the model outcomes to be inconsistent.

## Empirical Evidence on Topic Model Instability

In this section, we first briefly review the basics of the LDA model. The same notations, concepts and procedures are referred to in the later sections.

## Topic Model and Stability Basics

Let us consider a textual corpus consisting of a set of documents $d \in \mathcal { D }$ . Each document is a sequence of n words $w _ { n }$ drawn from a vocabulary ?? of size ??. A common task in analyzing text documents is to extract key insights, such as which topics are frequently discussed in the whole dataset and which documents discuss which topics. Statistical topic modeling techniques fit this need well.

Statistical topic models such as LDA can derive latent topics from textual corpora in an unsupervised fashion. Topic models serve as dimension-reduction techniques to represent each document as a mixture of different topics. Specifically, LDA assumes that there is a set ?? of size ?? latent topics in a document collection and topics are modeled as latent variables $z _ { n } \in \mathcal { T }$ for each word $w _ { n }$ . The generative process of LDA defines $\theta _ { d } ,$ , the topic distribution for document $d ,$ as being drawn from a Dirichlet distribution, $\theta _ { d } \sim D i r ( \alpha )$ . In addition, $\phi _ { t }$ is the topic-specific word distribution, which is also drawn from a Dirichlet distribution, $\phi _ { t } \sim D i r ( \beta )$ ?? and $\beta$ are the hyperparameters for the two Dirichlet distributions. Model parameters in LDA are estimated using Bayesian inference Markov chain Monte Carlo (Gibbs sampling) or variational inference. The two probability distributions $\theta _ { d }$ and $\phi _ { t }$ help researchers determine which topics are frequently discussed in the whole dataset and which documents discuss which topics.

When employing LDA to extract document topics, the topic modeling results may vary over different runs. Specifically, for a textual corpus $D ,$ let us suppose that a researcher runs topic model LDA with the same parameter setting twice and obtains two models $M ^ { 1 }$ and $M ^ { 2 } .$ <sup>3</sup> Due to the exchangeability property of $\mathrm { L D A } , ^ { 4 }$ we need to perform topic model alignment to make sure that, for two different topic models $M ^ { 1 }$ and $M ^ { 2 }$ the topic indices are matched. We employ the Hungarian algorithm (Kuhn, 1955) to efficiently solve the alignment problem. The Hungarian algorithm is designed to find an optimal permutation of the topic index alignment to maximize overall topic compatibility. It has been used in prior topic modeling literature for topic matching (Belford et al., 2018; Boyd-Graber & Blei, 2009; Greene et al., 2014). We provide alignment details in Appendix A.

Prior work examining topic model stability primarily focuses on extrinsic instability, i.e., stability with respect to the topic’s most representative words or document topic labels. For topiclevel stability, the literature has proposed metrics to assess whether the top representative words change between two runs (Chuang et al., 2015; Koltcov et al., 2016; Mantyla et al., 2018). In particular, the Top10 word stability is calculated as the percentage of shared words in the top representative words list of each topic: $\begin{array} { r } { S _ { t o p i c } ^ { t o p w o r d s } = \frac { \sum _ { t } | T o p _ { t } ^ { 1 } \cap T o p _ { t } ^ { 2 } | } { 1 0 } } \end{array}$ , where $T o p _ { t }$ is the set of most representative 10 words, i.e., with the highest probability, of topic t. For document-level stability, it is often a common practice that users assign the topic with maximum probability to be the document label. Accordingly, prior literature has examined changes in the document label to quantify topic model extrinsic document stability (Belford et al., 2018; Waal & Barnard, 2008; Yang et al., 2016). ## Topic Model Intrinsic Stability

While prior studies have examined the stability issue of LDA, discussion has mostly centered around extrinsic stability, which is closely related to the data exploration outcomes of LDA. However, they do not consider intrinsic instability, i.e., document-topic distributions and topic-word distributions, which are essential properties that drive extrinsic instability. We believe that intrinsic stability directly measures the change of probability distributions and needs to be thoroughly examined. Furthermore, intrinsic stability is more closely related to the robustness and reproducibility of subsequent analysis because many IS and management studies rely on LDA probability distributions, rather than the top 10 words or document labels, to construct variables of interest. For example, Mankad et al. (2016) constructed a “topic” variable to indicate whether a certain topic is discussed in a consumer review. The variable is equal to 1 if the topic probability $\theta _ { i t }$ is greater than a threshold and 0 otherwise, and the threshold is determined as the median probability across all reviews and topics. Therefore, this “topic” variable will change if $\theta _ { d }$ changes over different runs. Also, Shi et al. (2016) constructed a “business proximity” variable as the cosine similarity between the topic distributions of two company reports. Therefore, the reproducibility of “business proximity” relies on the stability of topic distributions. To better evaluate topic model stability and connect it with the topic-modeling stream of work in IS and management research, we propose explicitly measuring the intrinsic stability, as follows.

For topic-level stability, if a topic model assigns highly contrasting probabilities to the same word under each topic over different runs on the same data, it will be perceived as unstable. ## Model Stability Experiments

We next empirically evaluate model stability using four textual datasets. A large and growing body of IS and management research has applied LDA to analyze online consumer reviews for the purpose of analyzing customer word-of-mouth. To represent such contexts, we collected two consumer review datasets. The first dataset contained online product reviews collected from Amazon.com. We obtained 19,068 consumer reviews across five Amazon product categories: beauty, fashion, appliances, gift cards, and software (Ni et al., 2019). The second dataset contained online restaurant reviews collected from Yelp.com.<sup>6</sup> The dataset contained 92,731 reviews from 1,000 unique businesses that had received at least three reviews.

In addition to consumer review datasets, IS researchers also have applied topic models to text data from online knowledge Q&A platforms (Liu et al., 2020; Peng et al., 2020). To represent these alternate research contexts, we collected a dataset pertaining to the online knowledge Q&A platform StackExchange.com. Specifically, we collected 18,749 questions and their corresponding 32,898 answers from the StackExchange gaming board, which is one of the largest discussion boards on the StackExchange website.<sup>7</sup> Lastly, we collected an additional text dataset that contained company descriptions. This last dataset helped us represent contexts where topic models are applied by management scholars to gauge the information content in firm-related documents and reports. This dataset contained 29,867 company descriptions collected from corresponding Wikipedia company pages (Qader et al., 2018).<sup>8</sup>

Details of the datasets are presented in Table 2. Documents in the Company Description dataset were substantially longer than those in the other three datasets. We believe that these four datasets are representative of text data commonly found in management research that exploits topic modeling. All four datasets were preprocessed using a standard tokenizer and stopword removal tool. We limited the vocabulary to 5,000 unique words because the choice of a very large vocabulary is likely to result in slow inference and poor topic generation performance (Boyd-Graber et al., 2014). Robustness tests using different vocabulary sizes are presented in Appendix B.

## Experiment

In the experiment, we used Gensim’s LDA open-source implementation (Rehurek & Sojka, 2010). We used a uniform symmetric ?? (document-topic density) with a value of $\frac { 1 . 0 } { T }$ and a uniform $\beta$ (topic-word density) with a value of 0.01. For each dataset, we set epochs to 10 with a default iteration of 50.

## Discussion

The results of our experiments reveal the severity of the instability problem in traditional LDA models. The two extrinsic measures, $S _ { t o p i c } ^ { t o p w o r d s }$ and $S _ { d o c } ^ { l a b e l }$ , exhibit troubling findings: we found that, on average, nearly half of highprobability words changed under each topic, as did the topic labels of documents. For instance, if we consider the Amazon review dataset, more than half of the top words changed $( S _ { t o p i c } ^ { t o p w o r d s } = 0 . 4 5 9 )$ , and labels changed for more than two thirds of the documents $( S _ { d o c } ^ { l a b e l } = 0 . 3 1 2 )$ .<sup>10</sup> The instability issues similarly manifest themselves in the intrinsic stability metrics, $S _ { t o p i c } ^ { p r o b }$ and $S _ { d o c } ^ { p r o b }$ . If we imagine a researcher attempting to apply LDA to generate variables for subsequent econometric analysis in such contexts, it would be simply impossible for the researcher to achieve reproducible and reliable results. Again, business practitioners will also be unable to obtain actionable insights from the results of such unstable models.

We surmise that local optima may be one important and likely reason behind this instability problem. In LDA, since posterior distributions of model parameters may be computationally intractable, approximate inference methods such as Gibbs sampling and variational inference are used. Further, since LDA is a non-convex model, different runs could converge to different local optima. Thus, even with the same input data, the same inference algorithm may produce different results in two separate runs. Although computationally challenging, some prior work has attempted to understand the likelihood landscapes of LDA models. Lancichinetti et al. (2015) implemented a highly specified and constructed synthetic dataset. Using this highly controlled synthetic dataset, they showed that the likelihood landscape of LDA is very “rough.” In other words, it is highly inefficient to optimize the likelihood blindly by starting from random initial conditions.

Overall, we empirically observe that topic model LDA, driven by the intrinsic instability in probability distributions, is not stable in producing consistent model results. This may lead to reproducibility issues and inconsistent estimation in data exploration and empirical research. In the following section, we propose a data-driven method to mitigate this problem.

## Data-Driven Approach for Enhancing Topic Model Stability

In order to improve the stability of LDA-based models, we theorize that it will be helpful to provide some guidance about where to optimize the model. For example, in extracting insights from consumer reviews on restaurants, it is expected that the “Italian food” topic may contain several correlated words such as “pizza,” “italian,” and “gelato.” Therefore, by providing such information as guidance and specifying that “pizza,” “italian,” and “gelato” should have similar topic distributions, we would enforce the guidance about where to search in the likelihood landscape. Therefore, we define the concept of the topical word cluster. In a topical word cluster, words should be semantically and syntactically similar and should conceptually be understood as being about a topic. For example, for restaurant consumer reviews, we can build a topical word cluster that includes words (??????????????, ??????????, ????????????). We expect that by providing such word clusters, prior information would help to provide guidance about where to optimize a topic model and thus enhance model reproducibility and stability.

Our data-driven approach for enhancing topic model stability is implemented in two steps. First, manually generating topical word clusters is expected to be time-consuming and may be impractical as researchers may not even be sure about which topics are discussed in the dataset and which words are correlated with a topic. Therefore, we propose a wordembedding-based data-driven technique that automatically generates sets of topical word clusters from the dataset. Second, topical word clusters are then used to initialize model inference and incorporated into the LDA model as must-link and cannot-link constraints so that the model optimization process is more controlled. We adopt a Dirichlet tree prior approach to incorporate topical word clusters. We now introduce the details of our two-step data-driven method.

## Topical Word Cluster Generation

Word embeddings have increasingly become an important building block in many text analysis tasks. The word embedding of each word token is a multidimensional vector. The multidimensional vectors for all words are automatically learned from a neural language model on a large textual corpus in an attempt to predict words, given their contextual words (Mikolov et al., 2013; Pennington et al., 2014). Therefore, word embeddings learned in this way are believed to capture both semantic and syntactic meanings among words (Mikolov et al., 2013). Therefore, we train word embeddings on the text dataset and treat words with similar representations in the embedding space as topical word clusters.

We used a K-means algorithm to find natural clusters among words. The approach and general intuition we employed to determine the value of K. First, if K is much larger than T (number of topics), then we will have numerous topical clusters, with each cluster representing a small number of words. This choice will result in a large number of cannot-link pairs between topical clusters, imposing overly strong constraints on model estimation and eventually resulting in poor model fit and possibly unstable learning. If K is equal to $T ,$ then all words will be associated with a topical cluster and used to build the Dirichlet prior. This choice will result in a large number of must-link pairs within each topical cluster, which could also be considered to be a strong constraint on model estimation, resulting in poor model fit. Through our experiments across different datasets, we found that a setting of K that exceeds T by a number ranging from 5-10 generates fairly consistent topic stability and good model fit.

We outline our topical word generation procedure as follows: First, we train a word-embedding model, such as word2vec’s CBOW (continuous bag-of-words) (Mikolov et al., 2013) on the collection of documents. As we show in Appendix D, other word embedding models, such as word2vec’s skip-gram and FastText (Joulin et al., 2017), can also be applied since the objectives of word embedding models are similar: words with semantic and syntactic similarity are close in the embedding space. Next, we find topical word clusters among words using a K-means algorithm.

## Incorporating Topical Word Clusters in LDA for Stability

Once we obtain topical word clusters which specify word correlations, we incorporate them into the LDA model. We denote the K topical word clusters as $C _ { i } , i \in \{ 1 , \ldots , K \}$ , where each cluster $C _ { i }$ contains $n _ { i }$ words, i.e., $w _ { i j } \in C _ { i } , j \in$ $\{ 1 , \ldots , n _ { i } \}$ . Our goal is to allow topical word clusters, to influence the values of the hidden topics $z ,$ and then indirectly influence and stabilize the topic-word distribution $\phi$ and document-topic distribution ??.

LDA assumes that the hidden topic of a word is independent of other hidden topics, given a document’s topic distribution ??. While this assumption facilitates computational efficiency, it sacrifices the rich correlations among words. In our context, the generation of topical word clusters implies that those words would have similar probabilities under the same topic.

Thus, we want to replace the default LDA Dirichlet prior with a specifically designed prior that captures such word clustering information. Let us assume that two words v and w belong to a particular topical cluster, implying a similar topic distribution, $\mathrm { i . e . , } p ( v | t ) \approx p ( w | t )$ . In contrast, if v and w are two words that do not appear in the same topical cluster, we can use the original LDA assumption that their hidden topics are independently drawn from the Dirichlet distribution. Note that two words not being in the same topical cluster does not necessarily mean that they are about different topics. Hence, we do not want to force the two words to have different topic distributions. Therefore, we adopted a Dirichlet tree prior approach (Dennis III, 1991) to model topical clusters.

The Dirichlet tree prior is a generalization of the Dirichlet prior that allows flexible control on the topic-word multinomial. Figure 2 illustrates the structure of a LDA Dirichlet prior (flat) and a Dirichlet tree prior (tree structure). The Dirichlet tree distribution is a tree with words as leaf nodes. In standard LDA, the Dirichlet prior is a flat tree with only one internal node (root node) with V leaf nodes, where V is the number of vocabulary (words). Therefore, there is only a one V-dimensional multinomial distribution for each topic, $\phi _ { t } .$ For a Dirichlet tree prior, given topical word clusters, we can construct a tree structure and refine the generative process for $\phi _ { t } .$ . In the tree, words that share topical similarity are connected to an intermediate node before they connect to the root node. Each subbranch represents a topical cluster and words would have similar probabilities in the subbranch. A word that does not belong to any topical cluster is directly connected to the root node.

The generative process of the Dirichlet tree, LDA’s topic-word distribution consists of two steps. In the first step, starting from the root node, we draw a multinomial distribution for all the child nodes of the root node $\phi _ { I } \sim D i r i c h l e t ( \beta )$ , where $i \in$ ?? denotes all child nodes of the root node. One can think of it as redistributing the probability mass 1 at root node to its child node by this multinomial. In the second step, for all the internal nodes, we draw another multinomial distribution for all the leaf node connections to the internal nodes $\phi _ { L } \sim D i r i c h l e t ( \eta )$ where $l \in L$ denotes all leaf nodes of the internal node, and ?? is a Dirichlet prior. The probability $\phi _ { t }$ of a word w is then the product of the multinomial parameters from the leaf to the root. Therefore, to generate a word token $w _ { d , n }$ (the $n ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ word in the $d ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ document) from topic t, we traverse a tree path $l _ { d , n } .$ , which is a list of nodes starting from the root node. We start at the root node as $l _ { d , n } [ 0 ]$ , and then select a child $l _ { d , n } [ 1 ] \sim \phi _ { I } ;$ if the child is already a leaf node, we generate the word associated with this leaf. If the child is not a leaf node, we select $l _ { d , n } [ 2 ] \sim \phi _ { L }$ , and then generate the leaf’s associated word. This walk along the Dirichlet tree, which we represent using the latent variable $l _ { d , n } ,$ replaces the process of directly drawing from a topic’s multinomial distribution over words.

Note that in the Dirichlet tree distribution, we choose Dirichlet parameters $\beta$ and ?? to encode word clusters. Since Dirichlet distributions produce sparse multinomials when their parameters are small (less than one), a smal $\beta$ would encourage the root node to prefer sparse distribution of probability among its children. A common practice is to set $\beta = 0 . 0 1$ which allows for a sparser multinomial distribution (Steyvers & Griffiths, 2007), i.e., each topic only has a concentrated mass on either one topical cluster or a few normal words. When the Dirichlet parameters are large (much greater than one), it favors more uniform distributions so that the words have similar probabilities. Hence, we choose a large ??.

As we established that choosing the Dirichlet Tree prior facilitates the encoding of word clusters, it becomes important to know how we build this Dirichlet tree structure. In fact, this process is straightforward given that we already have a set of exclusive topical word clusters, learned from the aforementioned K-means procedure. Starting from a flat tree structure, we replace all correlated words with a single new child node of the root and then detach all the children of this new node that are correlated words. Figure 2 shows an example of a Dirichlet tree where the words “pizza,” “Italian,” and “gelato” are attached to an internal node; the words “line,” “enter,” and “wait” are attached to another internal node; and the word “sunday” remains a direct child of the root node.

The joint probability of a Dirichlet Tree topic model parameterized on $\alpha , \beta , \eta$ is:

$$
\begin{array}{c} p (\mathbf {w}, \mathbf {z}, \mathbf {l}, \phi , \theta ; \alpha , \beta , \eta) = \prod_ {t} \prod_ {i} p (\phi_ {t, i} | \eta) \\ [ \prod_ {d} p (\theta_ {d} | \alpha) [ \prod_ {n} p (z _ {d, n} | \theta_ {d}) p (l _ {d, n} | \phi , z _ {d, n}) p (w _ {d, n} | l _ {d, n}) ] ], \end{array}
$$

where i is the internal node in the tree, $\phi _ { t , i }$ is the probability of the internal node i at topic $t , z _ { d , n }$ is the word $w _ { d , n } \mathrm { ~ : ~ }$ s hidden topic, $l _ { d , n }$ is the tree path leading to the leaf that contains word $w _ { d , n }$ , and $p ( l _ { d , n } | \phi , z _ { d , n } )$ is the probability of the path $l _ { d , n } .$

We use Gibbs sampling for posterior inference. One fundamental advantage of the Dirichlet tree model is that the Dirichlet tree is conjugate to the multinomial so that it facilitates straightforward Gibbs sampling. The detailed conditional distribution derivation is omitted here for brevity and can be inferred from Andrzejewski et al., (2009) and Dennis III (1991). In original Gibbs sampling, word topic assignments ?? are randomly initialized. However, this is problematic for Dirichlet tree learning, as two words belonging to the same topical cluster could have very different topic-word distributions after random initialization, which could contradict the word cluster information and therefore lead to large variance in the subsequent model inference. To address this concern, we initialize the Gibbs sampling topic assignment ?? so that words in the same topical word clusters have the same initial topics. The Dirichlet tree prior-based Gibbs sampling is then applied to further encourage words in the same topical cluster to have similar topic-word distributions. As we will demonstrate in the experiment, the Dirichlet tree prior not only stabilizes the topic model inference but could also show improvement in the dimensions of overall model quality, such as model fit and topic coherence.

In summary, we propose a data-driven technique to stabilize the topic model LDA. We theorize that word clusters can stabilize topic-word distributions and, in turn, improve document-topic and overall model stability. Specifically, we used word embedding models to generate a set of topical clusters within which words exhibit semantic and syntactic similarity. We encoded a Dirichlet tree structure based on the topical word clusters and replaced the Dirichlet prior in LDA with a Dirichlet tree prior. The model inference using a Dirichlet tree prior can be performed efficiently using Gibbs sampling with topical word cluster initialization.

Second, in terms of topic coherence, the Dirichlet tree prior encourages words within the same word embedding cluster to have similar topic distributions and therefore improves the words coherence of each topic. Combining the results in Table 4 and Table 5, we can see that the stability improvement of Stable LDA does not come at the cost of topic model quality. Since the most straightforward outputs of topic models are the highprobability words of each topic, high topic coherence along with topic-word stability can help researchers better understand topic model outcomes and examine the latent themes in a textual collection.

Sensitivity analysis: In topic models, a key parameter is the number of topics. The choice of the number of topics affects the model fit and topic coherence. We present Stable LDA’s performance when different topics are used in Table 6. We can see that for different datasets, setting the number of topics to 50 resulted in the lowest model fit. However, for Amazon, Yelp, and the StackExchange Q&A datasets, the topic coherences were at the highest when the number of topics was 25, instead of 50. This is consistent with prior research showing that model likelihood and topic coherence are often not correlated and sometimes even slightly anticorrelated (Mimno et al., 2011; Röder et al., 2015). Moreover, we can see from Table 6 that the model stability is quite consistent across different topics and they are all significantly higher than the stability measures of the benchmarked models.

We now examine the effect of ??, which is the strength of the Dirichlet prior in the Dirichlet tree distribution, on the model performance. A large value of ?? would generate a more concentrated multinomial distribution. Note that $\eta = 1$ indicates that the Gibbs sampling for the Stable LDA model is initialized with topical word clusters, but the subsequent inference still follows the standard LDA’s Dirichlet prior. Therefore, the ablation study ?? = 1 offers insights into the effectiveness of the Dirichlet tree prior. We varied the value of ?? from 1 to 5000 and present its effects on model stability using the Company Description dataset in Table 7. The results show that while initializing Gibbs sampling with topical word clusters did improve LDA’s stability, the stability can be further improved by imposing a Dirichlet tree prior on the model inference. For example, on the Company Description dataset, the intrinsic stability metrics $S _ { d o c } ^ { p r o \bar { b } }$ and $S _ { t o p i c } ^ { p r o b }$ are 0.706 and 0.723, respectively. This indeed already represents an improvement in model stability over the LDA model. When we use a Dirichlet tree prior and introduce a topical cluster, we can see that the intrinsic stability of $S _ { d o c } ^ { p r o b }$ and $S _ { t o p i c } ^ { p r o b }$ improves to 0.769 and 0.838 at $\eta = 1 0 0 0 .$ , respectively. However, continually increasing the strength of the Dirichlet tree prior does not further improve the model stability beyond a certain point. Instead, imposing an overly strong association may even reduce the model fit and topic coherence, as can be seen when $\eta = 5 0 0 0$ Therefore, this sensitivity study highlights the importance of using a Dirichlet tree prior to impose word clusters in order to stabilize topic model stochastic inference. In Appendix H, we provide practical guidance on Stable LDA’s hyperparameter choice.

Despite the evidence above, it should be noted that, due to the stochastic nature of topic models and the possible presence of many local optima, the instability problem cannot be completely avoided. Hence, our view is that Stable LDA can improve the stability issue without hurting the overall performance of the topic models. Moreover, awareness of the problem and knowledge about the severity of its consequences is critical for researchers. It could help us understand the potential risks of applying topic modeling to analyze unstructured textual data and further focus our efforts on improving the robustness and reproducibility of our conclusions.

In the second study, we examined the relationship between the numerical rating of an online review and its discussed topics.

## Empirical Study: Online Knowledge Community

Our first empirical study was conducted in an online Q&A knowledge community context. Online knowledge communities are important venues where a broader audience can seek help and advice from experts and knowledgeable participants through question-and-answer threads. Specifically, we examined the relationship between the textual similarity of questions and answers and the usefulness of the answer. There is a growing body of IS literature studying the usefulness of answers in online knowledge communities and how they can better serve users’ knowledge needs and bolster the success of online communities. For example, Peng et al. (2020) studied medical Q&A sites and examined the linguistic congruence between questions and answers. Liu et al. (2020) used LDA to derive textual features for predicting the usefulness of solutions.

We employed the StackExchange Q&A dataset for the empirical study. We measured question/answer textual similarity using standard LDA and Stable LDA, respectively.

The independent variable, QASimilarity, was measured as the cosine similarity between the topic features of the question and the topic features of the answer. This measure is analogous to the one used in Peng et al. (2020). The unit of analysis was a question/answer pair. Following Peng et al. (2020), we included several control variables—(1) Sequence, i.e., the number of answers posted prior to the focal answer; (2) QuestionHelpfulness, i.e., the number of votes a question receives; and (3) Words, i.e. the word-length of the answer. The dependent variable, AnswerHelpfulness, was the number of votes an answer receives.

Since the helpfulness variable was highly skewed, with many answers receiving a helpfulness score of zero, we incremented the helpfulness score by 1 and then took the logarithm of the score. As a robustness check, we coded another binary dependent variable: ??????????????????????????????????<sub>??????????</sub>, which was set to 1, if the solution received at least one vote for being helpful, and 0 otherwise. We estimated two models, as illustrated in the following equations: an ordinal least square regression model (OLS) and a logistic regression model (Logit).

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {OLS: AnswerHelpfulness = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1} QASimilarity +} \\ \beta_ {2} \text {Sequence+ \beta_{3} QuestionHelpfulness+} \\ \beta_ {4} \log (\text {Words}) + \epsilon , \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Logit: Logit(AnswerHelpfulness} _ {\text {dummy}}) = \beta_ {0} + \\ \beta_ {1} Q A S i m i l a r i t y + \beta_ {2} S e q u e n c e + \\ \beta_ {3} Q u e s t i o n H e l p f u l n e s s + \beta_ {4} \log (\text {Words}) + \epsilon , \end{array}
$$

To demonstrate how topic stability may affect our empirical estimation, we generated 10 runs of topic models, estimated the above OLS and Logit regression models, and then compared the effect size, directionality, and statistical significance of the QASimilarity variable, learned through LDA and the Stable LDA models. The comparisons are presented in Figure 3, which is the boxplot of effect size and p-value of the QASimilarity variable over the 10 runs. For the regression model based on LDA-derived measures, some coefficient estimates of the QASimilarity variable switched directions from positive to negative over the course of 10 runs. In contrast, the coefficient estimate of the QASimilarity variable in the Stable LDA regression (OLS) model was consistently in the same direction (positive value range of 0.1 to 0.2). Moreover, the positive correlation between QASimilarity and QuestionHelpfulness obtained from the Stable LDA regression is consistent with that reported in prior literature (Peng et al., 2020). We conducted a robustness check for this relationship, which we present in Appendix I.

In terms of statistical significance and the p-value, the QASimilarity variable in StableLDA/OLS and StableLDA/Logit was consistently and statistically significant in all 10 runs (p-value < 0.001). However, the p-values of the QASimilarity variable in the LDA/OLS and LDA/Logit model were not stable. In some runs, the variable was statistically significant (p-value < 0.001), while in other runs the variable did not turn out to be statistically significant (p-value > 0.1).

We wanted to verify whether the instability in LDA’s model estimation could be primarily attributed to the instability of QASimilarity, which is a measure of cosine similarity between the topic features of the question and that of the answer. To do so, we calculated the Pearson correlation between QASimilarity values in different runs. Since we ran the LDA model 10 times, we obtained 45 pairwise QASimilarity values. Similarly, we calculated the Pearson correlation between the QASimilarity variable derived from the Stable LDA model. We compared the Pearson correlations in Figure 4 and observed that QASimilarity correlations in different LDA runs averaged 0.550, while those in Stable LDA runs averaged 0.870. This difference clearly shows that the QASimilarity variable of each sample differed greatly over different LDA runs, which may cause instability in parameter estimates in subsequent empirical models.

It is apparent that the unstable model estimation can disrupt researchers’ empirical analyses. Given the prevalent instability issue associated with statistical topic modeling, the derived independent or dependent variables may also exhibit high variations. Therefore, we recommend that researchers report the descriptive statistics of topic model-derived variables (similar to those in Figure 4) as well as the effect sizes and p-values (similar to those in Figure 3) to ensure robust and reproducible empirical analysis.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Given the growing and widespread usage of topic modeling to automatically extract insights from text, a key concern is that such models are unstable since they may produce different results each time they are run, even for the same dataset. In this work, we first empirically validate the instability problem using four real-world textual collections that are representative of text data commonly used in IS and management research. The inconsistency and lack of reproducibility of standard topic models may pose concerns for management scholars as well as practitioners. To alleviate the problem, we propose a Stable LDA method that leverages word-association relationships to enhance the stability of the topic model. The proposed method is unsupervised, simple to use, and minimizes the overall effort of researchers.

We evaluated the performance of Stable LDA using four textual datasets and found promising improvements in model stability. Even though the stability issue cannot be fully solved due to the stochastic nature of topic models, our findings indicate that Stable LDA improves stability while maintaining model quality. Further, we present two empirical case studies and demonstrate that the variables derived from Stable LDA can achieve more consistent and robust estimations in comparison to variables derived from LDA. Since LDA is often used across social science disciplines, including management science, marketing, finance, accounting, political science, and so on, we hope that this work will provide a readily applicable method that benefits a broad base of scholars and practitioners across a wide variety of applications where topic models are adopted.

Our research has certain practical implications as well. In realworld settings, companies collect massive amounts of text data from multiple online platforms. Topic models are often used in such commercial contexts to extract insights from user-generated content. For example, consumer-facing companies have always had a keen interest in obtaining consumer feedback and in using that feedback to improve their marketing and operational efficiencies as well as their profitability. In practice, topic models have also contributed to innovations in applications such as recommendation systems (He et al., 2019) and online advertising (Wang et al., 2015). We believe that the stability of topic models is crucial for their adoption in practice as consistent and accurate results help deliver actionable insights to business practitioners.

Our study still has certain limitations that can benefit from future work. First, our proposed framework is based upon topic models using LDA. While LDA is undoubtedly the most widely used topic modeling tool in research and practice, there have been several topic modeling extensions as well. <sup>13</sup> For example, supervised topic modeling (Mcauliffe & Blei, 2008; Yang et al., 2022) can be used when there are labels associated with each document, and hierarchical topic modeling can infer topic hierarchies (Downey et al., 2015). While these LDA extensions focus on improving LDA with respect to model fit and predictive performance, our work takes a different stance by attempting to improve LDA’s usability through increased stability. In future work, it would be interesting to incorporate our data-driven approach with these extensions and test the stability of the resulting models. Second, while our study examines the topic-modeling stability issue on four textual datasets, we did consider datasets with short-form text, such as social media text on Twitter. Future work could extend our study by employing datasets of short-form text. Third, in this work, it was not our intent to create a theoretical foundation for the topic model stability problem. Our research was motivated by our firsthand experiences in using topic modeling analyses of textual data. Our conjecture is that approximate inferencebased methods are inherently unstable due to the presence of local optima in the model objective function. Future research would be needed to develop a comprehensive theoretical foundation underlying the stability performance of statistical topic models so that algorithm design for robust topic modeling could become more grounded.

We also wish to emphasize that the issues raised in this work are not an attempt to invalidate the results of any past work using topic modeling. We note that some existing work that has used LDA to derive variables of interest may not have been affected by the instability issue of LDA.<sup>14</sup> In conclusion, the goal of our work is to provide information systems and management scholars as well as practitioners with guidance on the implementation of topic modeling in as robust a manner as possible. Awareness of issues such as instability and the irreproducibility of results can limit the possible misuse of topic models. We are making our Stable LDA implementation publicly available to enable other scholars to replicate and improve our research and to encourage further research.

## Appendix A

## Topic Model Alignment

In this appendix, we discuss how we compute the permutation between two topic models and calculate the alignment for measuring stability. LDA assumes that words are generated from topics by fixed conditional distributions and that topics within a document are infinitely exchangeable. For example, if a corpus has four words with assigned LDA $\left( K = 3 \right)$ topics {0,2,1,2}, then it would be the same as the four words being assigned to topics {1,0,2,0} as it's only a permutation of the topic index. Due to the exchangeability of topics in statistical topic models, even two equivalent topic models with the same model parameters could have mismatched topic indices (e.g., for the same “food” topic in consumer review, its topic index could be $" 2 "$ in the first model and $\mathbf { \Delta } ^ { 6 6 } 5 ^ { 9 }$ in the second model). To facilitate the comparison of two topic models, we needed to align the topic indices in the two models. We used the Hungarian algorithm (Kuhn, 1955), an efficient optimization algorithm to solve the topic alignment problem. It finds an optimal permutation of the topic index alignment to maximize the overall topic compatibility. It has been used in prior topic modeling literature for the purpose of topic matching (Belford et al., 2018; Boyd-Graber and Blei, 2009; Greene et al., 2014). The topic alignment algorithm is described in Algorithm 1. The inputs to the algorithm are two topic models, $M ^ { 1 }$ and $M ^ { 2 }$ , and the output of the algorithm is a topic-index mapping that maximizes the overall topic compatibility. For example, if the two topic models $M ^ { 1 }$ and $M ^ { \hat { 2 } }$ are exactly the same, which assign the same topic labels to the documents, the topic index mapping would be $v =$ $[ 0 , 1 , \ldots , T ]$ , indicating the aligned topic index for $M ^ { 2 } \mathrm { ^ , }$ topic 0 is 0, the aligned topic index for $M ^ { 2 } \mathrm { { ^ , } }$ topic 1 is 1, and so on.

## Benchmark Model Parameters

In this appendix, we provide details of the benchmark models and parameters used in the experiments. Doc LDA (Yang et al., 2016) aims to improve topic model stability using document labels, wherein documents with similar probability distributions will be assigned the same labels. Following Yang et al. (2016), we set ?? as 1.0 and ?? as 0.01, and we set the document constraint strength parameter as 1.0. We ran Gibbs sampling for LDA for 50 epochs as the burn-in stage and then obtained the pseudo-document labels as the most similar document for all documents. Granulated LDA (Koltcov et al., 2016) aims to improve topic model stability using word correlation constraints. Specifically, in the Gibbs sampling process, Granulated LDA randomly samples a focal word, and assigns neighboring words within a window size to create the same topics. Thus, words that appear often together will be more likely to fall in the same topics, which may stabilize the inference process. We followed Koltcov et al. (2016) in choosing a window size from 1 to 3. A window size of 3 achieved the best result. We also chose a step window over a kernel window or triangular window as it produced the best results, which is consistent with the results reported in Koltcov et al. (2016). Ensemble LDA (Belford et al., 2018) aims to stabilize topic model stability by training and factorizing an ensemble of topic models. Following the setting in Belford et al. (2018), we trained 100 base LDA models.

## Appendix F

## Comparing Approaches that Incorporate Word Associations into LDA

Our proposed Stable LDA incorporates topical word clusters, represented as a Dirichlet tree, into the LDA model to mitigate instability issues. There are other approaches that incorporate word associations derived from word embeddings into the topic model. In this appendix, we compare Stable LDA against two approaches: MRF-LDA (Xie et al., 2015) and CRFTM (Gao et al., 2019). MRF-LDA builds a Markov random field (MRF) on the latent topic layer of LDA to encourage correlated words to share the same topic label. CRFTM is an MRF-LDA extension with adaption for short text. However, the objective of both approaches is to improve the model’s topic coherence, instead of mitigating topic model instability. Therefore, they rely on external word embeddings instead of domain-specific word embeddings to obtain word associations. Moreover, both approaches can only incorporate a pair of words while our Dirichlet tree prior can incorporate a cluster of words as associations. Incorporating pairwise word association can be problematic for model stability because it may introduce undesirable word associations due to pairwise transitivity (i.e., x is correlated with y, y is correlated with z, but x may not necessarily be correlated with z). The results are presented in Table F1. We can see that neither MRF-LDA nor CRFTM improves model stability. The results confirm the importance of using topical word clusters and a Dirichlet tree prior for mitigating LDA instability.

## Guidance on Stable LDA Hyperparameter Choice

In this appendix, we provide practical guidance on Stable LDA’s hyperparameter choice. First, like most topic models, the number of topics ?? needs to be determined prior to the training. Prior research on topic modeling has recommended determining this number using perplexity or topic coherence metrics (Boyd-Graber et al., 2014). We follow the same recommendation. Second, for the choice of the word embedding method, as shown in Appendix D, common word embedding approaches, including word2vec CBOW, skip-gram and FastText, work very well. Third, for the choice of the number of topic clusters ??, as we discuss in the paper, a large K will create a large number of cannot-link pairs between topical clusters and hence impose overly strong constraints on model estimation and result in poor model fit and possibly unstable learning. Along the same lines, a small K will create a large number of must-link pairs within each topical cluster, which would also strongly constrain the model estimation, and result in poor fit. In our experiments across different datasets, we find that a setting of K, which exceeds T by a number ranging from 5-10 generates fairly consistent topic stability and good model fit. In all our experiments, we incremented T by 10 and set that as the choice of K. Finally, for hyperparameters ?? , ??, we recommend a uniform symmetric ?? with a value of 1.0 and a uniform ?? (topic-word density) with a value of 0.01, and for hyperparameter ??, we recommend a Dirichlet tree prior value of 1,000.
