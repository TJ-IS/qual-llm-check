## a b s t r a c t

Recent years have seen a substantial growth of social computing, where large numbers of individual users share content with others in online communities. Social computing systems have thus led to a profusion of highly heterogeneous data, further exacerbating the traditional problems of knowledge sharing. This has made Meta-knowledge (knowledge about knowledge) important and more widely used, as it helps users locate knowledge easily. However, the reasons for people's meta-knowledge contribution in the social computing context and the extent to which this may differ from traditional knowledge contribution remain largely unexplored. This gap is addressed in the present study. Building on social capital theory, and using a combination of survey and independent system data, we explore what affects individual meta-knowledge contribution on Flickr, a popular photo-sharing service.

## 1. Introduction

Recent years have seen a substantial growth in social computing systems [22,36] that serve as intermediaries for social relations [46], and are characterized by online community formation and user content creation [12,36]. Some of the best known social computing systems are content sites such as Wikipedia. Flickr. YouTube, social networking sites such as MySpace and FaceBook, and social bookmarking services such as del.icio.us [28,36,37]. Overall, these social computing systems are characterized by a high heterogeneity of information sources and make large amounts of information available to their users [45,54]: Wikipedia, for example, has more that 2 million entries in English alone [34], and Flickr has more than six billion uploaded photos.

This accumulation of large amounts of heterogeneous information calls for the use of meta-knowledge – i.e. “the description of additional information about pieces of data stored in a database or knowledge base” [45] – in order to facilitate the organization and retrieval of this knowledge in the repository [30,45]. Thus, the rise of social computing in recent years has been supported by increasing use of user-contributed annotation in the form of tags [9,14,28,42], which convey knowledge about the content. Tags are keywords (e.g. “lighthouse”, “Christmas”, “California”) used to annotate various types of content, including images, bookmarks, blogs, and videos [42,49], and are a form of meta-knowledge that can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve the discovery, retrieval, and understanding of relevant knowledge from the repository [45]. As tags provide knowledge about available content [14,29], organizers of content repositories often let users categorize content and share knowledge about it by tagging [9,29]. The popularity of tagging is attributed, at least in part, to the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts users gain from effectively organizing and sharing very large amounts of information [9].

One prominent example of a tagging-intensive social computing system is Flickr, a photo sharing with over 51 million users [57]. Tagging in systems like Flickr is an important change in the way photos are organized and shared [49], as photos by themselves, despite their high information content, do not contain inherent information about what the photos represent, whereas the tags make the photos searchable by the uploading user, as well as by others [3].

While contribution of <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge has been studied, it is not clear how the results would apply to meta-knowledge contribution: when a person makes a <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge contribution by responding to a question posted on a bulletin board, or uploading a picture to a online photo-sharing community, the contribution is immediately visible and directly bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to other members; and the contributor has some level of certainty that her contribution will bring the reward she desired. Meta-knowledge, such as tagging, does not inherently bring any direct bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to its audience. Although the trends and patterns emerging from a large collection of metaknowledge in a community could be valuable information to certain parties, the immediate bene<sup>fi</sup>ts expected by the contributor of individual pieces are achieved only when used in combination with <sup>fi</sup>rst-order contribution. In addition, the effects of meta-knowledge contribution, while long-lasting, may not be as immediate as <sup>fi</sup>rstorder contribution. While the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge are clear to most members, how meta-knowledge contribution would bene<sup>fi</sup>t the contributors and others may not be clearly understandable to all members, especially novice members. For example, the content of a photo sharing web site such as Flickr bene<sup>fi</sup>ts considerably from users adding meta-knowledge via tags especially as the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge in this example is not easily amenable to searching.

Therefore, from both theoretical and practical perspectives, it is important to investigate the antecedents to meta-knowledge contribution. In the present study, we aim to extend the use of Social Capital theory to explain contribution of Meta-knowledge, rather than <sup>fi</sup>rst order knowledge. We draw on, and extend, recent work on the criticality of the structural, relational, and cognitive dimensions of social capital, as well as individual motivations as the starting point to investigate meta-knowledge contribution in online communities. Extending the qualitative work of [3], the present study contributes to the information systems literature on social capital by developing a framework in which the latter helps to understand a fast growing IS-facilitated phenomenon — social computing. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings make it possible to compare the relative strength of metaknowledge contribution’ antecedents, and suggest that metaknowledge contributors are primarily socially driven.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Meta-knowledge

A number of scholars have discussed the differences between knowledge, information and data (e.g., see [1,2,53]). Metaknowledge, as the pre<sup>fi</sup>x meta implies, is knowledge about knowledge. Although it is generally conceptualized as some form of second-order knowledge that exists at the level above <sup>fi</sup>rst-order domain knowledge, the manifestation of meta-knowledge varies from different perspectives. From the philosophical perspective, metaknowledge refers to “knowledge that deals with the very nature of knowledge and knowing” [35](pp 7). A more strict view of metaknowledge considers it as knowledge of (the existence of) knowledge, or “knowledge (awareness) about the knowledge you possess” [8] (p. 222); in other words — knowing that I have specific knowledge. This conceptualization of meta-knowledge, analogous to what Socrates considered as wisdom, dates back to Plato; and it has been shown to be critical to knowledge retrieval for individual human beings (e.g., [58]). Some researchers take the learning perspective, and consider meta-knowledge as knowledge of the ways to produce knowledge [35], in other words — knowing how to gain specific knowledge.

Another view of meta-knowledge, popular among computer scientists (e.g., [30,45]), considers it as knowing the relevant contextual information of <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge; in other words — knowing what specific knowledge is about. For example, while a picture of the Home Insurance Building at the turn of the 20th century is <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge, knowing this piece of knowledge is about “history”, “Chicago”, “architecture”, “<sup>fi</sup>rst skyscraper”, “steel frame”, and “William LeBaron Jenney” would be meta-knowledge. At the implementation level, this form of meta-knowledge can be codi<sup>fi</sup>ed, stored, and queried as metadata (e.g., [48]). For our purpose, we adopt this view of meta-knowledge, and as we will discuss in the next section, content annotations has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a powerful representation of this form of meta-knowledge.

## 2.2. Meta-knowledge in knowledge repositories

The advance in computer technologies has enabled large scale electronic knowledge repositories in the past few decades. Such knowledge repositories are often characterized by a high heterogeneity of information sources and large volume of knowledge pieces deposited [45]. In addition, as we have discussed in the introduction, the rising of the Internet and social computing services has enabled many Web based knowledge repositories with millions of users serving as individual contributors. For such electronic knowledge repositories, meta-knowledge, or “the description of additional information about pieces of data stored in a database or knowledge base” [45], could play a valuable role in facilitating the organization and retrieval of knowledge in the repository [30,45].

As the importance of meta-knowledge is being understood, metaknowledge management has become a critical aspect in designing electronic knowledge repositories. While some developers rely on computer algorithms to scan and annotate contents, especially, non-textual contents such as videos, audios, and images (e.g.,[4,17,31]), many have recognized user-contributed annotations (tags) as a form of meta-knowledge that can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve the discovery, retrieval, and understanding of relevant knowledge from the repository [45]. In fact, using annotations as a tool for communicating ideas and sharing knowledge was popular among scholars during the medieval times, and has seen renewed signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in computer supported collaborations [56]. Using the example from the previous section, a user who is studying the Chicago School of architecture would be much more likely to <sup>fi</sup>nd the picture as a relevant knowledge piece from the repository when it was properly annotated with “Chicago” and “architecture”; and the additional annotations such as “<sup>fi</sup>rst skyscraper”, “steel frame”, and “William LeBaron Jenney” would help her to better understand the relevance and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of this knowledge piece.

## 2.3. Outcome expectations, social capital, and knowledge contribution

Given the value of user generated meta-knowledge to an electronic knowledge repository, user motivation to contribute meta-knowledge is a vital yet underexplored issue. Sustained participation and contribution from individual members is critical for the viability of a social collectivity, especially, a computer mediated virtual community [10]. Research has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed individual beliefs that motivate contribution of <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge contributions in electronic networks of practice [10,27,55]. Consistent with the expectancy-value tradition of predicting human behavior, contributions generally follow individuals' expectations of favorable outcomes from their contributions, such as reputation enhancement in the professional community, or being helpful to others [55]. Chiu and colleagues [10] also suggest that individuals are motivated to contribute knowledge to a virtual community by their expectations for bene<sup>fi</sup>cial outcomes for themselves and the community. These expectations alone, however, do not fully capture the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the social network on user contribution in a virtual community. As [10] have pointed out: “why do individuals spend their valuable time and effort on sharing knowledge with members in virtual communities, should be addressed from the perspectives of both personal cognition and social network” (p. 1875).

As knowledge contribution is an action performed in social contexts, the construct of social capital has emerged as key to understanding individual knowledge sharing in recent literature [6,18]. Social capital refers to the collective resources emerging from and embedded inside a social network, as a result of the interconnected relationships of its members [32,40]. It represents the contacts and social ties members form over time, and the gratitude, respect, trust, and friendship they have toward each other. It is considered a resource critical to a social community's functioning and survival [32]. At the individual level, the connections and reputation a speci<sup>fi</sup>c person built within a social collectivity also lead to the social capital he or she acquired by being a member. Such individual level social capital represents the resources a speci<sup>fi</sup>c member can tap into and bene<sup>fi</sup>t from as a member. Therefore, [32] de<sup>fi</sup>ned social capital as “the sum of the actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit” (p. 243).

In their exploration of the role social capital plays in knowledge creation within a social collectivity, [32] further categorized social capital into three dimensions: structural dimension, cognitive dimension, and relational dimension. Structural dimension includes network ties, network con<sup>fi</sup>guration, and appropriate organization. Cognitive dimension includes shared code and language, and share narratives. Relational dimension includes trust, norms, obligations, and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Each individual element within these three dimensions is hypothesized to facilitate knowledge creation in a social collectivity through combination and exchange [32].

As we have discussed, the concept of social capital and its effects are salient at both the level of social collectivities (e.g., [52]) and the level of individual members. [32] presented the three dimensions of social capital with the aim to explain the advantages of <sup>fi</sup>rms over markets in knowledge creation. Therefore, their analysis focuses on how a social collectivity as a whole bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the social capital possessed by its members. However, knowledge creation in a social collectivity ultimately stem from the contributions of individual members. Not surprisingly, a number of studies have extended Nahapiet and Ghosal's seminal work [32] to the individual level and explored the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of different dimensions of social capital on knowledge contribution of members of digital communities. [55] found the structural capital of centrality to be the strongest predictor of the knowledge a member contributes to an electronic networks of practice; and cognitive capital measured as tenure in the <sup>fi</sup>eld predicted volume of contribution. [10] also empirically assessed and con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the three dimensions of social capital, independent of personal and community related outcome expectations, on knowledge contribution of members of a virtual community. Similarly, <sup>fi</sup>ndings from [43] suggest individual members of a digitally enabled team are more likely to communicate and exchange knowledge with higher level of social capital.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Building on prior literature about <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge contribution and studies on content annotation as meta-knowledge, we develop our conceptual model that explains individuals' contribution of meta-knowledge. In our model, we follow [55] and [10], and take into consideration the effects of two sets of factors that are salient in the context of meta-knowledge contribution: individuals' outcome expectations as motivations, and social capital factors. Fig. 1 presents our conceptual model and research hypotheses. In the following sections we explain the relationship with meta-knowledge contribution for each factor.

## 3.1. Outcome expectations

Individuals' contribution of meta-knowledge requires them to review the pieces of <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge, think about the contextual information relevant to these knowledge pieces, and attach the meta-knowledge elevated as a result of this process to the corresponding <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge. Organizational members are motivated to contribute knowledge by the expectation to capture some of the value created in the process [32]. In the context of online communities, [55] have found reputation enhancement to be the personal bene<sup>fi</sup>t that motivates contribution of <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge, and [10] have shown that expectation to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the community also motivates contribution. These results are consistent with Bandura's social cognitive theory [5], which postulate that individuals' behavior are determined in part by their expectations of the outcomes of the behavior.

Recently, researchers have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a number of individual expectations that promote meta-knowledge contribution in a nonwork setting. [3] showed that an individual's motivations for metaknowledge contribution depend on two different target audience: self, and other users of the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge.

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst two hypotheses are developed with the aim to provide further empirical assessment of the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of individuals' outcome expectations on meta-knowledge contribution. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, users contribute meta-knowledge partly due to some self-directed utilitarian needs — they want to use the service to organize their own knowledge better, or to be able to remind themselves of contextual details related to it [3]. Therefore, similar to what [10] demonstrated in <sup>fi</sup>rst order knowledge contribution, personal outcome expectations, or the expectations to bene<sup>fi</sup>t directly and personally from the contribution, would motivate individuals to contribute metaknowledge. We therefore hypothesize that

H1. Individuals who expect enhancement in their own search, retrieval, and understanding of first-order knowledge as the outcome of metaknowledge will contribute more to the repository.

While [3] also found the expectations to help other users' discovery and understanding to be a motivation for meta-knowledge contribution, such community related outcome expectations have been found to motivate knowledge contribution in online communities [10,24]. The reason these community related outcome expectations would motivate contribution can be explained from two perspectives. First, when users add meta-knowledge, they are making their content (<sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge) more likely to be found by other users. Therefore, while meta-knowledge contribution itself does not enhance a contributor's reputation as a content producer directly, it may help enhance the contributor's reputation indirectly by attracting more attention to his/her <sup>fi</sup>rst-order contribution. Such expectation for reputation enhancement could be a strong motivation for contribution in a virtual community, in both work and non-work settings [23,24,36,55]. Lending support to this argument, [3] found evidence that users add meta-knowledge in speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways so as to enhance the exposure of their contributions and draw attention to their content. Second, by making it easier for other users to organize and understand the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge, it may also satisfy the contributor's intrinsic desire to be helpful to other people. As discussed in [55], such enjoyment of helping others could be another source of individual motivation to contribute. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2. Individuals who expect enhancement in other members’ search, retrieval, and understanding of first-order knowledge as the outcome of meta-knowledge will contribute more to the repository.

## 3.2. Social capital

Social Capital “facilitates the development of intellectual capital by affecting the conditions necessary for exchange and combination to occur” ([32] p. 250). Building on Nahapiet and Ghoshal's [32] Social Capital framework, a number of studies examined the impact of social capital on <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge contribution [10,55]. Drawing on this approach, in what follows we lay out our hypotheses that the three dimensions of social capital are instrumental in explaining meta-knowledge contribution.

## 3.3. Structural dimension

Drawing on Granovetter's discussion [15] of structural and relational embeddedness, [32] view structural embeddedness as concerning the properties of the social system and of the network of relations as a whole, and describing the impersonal con<sup>fi</sup>guration of linkages between people or units. Network centrality – which refers to the number of ties an actor has to other actors – is a key construct in this regard [7]. Thus, the structural embeddedness of an actor in a network can be manifested in the actor's degree of centrality — for example, the number of software development projects in which a manager participates [16], and highly structurally embedded actors are more likely to cooperate and comply with group norms and expectations [44]. In line with this view, [52] argue that individuals located within a business unit with higher centrality are more likely to learn and adopt organizational languages, values and norms through the inter-unit social interactions, hence improving the alignment of their value and vision with the organization, and promoting inter-unit resource exchange.

Researchers have observed the same relation in social collectivities with digitally connected members. Robert et al., [43] found structural capital to be more important to knowledge exchange when members of a group are connected digitally, compared to members of groups relying on face-to-face communication. A number of studies have also found a positive effect of individuals’ structural embeddedness on their knowledge contribution and knowledge seeking in online professional networks of practice [6,10,55]. Most of the past research has focused on the criticality of structural embeddedness for <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge contribution in work-related settings. Our study explores the antecedents to meta-knowledge contribution in online communities, where the individual motivations could be different. Therefore, on the one hand, structural embeddedness may still play a similar role in stimulating meta-knowledge contribution; on the other hand individuals could contribute more metaknowledge online as a means to become more embedded in the community, suggesting a possible inverse relationship between embeddedness and contribution. In balance, we anticipate a positive relationship between structural embeddedness of actors and their meta-knowledge contribution. Hence,

H3. Individuals with higher structural embeddedness will contribute more meta-knowledge to the repository.

## 3.4. Relational dimension

In contrast with structural embeddedness, the term relational embeddedness refers to personal relationships such as respect and friendship that people have developed with each other through a history of interactions [15]. The emphasis in the relational dimension is on the quality of connection, or the tie strength. Strong ties are characterized by intimacy and trust [7,20,40]. Such relationships in<sup>fl</sup>uence people's behavior [32], and enable actors to be privy to information that is not available to others [7]. In the virtual community setting, facets of relational dimension such as trust and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation also facilitate knowledge sharing [10].

In an online community, having a voluntary dyadic relationship with other members, which exposes the members to each other's content, is an indicator – at least to some extent – of a relationship that is closer and more intimate than the relationship with any other member. Therefore, individuals who have many voluntary, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally designated, direct ties to others, which include exposure to the other's contributed content, indicate a high level of relational embeddedness. Therefore, we expect to see a positive effect of relational capital on meta-knowledge contribution:

H4. Individuals who have higher levels of relational embeddedness will contribute more meta-knowledge to the repository.

## 3.5. Cognitive dimension

Cognitive capital represents those resources that provide shared representations, interpretations and meanings among members of a social collective [32]. One important aspect of cognitive capital is shared language and code [32], which is an enabling condition for knowledge exchange between members. As [27] noted: “communication in an online community typically involves a large number of participants with different social backgrounds and perspectives. The establishment of mutual understanding to comprehend conversations and knowledge contribution is inevitably more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult than face-to-face communication in a small group…” ([27] p. 43).

For meta-knowledge, a contributor needs to effectively extract and convey the contextual meanings that are most relevant to the community from <sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge. Such ability depends on an understanding of common vocabulary used by other community members, and takes time and practice to master. According to [55], cognitive capital develops as the person interacts over time with others and thereby learns the skills, knowledge, specialized discourse, and norms of the practice. Thus, cognitive capital consists of individual expertise as well as experience with applying the expertise within a certain group. Thus, they argue that tenure in the shared practice can serve as a measure of a person's cognitive capital, and will be positively related to knowledge contribution. Drawing on this view, we hypothesize that the longer a user is a member of an online community, the more he or she will tend to contribute

H5. Individuals with longer tenure in an online community will contribute more meta-knowledge to the repository.

## 4. Research method

The setting chosen for this study is Flickr (www.<sup>fl</sup>ickr.com). Flickr is a prominent example of a social computing photo-sharing system [26,36,47] whose content is created, annotated and viewed by its users.

A Flickr user can upload photos (<sup>fi</sup>rst-order knowledge), and annotate photos using tags (meta-knowledge): these are unstructured textual labels such as “lighthouse” or “California”, that provide knowledge about the photos uploaded [14,28], and are mostly added by the user who uploaded the photos [28]. Users can tag their photos using photo editing applications or through their photo pages on the Flickr website. While the latter method may be more time consuming, it represents the preferred method for the majority of taggers. A Flickr user can also belong to and post photos to multiple user groups, which are normally formed around a common subject of interest (e.g., Trains, Brooklyn). In addition, a Flickr user can designate other users as contacts, people whose photos the user follows (contacts are often reciprocal). Hence, we believe that Flickr would be good setting to investigate the antecedents of meta-knowledge contribution.

To test the hypotheses proposed in the previous section, this study employs a large scale study of tagging on Flickr, using two independent sources: user-reported data (via a web-based survey) and Flickr system data about actual usage. Using system data is a suggested approach for avoiding common method bias, which often poses a methodological problem in interpreting results from survey studies [50], and can be avoided by measuring the dependent variable using objective data. In this study, users’ tagging data was retrieved from the Flickr system, and therefore common method bias should not arise in interpreting our results.

Since we were interested in tagging behavior, we only approached users who had used at least <sup>fi</sup>ve unique tags. This re<sup>fl</sup>ects our focus on returning users who view Flickr as a venue for ongoing photo sharing. By setting the threshold of <sup>fi</sup>ve unique tags, we wanted to ensure that we get responses from users whose tagging was not an isolated, unrepeated experience. The practice of limiting the sample of respondents to enable focus on the most relevant population is common in the IS literature [25,33,38,39]. We also only approached users who tagged in English, to ensure that respondents understand the survey questions. We contacted a random sample of users, selected from a page of photos uploaded recently to Flickr, and emailed 1373 users an invitation to participate in the web-based survey. A total of 269 valid responses were received, representing a 19.6% response rate. The average respondents’ age was 35.1, and 52.8% of the respondents were male.

4.1. System data: relational, structural embeddedness and meta-knowledge contribution

Actual usage information for the participants was retrieved directly from the Flickr data services. System data such as users' number of photos or tags is available via the Flickr system's Application Programming Interface (API). The Flickr API allows third party services to communicate with Flickr and access the user's data (often requiring the user's permission). At the end of our web-based survey, respondents were asked to authorize our web service to access their Flickr account information. This way, Flickr data about the respondents who logged in was automatically extracted and recorded together with the participant's response to the questionnaire.

Users who add other users as contacts, are essentially exposed to their contacts' photos in two ways: <sup>fi</sup>rst, the adding user's Flickr page includes thumbnails of the contacts' photos, and second, the adding user grants the contacts access to restricted photos which only the contacts can view. In this study, we operationalize structural embeddedness as the number of groups within the community an actor is a member of, and relational embeddedness as the number of contacts the actor possesses. Further, drawing on the literature on annotation and meta-knowledge sharing (e.g. [19,21], the number of unique tags each user has assigned to all photos is used as an indicator of meta-knowledge contribution to the community. Following [55], who argue that tenure in the shared practice can serve as a measure of a person's cognitive capital, respondents’ tenure (number of years they have been a Flickr user, which is available via the Flickr API) was used as a measure of cognitive capital. In addition, the total number of photos contributed, used as a control variable, was obtained through the Flickr API.

## 4.2. Perceptual measures: outcome expectations

To measure Flickr users' speci<sup>fi</sup>c self and community outcome expectations for tagging, we developed two measures based on Ames and Naaman's qualitative work [3]. As indicated in the hypotheses section, these constructs re<sup>fl</sup>ect an individual's expectations of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts his contributed meta-knowledge (tags) about a speci<sup>fi</sup>c object (photo) can bring to either himself or others. On one hand, a user may specify tags about photos so that he can search for and retrieve these photos at a later date with ease. She may also use tags so that these photos can be shared with others in the community. Without the presence of tags, these photos possess little informational content in order to be searched and found, and may be harder to understand. The items for these two constructs – self outcome expectation and community outcome expectation – are listed in Appendix 1. These survey items were measured using seven-point Likert scales, with 1 indicating ‘strongly disagree’ and 7 indicating ‘strongly agree’.

All coef<sup>fi</sup>- cients – measurement and structural – are reported in the standardized form.

## 6. Discussion

Given the growing interest among practitioners and researchers in meta-knowledge as means of facilitating the organization and sharing of large amounts of information [9], these communities may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from understanding what motivates users to contribute metaknowledge. The present study is a <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt, to the best of our knowledge, to address this question empirically and quantitatively in a social computing context.

## 6.1. Limitations

Before we present the implications of our study, we need to note that there are several limitations of this study. First, our study applies a cross-sectional survey design; therefore, we cannot address the causality among the variables in our model. Although theoretical arguments can be made for the causal relationships between the independent variables (individual motivations and social dimensions) and the dependent variable (meta-knowledge contribution), our data analysis only permits us to demonstrate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant correlations in those hypothesized links. Future research can address this limitation by conducting a longitudinal study that tests these relationships. Second, our study was conducted on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c social computing service – the Flickr online photo sharing community. Investigations of other types of social computing communities – bookmark tagging as in del.icio.us, video tagging as in youtube.com - can help verify the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. In addition, future studies would help distinguish between different methods for tagging such as through the Flickr photo pages or through speci<sup>fi</sup>c photo editing applications. Third, by focusing on users who uploaded photos to Flickr, users who uploaded more photos were more likely to be invited to take the survey. While the characteristics of respondents in this study were similar to characteristics of larger-scale samples of taggers in other studies [41], future research can help address this point by using other sampling methods. Future research can also be helpful in exploring the characteristics of non-taggers or users who only tagged once.

Further, it should be noted that while actual system usage data come with advantages in alleviating common method bias, the measures we use by necessity, are limited by the usage data available and capture only one possible operationalization among many. For example, we measure meta-knowledge contribution by tracking the number of unique tags a user has assigned to all his/her photos. While this captures the quantity of contribution, other perceptual measures that capture quality of contribution could add further explanatory power to the research model. Similarly, cognitive capital is a complex construct that attempts to capture shared representations, interpretations and meanings among members of a social collective. In this study, we use respondent's tenure (number of years the user has been a member of Flickr) as a proxy for cognitive capital. While actual system data helps reduce measurement error, this tradeoff also means that there is potential for future research to conceptualize and measure cognitive capital using richer operationalization.

## 6.2. Contributions

This study extends previous theories of knowledge contribution by adopting it to study the factors that drive users to contribute meta-knowledge. To accomplish this, our research model uses recent work on the criticality of the three dimensions of social capital (structural, relational, and cognitive) and individual motivations as the starting point [32,55]. It then extends this social capital framework, which has been previously used to study knowledge contribution in organizational setting, to investigate meta-knowledge contribution in online social communities. Based on the qualitative work of [3], our research framework further develops appropriate set of individual level motivations that make sense in the meta-knowledge contribution context.

A strength of the study is the use of data from multiple, independent sources. The data included survey responses as well as system log data retrieved directly through the Flickr API, to measure the dependent variable. This enabled us to overcome potential common method bias [50].

The study's <sup>fi</sup>ndings have several implications for theory and practice: <sup>fi</sup>rst, the study contributes to the body of information systems literature on social capital by developing a framework in which the latter helps to understand a new IS-facilitated phenomenon – social computing – and explain users’ perceptions and behaviors in this context. The concept of meta-knowledge has been studied in the form of computer generated metadata for large digital repositories, and its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in facilitating content discovery and retrieval is well accepted. Social computing services, on the other hand, enable individual users to be contributors and consumers of both content and metaknowledge. Therefore, researchers also need to take a user-centric approach in understanding the dynamics of meta-knowledge generation and consumption in social computing environments. In this study we illustrated the role of social capital and individual motivations in user contribution of meta-knowledge. This understanding may serve as a useful <sup>fi</sup>rst step for further research of social computing phenomena.

Second, the study builds on insights identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in qualitative research [3], and extends them and enhances their generalizability, using quantitative methods, thus bene<sup>fi</sup>ting from both qualitative and quantitative perspectives. The quantitative approach in our study enables us to compare the relative strength of each antecedents of meta-knowledge contribution. We found the structural dimension of social capital, followed by motivation to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the public, to be the strongest predictors of contribution; while self motivation and cognitive dimension have least predicting power. Our results suggest that users of social computing systems are encouraged to contribute meta-knowledge mostly by the social aspects of these systems. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is particular interesting considering the fact that, compared to contributions of <sup>fi</sup>rst order knowledge or content, contributions in meta-knowledge are much less visible to other members.

In terms of implications for practice, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the present study suggest that, as expected, both individual level motivations and the social capital dimensions affect users’ meta-knowledge contribution (as measured by users' tagging level). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings therefore have implications for managers and organizers of social computing initiatives: it is advised that managers of collaborative content systems seeking to increase tagging activity focus their communication and marketing efforts on those factors that have a strong impact on the level of tagging. For example, the motivation of tagging photos for other users has a positive effect on tagging level. Therefore, organizers and managers of social computing systems are recommended to focus their marketing and user cultivation efforts in this area, by highlighting the possibility of being exposed to other, unknown users.

Two of the three Social Capital dimensions were found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with tagging level. This highlights the importance of relational embeddedness and even more so, of structural embeddedness. Therefore, further opportunities for social interaction are essential and the inclusion of other avenues for social interaction as part of the design of such systems need to be encouraged. Further, beyond the systems’ design, social interaction can be increased by encouraging users to use existing social interaction avenues.

While the research model developed in the present study investigates social tagging systems in personal, non-work setting, the model can be adapted to study what motivates people to contribute metaknowledge in organizational settings, by developing an appropriate theory for individual motivations and organizational settings in that context.

Further research may also be helpful in understanding how differ ent motivations in<sup>fl</sup>uence contribution in different content sharing systems. In addition, future research can help to explore the interrelationships among the three social capital dimensions, which was regarded as an important area for future research by [32]. Another avenue for further exploration is the examination of the strength of social ties [10,11] in social computing initiatives, and its role in explaining meta-knowledge contribution.

Social computing is a fast growing phenomenon, which has been, to date, largely under-explored. The present study, addressing one of the prominent social computing systems, is hopefully a useful step in this direction.
