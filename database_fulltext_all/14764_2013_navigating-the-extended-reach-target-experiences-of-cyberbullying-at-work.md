## a b s t r a c t

With research on workplace bullying having so far focused on faceto-face interactions via proximate physical presence, cyberbullying remains to be understood. Through a hermeneutic phenomenological inquiry of target experiences in India's IT and ITES-BPO sector, the paper not only addresses this gap but also highlights the distinctive features of workplace cyberbullying. The core theme of ‘navigating the extended reach’ which subsumes the major themes of ‘being pursued, receiving a settled score, feeling “haunted” and “hemmed in” and drawing advantage’ underscores the boundaryless, concrete, permanent, invisible and anonymous character of cyberbullying, which sometimes provides footprints and proof through which redressal and resolution are facilitated. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings emphasize the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of particular intervention strategies to address the special nature of traditional and cyber bullying at work.

## 1. Introduction

Contemporary workplaces are technologically anchored and driven. Technology is integral to organizational success and various advantages in ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, productivity, innovation, etc., accrue as a result. Technological developments not only augment organizational competitiveness but remain integral to organizational effectiveness and a greater inter-dependency can be expected over time particularly with technological improvements. Yet, concomitant with this are misuses of technology at individual, group and organizational levels, which have varying effects in terms of nature and degree of advantages and/or disadvantages for employees and employers at micro and macro levels within and outside the organization. Cyberslacking (alternatively termed as cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng or cyberbludging) at work, where the internet is used for non-work purposes during work time, is the most commonly researched misuse (Weatherbee, 2010).

Among technological developments are information and communication technologies and devices (ICTDs) which are an important communication resource for organizations and contribute to their functioning both in terms of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness. While the ubiquitous nature of ICTDs has proved to be of great bene<sup>fi</sup>t to the productivity of modern enterprises, the nefarious aspects of hi-tech communications have not escaped the workplace (Baruch, 2005; Piotrowski, 2012; Privitera & Campbell, 2009). Cyberbullying (also termed cyberabuse, cyberharassment, online bullying, online harassment, online abuse, electronic bullying, electronic harassment and electronic abuse) refers to inappropriate, unwanted, negative, hostile and aggressive behaviors that are displayed, sometimes publicly, by (a) perpetrator(s) through electronic means via online or wireless information and communication technologies and devices to (a) target(s) and that intimidate and harm the latter (Adapted from Piotrowski, 2012; Privitera & Campbell, 2009). Indeed, given the unavoidable reliance of workplaces on these technologies and devices, escape from such negative fall-outs for members of the workforce is impossible — on the contrary, such experiences can be expected to increase. As Borstoff, Graham and Marker (2007) assert, cyberbullying is emerging as the preferred form of harassment among employees in spite of the safeguard of organizational anti-bullying policies (Piotrowski, 2012).

Literature on cyberbullying at work is sparse (Lutgen-Sandvik & Tracy, 2012; Piotrowski, 2012; Privitera & Campbell, 2009) — not only is much of the literature on cyberbullying centered on children and adolescence particularly in relation to the school context (Privitera & Campbell, 2009), but workplace cyberbullying is much less understood even in contrast to the burgeoning literature on traditional workplace bullying where the focus is on face-to-face interactions (Lutgen-Sandvik & Tracy, 2012; Piotrowski, 2012). Baruch (2005) and Privitera and Campbell (2009) are two exceptions here. Baruch concluded that medium of communication makes no difference to the experience of bullying. Baruch's (2005) inquiry, conducted among employees of an MNC (multinational corporation) in the UK (United Kingdom), found bullying via email (notwithstanding the proof it provides) to be at the same level as bullying via traditional face-to-face contact, meetings, letters and phone conversations such that its negative individual and organizational outcomes like reduced job satisfaction, lower performance, increased absenteeism and higher intention to leave existed regardless of the mode involved. Privitera and Campbell (2009) demonstrated the concomitance of traditional and cyber bullying at work. Their study of workplace bullying among members of the Australian Manufacturing Workers' Union based in Queensland found 34% of the all male sample to have experienced traditional bullying with 1/3 of these respondents (who formed the 11% of the total sample reporting cyberbullying) also having been bullied through the use of ICTDs, namely, land and mobile phone conversations and emails.

Given the limited insights provided by the two aforementioned inquiries, there is a need to uncover the dynamics of this significant and growing workplace problem because the mediation of ICTDs brings into play, in the workplace bullying situation, unique characteristics which require to be understood if the phenomenon is to be effectively addressed. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the present study explores the essence of cyberbullying at work as experienced by targets, focusing on manifestations, sources, etiology and outcomes. In so doing, it endeavors to highlight the distinctive features of cyberbullying, with a view to deepening our insights into and providing bases to manage emerging developments in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of workplace bullying.

Following a brief description of traditional bullying at work (Section 2), the methods (Section 3) adopted by and <sup>fi</sup>ndings (Section 4) emerging from the study are presented and later discussed (Section 5).

## 2. Traditional bullying at work

Our understanding of workplace bullying so far stems essentially from research on face-to-face bullying (henceforth also referred to as traditional bullying in this paper) and covers basic features, sources, etiology, outcomes and levels of analysis. Workplace bullying, alternatively labeled workplace emotional abuse or workplace harassment, is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as subtle and/or obvious negative behaviors embodying aggression, hostility, intimidation and harm, generally characterized by repetition and persistence, displayed by an individual and/or group to another individual and/or group at work, privately and/or publicly, in the context of an existing or evolving unequal power relationship (Adapted from Einarsen, Hoel, Zapf, & Cooper, 2011; Hoel & Beale, 2006; Tracy, Lutgen-Sandvik, & Alberts, 2006). Conceptualized as a workplace social stressor that results in targets' distress and often victimization and trauma (Hogh, Mikkelesen, & Hansen, 2011; Nielsen & Einarsen, 2012), workplace bullying entails unwelcome and abusive psychological and emotional behaviors (see Einarsen, 2000; Einarsen et al., 2011 for typologies) enacted in workplaces via face-to-face interactions through proximate physical presence. Person-related bullying consists of behaviors such as making insulting remarks, excessive teasing, spreading gossip or rumors, persistent criticism, intimidation and threats. Task-related bullying includes behaviors such as giving unreasonable deadlines or unmanageable workloads, excessive monitoring of work, or assigning meaningless tasks or even no tasks (Einarsen & Hoel, 2001). While downwards bullying (from superiors to subordinates) is most common, horizontal bullying (between peers), upwards bullying (from subordinates to superiors) and cross-level co-bullying (where peers and/or subordinates join superiors) are also reported (D'Cruz & Rayner, 2012). Yet, regardless of the formal organizational relationship, bullying contributes to the growing powerlessness of the target who over time perceives himself/herself as having little or no recourse (Einarsen et al., 2011).

Etiologically, workplace bullying is attributed to characteristics of the individual protagonists, namely, bullies and targets (Zapf & Einarsen, 2011), and to features of work organizations (Salin & Hoel, 2011). Bullies engage in abusive behaviors to protect their self-esteem, due to lack of social skills and as micropolitical behavior whereas targets experience harassment on account of personality factors, social skills and group dynamics (Zapf & Einarsen, 2011). Organizational antecedents include organizational culture and climate, leadership, job design and work organization and organizational change (Salin & Hoel, 2011) and these operate either within the work-environment hypothesis where situational factors give rise to bullying behavior between individuals (Salin & Hoel, 2011) or within the ‘organization-as-bully’ conceptualization where bullying is embedded in organizational design elements (D'Cruz, 2012). Indeed, bullying could be predatory (being triggered by the bully's inadequacies) and/or dispute-related (resulting from differences and con<sup>fl</sup>icts between the bully and the target) (Einarsen et al., 2011).

Whereas bullying has conventionally encompassed an interpersonal rather than an organizational level of analysis (Einarsen et al., 2011), the latter view is being increasingly acknowledged currently (D'Cruz, 2012). In the former instance where one party systematically singles out and targets another party, bullying is personalized and emphasizes a sociorelational conceptualization (Keashly & Harvey, 2006). In the latter instance where the constant and pervasive use of oppressive and abusive organizational practices and procedures by employers and managers leads all employees of the particular organization to feel victimized, bullying is institutionalized and depersonalized (Einarsen et al., 2011; Liefooghe & Mackenzie-Davey, 2001), invoking a sociostructural conceptualization (Keashly & Harvey, 2006). In terms of organizational antecedents, the work-environment hypothesis coheres with the interpersonal, sociorelational conceptualization while the organization-as-bully is consistent with the depersonalized, sociostructural conceptualization (D'Cruz & Noronha, 2009). The coexistence of interpersonal and depersonalized bullying, known as compounded bullying, has also been reported (D'Cruz, Noronha, & Beale, 2012).

Though workplace bullying is considered distinctive from harassment arising from membership to social categories such as gender, race, sexuality, etc., this point is contentious with some researchers seeking to distinguish bullying from various recognized forms of harassment while others argue that these are closely linked and hence cannot be differentiated to the point of emphasizing one and excluding or trivializing the rest (Lutgen-Sandvik, 2005). Nonetheless, in viewing the intersections between different forms of harassment and bullying at work, it is important to recognize that while both phenomena can be jointly enacted, they should not be con<sup>fl</sup>ated to obscure their distinctive features and undermine their individual speci<sup>fi</sup>city, visibility and need for action (Lee, 2001).

Most research in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of workplace bullying has examined the interpersonal dimension, exploring target experiences, with limited attention to bullies, bystanders and organizations and to depersonalized bullying. The extant literature here underscores the severe strain that targets experience, manifested physically, emotionally and behaviorally and indicative of poor health and decreased well-being (Nielsen & Einarsen, 2012). Low self-esteem, poor self-con<sup>fi</sup>dence, self-hatred, sleep problems, anxiety, anger, depression, nervousness, insecurity, suspicion, bitterness, concentration dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, chronic fatigue and various somatic problems as well as suicidal thoughts are commonly reported (Hogh et al., 2011). By and large, targets are unable to successfully apply problem-focused coping strategies to ameliorate or resolve the situation and end up opting for emotion-focused, passive and avoidant strategies, particularly that of quitting the employer organization and looking for new positions (D'Cruz & Noronha, 2010a,b).

The limited available literature on depersonalized bullying highlights ambivalence as targets' response. Here, well-being and strain, especially physical strain, coexist as targets juxtapose the gains from their jobs against the demands. Yet, recognizing that workforce participation is inevitable and that most contemporary workplaces are characterized by depersonalized bullying, the adoption of emotion-focused coping strategies helps targets work through their ambivalence. Favoring the approach dimension which underscores the positive aspect of ambivalence and its congruence with their values, motives and goals helps targets reconcile with their dualistic positions and collude in their own abuse till such time as a better opportunity arises. Individualized routine resistance could provide some release from the oppressive work environment (D'Cruz & Noronha, 2009, 2012; Noronha & D'Cruz, 2013).

The present research seeks to extend our understanding of workplace bullying by exploring the manifestations, sources, etiology, outcomes and levels of analysis associated with cyberbullying at work, through an empirical study of target experiences within India's IT (information technology) and ITES-BPO (IT-enabled services-business process outsourcing) sectors. Through this endeavor, not only would the singular characteristics of workplace cyberbullying be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed but also some parity in our insights of traditional bullying and cyberbullying at work be achieved.

## 3. Method

The study explored targets' experiences of workplace cyberbullying in India's IT and ITES-BPO sectors. Adopting Van Manen's (1998) hermeneutic phenomenology as its strategy, the research purported to grasp the essential structure of the meaning of participants' experiences as they were lived (Van Manen, 1998). Phenomenology derives from the Greek word 'phenomenon' which means to show itself, to put into light or to manifest something that can become visible in itself (Heidegger, as cited in Ray, 1994) According to Bishop and Scudder (1991), 'phenomenology attempts to disclose the essential meaning of human endeavors'(p. 5). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the inquiry aimed at grasping the basic structure of participants' lived experiences. This re<sup>fl</sup>ected Van Manen's (1998) hermeneutic phenomenology which studies the world as it is experienced pre-re<sup>fl</sup>ectively rather than as it is conceptualized, focusing on the structure of meaning of the experience for the individual, and hence this approach was adopted. Van Manen (1998) portrays the methodical structure of phenomenology as a dynamic interplay between 6 research activities. According to him, the researcher turns to a phenomenon which seriously interests him/ her and commits himself/herself to this abiding concern. The single-mindedness of purpose results in full thinking and deep questioning, so that we can understand life wholly. The experience is investigated as it is lived rather than as it is conceptualized. In other words, the attempt is to renew contact with the original experience and to become full of it. The researcher then re<sup>fl</sup>ects on the essential themes that characterize the phenomenon. A true re<sup>fl</sup>ection on lived experience is a thoughtful grasping of what renders it special. The fourth activity is describing the experience and its essence through the art of writing and rewriting. Language and thought need to be applied to lived experience such that a precise depiction is made. In order to achieve all of this, the researcher must ensure a strong orientation to the fundamental question so as to maintain direction and to come out with valid <sup>fi</sup>ndings. He/she also needs to balance the research context by considering parts and wholes, that is, one should constantly measure the overall design of the study against the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance that the parts must play in the total structure.

Being a qualitative approach, hermeneutic phenomenology is well-suited to the study of sensitive topics that involve personal and painful participant disclosures, heightened participant vulnerability and appropriate researcher empathy (Jehn & Jonsen, 2010). Of the numerous protagonists within a workplace bullying scenario, targets were selected as the focus of the inquiry given both their easier availability relative to other stakeholders (Jenkins, Zapf, Wine<sup>fi</sup>eld, & Sarris, 2012) and the nascent stage of research on workplace cyberbullying such that target experiences by themselves would provide rich insights. Sectoral choice was made based on employee familiarity with, access to and use of ICTDs which are more strongly associated with new economy industries such as IT and ITES-BPO and present at all levels of the organizational hierarchy here as compared to old economy industries such as manufacturing and logistics.

Based on previous experience of studying sensitive topics where organizational access is denied, the researchers posted the research call on various group sites of IT and ITES-BPO employees as well as searched the internet for blogs, personal testimonies, etc., of cyberbullied IT and ITES-BPO employees whom they then contacted. Through these efforts, 114 IT and ITES-BPO employees from all over India responded. Based on initial discussions, 49 were screened out as their experiences did not fall under the purview of cyberbullying. Of the remaining 65, 43 initially agreed to participate but 17 later dropped out due to lack of interest and lack of time. The study was therefore completed with 26 people based in 9 Indian cities.

Van Manen's (1998) conversational interview was used to explore and gather experiential narrative material that would serve as a resource for developing a richer and deeper understanding of the phenomenon being studied. Though unstructured, the process was disciplined by focusing on the fundamental question that prompted the research. The clarity of the research question did not preclude exploring issues that emerged during the interview, since the researcher was aware that they could generate important insights into the phenomenon under study. The mandate of the interview was to capture participants' immediate pre-re<sup>fl</sup>ective consciousness as self-given awareness that belongs to and is possessed by him/her rather than as something that is perceived or represented or exists apart from the self. In this manner, the researchers explored participants' original experience in its full richness, depth and totality. Participants responding to the research call were spread all over India and hence telephonic interviews were conducted. Interviews, held as per the convenience of the participant, were generally 1 to 1.5 h long, conducted by the <sup>fi</sup>rst author in English, audio-recorded with participants' consent and later transcribed verbatim by the research staff. In a few instances where participants spoke in Hindi (the national language of India) during short phases of the interview, verbatim transcription followed by translation was undertaken. Informed consent, voluntary participation and con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality marked the ethical protocol of the study.

The treatment and analysis of data followed van Manen's phenomenological re<sup>fl</sup>ection which involved appropriately clarifying and making explicit the structure of meaning of the lived experience. Given that meaning is multidimensional and multilayered, Van Manen (1998) advocates the use of thematic analyses as themes touch at the core of the notion we are trying to understand, helping us to make sense of it. Since themes may not always completely unlock the enigmatic aspects of the experience, related sub-themes capturing details and nuances may be required to provide a comprehensive picture. In the present study, themes were isolated through sententious and selective approaches. In following the sententious approach (Van Manen, 1998), each transcript was read as a whole to capture the core/essential meaning of participants' experiences. That is, through a careful reading of the transcript, the fundamental meaning of the experience for the participant as emerging from the text as a whole was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. A selective approach (Van Manen, 1998) was then undertaken where the text is read and examined for the meaning of statements which are particularly revealing in relation to the core theme. Through this process, sub-themes. themes and maior themes that contributed to the core theme were developed. That is, each transcript was read repeatedly and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant statements relating to and illustrating the various dimensions of the essential theme were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and demarcated. Labels were assigned to these sub-themes and later standardized across transcripts. Within each transcript, sub-themes were examined for their linkages. A comparison across transcripts was undertaken to highlight congruence in the sub-themes and their linkages across participants. Next, across transcripts, those sub-themes that dovetailed together in meaningful yet distinct ways were formed into themes. At the next stage, transcripts were compared to identify linkages across themes and those themes that held together were developed into major themes.

Sententious and selective thematic analyses (Van Manen, 1998) of participants' interviews yielded 2 core themes, namely, 'discerning yet overlooking an additional weapon' and 'navigating the extended reach'. It is important to mention that the framework of traditional workplace bullying (in terms of manifestations, sources, etiology, outcomes, levels of analysis and category-based harassment as elucidated in Section 2) was consciously engaged as a <sup>fi</sup>lter during the data analysis process so as to facilitate identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the distinguishing aspects of cyberbullying and guard against the mere replication of existing knowledge. At the same time, care was taken to ensure an inductive approach (Crabtree & Miller, 1992).

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings, presented in Section 4 below, brie<sup>fl</sup>y outline the 2 core themes (Section 4.1) and then describe the particular features of workplace cyberbullying through the core theme of ‘navigating the extended reach’ (Section 4.2).

## 4. Findings

## 4.1. Participants' core themes

Participants' experiences were captured by 2 core themes, namely, 'discerning yet overlooking an additional weapon' and 'navigating the extended reach'.

The core theme of ‘discerning yet overlooking an additional weapon’ indicated that participants here considered ICTDs to be one more mode of workplace bullying, providing the bully with an additional route for harassment. Cyberbullying, often combined with traditional bullying, was perceived as another means to a similar end to the extent that its concrete proof, wherever present, was never considered to be a possible route to resolution. No participant viewed ICTDs as having made any difference to their experiences as targets, even in instances where the modes left footprints they could use to resolve the situation. ICTDs were seen as having no differentiated impact, and where proof remained, its potential for problem solving was overlooked.

The core theme of ‘navigating an extended reach’ captured the breadth, depth and complexity of the dynamics that ICTDs brought to participants' interface with bullying. Narratives of this group centered around experiences of and coping with this extended reach. Here, cyberbullying, often combined with traditional bullying, presented wide-ranging implications for participants' exposure to being abused in terms of spatial, temporal, personal and social boundaries and, in some instances, for their experiences of seeking resolution in terms of concrete proof. That is, technology allowed participants to be harassed regardless of spatial, temporal, personal and social limits such that the bullying experience was considered to have engulfed participants' lives beyond the constraints inherent in traditional bullying. Yet, recognizing the footprints left behind by various technology modes helped participants defend their rights and pursue redressal.

While ‘discerning yet overlooking an additional weapon’ highlights the confounding between cyberbullying and traditional bullying, ‘navigating the extended reach’ vividly portrays the distinguishing features of cyberbullying vis-à-vis traditional bullying and is presented in this paper.

## 4.2. Navigating the extended reach — highlighting the distinctive character of cyberbullying at work

'Navigating the extended reach', which comprises of 4 major themes including 'being pursued, receiving a settled score, feeling ''haunted'' and ''hemmed in'' and drawing advantage', captures participants' experiences of how cyberbullying, in conjunction with traditional bullying, permeated targets' spatial, temporal, personal and social boundaries to unleash strain and harm while simultaneously providing concrete proof for its resolution. This core theme, its major themes and sub-themes are presented now (See Table 1, Column A for an overview), along with illustrative vignettes citing participants verbatim (the inclusion of quotes where participants have spoken in Hindi is accompanied by a translation in English inserted in parentheses).

The 16 participants included here reported person-related and task-related bullying behaviors via direct face-to-face interactions, land and mobile phone calls, text messages/SMSes (short message services), instant messaging (IM), emails, website posts (including social networking sites) as well as hacking computers and email and skype accounts. Of the 19 events they described, 6 events were exclusively cyberbullying, 18 were of interpersonal bullying (combined with category-based harassment linked to regional identity in 1 case and sexual harassment in 4 cases — see Note 1 for the Indian sociocultural context which is linked to category-based harassment) and 1 was of depersonalized bullying. While interpersonal bullying was either downwards (8), horizontal (6), upwards (3) and/or cross-level co-bullying (1) and sexual harassment was displayed by men to women peers, targets were bullied either individually (15) and/or along with others (3), by individual (12) or groups (5) of bullies. The bully's identity remained unknown in one instance. Bullying occurred privately only (6) or both privately and publicly (12) (Table 2 summarizes participants' experiences and their link with major themes and is referred to participant-wise throughout this section to exemplify the <sup>fi</sup>ndings).

## 4.2.1. Being pursued

Pervasiveness de<sup>fi</sup>ned participants' experiences of bullying, with technology helping bullies to go all out against targets. Pervasiveness permeated task performance, career development and employability, time, space, relationships and propriety.

Micromanagement during of<sup>fi</sup>ce hours constituted one dimension here, as reported in 3 instances. The use of IM in conjunction with technology-linked updates on employee performance was described by 2 participants. In one case, a participant was cyberstalked by his team leader (TL) all through his shift. Constantly monitoring the participant's work through call center technology, the TL used IM to repeatedly comment on and question the former's performance. The participant had to attend to his customer on call and answer his superior's queries simultaneously, severely constraining task performance. The struggle to divide his time and attention and to maintain top performance created extreme stress for the participant (Participant 10). Another participant shared that his boss would keep following up with emails, phone calls and even visits to his cubicle to check whether he had completed tasks assigned to him sometimes even 5 min ago. The excessive supervision interfered with task performance both in terms of divided time and attention and in terms of precipitating emotional distress (Participant 5).

Pervasiveness was linked to spatial and temporal orientation where work spilt into personal life even for trivial reasons. Being called up beyond of<sup>fi</sup>ce premises and of<sup>fi</sup>ce hours, sometimes even late into the night or on leave days/holidays, was indicated by numerous participants (for example, Participants 1, 5, 8, 9, 15). Often, the call had no important reason but provided an opportunity to bully. While work to personal life spillovers can be explained away/covered up by or confounded with expectations in and demands of today's workplace, participants maintained that their experiences stemmed from the bully's desire to harass them. Participants reported that not only was this used as a means to assert position but also prevented them from leaving the of<sup>fi</sup>ce behind and recovering from the day's dif<sup>fi</sup>cult experiences. Work–life balance and coping were hampered.

In the evenings, she (boss) will call for no reason or SMS…‘Have you completed this? Have you seen that? Do this tomorrow…’, she will say. But what is the need? It (the work) can be managed in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce next day. So this kind of harassment goes on…in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce, outside the of<sup>fi</sup>ce…no peace or time-out is there.

Pervasiveness was apparent through the use of technology to turn a professional relationship into a personal one, as evidenced in 4 incidents (Participants 2, 3 and 4). In addition to spatial and temporal pervasiveness in these instances, technology facilitated the breach of social limits. ## 4.2.2. Receiving a settled score

The indirect and sometimes anonymous nature of communication facilitated by technology not only served as an alternate mechanism by which bullies could reach targets but also reduced bullies' inhibitions. Consequently, vindictive behavior often at variance with direct face-to-face interaction was observable, with technology allowing for varying degrees of public display and for the eventual establishment of bully identity if it was not already known. Whereas in some instances such behavior occurred during of<sup>fi</sup>ce hours, within of<sup>fi</sup>ce premises and using of<sup>fi</sup>ce technology, an extra-organizational element or a combination of intraorganizational and extra-organizational resources was present in other cases. Moreover, vindictive behavior was not necessarily dispute-related stemming from bullies' perceived injustice but could be a means of expressing and coping with predatory sentiments linked to bullies' personality and competence.

A participant whose TL was very jealous with his new employment offer used technology to harass him during the notice period but remained neutral in face-to-face interactions. Here, call center technology which allowed for the constant monitoring of the participant's work provided inputs to the TL who then used IM to repeatedly query and doubt the former's work such that he had to divide his time and attention between the customer on call and the TL's demands. Being thus cyberstalked all through his shift hampered the participant's task performance and precipitated severe distress (Participant 10).

Three participants described positive direct interactions with their bullies who misbehaved through cyber means such as emails, phone calls and IM. In participants' view, these modes worked as shields through which the bully's true self, restrained during face-to-face meetings, emerged. One participant, whose peer treated her well in direct encounters but bullied her through land and mobile phone calls which he sometimes made in the presence of other colleagues, attributed his abusive behavior to his threatened professional self (Participant 15). Another participant whose superior's personality triggered the latter's bullying behavior resorted to IM to harass her. Any issue, either work-related or personal, that the boss could not discuss on a face-to-face basis was taken up on this mode (Participant 13).

Overtly, on the face, he (colleague) was very friendly…very nice…he would listen. But on the phone, he was rough and abusive…he would raise his voice, his tone was rough.

The use of social networking sites to air grievances in spite of pleasant face-to-face interactions was shared by a participant. Here, during their notice period, employees in the participant's organization who were targeted for a layoff posted negative comments about their supervisors and managers (including the participant) on the website. Dissatisfaction with the employer and observations about superior behavior including their work habits, their managerial styles, their individual idiosyncrasies, their personal relations, etc., were discussed. With each post being a trigger for a host of responses, sometimes even from those who had no link with the person(s) being talked about, a ripple effect spiral encompassing both distortions and a ‘buzz’ crept in. While these subordinates never imagined that their superiors would see their virtual interactions, the site itself had no access restrictions and was easily available to any one sur<sup>fi</sup>ng the net doing searches with supervisors', managers' and the organization's names. The participant had 2 explanations to offer: on the one hand, the strain of the imminent layoff prompted subordinate behavior where they resorted to this forum to give vent to thoughts and feelings that they were unable to share directly with their superiors; on the other hand, their age-based familiarity with ICTDs not only led them to such outlets but also blinded them to possible technology-related implications such that their inhibitions about private comments and sensitive issues and their caution about public spread and individual and organizational reputation were reduced. The participant chanced upon these posts during a search using her name and then informed her superiors about it (Participant 14 [Event 14A]).

A participant who refused the romantic advances of a colleague found that when she was away from the of<sup>fi</sup>ce for a few days, the colleague misused her of<sup>fi</sup>ce computer system which was open for upgrading purposes (Participant 3). During a night shift when only a few colleagues were present in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce, the bully accessed her system, acquired remote access to it, hacked her various email and skype accounts and gathered information from the system and the accounts. Based on the information so gathered, the colleague was able to identify a prospective groom of hers whom her family had identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for her based on a long-standing relationship between both families, whose status as a prospective groom was discernible from data on her email and skype accounts and whose contact details were available from her email and skype accounts. The colleague then proceeded to send this person numerous anonymous emails as well as SMSes over several weeks containing false messages about the participant's character being morally loose particularly in relation to men. Being informed about the receipt of the emails and SMSes by her <sup>fi</sup>ancé, the participant guessed the identity of sender from the earlier rejection of the bully's romantic interests and from the content of the emails and SMSes. Using technology, she not only con<sup>fi</sup>rmed her suspicions about the source of the misbehavior but also established that while the retaliation was initiated in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce, its continuity was maintained beyond of<sup>fi</sup>ce premises, time and resources.

Another participant indicated being the target, along with 2 other colleagues, of an anonymous, abusive email, sent by an apparently disgruntled entry-level employee to all supervisory and managerial levels in the organization. The contents of the email indicated that the sender, who was at the lowest organizational position, felt wronged and was using this medium to highlight his/her grievance. While attempts to identify the sender proved futile, circulation and/or display of the email to others in the organization who had not received it made it a workplace discussion point for a while. Being a ‘hot topic’ at various of<sup>fi</sup>ce fora brought in distortions and created a ‘buzz’ around the actual event (Participant 6).

The (e)mail was sent to everyone in the organization, all the bosses and team leaders. People must have showed it around to others and everyone was talking about it. You get certain vibes, etc., when you move around the of<sup>fi</sup>ce, you get a sense that people know from the grapevine… and they are a little wary when they see you because they do not know your reaction.

Cross-level co-bullying, reported by one participant (Participant 14 [Event 14B]), brought out the complex interface between technology and bullying. The participant's TL being threatened by her competence and desirous of maintaining cordial relations with her subordinates instigated the latter to misbehave with her. Using text messages via mobile phones, subordinates provided the TL with real-time information about team meetings facilitating his constant physical interruption of and interference with proceedings through which he bullied the participant in front of and along with her juniors. Apart from her authority being publicly undermined, the participant felt that her autonomy to function as she chose was compromised.

Once vindictive behavior was initiated via cyber means, its pervasiveness across time, space and social boundaries induced feelings of being pursued in targets.

## 4.2.3. Feeling ‘haunted’ and ‘hemmed in

Technology compounded the strain associated with workplace bullying. Physical and emotional distress was described by all participants, quali<sup>fi</sup>ed with references to the relentlessness and vulnerability that technology brought to the experience.

Headaches, sleep problems including insomnia, blood pressure, gastrointestinal problems, body fatigue and physical tiredness as well as fear, anxiety, humiliation, anger, depression and emotional exhaustion were reported. Women facing sexual harassment were disturbed by the violation of their honor. Participants spoke of going through a ‘very, very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult time’.

I suffered great trauma, my physical and mental condition were not good. I was not able to eat, I was not able to sleep at night. I was under great shock and stress.

Participants highlighted that technology resulted in them being bullied regardless of spatial, temporal and social limits, precipitating feelings of ‘being trapped’. The realization that bullies could identify and contact them, track their movements and activities, reach them anytime and anywhere, get in touch with their signi<sup>fi</sup>cant others and spread information (including false and malicious information) about them to varying degrees of a public scale, possibly even in the future, and all of this anonymously at least for some time, greatly disturbed participants. The feelings of ‘being trapped’ were accentuated by participants whose professional choices were limited sometimes due to personal circumstances.

According to participants whose work–life balance was blurred, ‘the nightmare never ends’, continuing ‘even at home, on weekends…always’, providing them with little respite and opportunity to recoup. With work situations always on their minds, these participants were swamped with negative thoughts and feelings and were deprived of breathers through which they could replenish their emotional strength.

Another participant stated, ‘even if one changes one's job to escape the bully, the past can come back to you (at) any time there (in the new organization)’. That group/bulk emails and public posts can wreak ‘wide-ranging and long-term havoc’ was pointed out by others.

Women participants facing sexual harassment spoke of their fear for their physical safety and their concern about their personal and professional reputation. That women could be ‘picked up randomly’ and ‘stalked’ as well as have replays of these experiences in the future if bullies get in touch with their spouses to malign them deeply upset these participants.

He had accessed my personal stuff on the computer…photos, mails…so kuch bhi kar sakta hai. To jo bhi mera future husband hoga, wo to usko bhi bhej sakta hair…mujhe pehle se itna clarify karna padega…(He [bully] can do anything. So whoever I marry, he can send things to that person…I will have to clarify this experience in the beginning itself with my future husband).

Bullies' discrepant behavior in instances where direct interactions were positive but cyber interactions were negative led to confusion and distrust, adding to participants' distress. Insights into the bully's true feelings towards them as demonstrated through ICTDs constrained targets from relating to the former in spite of their pleasant behavior during face-to-face meetings.

Participant narratives highlighted that the feelings of powerlessness common in instances of bullying were accentuated by the use of the cyber mode, owing to its pervasive character. Yet, this air of defencelessness was reversed in instances where participants could use the proof generated by ICTDs to resolve the problem, realizing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the situation. Notwithstanding their consequent reclaiming of agency, the power of technology in general left them with a residual sense of helplessness as they acknowledged that though they had managed this time, technology could rear its ugly head at any point but not always provide opportunities to be nabbed.

While extolling the virtues of technology in terms of the associated ease, pace, connectedness, efficiency and effectiveness in both personal and work matters, participants shared that these experiences underscored the related disadvantages. Participants stated that though they espoused a general awareness that the misuse of any entity was always possible, their experiences of cyberbullying startled them with the extent of harm technology could unleash. Recognizing that reliance on technology was unavoidable in the contemporary world, their response was mixed. On the one hand, seeing it as a singular experience linked to the bully's nature, participants expressed hope in the maturity of most people such that these instances would be few and far-between. On the other hand, uncertainty, bordering on distrust in a few instances, was described as participants realized that it was never possible to completely get away from ICTD-linked threats and dangers to one's personal and professional life. This latter insight rendered the relief felt by those who had been able to resolve their problem or move away from the bullying situation temporary and/or partial. Participants reported being left with an air of caution which permeated their own use of ICTDs and an altered view of security and well-being, Notwithstanding their move towards recovery from the experience, their progressive outlooks mingled with an underlying sense of anxiety and helplessness about future encounters of this nature.

Technology is here to stay…it has its value but I realize how much it can damage. One has to manage somehow

## 4.2.4. Drawing advantage

Technology presented targets with means of resolution, contingent upon the mode involved. While phone conversations were not relevant in this regard, phone caller identities and call records, SIM (subscriber identity module) cards, IM identities, text messages/SMSes, emails, IP (internet protocol) addresses and website posts were useful due to their concrete and permanent nature which left footprints or provided proof. Their threatened or actual use by the target independently and/or by the employer organization solved the problem partially/completely, immediately/over time. Proof was available for 14 events. Its use in 9 events facilitated resolution in 8 instances. By capitalizing on and reciprocating the extended reach through the use of proof, targets could reclaim their position/protect themselves.

By blocking a bully's IM identity on her system, a participant reduced the modes by which he could contact her thereby informing him that she was not interested in a romantic relationship with him. This, coupled with her refusals to take his internal calls, her direct indication to him to leave her alone and her ignoring him whenever he approached her face-to-face, ended his pursuit (Participant 4 [Event 4A]).

Caller identities available on land and mobile phones helped targets in identifying that the particular call they were receiving was from the bully. Participants getting these calls directly could refuse to take them as was reported in 2 cases (Participants 2 and 4). Moreover, if these calls were made to their mobile phones, the availability of a record on their call registers served as proof which could be used to take action if required. as 3 participants stated (Participants 2. 4 and 11). One participant whose colleague was romantically interested in her and continued to pursue her by calling her on her mobile phone on Sundays, in spite of having received an earlier warning from his manager that his of<sup>fi</sup>ce-based harassment towards her could invite termination, described her successful invocation of this proof. Indeed, once she informed her colleague that she would provide a record of his calls and of his initial email to her to the employer's HR (human resource) department and ensure his exit from the organization, he ended his advances.

It (his calls on Sundays) went on for 2 months. Finally. I told him that I would directly contact HR and ensure he was terminated. I told him that I had enough of proof because of his earlier (e)mail and his phone calls. Then he stopped, and two months after that, he left the job.

Two participants effectively used the abusive emails they had received to seek redressal (Participant 7 and Participant 16 [Event 16B]) while a third participant invoked the emails received as proof that could possibly be used if the bully did not cease his behavior (Participant 4 [Event 4B]).

In one of the aforementioned instances where the TL's bullying behavior stemmed from a regional bias, his preference for email communication from subordinates provided the target with documentation that he could successfully use (Participant 16, Event 16B). Here, in spite of the target complying with the TL's demand for email communication, the TL would not respond to the participant's requests for task-related clari<sup>fi</sup>cations but would constantly <sup>fi</sup>nd fault with his work and threaten his continuity with the organization, communicating this orally in front of the participant's peers and superiors and via email. Project failure attributed by the TL to the participant via a public email to his team mates and project and operations managers resulted in him being forced through false information and blackmail to immediately accept a PIP (performance improvement program) without a due process while actually continuing with his regular work. The possibility of termination as a result of these developments, linked to the TL's influence, could not be ruled out, Informal discussions with the HR department about these events highlighted the breach of protocol involved here. While HR considered the situation a fait accompli in which they could make little difference since the participant had already signed up for the PIP, they nonetheless encouraged him to submit his experiences in writing. Following an open forum meeting with one of the organization's directors who was visiting from overseas and who exhorted employees to seek direct redressal for any problems they faced, the participant compiled all the email communication between him and the TL and approached the person. After understanding the situation, the director immediately warned the TL about his bullying behavior in front of the target. Though the TL later privately challenged the target to get any further action implemented against him, his bullying behavior stopped. In the participant's opinion, the bully was taken aback both by the former taking him on and by the extent of documentation available with him — the bully had never imagined being reported with such detailed proof that he himself was responsible for creating and that the participant had been painstakingly saving. The participant also made a formal complaint to HR about his experiences. The participant, who was taken off the PIP soon after this meeting, was informed later that the director, after pursuing the complaint at a very high level in the organization including the HR department within days of receiving it, followed it up with another private interaction with the TL on the matter.

Website posts were referred to by top management in handling the situation where targets of a layoff had held negative virtual discussions about the employer organization and its managers and supervisors. Targets and other employees remaining with the organization were told that the employer had to have access to whichever websites its name was associated with (Participant 14, [Event 14A]).

We (managers) had a meeting with them (the employees who were still with the organization), demanding access to every group which has the organization's name.

The use of multiple technologies was described in the case (Participant 3) where a rejected suitor entered his colleague's of<sup>fi</sup>ce computer while she was out of station, hacked her email and skype accounts, acquired remote access to her system and sent out anonymous emails and SMSes maligning her to a prospective groom of hers. When the emails and SMSes continued, the participant and her <sup>fi</sup>ancé decided to trace their origins to firmly establish the identity of the sender. While relving on the IP addresses of the mails did not help in providing any clues and the SIM cards of the numbers from which the SMSes were sent, being traced to various Indian cities, were not useful in giving any insights, the participant took 2 of her seniors at work into con<sup>fi</sup>dence requesting their help so that her present and future personal and professional reputation and position were not jeopardized. Supporting her in this experience, the seniors decided to check both CCTV (closed circuit television) footage of the of<sup>fi</sup>ce during the period when the participant was away and hard disk logs of the participant's system. They found evidence of the bully sitting at and using the participant's of<sup>fi</sup>ce computer once (via CCTV footage) and of him remotely accessing her system in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce once (from hard disk logs)– both on a single night when she was out of town – as well as evidence of her of<sup>fi</sup>ce system being remotely accessed on several occasions from locations bevond the office (through hard disk logs). Essentially. the CCTV footage and hard disk logs of remote access particularly within the of<sup>fi</sup>ce itself established the bully's identity. Based on the data gathered, the 2 seniors were supportive, showing the participant various extra-organizational redressal options available to her. However, they did not take it up as an organizational matter as they opined that a personal relationship was involved and the communications could not be traced to the of<sup>fi</sup>ce. Apart from the seniors' view that this was not an organizational issue, the participant was reluctant to approach the HR department despite the organization's sexual harassment policy since a previous case had been ineffectively handled by them such that the problem here had not only persisted but developed into an of<sup>fi</sup>ce discussion point. The participant was left to deal with the matter on her own. Using her evidence, she confronted the bully. Though he insisted that he was innocent, she stood her ground such that the former <sup>fi</sup>nally had no choice but to stop sending anonymous emails and SMSes and remotely accessing her of<sup>fi</sup>ce system and to refrain from contacting her.

Reliance on IP addresses to trace anonymous emails was referred to in one instance (Participant 6). Here, an abusive mail targeting 3 superiors had been sent publicly to supervisors and managers within the organization but the identity of the sender remained unknown in spite of several attempts by the target, his friends and his employer organization to trace this information via the IP address. Yet, since the event was neither repeated nor manifested in any other way, the target did not pursue the matter after some time.

Being able to resolve their problem through proof helped 6 participants (Participants 2, 4, 7, 11, 14 [Event 14A]) to remain with their employer organization, though one participant here being disillusioned with the bullying experience quit as soon as a better offer came up (Participant 16 [Event B]). A seventh participant whose problem was resolved due to reliance on proof had intended to quit anyway for career-related considerations and proceeded with her plans (Participant 3). An eighth participant whose one-time experience of upwards bullying through a public anonymous email was never solved continued with the organization as the matter never recurred (Participant 6). Others with proof were able to resolve the situation without its use and hence remained with the organization (Participant 9) or had quit anyway for career-related reasons and hence did not seek resolution (Participants 10 and 16 [Event 16A]).

I had kept everything (proof) carefully and it helped. He (bully) was shocked that I had all records — he never expected it. I was happy that I could get justice. But I did not feel it was worth continuing there, I had lost faith in the company. So I began searching for better options, and when something better came up, I left.

Those who were unable to resolve the problem included 2 participants who had not even thought of the option of using the proof available with them (Participants 5 and 13) and 4 participants who had no proof (Participants 1, 8, 14 [Event 14B] and 15). Here, targets coped on their own, relying on their inner strength and their informal support network within and beyond the workplace, and in some instances on periodic direct confrontations with the bully and on their own initiatives. Formal intra-organizational avenues for redressal, including escalation to line/functional superiors and referral to the HR department, were not perceived as worth pursuing, even in cases where participants knew about the existence of anti-bullying organizational policies. Indicating uncertainty whether these formal mechanisms would operate in objective ways, given issues of organizational dynamics, power equations, etc., participants did not wish to invite further harassment and victimization. While 2 stayed on with the current employer due to personal circumstances (one woman intended to quit and did so as soon as her personal circumstances permitted whereas the other was fortunate to have the bullying situation resolve itself after 6 months due to extraneous circumstances), 2 others quit or planned to quit as soon as a better job offer arose. For the remaining 2 (Participants 1 and 8), HR was directly involved in the bullying process and participants' employment was at stake. Except for these last 2 participants who took up the matter through extra-organizational avenues such as collectivization, government intervention or legal action, none of the others even considered these options as they did not see themselves within the purview of labor laws, their employer organizations had discouraged them from such initiatives and they neither knew of nor valued such endeavors in their sectors.

## 5. Discussion

The emergent phenomenon of workplace cyberbullying is recognized as an increasing and important problem being faced by employees and employers. The present study uncovers how the mediation of ICTDs renders this issue unique and complicated, thereby addressing a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gap in knowledge. Through a qualitative inquiry rooted in Van Manen's (1998) hermeneutic phenomenology exploring targets' experiences of cyberbullying at work, the core theme of ‘navigating the extended reach’ which subsumes the four major themes of 'being pursued, receiving a settled score, feeling ''haunted'' and ''hemmed in'' and drawing advantage' was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. The study <sup>fi</sup>ndings underscore how cyberbullying, in conjunction with traditional bullying, permeated targets' spatial, temporal, personal and social boundaries to unleash strain and harm while simultaneously providing concrete proof for its resolution.

Theorizing from the core theme, major themes, themes and sub-themes (as shown in Table 1 Column B), three special characteristics of workplace cyberbullying, namely, its boundaryless spread, its concreteness and permanence and its invisible and sometimes anonymous nature, can be distilled. Elaborating on these aspects in the following paragraphs, the study's conceptual contributions to the workplace bullying literature are brought out. In so doing, the complexity of workplace bullying is underscored, facilitating a nuanced understanding and a foundation for appropriate intervention.

The boundarylessness of cyberbullying encompasses three aspects, namely, scope of bullying behaviors, work–life balance and range of audience. While the scope of bullying behaviors refers to whether bullying is person-related (including social/cultural intrusions in instances of sexual harassment) or task-related, the issue of work–life balance indicates whether bullying crosses time, space and relationships to move beyond the of<sup>fi</sup>ce into the home involving targets' family members/signi<sup>fi</sup>cant others. Audience range implies whether those privy to the bullying situation were (a) the bully[ies] and the target[s] alone (fully con<sup>fi</sup>ned); (b) of<sup>fi</sup>ce personnel extending from team to project to department to organization either directly associated as targets and observers or indirectly associated by hearing about it and/or targets' family members and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant others directly associated (partially con<sup>fi</sup>ned); or (c) society at large indirectly associated (uncon<sup>fi</sup>ned).

ICTDs allow people to be bullied anywhere and anytime beyond the of<sup>fi</sup>ce premises and work time. Moreover, under circumstances where organizations and their employees depend on ICTDs for work, combined with the competitiveness of today's business context, spatial and temporal boundaries lose their signi<sup>fi</sup>cance as employees are expected to be available for and can be contacted about work all the time, allowing for unrestricted and unrestrictable exposure to cyberbullying. Bullying is then experienced constantly, urging a review of its persistence conceptualization in terms of repetition and time-span. ICTDs facilitate the spread of cyberbullying. That is, due to technology, cyberbullying need not be limited to the parties involved or within the organization. Either the group central to the bullying could use technology not just to bully but to publicize the bullying or bystanders in the know of it could add to the situation by joining in directly and/or by publicizing thereby becoming accomplices. While maturity is key to the discretion displayed, irresponsible behavior violates con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality. Further, the widening audience could mean private spats become open <sup>fi</sup>ghts, sometimes bordering on ‘dirty linen being washed in public’ or even whistleblowing. Unlike traditional bullying where the problem is more contained spatially and hence likely to be an intra-organizational issue, technology transcends these barriers such that a bullying situation can be played out beyond organizational boundaries. With internal organizational matters becoming public, external stakeholders could well be involved. An outward spiraling dynamic such as this can complicate the issue and exacerbate the outcomes for all parties.

The mode of cyberbullying in<sup>fl</sup>uences its concreteness – that is whether it has a form that can be downloaded, saved and replayed (like emails, pictures, etc.) or can remain available for viewing and replays (like posts, blogs, etc.) – and hence its permanence. This has several implications. Where the material is available only to targets, even one such experience can be repeated simply by replaying it, once again calling into question the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of bullying in terms of persistence where frequency and duration come into play. Where the material is shared publicly, not only does the range of the audience become relevant but also their further dissemination and replays of it could create a ‘buzz’ marked by distortions.

While the invisible and anonymous nature of cyberbullying has been emphasized, this operates in a complex way. Depending on the form and mode used and the aspects of boundarylessness involved, the exposure to bullying could be available only to the target (as in personal texts and emails) and not others (as in group texts and emails, websites, blogs), unless the target brings it to others' notice, and this has implications for bystander behavior and target coping. Where bystanders are unaware of it, they cannot respond unless targets apprise them of the situation — and targets may be reluctant to do so for various reasons including impression management, stigmatization, etc. Targets are then left to cope alone. Where bystanders are aware of it either because it has reached them directly or targets inform them, a range of responses from indifference to support can be expressed. Further, bully identity may not be available in the immediacy of the event or at all, raising the issue of the source, authenticity and motive. Knowing or not knowing bully identity could work positively or negatively. Thus, while analytical differences are important to tease out the simultaneous and sometimes subtle manifestations of numerous convergent entities, their complex and dynamic intermingling to the point of a nuanced fusion must be deciphered and recognized.

## 6. Conclusion

By highlighting the nature of workplace cyberbullying vis-à-vis traditional bullying, the study <sup>fi</sup>ndings not only address a gap in the literature but also emphasize the need for workplace bullying intervention to take into account the differences between traditional and cyber bullying when developing applications. Indeed, the many similarities between traditional and cyber bullying should not gloss over the special character of the latter which has far-reaching implications for all stakeholders. Strategies to deal with workplace bullying cannot be uniform across traditional and cyber bullying but must embrace the particular nature of each. For this, the awareness of top management and of HR personnel about traditional and cyber bullying and mechanisms and avenues for their redressal is critical to the development, availability and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of organizational intervention. Yet, as with traditional workplace bullying, the mere creation of appropriate measures is not enough — their implementation must be sound. Participants' reluctance to avail of formal intra-organizational avenues based on perceptions of their ineffectiveness due to organizational dynamics is consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of earlier studies on traditional bullying (D'Cruz & Noronha, 2010a,b), raising alarm bells that cyberbullying not only needs intervention distinct from traditional bullying but also the execution of these provisions should not re<sup>fl</sup>ect and perpetuate the problems associated with similar endeavors in traditional bullying. Indeed, it would be ideal if cyberbullying catalyzes the success of workplace bullying interventions in general, overcoming the long-standing inertia commonly linked with such mechanisms.

As younger generations whose lives are fused with technology join the workforce, as organizations continue to be driven by emerging technology to derive competitive advantage, as individuals seek optimal professional development which necessitates interface with technology and as technology itself evolves and penetrates human life at even younger ages than it does now, cyberbullying at work will not only increase but morph in various ways, and keeping pace with this in terms of research and intervention is imperative.
