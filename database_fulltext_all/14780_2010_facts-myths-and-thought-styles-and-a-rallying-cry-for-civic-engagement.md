# Facts, myths and thought-styles. . . and a rallying cry for civic engagement

Dave Wastell <sup>a,</sup>⇑, Sue White <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Nottingham University Business School, Wollaton Road, Nottingham NG8 1BB, UK

<sup>b</sup> Institute of Applied Social Studies, Birmingham University, Birmingham B5 2TT, UK

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Available online 11 November 2010

Keywords: Facts Myths Information systems Thought collectives Thought styles Theory Public services Child protection

## a b s t r a c t

This polemic concludes with two programmatic recommendations for the Information Systems (IS) field. First, a call for more research in the public services, and for greater civic engagement generally; second, that we shake off our enchantment with Theory, not least in the interests of dissemination and communication, especially with non-academic audiences. Our critique of theory uses Barthes’ structuralism to argue that theories are myths which order, interpret and normalise the world. Leading into this, we address the fundamental question ‘‘what is a fact”, drawing on the epistemology of Ludwik Fleck (his concepts of thought-style and thought-collective in particular) which we believe deserves more recognition in IS. The empirical material for our analyses comes from two case studies, both from the domain of child protection in the UK. The paper is introduced with a brief exposition of the myth that information systems are factrepositories. We stress that, though based on domestic examples, our messages are universal and strategic for the field. To set the scene, the paper opens with some lines from Aeschylus, describing a mythical event at the outset of Agamemnon’s ill-fated Trojan campaign.

## 1. Introduction

As our Kings set out, a black bird and a white bird, sailed over

Then those two birds flushed and drove and killed a heavy hare with her twins.

Calchas the Seer cried to the whole army and the two Kings

What does this mean? I will tell you. It means Victory with a twist.

But when you have emptied Troy of her blood and her babies,

Then you can expect the anger of heaven.

Artemis, the moon-faced, the goddess, the mother of the hares

Avenging Artemis, who stands casting the shadow of a great Queen.

Bloody footprints staggering through this palace, generation to generation.

This is how Calchas the Seer unriddled the murder of the hare.

(Ted Hughes’ translation of Agamemnon, by Aeschylus<sup>1</sup>)

The natural (mythical) way of thinking of an information system (IS) is as an organised repository of facts about the world, ‘‘reality-mapping” as Lyytinen (1987) put it. But what is a fact? With this blunt question, Ludwik Fleck began his sem inal book, Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact (1979). Published in 1935, GDSF foreshadows the better known work of Kuhn and other sociologists of knowledge, which has spawned, inter alia, the field of science and technology studies (STS). Facts are so central to our business in IS, but what the devil are they? Fleck can help us here. His work, ‘‘a brilliant and unexploited resource” as Kuhn generously acknowledges,<sup>2</sup> deserves to be better known, and we will draw much on Fleck’s work in this disquisition. The parenthetical ‘‘mythical” will be elucidated along the way; so-called facts are carriers of myths, myths of happy families, the omnipotent State, aligned organisations (Wastell, 2007). Another apt term is Propaganda (Ellul, 1965)! Theory is a central figure in the Myth–Fact relation, and we shall take an irreverent pot-shot or two at this most sacred of cows along the way. But for now, just note the ‘‘fact” in our epigraph:

‘‘Then those two birds flushed and drove and killed a heavy hare with her twins.”

Plus an un-related second fact to be introduced in due course:

‘‘No problems when Mum left the room, continued playing happily.”

‘‘Data, data, data, I can’t make bricks without clay” famously exclaimed Sherlock Holmes. Bring it on! Two ‘‘factual exhibits” will be considered here, both relating to a single information system, the UK’s Child Protection Register (CPR). Until its abolition in April 2008, the CPR provided a simple list of children living within the immediate area of UK local authorities, who were considered at imminent risk from neglect or abuse. In many ways, it functioned rather well, though it had its critics. During the lead-up to its abolition, other more ambitious, national systems were being developed which were expected to replace its functionality, for example the ContactPoint data base of all children in England. This system, purported to help identify and protect vulnerable children (Peckover et al., 2008), has recently been abandoned,<sup>3</sup> being seen as a costly venture, headed for inevitable failure

Although our case material is necessarily parochial, the chords we strike are intended as universal and strategic for the IS field. Although the particular vicissitudes of ContactPoint are not directly addressed, it is pertinent to ask why it drew so little attention from IS scholars. Such neglect of the public services, the preference for commercial business, is not a phenomenon unique to the UK; there may be local variations, but there is a general bias in our scholarship to be corrected. To this end, the present polemic is a call-to-arms for more IS research in the public services, where we argue our expertise is needed and we have a civic duty to assist. To make our work ‘‘really matter”, we also need to interrogate our research practice, chal lenging in particular our fetishization of theory. But more of this later; first, our two stories.

## 2. Exhibit 1: the strange history of Shannon Matthews’ kidnapping

The ‘‘kidnapping ‘‘of Shannon Matthews caused something of a press furore in the UK in 2008. Paraphrasing Wikipedia:

‘‘Shannon Matthews, a 9 year old British girl, disappeared on the afternoon of 19 February 2008 in Dewsbury, West Yorkshire, England. The search for Shannon became a major missing person police operation before she was found alive nearly 2 months later, only a short distance away in the house of Michael Donovan, the uncle of Shannon’s mother’s boyfriend. Donovan was charged with the kidnapping and false imprisonment of Shannon, whilst her mother Karen Matthews, was charged with child neglect and perverting the course of justice.

The investigation, carried out by The West Yorkshire Police, questioned 1500 motorists and also searched 3000 houses. By early March, more than 250 officers and 60 detectives were involved in the investigation, about 10% of West Yorkshire Police’s operational strength. This became the largest police search for a missing person in the country, since the Yorkshire Ripper investigation around 30 years previously

The joint trial of Donovan and Matthews at Leeds Crown Court concluded on 4 December with both defendants being found guilty, given an eight years sentence. It was revealed that the plan had been for Michael Donovan to release Shannon Matthews at Dewsbury Market, drive around the corner to ‘discover her’ then take her to a police station and claim the £50,000 reward. This would then be split between Karen Matthews and Donovan.”

The child protection register had plaved an important part in the case, as a “non-human actor" (Latour, 1987). Shannon had been placed on the register in 2003, but had subsequently been removed. Press reports seized on this as further evidence of the dysfunctionality of the UK Government’s ‘‘performance management” regime, dubbed ‘‘Targets and Terror” (Bevan and Hood, 2006). Under increasing general criticism at the time, this top-down regime subjected all UK local authorities (Kirklees Council near Leeds, in this case) to comprehensive external auditing and assessment, with consequential sanctions for poor performance. In the case of social services, the inspectorial body was (and is) Ofsted (Office for Standards in Education). One of its key targets was the number of children on a child protection plan (the equivalent of being on the register, after its abo lition) for more than two years, and Kirklees had been criticised in previous inspections that its percentage was too high, with the minatory recommendation that the figure should be brought down.

Taking up this strand of the story from a contemporaneous press report (entitled ‘‘Shannon was betrayed by government child protection targets”) in the Daily Mail (5th December, 2008), an intriguing IS sub-plot emerges:

‘‘As Matthews and Donovan await sentencing for the kidnapping, one Kirklees Council source claimed social workers warned of the dangers Shannon faced but were overruled by their departmental bosses. ‘‘The social worker with the family was put under massive pressure to take her off the register” said the source. ‘‘She told her line manager that she should not be de-registered because of Karen Matthews’ inability to care for her, the fact the house was chaotic, and the fact that she had multiple partners and multiple people coming into the house.” All the alarm bells were ringing. But councils are under pressure to take children off the register or reduce the number of children in care to meet government targets.”

Thus ends our interrogation of the fact that Shannon was no longer deemed ‘‘at risk”, according to the CPR.

## 3. Exhibit 2: the Saga of Evie and Seb

The data for this exhibit have been extracted from an ethnographic study of a child protection investigation, reported in full in White and Wastell (in press). The case involves a young mother (Evie Hatter), her son Seb (6 years) and his half brother (Abel, 7 years). The main data source was the multi-disciplinary case file (an IS worthy of investigation in its own right), containing documentation from all the involved professionals: the consultant child psychologist (CCP), social workers, nurses etc. Seb was the prime subject of the investigation. The opening observations of CCP comment starkly on his ‘‘challenging” behaviour: ‘‘Lighting fires, no respect for authority, Very aggressive to others – lashes out. . . always been very clingy, bed wetting”. As part of the investigation, specialist assessments were to be undertaken by social services and other agencies. It was also resolved to engage the local Child and Adolescent Mental Health Day Unit to provide a mix of family support, individual counselling and further assessment.

Four sessions with the Day Unit were arranged. The first seemed to go well. One of the nurses (N1) observed:

‘‘Seb came across as a pleasant little boy who was very helpful when it came to making a cup of tea and getting biscuits ready. . . All his play was age appropriate and during this time he was quite chatty. No problems when Mum left the room, continued playing happily.”

The second session also began encouragingly, with significant progress seemingly underway. However, all was not what it seemed, as a nurse’s note revealed:

‘‘However as soon as Seb left the room, Evie became tearful and informed me that things were awful. . .she said there had been no change in Seb’s behaviour, nor in her view of him and at present she doesn’t want to be around him at all.”

It was decided that the final session should also include Abel. Themes of helpless ambivalence emerge again in the notes regarding Evie, but with even greater force:

‘‘Evie very dependent and low. Relayed an incident where she had bought 6 cakes. However the cakes disappeared. Evie reports becoming furious . . . she grabbed both boys by the hair and banged their heads together until they told the truth. Feels guilty for hurting them, but cannot fully accept responsibility for this. . .”

Over the Day Unit sessions, the progressive formulation of Evie as ‘needy and dependent’ steadily emerges, and of Seb’s behavioural problems the result of ineffectual parenting. External events over the period included reports of the children being ‘out of control’, and most worryingly a fire-setting incident in which Abel was injured. A child protection case conference was therefore arranged at which a range of reports were presented, including the Social Services’ investigation which concluded:

‘‘that Evelyn is able to understand parent management strategies with but has difficulty putting them into practice... However concerns now appear to have increased and we would question Evelyn’s attachments specifically to Seb and her ability to improve the children’s situation.”

The second report was submitted by the NSPCC (National Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children). Here is a key passage:

‘‘Evie is beginning to understand the seriousness of their situation [but] there are problems with attachment which we believe stem from insecure attachments in childhood. One possibility is that she or others may conclude she is unable to care for them [her children] appropriately.”

At the Conference itself, there was a clear consensus that the children were at serious risk, and that they would need to be entered on the child protection register. However, the exact nature of their registration provoked some animated discussion between the various professionals. The lengthy ‘‘categorisation debate” borders on the pedantic if not the comic.

‘‘CCP says he feels that in the case of Seb, registration should be for emotional abuse ‘‘actual” and for Abel emotional abuse ‘‘likely” because Abel is not currently suffering significant harm. . . SW [social worker] said she agreed but that both chil dren should be registered with physical abuse ‘‘actual” . . .and emotional abuse ‘‘likely”. CCP said he agreed but he felt that the category of physical/emotional abuse ‘‘actual” should apply to both boys because they have both suffered significant harm. . . Detective Constable H said that she agreed with everything that had been said . . .”

And so the ‘‘fact” of Seb being ‘‘at risk” was born. Our report of the case (ibid) continued, based on direct observation of a child protection review conference held 4 months later. A range of services had been put in place as a result of Seb’s registration, including the assistance of two family support workers. Evie was very appreciative of their help but was also keenly aware of the precariousness of her situation. Whilst she needed to be ‘‘better” she must avoid being ‘‘really better”, else the support gathered about her could be taken away:

‘‘EVIE: I’m worried that if there’s a big improvement they’re going to cut off the support. If they think the children don’t need to be on the register anymore, I’m going to lose all the support and I’m really concerned about that because I don’t want that to happen and I know that it can do.”

In further exchanges, she is reassured that she is indeed better, but not really better and the meeting closed. In our paper, we commented on Evie’s performance as an ‘ideal service recipient’, a tour de force requiring ‘‘delicate balancing in a moral mine-field”:

‘‘Not only must Evie confess responsibility, she must also exonerate and excuse herself. She must show positive change, yet retain eligibility for services. She must construct herself as caring about not caring adequately. She is by no means a passive stooge, she contributes in full part to the decision-making process and its shifting categorisations. (White and Wastell, in press)”

And so the ‘‘fact” of Seb still ‘‘at risk” was artfully maintained, temporarily at least.

## 4. Enter Fleck, bearing concepts

Although Fleck’s book is a slim volume, to decoct an entire system of thought into a tractable ‘‘text-book” summary or primer is as impossible as it is undesirable. And there would be irony too, given Fleck’s reflections on the production of vademecum knowledge – knowledge ‘to go’! Here we will limit ourselves to some key, germane concepts. Exactly as the title suggests, GDSF is a book about the production of facts in science. Fleck takes one such fact, the so-called ‘‘Wassermann reaction” (a test for antibodies in blood serum) which is held to be diagnostic of syphilis, a fact established through the experiments of August von Wassermann at the turn of the century, with the key papers published in 1906. Fleck sets his central intellectual task at the end of the preface: ‘‘How, then, did this empirical fact originate and in what does it consist?”

This question sums up, for him, what should be the proper subject and method for epistemology, namely historical enquiry into the socio-cultural origins and development of knowledge. Accordingly, GDSF begins with the first appearance of the concept of syphilis in the 15th century, tracing its progressive development from an ‘‘ethical-mystical disease entity of carnal scourge” (p. 5) to its modern incarnation as a well-defined medical condition. In parallel are traced developments in medical science, in particular the long-standing idea that disorders of the blood are responsible for disease, leading to the emergence of modern forms of bacteriology and serology in the nineteenth century. Only through the co-evolution and confluence of these trends was the fact of the Wassermann test made possible. Its actual birth was manifestly a difficult one, which Fleck sees as paradigmatic of the way science really works:

‘‘From false assumptions and irreproducible initial experiments an important discovery has resulted after many errors and detours. (p. 76).”

That Fleck’s factual exemplar is an exotic one does not limit the range of application of his ideas. They can be directly applied to the workings of scientific communities, and some critical reflection on our discipline, based on Fleck, would certainly be salutary. But his genetic epistemology can be applied to any ‘‘fact”, including the mundane facts that populate information systems. The categorisation of both Evie and, in turn, Seb as having attachment difficulties, for instance, and of Seb being ‘‘at risk”. Indeed, our ethnography is itself a piece of genetic epistemology.

The concept of the ‘‘thought collective” (Denkkollektiv) is central to Fleck’s analytical schema:

‘‘If we define ‘‘thought collective” as a community of persons mutually exchanging ideas or maintaining intellectual interaction, we will find by implication that it also provides the special ‘‘carrier” for the historical development of any field of thought, as well as for the given stock of knowledge and level of culture. (p. 39).”

Fleck designates the set of beliefs and values common to members of a given collective as its ‘‘thought style” (Denkstile), defined as ‘‘directed perception, with corresponding mental and objective assimilation of what has been so perceived”:

‘‘The individual within the collective is never, or hardly ever, conscious of the prevailing thought style, which almost always exerts an absolutely compulsive force upon his thinking and which it is not possible to be at variance. (p. 41)’

Anticipating Giddens’ (1984) ideas about the duality of structure, it is not merely that the thought-collective constrains what can be thought, but that without such social structures, there can be no knowledge:

‘‘Cognition is the most socially-conditioned activity of man, and knowledge is the paramount social creation. . .. Without social conditioning, no cognition is even possible. (pp. 42–43)”

‘‘The true creator of knowledge is not an individual but the thought collective. (p. 123)”

Fleck devotes much space to the analysis of the social structure of thought collectives, making the key distinction between small ‘‘esoteric” circles (the expert elite) and wider ‘‘exoteric” circles (of teachers and practitioners). Fleck also distinguishes between journal science (provisional, contradictory, fragmented) and vademecum science, which represents its ‘‘critical synopsis in an organised system” (p. 118), such as a manual or textbook. Characterised by orderliness, consistency and certitude, vademecum science is ‘‘taught and generally used”. It is the means by which exoteric knowledge becomes ‘‘dominant and binding”; it constitutes ‘‘thought constraint, which determines what cannot be thought in any other way, what is to be investigated or ignored” (p. 123).

And what, then, of ‘‘facts”? Fleck’s account (p. 95) of their genesis is unusual:

‘‘The general aim of intellectual work is therefore maximum thought constraint with minimum thought caprice. This is how a fact arises. At first there is a signal of resistance in the chaotic initial thinking, then a definite thought constraint, and finally a form directly to be perceived. A fact always occurs in the context of the history of thought and is always the result of a thought style.”

## 5. Shannon, Evie, Seb . . . and Ludwik

## We murder to dissect – Wordsworth

It is easy to miss the obvious. Looking back on our two vignettes, what stands out most, from the perspective of the IS field, is the sheer ubiquity of information systems in the public services. In the management realm (targets, PIs, etc.) and in front-line work (e.g. the multidimensional case notes), they are integral to the practice of both managers and professionals. Having made this point, it is time for Fleck.

Inspecting our two cases, and the two practices on show, what thought collectives and styles do we encounter? In Shannon’s case, there is an obvious ‘‘paradigm” at work, variously dubbed managerialism or the new public management (NPM). In the UK, Dillow (2007) argues that managerialism is a defining feature of the ‘‘New Labour” administration, and Chard and Ayre (2010) trace its distinctive ideological contours in the domain of child welfare. Managerialism’s defining features are: that a central elite know best; that strong top-down management is the key to quality and performance; that workers are self-interested and inefficient; that the standardisation of processes and explicit targets drive quality, which is also ensured by rigorous micro-management using performance indicators (PIs).

Elsewhere we have shown how powerful this mind-set can be for the denizens of its thought collective. In a series of papers,<sup>4</sup> the results of a collaborative research project (involving IS and social work academics) are presented, which describe the vicissitudes of a national computer system, the Integrated Children’s System (ICS). We argued that the ICS ensnared social workers in an ‘‘electronic iron-cage”, forcing them to work according to a baroquely detailed ‘‘process model” enacted in software. Our analysis showed how the progenitors of this system (a cadre of senior civil servants and academic advisors) were so much in thrall to their managerialist paradigm (embodied in the ICS) that even the manifest problems the system was encountering in the field were re-interpreted as consistent with their preconceptions: reports of disquiet and complaints from practitioners were hubristically seen as resistance from a recalcitrant work-force, resisting ‘‘modernisation”. Such resistance confirmed the need for more micro-managerial control, it did not call it into question!

The Matthews scandal provided a clear example of the dysfunctionality of managerialism, and performance management in particular. The tragic death of ‘‘Baby P” (now named as Peter Connelly) in the London Borough of Haringey provided an even more stark example, coming to light at much the same time (White et al., 2010). Despite being on the CPR, and seen by numerous professionals, ‘‘Baby P” had died a brutal death at the hands of his mother, her boyfriend and a lodger. Press reports drew attention to the deficiencies of the ICS and the role it had played. Based on our ICS research, we wrote a long article for The Guardian (a leading national newspaper) entitled ‘‘Computers can hinder child protection”. Our research had revealed how the ICS, although designed to reduce risk to children, had paradoxically increased it, by inviting dangerous work-arounds and short-cuts as social workers strove to meet rigid targets.

Many others have written about the dysfunctional effect of performance management in the UK public services (e.g. Chard and Ayre, 2010; Bevan and Hood, 2006), and critiques from within the professional community are also emerging, including rather belatedly from senior managers in children’s services (Chard and Ayre, 2010). The thought style is certainly fraying, although paradigms can be remarkably resilient (Kuhn, 1970). Lord Laming, famous in the UK for his high-profile investigations of child death tragedies, is the high-priest of managerialism in social care. In his most recent report (Laming, 2009), whilst correctly diagnosing the failures of managerialism, he perversely goes on to prescribe more of the same, a larger dose indeed:

the proverbial ‘‘man with a hammer”! Nonetheless, there are clear signs that managerialism is nearing its demise, at least in its present guise. The newly-formed coalition government in the UK has firmly set itself the task of dismantling the centralised, command-and-control state of its predecessor.<sup>5</sup> Kuhn notes that paradigms are only abandoned once an alternative is available, and in children’s services, the green shoots of new approaches are beginning to appear (Ayre and Preston-Shoot, 2010).

Let us turn to our second vignette. What do we learn here of the genesis of facts? Superficially, we again see facts as political productions, information unmasked not as a neutral ‘‘mapping of reality” but inextricably bound up with organisational politics (Robey and Boudreau, 1999). Whereas Exhibit1 provides a high level illustration of, as it were, the macropolitical reality of fact production, exhibit 2 shows the processes as they evolve diachronically in the every-day interactions of ‘‘streetlevel bureaucracy”. Politically, organisations are complex landscapes, with multiple interweaving ‘‘logics of action”,<sup>6</sup> to use a term of Bacharach et al. (1996); conflicts and tensions between such logics are worked out in local interactions, a process they dub the ‘‘micro-politics of dissonance reduction”. Various discrepant logics are manifestly in play in our vignette (of CCP, Evie, the NSPCC), but dissonances are progressively reduced, and ultimately a relatively stable ‘‘political settlement” is achieved. Although our two scenarios are historically distinct, with ‘‘Evie and Seb” taking place before the apogee of performance management, it is not difficult to imagine how the original micro-politics would have progressed differently in the later context, subject to the managerial logic of targets and timescales. Would the ‘‘fact” of Seb’s registration have emerged and been sus tained in Kirklees in 2008 – probably not, would have to be the answer. Back to the actual reality, our ethnography shows the fact of Seb’s registration to be a complex socio-political construction, built through a contested, negotiated process which ebbs and flows, eventually moving to a resolution. No ‘‘simple fact” is this. That facts are political may seem trite, but the key point is methodological, that this was only known ex post facto (so to speak) once the ‘‘genetic epistemology” had been done.

But what of the specific thought-style, or styles, in play? There are, of course, several we could focus on, including the very idea of social work itself. Here we shall concentrate on one aspect of the case, not the registration of Seb, but a fact which made a much earlier appearance, the very innocuous fact (to the lay reader, at least) mentioned in the Introduction to this piece. Let us cast our mind back to the first quotation from the multidisciplinary notes. Nurse N1’s observations record the following fact:

‘‘No problems when Mum left the room, continued playing happily.”

Masquerading as a simple observation, it is far from it. In this case note, we see the first emergence of a theoretical categorisation from developmental psychology and object relations psychoanalysis (e.g. Bowlby, 1953; Bowlby, 1969) – a corpus of ideas known as Attachment Theory (see Howe et al., 1999 for a vademecum version of this theory written for social workers). This theory (AT) is a hugely powerful, and malleable, diagnostic and therapeutic framework in child welfare. Behavioural motifs such as shown by Seb are held by AT to be diagnostic of the presence or absence of a range of ‘attachment disorders’ (Howe et al., 1999). Its implicit invocation at this early stage is extremely consequential in terms of the way the case is formulated throughout, i.e. how the various professionals ‘‘make sense” of the situation, the production of the ‘‘facts”, and to its ultimate denouement. The leitmotif returns more and more strongly as the case unfolds, although on this first occasion, no particular alarm is raised

In the full paper (White and Wastell, in press), we concluded our reflections by lamenting the strong tendency of professional discourse:

‘‘to move from the idiographic and nuanced to an impoverished discourse of crude categorisations, furnished either by favoured psychological theories or administrative fiat. Rather than ‘‘ascend to the particular”,<sup>7</sup> collective professional categories are seen to create simplistic epistemic objects out of the complexity of lives in the living. Borrowing the vivid phraseology of Feyerabend (1999) abundance is conquered; singular instances are annihilated by tyrannical universals. . .”

The power of thought-styles is more vividly seen in the following example. Professional training courses play a key role in the socialisation of individuals into thought-collectives, as Fleck notes. Consider the following extract from a social worker’s reflective commentary, written as part of a post-qualifying programme:

‘‘The overall relationship between Ms. S. and Mr. H. was so dysfunctional that it seriously impacted on the attachments between Ms. S. and the children.... Initially I observed Jake’s attachment difficulties and suggested ways of Ms. S. offering a more nurturing style of parenting in an attempt to deal with this. I allowed myself to focus on symptoms of Jake’s attachment difficulties . . .in order to try and support Ms. S. in managing Jake.”

It is particularly noteworthy that, even as a part of a self-critical commentary, theoretical knowledge is invoked to ascribe certainty to an ambiguous situation. In this example, the social worker uses Attachment Theory to reinforce her formulations of the case and indeed as an integral part of her reflective ‘‘confessional” account. The theory has become treated as self-evidently true! It is not hard to see why theory has such a potent hold. Writing without apparent irony, leading proselytizers of AT argue:

‘‘[T]heories help to organize what we know. Theories also provide an economy of effort. They allow conceptual short-cuts to be taken. If the theory is powerful one, it might only take a few observations to locate a particular phenomenon as an example of a class of objects or behaviours. . .. Hypotheses help to guide future observations, the results of which aid practitioners in further testing and refining their initial assessments and observations (Howe et al., 1999, p. 228).”

We could not do better in proclaiming the malevolence of theory in pre-determining mind-sets and propelling confirmation biases! Returning to Fleck, we see how integral Theory is to ‘‘thought-styles”. When Fleck speaks of the ‘‘signal of resistance” that becomes a ‘‘thought constraint”, magically producing the ‘‘form directly to be perceived”, Theory is the sorcery he has in mind!

## 6. From facts to myths – sorry, myths to facts!

Soyez mystérieuses – Paul Gauguin

In a classic paper, entitled Management Epistemology, Stamper (1985) also attacks the common-sensical idea that Infor mation Systems are simply repositories of ‘‘neutral facts”. He cites two information systems in a local authority, one run by social workers to dispense small maintenance payments for houses classed as ‘‘unfit for habitation”. The other, run by the planning department, used the same categorisation, but to identify properties as derelict for urban planning purposes. Subsequent integration of these two systems led to many houses spuriously being designated derelict. This semantic confusion mattered little in the short term, as planning procedures required careful appraisal of individual properties. When, however, as a result of policy changes, the information subsequently was released to commercial estate agents, prices fell and property developers moved in. The denouement is poignantly described by Stamper:

‘‘The result, sadly ironic, was that the social workers, by using the law to help families with short-term repair problems, had accelerated their displacement from familiar neighbourhoods and even into homelessness in some cases.”

The point is well made, and Stamper goes on to call for a ‘‘new” paradigm in IS taking epistemology more seriously. He invites us to ‘‘raid philosophy for relevant ideas and to adapt them to solving practical problems where facts, values and obli gations are always mixed up”. Challenging the orthodoxy that information systems are repositories of objective facts is hardly new; a doughty minority of IS colleagues, marching under the so-called language action (LA) banner, have been tilting at this windmill for some time. Of note, is a recent special issue of the Communications of the ACM (Weigand, 2006), providing an ‘‘LA retrospective”, twenty years after the first publication of Winograd and Flores (1986). Lyytinen’s (1987) ‘‘formal language development” model, posited as an alternative to ‘‘reality mapping”, sees information systems as communication systems mediating messages, not storing facts:

‘‘The meaning of messages cannot be separated from the human action producing these messages. An IS is a social institution formed by social practices . . . understanding message meaning cannot be separated from the social practices into which the message exchange is interwoven. . . The managerial use of the IS is driven by the need to understand the meaning of data for action.”

Fleck’s work readily fits this critique, complementing and strengthening it. His work, as we noted, is not well known in our field, though there are sporadic mentions. In a rare example, Tuomi (2000) uses Fleck to reverse the conventional hierarchy (of data, information, knowledge) that data (i.e. simple facts) are meaningless until interpreted and meaning ascribed, whereupon they become information, which then becomes knowledge when significance and judgement are applied, or some variation on this basic hierarchical theme. Drawing on Fleck, Tuomi argues that data do not appear first on the scene, they emerge rather late in the day:

“Data can emerge only if a meaning structure, or semantics, is first fixed and then used to represent information. The meaning structure is articulated to become focal and structured. At this point we have created data. To arrive at this point a lot of cognitive effort and design work is needed. In most cases, there also has to negotiation among all interested parties to discuss the specific way the meaning is fixed. . . by defining a conceptual model for the database.”

And of course we know this from our own practice as researchers. Any piece of empirical research in an information system, albeit a time-limited and bespoke one; it is done to generate data, to answer a particular ‘‘research question”. The canonical research project begins with a literature review from which the much-prized theoretical framework<sup>8</sup> is assembled. The methodology then develops from this. When these preparatory exertions are complete we sally forth into the real-world research instrument in hand (questionnaire, interview schedule, whatever). And, at last, just before midnight, the data dutifully troop in, suitably ordered and configured. And unsurprisingly, the world looks much like the ontology of the theoretical framework; it has filled itself out, ‘fizzed and taken on form the way a paper flower expands in water’.<sup>9</sup>

Finally, let us pursue a little further the language-action (LA) view of Information Systems as communication systems, as mediating messages, or linguistic acts. Beynon-Davies (2010) has recently made a similar argument, arguing that an IS ‘‘is a special class of communication system involving the use of signs”. Here we shall draw on the work of Roland Barthes, both his seminal treatise on modern myths (1972) and his structuralist analysis of semiological systems, as elaborated in the Fashion System (1990), but suitably elaborated and adapted.<sup>10</sup> We are back to our ‘‘mysterious” opening, and it is time for demystification.

The essence of Barthes method (NB, method not theory) is the analysis of ‘‘utterances” (linguistic entities) in terms of a hierarchy of ‘‘systems of information”. Following Saussure, the basic unit of meaning is the sign, the union of a signifier (Sr) and a signified (Sd). Going back to our simple fact, we have two obvious systems: what Barthes calls the ‘‘real code”, i.e. the child playing happily in the absence of his mother, and the written expression of this in the multi-disciplinary record (the IS), which Barthes calls the “written code". At the level of the real code. the signifier is the empirical phenomenon of the “contented child/absent mother”. For the lay observer, it is not clear what, if any, particular meaning seeing such a spectacle would convey, and it is very likely to go unremarked. For the nurse, on the other hand, there is a very clear signified (Sd), namely a potential attachment disorder: the child’s equanimity is unusual, suggesting an avoidant/dismissing attachment pattern. At the level of the ‘‘written attachment code”, we have the entry in the record, the sentence which we have already encountered:/No problems when Mum left the room. . ./; this is the signifier, with the sign of the underlying real code as its referent, i.e. the conceptual (or propositional) representation of the ‘‘contented child/absent mother”.

Intuitively, and conventionally, our analysis would stop here, with two levels (as depicted in the lower half of Fig. 1): the behavioural evidence and its denotation in the IS. But Barthes goes further. Naïvely, there is merely a note in a file, which denotes a concrete real-world event. But Barthes now invites us to consider what this note (sign) connotes. What it connotes, of course, is the existence of a large corpus of concepts, techniques and therapies called Attachment Theory. This sign is level 3 of our analysis. But there is more to come. Barthes now asks us to think about what this sign means at a higher level still, the rhetorical level. Here, the level 3 sign becomes a ‘‘phraseological” signifier<sup>11</sup>; it specifies a particular way of thinking about the world, of configuring reality in terms of the role of the professional and the ontology of Attachment Theory.

‘‘Phraseology constitutes a connotative message, aimed at transmitting a certain vision of the world. . . (ibid, p. 36)”

In other words, not only does Attachment Theory exist (level 3), but it constitutes a natural, self-evident worldview; more than this, the rhetorical system says ‘‘this is how the world really is”. The use of natural is key here. AT is anything but natural, it is the provisional, man-made, cognitive product of an esoteric elite. But in the vademecum realm of the thought collective, where it is ‘‘dominant and binding”, determining ‘‘what cannot be thought in any other way”, it seems to be nature itself.

In Mythologies, Barthes describes this rhetorical function as myth-making. Myth here does not mean its common usage, as something untrue or non-existent. For Barthes (1972) myth is a type of speech, a ‘‘second order semiological system”, parasitical on the more immediate, denotational function of empirical ‘‘language objects”.<sup>12</sup> Like the killing of the hare (our first fact) there is a deeper significance to be sought which will make sense of the world. Myth provides this. Although built from the bottom up, properly the structure of Fig. 1 begins at the top, with a worldview (the Myth), and then works downwards: another reversal of the conventional data-knowledge hierarchy. The Myth thus makes the Facts: no Myth, no Fact. . .. No Attachment Theory, no attachment disorder. No woman, no cry! We could go on: no fish, no fowl; no hair, no hair-cut<sup>13</sup>. . . or is that ‘‘hare”?

The function of modern myths is not so different from the ancient ones, and a sign edifice identical in structure to Fig. 1 can readily be imagined for the murdered hare, Nurse N1, a latter-day Calchas! Just as the tragedies to befall the house of Agamemnon, descried by Calchas from the hare’s inauspicious death, are portrayed as natural and inevitable, modern myth performs a similar ideological function<sup>14</sup>:

‘‘Myth is constituted by the loss of historical quality of things. . . the quality that they were once made . . . what is got rid is their contingent, historical, fabricated quality. (p. 142)”

Myth thus converts History (the concrete event, its description, the messy realities of social science, etc.) to Nature (a priori, complete, incorrigible). Myth mystifies, peddling the ‘‘simplicity of essences”, endowing a natural and eternal justification for the historical and political. AT does not merely produce facts, it attempts to validate itself in the mundane practices of its votaries, masking ideological claims as factual statements. Theorisation is not an inductive process, as we like to think of it, or even abductive, it is purely semiological. Reversing the naturalist fallacy, myth ingeniously moves from ‘‘ought” to ‘‘is”, oh so stealthily performing its ‘‘cultural work” of naturalizing the status quo (Wastell, 2007). In the words of Fitzpatrick (1992, pp. 42–43):

‘‘Myth thus ventures forth to create the real, to endow it with forms and norms. . . a sustained creative force extending itself to and ordering a temporal world. Thus . . .by wedding the truths of Eternal Objects to times, [myth]:

Healed the abyss of torpid instinct and trifling flux,

Laundered it, lighted it, made it lovable with

Cathedrals and theories<sup>15</sup>”

Thus we pronounce Attachment Theory to be a myth, as are all social theories to the extent that they form an accepted thought-style which dominates a practice. And we have brushed against others, small ones and big ones, in these divagations, such as Reality Mapping in our opening paragraph (and the larger myth of techno-utopianism which stands behind it). And of course, the myth of Managerialism, for which Barthes (1972, p. 130) coined the neologism governmentality, ‘‘Government presented as the Essence of Efficacy”. . . the very idea!

So ends the Scherzo.

## 7. Implications for information systems research

We have found a strange footprint on the shores of the unknown. We have devised profound theories, one after another, to account for its origins. At last, we have succeeded in reconstructing the creature that made the footprint. And lo! It is our own. – Eddington.

Deflating and returning to Earth, in this final section we shall adumbrate some broad implications for IS research. Though based on parochial exemplars, we emphasise again that our messages are intended to be generic, addressing both the strategic direction of the field and reform of our research practice.

First though, an alarum for management practice: forgive the slogan, but Epistemology Matters! We have seen that epistemology is not the arcane preserve of the philosopher but a very practical business indeed. Managers, indeed professionals of any stamp, must be good epistemologists if they are to be effective practitioners. Stamper’s exemplars show this, and the Shannon Matthews case too. Performance indicators do not constitute ‘‘valid knowledge” of the distal processes and practices of the organisation, and managers risk disastrous errors if they assume otherwise. And how can managers gain valid knowledge; only by going and looking, ‘‘shooting the breeze” and practising a little ethnography of their own, not to ‘‘micro-manage” practice but to understand it. Without this appreciation of contingency and context, management information is meaningless, or worse. ‘‘If you can’t measure it, you can’t manage it”, proclaims the popular ukase. But measurement provides only a distorting, illusion of control, symptomatic of a failing paradigm. What is certainly true is that you can’t manage what you don’t understand. Managers should, first and foremost, be anthropologists of their organisations, that should be their epistemology! Their first question should always be, in the words of Ludwik:

How, then, did this empirical fact originate and in what does it consist?

## 7.1. Making IS matter – civic engagement research

For the IS ‘‘thought collective”, we make two main recommendations, to pay more attention to the public domain and to curb our fetish with Theory. Regarding the former, yes, there is certainly a smattering of IS research in the public domain, such as a recent Special Issue of this Journal (Hackney et al., 2008). But it remains a minority genre, despite the vast investments on ICT in the public sector, the burgeoning of e-Government as a global phenomenon, and the highly prominent failure of many major IT projects in the public sphere. Where, we may pertinently ask, was the IS community (in the UK) as ContactPoint stumbled towards inevitable failure (Peckover et al., 2008)? And what of the ICS? Or indeed the ill-starred Vet ting and Barring system,<sup>16</sup> suspended by the UK Government earlier this summer.

The latter is an interesting example. It may well have escaped our attention as it was not explicitly an IT project. But it is fundamentally an information system, and a highly consequential one to boot, involving the gathering of a range of data about individuals, some hard and some soft, and making decisions based on this about their suitability to work with children. Perhaps we have become rather too enthralled with technology? Galliers’ exhortation (Desouza et al., 2007) ‘‘to raise our sights beyond the IT artifact” is a timely injunction, to be heeded before it is too late. More generally, he advocates a broader, more catholic prospectus for the ‘‘discipline”:

‘‘We need to make the boundaries of our field more porous, to open up to the wider social science community. I use the term trans-disciplinary to describe this approach. It is the spaces between disciplines that require investigation and from which new knowledge will emerge (pp. 267–268).”

We hope we have provided here a good example of what Galliers had in mind. Techno-myopia is one problem, but perhaps we also spend too much of our time doing the wrong things, writing self-referential journal papers, for instance, when we should be speaking out and entering the public debate<sup>17</sup>? We strongly concur with Desouza et al. (2007) that the determination to make an impact, to make IS ‘‘really matter”, needs to replace the obsession with publication in soi-disant ‘‘top-quality” journals, which no-one but ourselves reads. How absurd is this! We should consider the full range of outlets for our work, including journals in other fields. This can be remarkably effective: our first ICS paper (Broadhurst et al., 2010), published in the British Journal of Social Work, received over 5000 downloads in its first year of publication in ‘‘advance access” form. Changing the culture of our thought collective will be hard, as Desouza et al. acknowledge, especially given its ingrained incentive structures and methodological prejudices, but boldness is all – we can ‘‘Begin it now” (ibid, p. 265)

Health-care projects do attract significant attention, but IS research is desultory in other areas of the public services. The clarion call for more has been made before. Writing in this Journal, one of us averred (Wastell, 2006, p. 213):

‘‘There is a general tendency in our field to privilege the private sector over the public; the overwhelming majority of research papers in mainstream IS journals deal with the former. Yet, IT investments in the public sector are vast and the transformations sought are at least as challenging, if not more so given the complexity of most public sector organisations compared to their business counterparts. There is certainly much interesting work to be done, and we are needed too. There is a general lack of capacity within the public sector to carry forward the change agenda . . . and collaborative research projects with academe have an important contribution to make in building this capacity.”

There is still much to be done, more than ever. In what is popularly called in the UK, the ‘‘age of austerity”, it is imperative to help out where we can. Government deficits are now the norm in much of the world, meaning that for the public services, more will need to be done with less. Building internal capacity for design and innovation will thus be critical. As we have noted, many current systems are poorly designed, often producing ‘‘contradictory organizational consequences”, in the euphemistic phraseology of Robey and Boudreau (1999). This is obviously the case for performance management, where the dysfunctionality of the target/audit regime is directly implicated in the scandal of Shannon Matthews, and there is much critical comment in the public discourse. For the IS of our second exhibit (the multi-disciplinary record), the problems are less obvious, but our research on the ICS has shown that there are major design issues to be addressed for electronic records in social care (White et al., 2010). As IS folks, we have much to say about design: our knowledge base is formidable, we have expertise to offer, and more importantly, much of value to learn in return (Wastell, 2010).

Money will be tight for all in the times ahead, including funding for research, and the need to demonstrate ‘‘research impact” will only grow stronger. But money is not required to do research, we should simply get out there and do it, as an ‘‘external good” (MacIntyre, 2007), an exercise of civic engagement. Desouza et al. (2007) have urged the case for ‘‘academic social responsibility” in IS research, calling for more ‘‘social activism” aimed at making ‘‘the world a better place”, rather as Flyvbjerg (2007) exhorts for social science in general. We could not agree more. In this piece, we have elided two forms of professional practice, IS research and social work. But we can go further than mere juxtaposition, and fashion an ethical slogan for the new era. How about IS research as social work, as a ‘‘helping profession” (Schein, 1999)!

## 7.2. Against Theory – reclaiming abundance

If not ‘‘Against Theory”, we are certainly highly sceptical. In IS research, and management research more broadly, theory is lionised. Egregiously, we describe theory as a lens, but what a preposterous metaphor! A lens might magnify reality, but it does not configure it. Theory, on the other hand, does distort, conforming the world to its own metaphysics. More like a pastry-cutter, or a stencil, than a lens! We have seen how theory operates to distort professional practice, enchanting by simplifying, creating spurious certainty. As a ‘‘transitional object”, it gives psychological security in an uncertain and complex world; but like all transitional objects, it can become a fetish, holding its votaries in fixated thrall (Wastell, 1999). By translating mundane human experience, articulated in vernacular language, into its rarefied and highly constrained vocabulary, Attachment Theory produces mystification not insight. It is a form of kitsch, whose essence is the production of ‘‘ontological security” (Giddens, 1984) through repetition:

‘‘Precisely by deflecting the creative and the uncertain, kitsch advances the repetitive, the secure and the comfortable, supplying the reassurance that what is to come will resemble what has gone before, that the hazards of innovation and uncertainty are far away, and that one is safe and secure in the routines of an unadventurous genre. (Binkley, 2000, p. 205)”

Just as with our managers and social workers, for IS research-practitioners, when we go forth armed with our precious ‘‘lenses”, theory is a ‘‘thought constraint”, magically producing the ‘‘form directly to be perceived”. A forteriori even. To the man with the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the whole world looks like TAM! And the same with the Strategic Alignment Model. ‘‘TAM, SAM, CALIBAN – had a new master, get a new man”. All kitsch, take your pick. . . and by this, we mean not to lampoon the original myth-makers (theory-builders), so much as their imitators and acolytes. That scourge of Theory, Latour (2004: 67), put it very well. Rebuking researchers seeking to appropriate Actor Network Theory (ANT) as a ‘‘lens”, he is scathing:

‘‘My kingdom for a frame! Very moving; I think I understand your desperation. But no, ANT is pretty useless for that. Its main tenet is that actors themselves make everything, including their own frames, their own theories, their own contexts, their own metaphysics, even their own ontologies. So the direction to follow would be more descriptions, I am afraid.”

Rather as we have done, assiduously eschewing theory in our two vignettes, anchored firmly in the realm of the vernacular, even if it is the Daily Mail!

Kicking the Theory-habit should also help suture the much-lamented gap between our research and IS practice. Enchantment with theory is partly responsible for this estrangement. The translation of the vernacular, phenomenal world of users into arcane, technical vocabularies is a form of mystification that inevitably creates insularity and hinders communication. Cold turkey may be tough. An amnesty for models, frameworks and theories should certainly be declared; let them all be handed in with impunity, renouncing their seductive Euclidean neatness and recondite terminology; time to ‘‘dig in the dirt” that their geometric ordering inevitably creates and sweeps from view (Wastell and McMaster, 2008). Ciborra (2002, p. 21) asks pertinently: ‘‘How come researchers have come to privilege the geometry of a line connecting abstract concepts in a model while they remain blind to the blurred reality that any, even slight, ethnographic study would have revealed?” The direction, indeed, is more description – go forth and see what treasures you may find, and if theory be sought, let it be grounded, comprising tentative, testable generalisations recognisable to the ‘members’ themselves.

An attitude of critical self-reflection is required in all forms of practice. Latour (1987, p. 22–23) distinguishes between ‘positive and negative modalities’. In the former, claims are made which preclude the interrogation of their conditions of production, whereas in the latter, such implicit claims are challenged, thereby admitting comment on their usefulness and veracity. In a similar vein, Rorty (1989) argues that human beings carry a culturally-sustained ‘‘final vocabulary”, which they employ to justify their actions and beliefs, and indeed their lives, and whose contingency is more or less ignored. Professionals, like other human agents, have their final vocabularies; recognition of this enjoins the need for a degree of seemly humility. For Rorty, humility is encouraged through exposure to as many final vocabularies as possible in order that we constantly recognise the contingent nature of our own. Theories can expand the vocabulary, but not if we confuse them with the real world, conquering abundance with tyrannizing universals (Feyerabend, 1999). In short:

‘‘The only theory worth having is that which you have to fight off, not that which you speak with profound fluency. (Hall, 1992, p. 280)”
