# Dealing with self-report bias in mobile Internet acceptance and usage studies

Keywords: Self-report bias Method bias Mobile applications Acceptance Usage

## A B S T R A C T

Self-report bias is a known validity threat to IS usage studies. Respondents may find it even more difficult to assess their actual usage of mobile services as these can be used in highly variable contexts. This paper examines the extent of self-report bias in mobile acceptance and usage studies and suggests countermeasures. We demonstrate that several Type-1 and Type-2 errors are made when relying on self-reports rather than log data. Weighing can partly mitigate self-report bias as older people are more accurate than younger people. Log data replace self-reports in order to reduce self-report bias in mobile acceptance studies.

## 1. Introduction

A vast body of knowledge is accumulating on why consumers accept and use mobile Internet services (also labeled as mobile applications) [1–3]. These studies largely rely on self-reports to assess usage levels [e.g., 1,2,4,5,6] with only few exceptions [e.g., 7]. Earlier studies on information systems (IS) usage show that using self-reports can introduce bias as respondents find it difficult to recall past behavior [8–10]. Other studies suggest that selfreport is only a marginal problem [11]. For mobile voice services, self-report bias has well been demonstrated by epidemiologists [12,13] as well as communication scholars [14–16]. For mobile data traffic, Gerpott [17] shows that self-reports on megabyte consumption are biased, which threatens the validity of explanatory models. However, self-report bias regarding specific categories of mobile Internet services has not yet been assessed.

Studies on self-report bias of IS usage have been mainly done in organizational settings, and cannot simply be generalized to mobile Internet services for the general public. First, compared to stationary computing, mobile services can be used anywhere and anytime [18]. The resulting variety of temporal and spatial contexts of use increases the cognitive difficulty of recalling usage behavior. Second, mobile services are being used for work and private purposes. This implies that mobile services can be used to support a wide variety of tasks, which again increases the cognitive difficulty of recalling past behavior. Third, smartphones offer such a wide variety of services that respondents may find it difficult to distinguish and classify services that they use on a daily basis. Fourth, as smartphones are typically carried around all day, mobile services are far more ingrained in the everyday life of people than stationary applications. As a result, respondents may find it difficult to reflect on which types of services they use where and when.

In this paper, we assess if and how self-report bias threatens validity of studies on mobile Internet service acceptance and usage. We do so by comparing log data collected directly on smartphones with self-reported usage levels from survey questions. We compare nine categories of mobile Internet services with varying usage levels. Besides examining the magnitude and source of selfreport bias, we examine whether using self-reported usage levels leads to Type-1 or Type-2 errors in an explanatory model. Based upon the analyses, we suggest counter-measures to mitigate selfreport bias.

The paper contributes to the field of mobile Internet service acceptance and usage by illustrating how self-report bias may threaten the validity of such studies. More generally, the paper contributes to discussions in IS on self-report bias and method bias in system usage studies [9,19,20]. Practically, the paper has implications

## 2. Background

Self-report bias is a well-known methodological issue, and has been discussed in domains like medical research [21], organizational behavior [22,23] and consumer marketing [24]. Self-report bias may result from recall inaccuracy as well as the tendency to respond to questions in socially desirable ways [25,26]. Self-report bias depends on the nature of the construct, the cognitive capabilities of the respondent, the disposition of the respondent towards socially desirability and situational and task-related conditions [22,27,28].

In the field of Information Systems (IS), self-report bias is typically discussed in relation to the concept of system usage, which is prominent in IS success models [29] as well as IS acceptance models [30]. In a literature review, Jeyaraj et al. [19] find that almost all IT acceptance studies rely on perception scales to measure systems use. Self-report bias is demonstrated in several IS usage studies. Collopy [31] finds that computer use is not well estimated by users as infrequent users overestimate and frequent users underestimate usage levels. However, other IS scholars argue that self-report bias is not a major issue. Deane et al. [11] show that log data and self-reports on computer usage are moderately to strongly correlated. Venkatesh et al. [32] found a significant relation between intention to use and actual usage figures.

Besides showing that computer-recorded usage data and selfreport scales are weakly correlated, Straub et al. [9] also show that self-report bias threatens the validity of explanatory models: effect sizes for the TAM model are higher when using self-report scales than when using computer-recorded usage data. Recently, debate on how to conceptualize self-report bias has been (re)started. For instance, Burton-Jones [20] argues to distinguish knowledge bias (i.e. not providing accurate assessments because of the respondents’ lack of knowledge of the trait score) and rating bias (i.e. not providing accurate assessments because the respondents’ unwillingness to do so).

Self-report bias regarding mobile phone use has especially received attention from epidemiologists, in order to validate studies on health risks associated with mobile phones. Several epidemiologists show that users overestimate both the frequency and duration of calls [13,33]. Others find that users underestimate call frequency but overestimate call duration [12,34]. One study shows that consumers are better at recalling frequency than total duration [35]. Recently, scholars from communication science have similarly shown self-report bias for mobile voice calling [14–16]. Demographics like gender and age are related to self-report bias according to some studies [16,36] but not to others [14,37]. Frequency of use is related to self-report bias according to some studies [12,16] but not according to others [37].

As far as we are aware, only one published study examines selfreport bias for mobile data services. Gerpott [17] examines selfreport bias by comparing several perception scales with the actual number of megabytes transmitted over the mobile network. He finds that self-report bias may be a large threat to explanatory models as correlations are higher when using self-report scales than with log data. He finds such inflated correlations for not only other subjective constructs but even for objective ones such as tariffs and costs levels. While Gerpott’s study provides insights in self-report bias for mobile data usage, he does not examine the different categories of mobile Internet services, which can be used by consumers.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Sample

A user panel comprising 20,000 households was used to sample respondents. The user panel is representative for the Dutch population in terms of demographics. The panel is regularly renewed through active recruitment (i.e. no self-selection bias is involved) and panelists are typically not compensated for taking part in surveys.

From the panel, a random sample was drawn. Next, an initial questionnaire was sent to the persons in the sample inviting them to participate in the study. As the software to collect log data only works with iPhone and Android smartphones, Blackberry and Windows phone users are excluded from the study. The initial questionnaire extensively explained how log data would be collected, stored and analyzed in the study. As the first round of recruiting did not lead to sufficient response, the procedure was repeated but only including the subset of panelists that were known to possess a smartphone. Finally, in a third recruiting round, panelists were approached who had already participated in an earlier study in which log data on smartphone use was collected.

After data cleaning for partial non-response, the three rounds of recruitment resulted in data from 1653 persons that filled in the initial questionnaire, out of which 519 were willing to participate in the study. Of the reasons for non-participation provided, the core reason was privacy (by 16% of the respondents). For 15% of the respondents the reasons were related to typical non-response reasons, such as holidays, sickness and travelling abroad. Technical reasons were mentioned by 2% of the respondents, and 3% indicated their employer would not allow them to download apps on their phone. Other reasons provided included low usage of the smartphone and no experience or know-how to install applications on the smartphone.

Although 519 respondents initially indicated that they were willing to participate in the study, only 369 downloaded and installed the app. Only 233 participated for the full four weeks of the study. Reasons to drop out during the study were related to technical problems, like battery drainage and reduced performance of the phone. Some respondents dropped out because they upgraded to a new version of their operating system. Sample characteristics are given in Table 1. On average, participants were 46 years old.

## 3.2. Log data measures

To carry out smartphone measurement, a number of software tools are available, for example LiveLab [38] and Device Analyzer (deviceanalyzer.cl.cam.ac.uk). The present study utilizes the commercially available smartphone measurement application from Arbitron Mobile. The measurement application runs on the background of the mobile phone, and transmits log files regularly to the server. The application can be downloaded from the Apple and Android app stores. Participants were given the opportunity to view a dashboard with their personal usage numbers during the period of the study. The software was pretested in 2011; identified technical problems with the software were solved afterwards.

The software logged each action of the user over a period of 28 days (30 October–27 November 2012). Each time an application is launched, the software logs the application name, date and time, and duration in which it is displayed on the foreground of the device. The software classifies applications into specific service categories using automated content analysis, see Table 2. The researchers manually checked and verified the most frequently used applications and found no errors. In the paper, we include information services (i.e. browsing, maps/navigation, news/ information), business services (i.e. productivity tools), entertainment services (i.e. gaming, online music), and communication services (i.e. social networking, instant messaging/chat, and voice/video over IP). More traditional services like voice and SMS are not included as they are outside the scope of mobile Internet services. Mobile transaction services like payment and ticketing are not included as current usage levels are very low in the Netherlands

We extract metrics from the log data to measure the frequency of use. Frequency of use is measured through the number of days over the 28-day observed period in which the service category was used at least once. Descriptive statistics are given in Table 3. Most frequently used are instant messaging, social networking and browsing services. The frequency distributions of the variables do not exhibit extreme outliers.

## 3.3. Survey measures

To measure the perceived use of mobile service categories, participants filled out a short questionnaire. Participants were asked to which extent they were currently using specific categories of mobile Internet services. The survey scale used a five-point scale that is often used when measuring perceived usage levels [e.g., 32]. Table 4 displays the mean values and standard deviations for those respondents that were included in the final sample.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Self-report bias

Correlations between log data metrics and self-report measures are moderate to strong, see Table 5. Especially social networking, gaming and productivity show strong correlations. Correlations are low for news/information, online music and VOIP service categories.

Appendix 1 visualizes the extent of self-report bias across the different answer categories. For Browsing, Social networking, VOIP and Productivity, respondents appear rather accurate when assessing the number of days they actually used the service categories. Still, for these four categories, there are severe outliers, especially for those that report to ‘Never’ use the service category. For Gaming, respondents that report a weekly usage overestimate their actual use. The Gaming category also shows a high variance in the boxplots of each answer category.

Severe underestimation takes place for Instant messaging/Chat. The median value for those reporting ‘Never’ to use Instant messaging is in fact 10 days per month, and the same holds for those answering ‘Tried it’. On the other hand, few of the respondents answering ‘Daily basis’ overestimate their actual usage.

Overestimation is present for Maps/Navigation, Online music and News/Information. Most people, who report to use Maps on a weekly or daily basis, actually only use the service category a few times per month. For News, there hardly appears to be a relation between the log data and self-reports. Most respondents overestimate their actual use. For News, the bar charts for those answering ‘Never’ and ‘Daily basis’ even look highly similar.

## 4.2. Self-report bias as a threat to explanatory models

We test multiple regression models that explain mobile service use through demographic variables. We purposefully use demographics as predictors rather than subjective constructs to refrain from the broader issue of common method bias.

Table 7 shows the result of linear regression analysis. Explained variance is similar when comparing self-report and log data models. Only for Browsing, explained variance is inflated when using the self-report measures. While roughly half the effects found in the self-report models are corroborated through the log data models, the other half is not. As such, these effects pose potential Type-1 errors. At the same time, several effects are found in the log data models that were not present in the self-report model. As such, these effects pose potential Type-2 errors.

Table 8 summarizes the corroboration and potential Type-1 and Type-2 errors based on Table 7. Specifically, we find that the effect of age on use is mostly corroborated with the log data. Gender is corroborated in three models, but is not found to be significant in two other models. Income level, household size and employment status pose the most risk for Type-1 and Type-2 errors.

Type-1 and Type-2 errors do not appear to be consistent for specific service categories. For almost all service categories, at least one potential error is present. For VOIP, not even one of the effects found in the self-report model is corroborated when using log data.

## 5. Discussion

Our findings show that self-report bias is not equally present for all categories of mobile services. For Browsing, Social networking and Productivity service categories, respondents assess their usage relatively accurately. Self-report bias is strongest for Maps, News and Online music, which are all overestimated, and Instant messaging, which is underestimated. Both hedonic and utilitarian categories of services appear in the list of accurately and inaccurately estimated service categories. The acceptance rate of the mobile Internet service categories also does not play a clear role: self-report bias is found both in less popular service categories like Maps and Online music and in more popular service categories like News and Instant messaging.

## 5.1. Is self-report bias a significant threat to mobile acceptance and usage studies?

Although self-report bias is present for several service categories, self-reports do correlate with objectively measured usage levels. However, we did show that several Type-1 and Type-2 errors are made when relying on survey data. Our findings show that while effects of age on usage levels are relatively robust, effects of gender, income, household size and employment status are not. This is especially concerning since several mobile IS acceptance and usage studies have relied on self-reports to draw inferences on the effects of age [39], gender [39–41], income [42], work status [42] and education level [43].

Such potential errors were found in our study, even though the predictors in our models are demographics, which can be assumed to have limited measurement error. That is, when using other subjective constructs as predictors, the risk of drawing false conclusions is even higher due to common method bias [44]. More research is required to retest well-established explanatory models on mobile Internet service acceptance and usage with log data metrics rather than survey metrics, in order to corroborate these results.

## 5.2. Possible counter measures

Weighing could be a means to reduce response bias found in this study. Earlier studies on mobile voice and texting services that show that overestimation and underestimation cannot be well explained through demographics [14]. However, we did find that older people assess their usage more accurately than younger people, and people living alone are more accurate than living in families. More studies are needed to confirm these findings, and to assess whether weighing provides a reliable counter measure.

Other counter measures to reduce self-report bias should be considered, such as specifying the context of survey items and using multiple measures. Various studies discuss the cognitive process of answering questions about behavior and suggest ways to improve accuracy [28,45]. Future studies may systematically vary other aspects that lead to self-report bias, i.e. individual and situational factors that make respondents more prone to give socially desirable answers [46]. Adding different types of selfreport survey scales would of course allow even more fine-grained analysis. Especially asking respondents to report the number of minutes and sessions per day of the week would allow more finegrained comparison of log data and self-report data.

Logging software to measure usage of mobile applications on smartphones is becoming increasingly available. Using logging software to collect data on actual usage eliminates issues of recall and social desirability bias. While this paper demonstrates the merits of using log data over self-report data, there are also limitations. For instance, especially earlier versions of the logging software used in this paper contained bugs that lead to unreliable data. As logging software is improving and as there only two main operating systems left to program for, faultiness in software should be reduced. Another main limitation of using logging software is associated non-response bias. As logging software is more intrusive then answering a survey, respondents may choose not to participate in a study due to privacy limitations. In our study, we have shown that such non-response bias can be harnessed, as only 16% of the original sample refused to participate due to privacy concerns. We harnessed privacy concerns of potential respondents by applying four tactics. First, we separated data collection and data analysis between two different organizations. Data that is being collected is anonymized, and the party analyzing the data cannot trace it back to individuals. Second, we partnered with well-known organizations to foster trust of respondents, in this case with a national statistical office. Third, we provided explicit informed consent forms that made transparent what would be done with the data and by whom, supported by a clear communication strategy. Fourth, dashboards on usage were made available and visible to the respondents to increase transparency on what was being collected, although these dashboards were sparsely consulted.

## 5.3. Limitations

A limitation of the study is that there is no commonly accepted scale for IS usage [47,48]. Sun and Teng [48] find that there is a very wide variety of IS usage constructs in literature and no agreement on what to use. Doll and Torkzadeh [49] argue that more frequent and longer use need not increase productivity and therefore argue to measure the effects of use. Burton-Jones and Straub [47] have reconceptualized the systems usage construct by incorporating the structure and function of systems use. Future studies could compare log data metrics with a broader set of IS usage scales used in literature.

Our study design does not allow distinguishing recall bias from social desirability bias [cf., 20]. While Joinson [50] demonstrates that social desirability bias is limited when using anonymous webbased surveys, as done in the present study, Booth-Kewley et al. [51] show this effect does not exist. Lelkes et al. [52] show that complete anonymity may reduce social desirability response but at the same time increase recall bias since accountability and motivation to answer precisely and thoughtfully reduces.

## 6. Conclusions and future research

Self-report bias poses a significant threat to the validity of mobile Internet acceptance and usage studies. This study is important not just for IS Usage models but also intention-to-use models since a common assumption is that intention to use correlates with actual usage. Log data and self-report metrics are moderately to strongly correlated, but for four out of nine service categories, respondents severely misestimate their usage levels. Relying on self-reports rather than log data produces misleading conclusions, as we demonstrated in this paper. Moreover, there are distinct different patterns between different categories of mobile Internet services.

Most IS usage research studies still apply mono method designs. It is remarkable that several scholars develop IS usage scales without calibrating them with directly observed log data, especially as logging software to directly measure application usage on smartphones is becoming increasingly available. Mobile technologies will increasingly enable multi-method or mixed method studies. The costs of collecting log data on behavior are reducing, thus it becomes a viable option for IS scholars. We thus urge scholars to substitute perception data on usage or intention to use with log data metrics.

Practically, our study puts a warning sign to (marketing) firms, policy makers and consultants that rely on self-reported acceptance and usage rates. Relying on self-reports alone will likely produce misleading conclusions and erroneous policy or management implications. Combining self-reports with log data will be increasingly the way forward for analyzing the mobile market.

We hope this study provides new avenues for studying the issue of self-report bias in mobile acceptance and usage studies as well as IS in general. New techniques, such as the smartphone (and tablet) measurement approach utilized in this paper, are becoming available to automatically collect log data on systems usage. Surveys will remain relevant as a cheaper and easier tool to measure perceived usage levels as well as capturing subjective motivations and attitudes that explain usage levels. While doing so, calibrating survey measures on usage levels should be constantly on our mind.
