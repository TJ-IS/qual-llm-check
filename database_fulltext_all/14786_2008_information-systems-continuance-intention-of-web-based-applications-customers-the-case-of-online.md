## A B S T R A C T

The proliferation of the Internet has not only allowed businesses to offer their products and services through web-based applications, but it has also undermined their ability to retain their customers. It has reduced search costs, opened up barriers to entry, and diminished distinctiveness of firms. Effective retention of customers allows firms to grow in size and popularity, thereby increasing their profitability. We extended Commitment–Trust theory, an expectation–confirmation model, and technology acceptance theory to develop a model of IS continuance intention of customers of web-based applications. Relationship commitment and trust were found to be central to IS continuance intention. Also, perceived empowerment influenced relationship commitment, while perceived security influenced trust. Our findings thus supported traditional intention factors, highlighting the role of trust as a stronger predictor of intention than commitment but, contradicting findings from marketing research, trust was found to be a stronger predictor of retention in the e-commerce context.

## 1. Introduction

The drive for differentiation and speed-to-market has led in increased use of online banking. However this has also resulted in a large number of comparable alternative sites and users find it easy to switch between them. Furthermore, factors affecting adoption of a site depend mainly on its functionality and user interface, two characteristics that can be easily imitated. In our study, we examined users’ continuance intention.

Expectation–confirmation theory [3], based on cognitive beliefs and affect, and Commitment–Trust theory [20], which emphasizes relationship commitment and trust as central to successful relational exchange, are both relevant to continuation. Though prior studies have discussed the importance of trust [5,11,19], little empirical work has been performed. The objective of our research was therefore to develop a model of relationship benefits, termination cost, commitment, empowerment, shared value, etc. as determinants of the IS continuance intention. We then validated the model empirically through a survey of online banking customers.

## 2. Research model and hypotheses

Based on the Commitment–Trust theory, Morgan and Hunt ([20], from now on MH94) constructed a Key Mediating Variable (KMV) model to prove that relationship commitment and trust were central to successful relational exchange. Building on this, expectation–confirmation theory (IS continuance intention) and TAM (perceived empowerment), we hypothesized that relationship commitment and trust mediated the relationship between six precursors and IS continuance customer intention. Fig. 1 shows our model.

The original KMV model was modified to fit online banking by

(1) replacing the consequences of relationship commitment and trust with the IS continuance intention construct;

(2) adding user empowerment as a precursor to relationship commitment, and replacing opportunistic behavior, a precursor to trust, with perceived security because IS continuance intention, empowerment, and security issues are more applicable than traditional marketing.

From a customer perspective, service encounters on the Internet must be safe and secure to conform to expectations. Once customers perceive their interactions as secure, they have confidence and, will be willing to share sensitive information. The level of risk that customers are willing to accept in future transactions indicates their level of trust in the partnership.

## 2.1. Relationship commitment and trust

Relationship commitment assumes that a relationship is important and should be maintained. Legal and policy issues make banks specify that they will not share information with third parties without the customer’s permission and communicate this policy on their site.

Therefore, we expected that,

H1. Customers who are committed to their relationship with their online bank are likely to continue using the services offered by that bank.

## 2.1.1. Trust

Trust is important in Internet environments: ample trust is needed for the successful operation of such computing environments [7]. Trust involves the ‘‘willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party’’ [19].

Our view of perceived security implies that there is customer control over their transactions. However, we assumed that intention depends on commitment and trust because relationship commitment implies that customers and a business believe that a relationship is worth retaining indefinitely and that trust in a partner whose services are consistent and reliable can reduce stress and provide more stability. Therefore,

H2. Customers who trust their online bank are likely to continue using the services offered by that bank.

Trust is also assumed to influence relationship commitment: trusted relationships are very highly valued, and partners desire to commit to such relationships [25]. In web-based electronic commerce, customers must first trust the business before they commit to it, since maintaining a relationship with the company involves potential vulnerability. Thus, our third hypothesis was:

H3. Customers who trust their online bank are likely to commit themselves to their relationship with that bank.

## 2.2. Other determinants of relationship commitment and trust

## 2.2.1. Relationship benefits

Products yield desired benefits when they fulfill the consumers’ needs. Therefore, relationship benefit occurs when customers feel that product offerings are effective and up to date. For example, online bill paying eliminates the costs of stamps and ordering checks and customers can review their account activity, apply for loans, etc. Firms that deliver superior relationship benefits will be highly valued, and customers will commit themselves to maintaining relationships with them. Therefore:

H4. Customers who receive high benefits from a relationship with their online bank are likely to commit themselves to that relationship.

## 2.2.2. Relationship termination

Relationship termination implies difficulty in substituting services due to switching costs. Such costs may be monetary or inconvenient in nature, such as a learning curve or loss of online history. Anticipating a high switching cost, customers will maintain the existing relationship. Therefore:

H5. Customers who expect high costs for terminating their relationship with an online bank are likely to commit themselves to that relationship.

## 2.2.3. Perceived (user) empowerment

Empowerment implies that customers want to bank at their convenience and to control (or perceive to control) their banking. This differs from a marketing use of the term, which involves the idea of service satisfaction. E-banks empower their customers to perform their needs whenever, wherever, and however they desire; even novice users have immediate access to vital banking functions, 24 h a day, 365 days a year.

Our study highlighted two major features supporting user empowerment: perception of control and freedom to use their own initiatives. Kappelman and Guynes [18] showed how a bank provided its employees with a small empowering experience during the implementation of a large-scale IS change; it resulted in a large payoff in user perception of control and their initiative. Therefore, empowerment was viewed as one way to incorporate perception of control in the KMV model as a precursor to relationship commitment. Therefore,

H6. Empowered customers are likely to commit themselves to their relationship with an online bank.

## 2.2.4. Shared value

Shared value was defined as the extent to which partners had mutual understanding about their behaviors, goals, and policies. Individuals attempt to reduce uncertainty by observing the actions in which they engage. Hart et al. [16] found that shared value was a factor leading to trust. Sitkin and Roth [23] claimed that shared value was a precursor of trust, because individuals with dissimilar values would be perceived as operating under a different worldview. As a result, they may be branded as ‘‘untrustworthy.’’ Therefore:

H7. Customers who share similar values with their online bank are likely to commit themselves to their relationship with that bank.

H8. Customers who share similar values with their online bank are likely to trust that bank.

## 2.2.5. Communication

Communication involves increased credibility, timeliness, and accuracy of information exchanged. It is a major precursor of trust [20]; it can induce trust by helping to resolve disputes, and align perceptions and expectations [8]. In e-commerce, customers are likely to trust a firm that responds to their electronic complaints quickly, makes its policies regarding privacy and security available, quickly confirms that a transaction has occurred, or informs them about new offerings and anticipated system downtime. Therefore:

H9. Customers who experience effective communication with their online bank are likely to trust that bank.

## 2.2.6. Perceived security

Perceived security is defined as the perception that interactions are safe and secure. Two components underlie this definition— perception of security when using the web to transmit the sensitive information and when using the services offered by the banking site. Perceived security can be improved by enhancing the user perception of safe and secure transactions while interacting with the site. Therefore:

H10. Customers who perceive that their online bank’s services are secure are likely to trust that bank.

## 2.2.7. Control variables

To increase the internal validity of the findings, four situationspecific variables were incorporated into our study: convenience, reputation, cost, and quality of banking service. These four are most frequently mentioned in previous studies as important factors in the customer’s choice of financial institution.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Pilot study

Before conducting the survey, the instrument was given to a group of fifteen graduate students who stated that they had used and were familiar with online banking. The pilot respondents were interviewed after completion of the questionnaire. Their comments resulted in refinement of the instrument in terms of its length, format, readability, and clarity.

The sponsoring financial institution was also asked to review the questionnaire for its appropriateness to the firm’s business– client relations. This review resulted in elimination of a specific item measuring trust – ‘‘the bank cannot be trusted at times’’ – due to its negative connotation. The exclusion of this item did not pose a major threat to construct validity, since there were five additional items assessing trust. In addition, one of the remaining items (‘‘the firm provides service I completely trust’’) had a similar meaning to that eliminated. Some wording changes were also made.

## 3.2. Samples and data collection

This study employed a field survey of online banking customers to gather data for hypothesis testing and to address research objectives. A well-established financial institution with an online banking operation was asked to participate in the study. This institution is currently one of the twenty largest banking and financial institutions in the US, and operates a nationwide branch network. A web-based Internet banking service was introduced in the early 1990s; it links to the bank’s backend server.

The backend system is an integration of several disparate systems due to recent mergers and acquisitions: some were mainframe-based and others were client/server based. Through common web browsers, individuals with an account user name and password can use the system to check their account balance, view detailed statement, transfer money between their accounts, pay bills, and access customer service functions such as order a check, stop payment, change address, etc.

The sample consisting of 4667 users were randomly drawn from the customer database. To ensure that the samples were regular users of online banking, only those having accessed their accounts online at least once a month in the past 6 months were eligible.

Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire sent from the headquarters of the institution and signed by an Executive Vice President. Participation in the study was voluntary, and customers were assured that their individual responses would be treated as confidential. The instruments and associated cover letters made no reference to commitment, trust, or IS continuance intention. Instead, the cover letter stated that the purpose of the study was to ‘‘constantly improve the online banking experience.’’

In order to increase the response rate, 2333 users were sent a reminder two weeks later, asking them to complete the survey if they had not already done so. To test whether there was a late response bias, a t-test (equality of means) was conducted to compare the earliest one hundred respondents to the last one hundred on all characteristics and demographics. The bank did not allow further follow-up requests at the risk of being perceived as a ‘‘spammer.’’ Results, as displayed in Table 1, showed that the two groups of participants did not differ significantly (p < 0.5).

Since the sample was drawn from customers of one financial institution, an effort was made to ensure that it did not differ significantly from the overall population. The demographic profile of the sample was compared with that of general Internet users. Table 2 lists the distribution of the sample’s demographics against the distribution of general Internet users’ demographics, as reported in the GVU Center WWW User Survey [14]. Though there are some minor differences, the distributions of both populations exhibit generally similar patterns in all categories. People between 21 and 50 years of age, for example, constitute three quarters of both Internet users and survey respondents. There are only 0.4% of survey respondents below age 20 as compared to 6.09% of the total Internet population, but that is possibly because users below 20 years of age have little need for online banking.

1058 sets of the questionnaires were returned during the 3- month period. Sixteen responses contained a large number of missing data, and were excluded from the study. Respondents who reported a length of usage less than 6 months and frequency of usage less than once a month were excluded from the analysis. This resulted in a final sample of 1004 and a final response rate of 21.5%. This rate was considered acceptable for mail surveys previously conducted by the bank, which had typically yielded response rates of 10–12%.

## 3.3.1. Relationship commitment and trust

Six items for relationship commitment and trust were adopted from the original MH94 scale. One item (‘‘the relationship with. . . is very much like a family’’) was dropped because it was deemed more appropriate for personal than business–client relationships.

## 3.3.2. Precursors of relationship commitment

For relationship termination cost, a five-item scale used by MH94 was used, with an additional item measuring the ease of finding a comparable alternative should the customer decide to terminate the relationship. With minor wording modifications, three items from MH94, measuring rate of return, satisfaction, and product performance, were adopted to represent relationship benefit. The fourth item, measuring added value beyond the customer’s expectation, was added to represent MH94’s notion that firms must continuously add value to their offerings.

User empowerment consisted of two components—freedom of initiative and judgment, and perception of control. For freedom of initiative and judgment, five items were adopted from Hartline and Ferrell’s [17] eight-item scale, which measured employees’ freedom to use their own initiative and judgment in performing their jobs. Three items were not used because they asked questions specific to the employee–manager relationship. A three-item scale for perceivedcontrolwas adaptedfromthe measures used byHarrison et al. [15], who developed the scale from the theory of planned behavior (TPB). These measures were considered appropriate because TPB has been proven to be reliable and valid in a variety of settings.

Shared value was operationalized with a four-item scale, measuring the extent to which banks understood customers’ behaviors, goals, and concerns, and the extent to which customers understood their bank’s business policies.

## 3.3.3. Precursors of trust

For communication, the original four-item scale in MH94 was reduced to two. The two were dropped because they were deemed inappropriate for online banking. In addition, two additional items were included to reflect the MH94 assertion that communication could be established through the sharing of meaningful and timely information, both formally and informally.

Two components underlie the definition of perceived security— perception when transmitting sensitive information, and perception when using the services offered by the site. Measures for each component were represented by a two-item scale, adapted from Salisbury et al.’s [27] scale for perceived web security.

## 3.3.4. IS continuance intention

The high costs of collecting real data on customer activity in maintaining or terminating their banking relationships and the unpredictable temporal pattern of customers leaving the bank prevented the use of real IS continuance intention data. However, most previous retention research has used intent attitudes as surrogates for actual behavior (e.g. [12,22]). Furthermore, TRA specifies that behavioral intentions constitute the most immediate determinants of actual behavior [10]. Therefore, customer intention to remain with a bank should be an acceptable predictor of actual IS continuance intention and, thus it was used in our study. This variable was operationalized with a three-item scale asking respondents their chances of switching from or terminating the use of online bank within the next 3, 6, and 12 months.

## 3.3.5. Control variables and demographic data

The four control variables were each operationalized using a twoitem scale. Demographic data from individual customers were also collected to verify the representativeness of the sample as well as for possible alternative explanations for proposed causal relationships.

## 3.5. Data analysis

The research model was analyzed with SEM using Amos 4.0. Before fitting the model, the selection of control variables in the structure model had to be performed. Moore [28] incorporated control variables that were significantly correlated with dependent variables in the statistical model at $p < 0 . 0 5$ . In our study, however, the large sample size $( n = 1 0 0 4 )$ would have caused a significant result if any variable had a correlation of 0.062 or higher. Using the 0.05 or 0.01 significance level at this sample size can lead to a test with inflated statistical power [6] and cause a serious problem of distinguishing between significance and meaningfulness [24].

Williams [26] proposed general guidelines for interpreting the ‘significance’ of correlation:

 <0–0.20 slight correlation; almost no relationship,

 0.20–0.40 low correlation; small relationship,

 0.40–0.70 moderate correlation; substantial relationship,

 0.70–0.90 high correlation; marked relationship,

 >0.90 very high correlation; solid relationship.

Following these guidelines, control variables with moderate or higher correlation to dependent variables (i.e., relationship commitment, trust, and IS continuance intention) would be incorporated in the model. Table 6 lists means, standard deviations, and correlations for the independent, dependent, and control variables. Only quality had a correlation value of more than 0.4 (with trust and commitment). Therefore, the two paths (quality to trust and quality to commitment) were also used in our model.

## 4. Research findings

## 4.1. Test of model fit

The model was tested for model fit and parameter estimates. Our study used several fit indices that were less susceptible to model complexity and sample size: relative chi-square $\bar { ( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathsf { d } . \mathsf { f } . ) }$ Comparative Fit Index (CFI), RMSEA, GFI, and the Adjusted GFI (AGFI). Relative chi-square was calculated by dividing the chisquared statistic by its degrees of freedom. Gefen et al. [13] indicated that a ratio smaller than 3–1 was considered acceptable, while the chi-squared should be as small as possible. CFI was proposed by Bentler [1]; with values close to 1 showing a very good fit. Finally, using a complexity-inclined index, F0, RMSEA was adjusted to compensate for the effect of model complexity and, thus, to provide a more unbiased indication of a good fit. A heuristic for using RMSEA was proposed by [4]; values of 0.08 or smaller indicate acceptable fits. The GFI and AGFI values surpassed the 0.90 threshold indicate acceptable fits [2].

Fitting the proposed model to the entire sample resulted in a chi-square value of 3076 (d.f. = 787, p < 0.01) and a relative chisquare value of 3.91. Furthermore, the model had a CFI value of 0.98 and a RMSEA value of 0.054. As indicated by Square Multiple Correlations (SMC), the model explained 62.7% of the variance in trust, 39.6% of the variance in relationship commitment, and 22.1% of the variance in IS continuance intention. Overall, the model showed evidence of a good fit. The CFI value obtained was more than 10% higher than the original KMV model in MH94. The RMSEA value was well below the 0.08 threshold. However, the relative chisquare was above the acceptable norm of 3–1, as proposed by Gefen et al. Finally, the GFI and AGFI obtained values of 0.94 and 0.93, respectively, exceeded the minimum threshold (Table 7).

## 4.2.1. Relationship commitment and trust

Consistent with H1 and H2, relationship commitment and trust were positively related to IS continuance intention. The data also showed that trust had a positive effect on relationship commitment (H3).

## 4.2.2. Precursors of relationship commitment

According to the proposed model, four variables were hypothesized to affect relationship commitment. Consistent with H4, termination cost (H4) was negatively related to relationship commitment. User empowerment (H6), as described in H6, was positively related to relationship commitment. However, inconsistent with H5 and H7, relationship benefit (H5) and shared value (H7) had no significant effect on relationship commitment.

## 4.2.3. Precursors of trust

The effects of three precursors of trust were examined. The data confirmed that shared value was positively related to trust (H8). Consistent with H9, communication was positively related to trust. Finally, perceived security was positively related to trust (H10).

## 4.2.4. Control variables

Quality was incorporated into the fitted model as a factor affecting trust and relationship commitment. The path coefficient between quality and trust was significant at the p < 0.01 level while the path coefficient between quality and relationship commitment was significant at the p < 0.05 level.

In summary, the path coefficients of the proposed model confirmed all the hypothesized relationships, except that between relationship commitment and two of its precursors—relationship benefit and shared value. ## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Discussion of research findings

IT enhancements in security, communication, and shared value apparently influenced users’ trust of online banking. Trust, in turn, can significantly influence IS continuance intention of users, as suggested by CRM. Perceived user empowerment and trust can influence relationship commitment, as it influences IS continuance intention of users.

The validations of H1, H2 and H3 are in line with previous studies in traditional business contexts. Therefore, our findings implied that there are few if any fundamental differences between factors determining IS continuance intention in traditional business settings and user retention for businesses operating in e-commerce settings. In support of this view, Reichheld et al. [21] indicated that leading e-commerce executives claimed that the traditional principles of customer loyalty were strengthened when businesses incorporated e-commerce sites or moved onto the Internet.

Cost saving was cited repeatedly by many respondents in the open-ended section as a reason they switched to the institution’s online banking. In addition, satisfaction was found to have strong associations with other exogenous variables, as evidenced by high correlation values between relationship benefit and other variables in the model: communication (0.7), shared value (0.7), and user empowerment (0.7). Therefore, there may be a satisfaction halo effect (or multicollinearity) that resulted in the relationship between benefits and commitment disappearing when all exogenous variables were included in the analysis. Finally, the effect of benefit on commitment was expected to be lower in the ecommerce environment. Because the Internet increased competition, firms operating there have been forced to offer high incentives to encourage customer switching. Since people who adopted a service due to incentives are likely to look for better deals elsewhere, such users are less likely to commit to a long-term relationship.

The test failed to support a path between shared value and relationship commitment. A structural problem may be present, since there could be strong associations between components of shared value and other exogenous variables.

Prior research has suggested that commitment usually has a strong effect on IS continuance intention. However, the standardized effect in our study revealed a stronger effect from trust than commitment. This suggests that, in the e-commerce context, users continue using a system primarily because they think it is trustworthy. ## 5.2. Implications for research

Web-based applications have separated e-commerce from traditional businesses and have caused the effect of IS continuance intention to be more profound. Online Internet users presume that most viable sites can provide more benefits due to economies of scale, and provide more customized services due to better techniques of collecting preferences. Customers are also afraid of having to pay switching costs [9].

## 5.3. Implications for practitioners

From the practitioner’s point of view, IT managers can take advantage of the decreasing costs of IT and the proliferation of the Internet.

The perception of security in interacting with a firm’s web site can be achieved by maintaining three conditions. First, the firm must address user concerns about computer crimes. Secondly, the firm must prevent invasion of privacy. Finally, the firm should attempt to provide error-free transactions and allocate sufficient resources to correct any error.

Our findings revealed specific strategies that businesses can take to foster trust and commitment in e-commerce settings. They should seek to increase termination cost, empower customers, share similar understandings of user needs, goals, and policies, implement effective communication strategies, and ensure customer perception of security.

## 5.4. Limitations of the study

Although our study provided insights into the factors affecting IS continuance intention, the results must be interpreted with caution. First, independent variables in the proposed model explained only 22% of the variance in IS continuance intention. Secondly, although the results generally supported most hypotheses, the use of self-reported scales to measure the study variables makes it possible that common method variance may account for some of the results. While the results of validity and reliability tests provided sufficient confidence, studies with multi-method, multitrait measurements could yield more powerful results.

Several external validity limitations also existed in the study. First, all the survey respondents lived in a specific region (the West Coast of the US) and the sample was drawn from the customer base of only one institution. This could limit the generalization of our findings to other populations.

The fact that the sample consists of regular online banking users with previous six-month continuous usage may also have
