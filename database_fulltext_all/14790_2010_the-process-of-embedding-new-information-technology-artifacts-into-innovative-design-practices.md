# The process of embedding new information technology artifacts into innovative design practices

Ryan J. Baxter <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Nicholas Berente <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Bentley University, 175 Forest St., AAC 289, Waltham, MA, 02452-4705, United States

<sup>b</sup> University of Michigan, School of Information, 1085 South University, Ann Arbor, Michigan 48109-1107, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 19 February 2010 Accepted 19 April 2010

Keywords: Embeddedness IT-enabled innovation IT adaptation IT assimilation Design 3D computer-aided design Architecture Frank Gehry

## a b s t r a c t

The design and creation of anything innovative requires knowledge creation, which in turn often depends upon the introduction of innovative information technology (IT). Since design-related practices are deeply integrated with particular IT artifacts, it is no trivial task to migrate practice-based knowledge to unfamiliar IT artifacts. To explore the challenges associated with such migration, we develop the concept of embeddedness of IT artifacts by drawing on research that highlights the critical role of representational artifacts in knowledge, design, and distributed cognition. We then inductively analyze interview data from an in-depth case study of Frank Gehry, a world-renowned and radically innovative architect known for his use of sophisticated 3D computeraided design (CAD) technology. By studying construction <sup>fi</sup>rms transition from 2D CAD to 3D CAD, we identify four relevant themes associated with embedding new artifacts into knowledge-creating practice: (1) motivating the new artifact; (2) anchoring the new artifact in the old; (3) experimenting with the new artifact; and (4) con<sup>fi</sup>dence in using the new artifact. Through the generation of a process theory of embedding, we elaborate on how this perspective complements and extends research on IT adaptation and assimilation, and discuss the relevance of continuing to develop the IT embeddedness perspective given the continuing need for increased levels of IT-enabled innovation.

## 1. Introduction

“Out here, neither of us could come up with clear-cut decisions as to how long it was going to take, it was so complicated, you know. And even though we had all the information in the world, it didn't change about how hard it was.” (Manager of FrameCo responsible for framing and drywall on Frank Gehry Project).

FrameCo<sup>1</sup> is a drywall subcontractor that is responsible for taking the curving and undulating designs of world-renowned architect Frank Gehry and transforming them into <sup>fi</sup>nished interior walls of a building (see Fig. 1 below). In the above quote, “all the information in the world” refers to FrameCo's use of CATIA™, a sophisticated three-dimensional computer-aided design (3D CAD) tool, historically used in aerospace and automotive applications, and brought to complex architectural applications by Gehry. CATIA contained complex mathematically accurate digital models of the curving walls that FrameCo needed to frame and <sup>fi</sup>nish. As the quote indicates, even though FrameCo had “all the information in the world” in a powerful IT artifact (one on which they had received training) it was simply not a straightforward solution. FrameCo was accustomed to using 2D CAD representational artifacts, but had not yet embedded CATIA into their practices.

In general, innovative information technology (IT), including CAD (Argyres, 1999), has long been regarded as a catalyst for enabling <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and innovation through mechanisms such as process innovation (Davenport, 1993) and organizational transformation (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1992; Markus, 2004; Orlikowski, 1996). In order to attain IT-enabled performance and innovation the IT must be aligned with organizational resources, processes, and structure to extract maximum value (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Leonard-Barton, 1988). At a broad level, the study of how innovative IT diffuses into organizations beyond adoption and implementation is called assimilation (e.g., Fichman, 2000; Gallivan, 2001). At a practice level, researchers often emphasize the process of mutual adaptation whereby both the IT artifact and the local practices engage in cycles of change and adaptation toward eventual alignment (Leonard-Barton & Deschamps, 1988; Orlikowski, 1996; Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994).

The post-implementation process of aligning IT with existing institutionalized practices, entrenched culture, and long-standing traditions, is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult (Robey & Boudreau, 1999), even more so when dealing with disruptive and innovative changes (Carlile, 2002). Further complicating the matter is the observation that IT implementations rarely involve “Green<sup>fi</sup>eld” sites (i.e., new organizational units or organizational units without incumbent IT) (Brynjolfsson, Renshaw, & VanAlstyne, 1997); thus, new IT must displace existing IT in order to be effectively utilized. Thus, we are interested in better understanding how the interactions between the new and incumbent IT artifacts to create new knowledge for innovation.

Design teams are particularly interesting in this regard, because by the very knowledge creating nature of their activity, they are continually required to accommodate new IT artifacts as a matter of course (Berente, Srinivasan, Yoo, Boland, & Lyytinen, 2007); yet, their “hard-won” design knowledge is likely integrated with particular artifacts and routines (Carlile, 2002). Thus, to guide our research we ask the following general research question:

• By what process do novel IT artifacts displace well-established IT artifacts to become embedded in knowledge-creating practices?

To address this question, we focus on construction project involving a building designed by Frank Gehry — which is a context where we can observe a high degree of tension between an existing IT artifact that is deeply embedded in work practices and the demand for a new IT artifact that is required to develop new knowledge necessary to produce innovation. Frank Gehry is a world-renowned architect recognized for his strikingly unusual and dramatically shaped buildings. To bring about complementary innovation across a variety of collaborating <sup>fi</sup>rms, Gehry's design practices involve signi<sup>fi</sup>cant departures from established processes within the AEC <sup>fi</sup>eld, including the use of CATIA (Boland, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2007; Gal & Berente, 2008; Gal, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2008). To get their radically designed buildings constructed, Gehry enlists a variety of contracting and engineering <sup>fi</sup>rms, and trades — from long-time collaborators who understand the Gehry way of doing things, to people working with his <sup>fi</sup>rm for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time. While the innovative designs create challenges for even the most experienced partnering <sup>fi</sup>rms, <sup>fi</sup>rst-time collaborators often <sup>fi</sup>nd it particularly daunting. In order to bring Gehry's conceptual design into reality, construction <sup>fi</sup>rms needed to change their practices, which includes substantial use of 3D CAD so that they can communicate, coordinate, and build. The source of tension resulting from using CATIA stems from recognizing that the architecture, engineering, and construction (AEC) industry is rooted in a well-established set of institutionalized processes based upon a proven assortment of artifacts. One of these artifacts is 2D CAD which is so widely used and taken-for-granted that one might say it is embedded in existing practices.

The challenge, of course, is that they cannot easily separate their practices from the underlying logic that ties 2D CAD and what they do in the <sup>fi</sup>eld together as well as how they coordinate internally and with other <sup>fi</sup>rms. Because the data we analyzed comes from longitudinal re<sup>fl</sup>ections of project participants, we were able to theorize a process model based on the transition of 2D CAD to 3D CAD in supporting a radical architectural project design by Frank Gehry

The organization of the paper is as follows. First, we will develop the concept of embeddedness in situations of knowledge-creating design, and then describe the ways in which representational artifacts in general (Section 2), and then 3D CAD in particular (Section 3), is embedded in design practices. We then present our research method and overview of the selected <sup>fi</sup>rms (Section 4). Next (Section 5), we report the <sup>fi</sup>ndings related to embedding 3D CAD, including four process components, followed by discussion (Section 6) of our process model. We conclude (Section 7) by noting limitations, implications, and future research directions.

## 2. Embeddedness of IT Artifacts

To embed (verb) means “to make something an integral part of” (Merriam-Webster, 2009). As noted in the introduction, our focus is on how IT artifacts become embedded into work practices so that innovative knowledge can be created. To understand this from an IT artifact and practice level we look to literature within design studies and distributed cognition.

Representational artifacts are embedded in design processes. Designers generate their designs in conjunction with a variety of physical and digital artifacts, each intended to represent some aspect of the design, These representational artifacts, including drawings, sketches, diagrams, models, requirements and speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, and designers use them to communicate with others and with themselves throughout the design process (Rosenman & Gero. 1996). Representation is particularly important to design, as iteration across representations – both individually and socially – is the fundamental activity of all design practice (Berente & Lyytinen, 2009). The surfacing, or “externalization,” of tacit knowledge through representation is critical to knowledge creation (Nonaka, 1994). Physical artifacts such as plans, models, prototypes, and “procedural artifacts” support the externalization necessary for design (Perry & Sanderson, 1998), therefore, such artifacts are embedded into the everyday activity, or “object world,” of designers (Bucciarelli, 1992) because they form the routinized communicative and functional basis of core work practices.

The focus on representation is fundamental to early discussions on the cognitive aspects of design. The design process has been characterized as a “nested series” of “generate-test cycles” (Simon, 1996, p. 129), where representations of designs are created then tested against cognitive schema for goal satisfaction. Much of designer-artifact interaction is associated with representing the design, then learning from and modifying representations. From this cognitive perspective, technological artifacts are essential for distributed cognition, as they support the dialog and interpretation required for communication and learning (Boland, Tenkasi, & Te'eni, 1994). They do so by enabling (1) computation, (2) communication support, and (3) knowledge capture across time (Hutchins, 1995, 2000). First, Hutchins describes how artifacts can be used as computational media that “amplify the cognition of the artifact user” (Hutchins, 2000, p. 7), and in a context of design such artifacts include the wide range of mathematical tools – from calculators to advanced simulation – that compute characteristics of the design based on representations. Mathematical computation can also enhance the visualization capabilities associated with a design artifact (Gershon & Eick, 1995).The second way that cognition can be distributed across artifacts involves communication support through representational practices, where individuals distribute their cognition across representational artifacts both to communicate re<sup>fl</sup>exively (interactively with themselves) and with others. The <sup>fi</sup>nal mode of distributed cognition – knowledge capture – describes the way that representational artifacts embed knowledge from the historical learning of groups, thus avoiding the need to recreate all or a portion of this learning activity.

Because of the routinized and cognitive aspects outlined above, embedded IT artifacts, by their role in the continuous production and reproduction of practice, become extensions of designers. In situations where an IT artifact is embedded in practice, the use of the tool becomes taken-for-granted – hidden in the action – much like how a carpenter does not focus on the hammer when nailing. Various expressions from the literature have been used to capture this idea of the embeddedness of activities in practice, including Heidegger's “ready-to-hand” (Introna, 1997; Winograd & Flores, 1986), Polanyi's (1966) “in-dwelling,” and activity theory's notion of “mediation” (Engeström, Miettinen, & Punamäki-Gitai, 1999; Nardi, 1996)); whereby individuals are not conscious of the artifact (tool, or equipment) itself when engaged in activity, but that tool disappears in enabling the activity. Winograd & Flores illustrate this using the Hiedeggerrian notion of “ready-to-hand” with the practice of typing a draft:

“As I sit here typing a draft on a word processor… I think of words as they appear on my screen. There is a network of equipment that includes my arms and hands, a keyboard, and many complex devices that mediate between it and the screen. None of this equipment is present to me…” (Winograd & Flores, 1986, p. 36–37).

Thus, to be embedded the “equipment” must not itself be the focus of activity, but instead be prior to that activity (Introna, 1997). Designers iteratively manipulate the representations enabled by the IT artifact, and because of repeated practice doing so, do not focus on the artifact itself in routine activities. Similarly, design teams acting out of reproduced patterns of behavior do not attend directly to artifacts in this design activity, but instead focus on the representational content and its implications that are enabled by the activity.

Thus, designers leverage embedded IT artifacts to iteratively interact with the representations individually or socially across teams (Berente & Lyytinen, 2009). Design representations act as extended memory of designers (Gero, 1990), and they are embedded in communication patterns (Perry & Sanderson, 1998). Sometimes different representations of a design are required to guide the multiple interpretations necessary for a complex design (Rosenman & Gero, 1996). 2D CAD is a primary form of representation that is deeply entrenched in the AEC industry and a variety of different groups of designers, fabricators, and builders have embedded 2D CAD fully into their work practices.

## 3. 2D and 3D CAD embedded in AEC practice

AEC work involves temporary projects that generally span more than one year. In these projects, multiple, interdependent organizations come together, sometimes without a prior history of working together. To collaborate effectively, projects are organized according to highly institutionalized practices that are entrenched within the industry and reproduced in each project. Contracting is the primary mechanism for governing and enforcement of behavior in AEC, and much of this contracting centers on speci<sup>fi</sup>c, well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned 2D CAD representations of the building structures.

The typical AEC process involves the transmission of electronic 2D CAD drawings of a completed design from the architect to the contractor, sub-contractors and trades — each adding relevant detail to the drawings. Firms are intentionally minimalistic in providing drawings to each other, and they are careful not to provide information that is outside of their explicit responsibility and scope of their work. These information-sharing rules are partly the product of the high specialization in AEC, and stem from litigious environment in which one party may seek recompense from another if they can prove that the information led them to a poor outcome. Rather than liberally sharing information, <sup>fi</sup>rms are cautious since it may lead to exposing of an error or inconsistency and the associated liabilities. Informal communication and coordination between the architect and the builders takes place prior to and during the project, yet there also is an accompanying formal process that documents all requests for and clari<sup>fi</sup>cations of the information (e.g., RFC and RFI). The architect will respond to these requests to provide additional design guidance, clari<sup>fi</sup>cation, and likely impact to the aesthetics. This also ensures the attribution of responsibility and risk tasking among the involved parties.

2D representations have a well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned functional relationship with building practices. In particular, builders rely on a knowledge base of means and methods for converting design ideas into physical buildings. Means and methods are concepts of how to build and assemble components. These are the implicit and explicit instructions that connect the architectural and more detailed shop drawings to the physical and material realm of construction practices. The means and methods are built up in the knowledge repertoire often learned by apprenticeship and through a community of practice (Lave & Wenger. 1991). In this industry, there is little change in the functional relations between the representational information found in the architects' and others' designs and the means and methods, except on an incremental basis with limited customization (Baxter, 2008). Fig. 2 describes the relationship between means and methods and the representational information. In traditional projects, 2D CAD and related drawings along with detailed speci<sup>fi</sup>cations about the required materials are the primary carriers of this representational information.

Fig. 2 points to the embedded nature of 2D CAD in existing building practice (i.e., means and methods). For example, assembly and/or manufacture of materials following a designed approach (i.e., not haphazardly) requires the application of knowledge of how things go together, which is in turn dependent upon some system of measurement and layout. 2D CAD is an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient means to represent information in the traditional construction context because it has the capacity to specify suf<sup>fi</sup>cient information to enable and empower the application of construction practices, which require material selection, measurement and layout, and assembly. The capacity of 2D to represent information adequately for means and methods depends upon the level of complexity of the design and requisite means and methods. In highly innovative contemporary projects (like those of Frank Gehry) 2D CAD-based practices have problems dealing with complex geometry ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and intuitively, and recently there has been pressure to move to 3D CAD for such technical concerns, but this upsets the institutionalized structure of project-based AEC collaboration.

## 3.1. 3D CAD and Gehry's innovative practices

Three-dimensional computer-aided design (3D CAD) is a genre of IT artifact that is particularly well equipped to support the knowledge-creating activity of certain design activities. The rich visualization associated with 3D CAD and the relatively straightforward manner which virtual prototypes are created enable this artifact to aid in the design process unlike 2D CAD (Baba & Nobeoka, 1998; Yap, Ngwenyama, & Osei-Bryson, 2003).

First, individual designers can design more complex 3D models and interact with complexity in generating the design, using abductive reasoning (Baba & Nobeoka, 1998), where they can relatively quickly create multiple intermediate models, or “hypotheses,” then test these hypotheses against each other, against simulation, or against interpersonal discourse. 3D CAD can capture “complex mental models” (Yap et al., 2003), and fully capture an entire design (Baba & Nobeoka, 1998). Thus, it supports a designer's need to iterate visualizations between speci<sup>fi</sup>c details of a design and the whole design (Majchrzak, Chang, Bar<sup>fi</sup>eld, Eberts, & Salvendy, 1987).

As design is a social activity, 3D CAD supports communication in a number of ways, including the partial codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of tacit knowledge and the synchronous integration of many different forms of knowledge (Yap et al., 2003). 3D CAD can also provide the technical ‘grammar’ to enable the ‘structured dialog’ required for a complex design effort (Argyres, 1999), or to highlight the implications that result when a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant design change is made (Carlile, 2002).

Finally, the mathematical basis of 3D CAD models enable a wide variety of computational activities, such as digital pre-assembly and interference checking, simulation such as <sup>fi</sup>nite-element analysis, and advanced visualization and rendering (Baba & Nobeoka, 1998).

Not all 3D CAD systems are alike, however, as some are highly complex, integrated, and very powerful, while others might be easy-to-use extensions of 2D drawing packages. Therefore, it is essential to <sup>fi</sup>t the appropriate 3D CAD system to the activity (Baba & Nobeoka, 1998), so that the new design practice is appropriate for the type of knowledge creation required.

While conceptual design has embraced 3D CAD more quickly, the construction industry, as a whole, has been slow to move to the newer technology. Yet, despite the construction industry's reputation for lack of agility and accommodating change rapidly across its diverse and distributed professional groups (Nam & Tatum, 1989; Slaughter, 1993), there are various examples of completed architectural projects that rely on 3D CAD to achieve complex, sculptural like shapes. These changes have been noted as radically disruptive and innovative in several studies that have emphasized the industry level or inter-organizational coordination aspects associated with 3D CAD in Gehry projects (Boland et al., 2007; Yoo, Boland, & Lyytinen, 2006). While scholars have studied the role of representational artifacts such as CAD in supporting knowledge-creating activity in design for some time (e.g., Henderson, 1991), there is less understanding of the embedded nature of different forms of CAD within the practices of builders and contractors responsible for taking an idea and making it a physical reality — a building. This is especially critical for Gehry's success given his dependence upon those builders to quickly embed 3D in a way that allows them to come up with innovative solutions to the radical design ideas.

Gehry's 3D-based approach provides a sharp contrast to the traditional 2D-based approach in several ways. With Gehry's process, architectural designs change more frequently throughout the project, and some contractors are involved in more extensive design activity early in the process rather than incrementally adding detail. This is partially due to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater complexity associated with Gehry's buildings, as they inevitably involve dramatic, non-orthogonal shapes and forms (See Figs. 1, 3, and 4). Yet, it is also due to the artistic expression within the design process of Frank Gehry, who will make changes well into the construction process (long after the sign-off of the end customer). More routinely, projects may simply require change due to cost or time pressures after later stages of the design or even construction have commenced. Therefore, builders and trades involved in Gehry projects necessarily engage in more design-related aspects than in other projects, and they simply cannot stay in step with Gehry and understand the complicated building geometry without becoming integrated with the representational 3D CAD design model. We studied how Gehry's collaborators went about embedding 3D CAD in their practices, and in the next section, we review the study methodology and the selected <sup>fi</sup>rms

## 4. Method and overview of selected <sup>fi</sup>rms

Data gathered and utilized in our analysis was part of a larger study of the impact of 3D technologies in the architecture, engineering, and construction (AEC) industry. The current study is distinctive in its attention to the concept of embeddedness and in its focus on the contractor and sub-contractors. The data we leveraged consists of interviews of participants associated with the Gehry project of the Peter B. Lewis Building in Cleveland, Ohio on the campus of Case Western Reserve University. For this project, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 58 interviews from 30 different participating organizations related to the Peter B. Lewis Building. We also drew upon a handful of articles written in trade magazines that include interviews of the participants of the <sup>fi</sup>rms we have selected. All names other than Gehry Partners are pseudonyms.

![](/api/attachments/CEH45GHN/fulltext/images/d4a6cc941ef246512a5af2f67d7611c120cb1393c9cf95280da8c15dec58f13e.jpg)

For the purposes of our study, we selected three organizations that met two criteria. The <sup>fi</sup>rst was that these <sup>fi</sup>rms have an extensive role in the building phase of the project. The second was that none of them had prior experience with 3D CAD or with a Gehry project. The purpose of selecting these organizations was to identify the most salient aspects of the transition to 3D CAD. Given the selection criteria, we anticipated <sup>fi</sup>nding more examples of the challenges of embedding 3D CAD. Our purpose was not a pure comparative analysis since the interviewers collected the data via relatively open ended and unstructured interview methods. The focus of many of the interviews involved re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on and describing the impact of 3D CAD on work practices and on the inter-relationships among the project <sup>fi</sup>rms. Nearly every participant had numerous years of experience using 2D CAD on traditional projects.

These three sub-cases represent a collection of unique, extreme cases within a larger case (Yin, 2003). Our approach was to identify key aspects in the data related to contractors' and sub-contractors' experience and impact of using 3D CAD. Consequently, our coding efforts were thematic in nature. We focused on understanding via direct interpretation which involves developing the researcher's awareness and understanding of events by probing deeply their signi<sup>fi</sup>cance to the project overall (Stake, 1995). This is important when considering that a single case study or limited data collection and observations may not produce insights by solely high occurrences of a theme, but rather by their signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. Part of this process also involves relying on the participants, most of whom were highly experienced in their respective professions and could speak knowledgably about the differences between 2D and 3D CAD, including comparisons between the Gehry project and previous traditional projects.

The themes reported in Section 5 were developed through pattern matching logic (Eisenhardt, 1989; Miles & Huberman, 1994; Yin, 2003) in which data collected is guided by the research question — which in this case was to explore the way in which IT artifacts become embedded in practice. We organized interview transcripts and thematically analyzed them using ATLAS.ti™, computer-aided qualitative data analysis software. Data used to construct the episodes was reviewed and discussed by the authors. ## 5. Findings and themes of embedding 3D CAD

The results of our study concluded in four themes that helped to re<sup>fi</sup>ne and contextualize the process of embedding a new IT artifact. Given the focused sampling of three <sup>fi</sup>rms on a single project and from a speci<sup>fi</sup>c industry, our emphasis was on producing an accurate account of common and different experiences transitioning to 3D CAD. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four key themes, which together formed a pattern that was consistent across the <sup>fi</sup>rms. Each of these will be addressed in turn:

1) Motivation to embed 3D CAD in practice

2) Anchoring in the familiar

3) Experimenting with speci<sup>fi</sup>c uses of 3D CAD in practice

4) Confidence in using 3D CAD

We found that <sup>fi</sup>rms' recognition and interpretation of 3D CAD early in the project shaped their subsequent motivation toward the adoption and use of 3D CAD. Yet, their recognition for the need of 3D and their resulting approaches all varied. ## 5.1.1. Gehry's management and role in initial reactions of GenCo, CreteCo, and FrameCo

There was variation in how these <sup>fi</sup>rms understood and recognized what the role of 3D CAD was to be. For example, GenCo states that “this process is going to be something different” while CreteCo's response is that they “hadn't thought about using much 3D. FrameCo, like CreteCo, initially struggled through 2D until they recognized the need to use CATIA. Gehry was aware of the variation in how some <sup>fi</sup>rms recognize and process the impact of 3D CAD in the project. When working with new contractors, Gehry found that simply requiring use of CATIA might intimidate the contractors, and scare them away (e.g., they would bid unreasonably high on jobs). Rather than specifying the need for CATIA ahead of time, sometimes they would introduce CATIA slowly, as the need arose, or let the contractor attempt to work within their own representational system. Either way, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts working with CATIA became more salient and understood by the collaborators. A partner from Gehry described this withholding information practice as follows:

…you have to be very careful how you present the information because the first reaction could be; this isn't going to help me. This building must be really difficult; and there's a situation in which we don't even introduce the information because we get in trouble… we used to do that a lot in the early days, and we realized if we show it to them now, they're going to see it as a problem. If we… let them get in trouble and then we'll get them out of trouble with it [3D CAD] and they'll adopt it (Partner from Gehry Partners).

From GenCo's perspective, it appears that Gehry did not withhold any information as GenCo knew about their extensive use of CATIA prior to meeting with them and learned more through their visit. It is less clear with CreteCo because they don't report on initially intending to use a lot of 3D, a CreteCo manager did note that, “…when we were into the process we were always being told by GenCo and Gehry that the 3D AutoCAD extractions would work for us, that the need for CATIA for our work wasn't there — that that the reference points could be extracted from the CATIA that was on the job site” (CreteCo Manager). Considering that CreteCo did have in-house engineering personnel and had more extensive experience with AutoCAD, it appears that Gehry was not as concerned about managing CreteCo's reaction. CreteCo knew about it, but they thought extracts from CATIA into 3DAutoCAD would be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient. Although there was no explicit acknowledgement by Gehry, it does appear that there was some strategic information withholding happening with FrameCo. After FrameCo recognized the importance of CATIA they worked with a 3D consultant and the architect to jointly develop their means and methods of framing. ## 5.2. Theme II: Anchoring in the familiar

In general, by anchoring we mean that individuals respond to novelty by relating it to the familiar. In responding to the novelty of 3D CAD two <sup>fi</sup>rms found that individuals could teach themselves suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently for the project while another <sup>fi</sup>rm outsourced some work to a third-party CATIA operator. The preceding table indicates how these <sup>fi</sup>rms responded to the novelty of 3D CAD differently (Table 4).

GenCo and CreteCo both found that on the job training was suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for their use of 3D CAD. Like FrameCo, GenCo had also sent some employees to be trained in Michigan and reported the following results:

We signed CATIA individuals…up for a class for a company that does CATIA training in Detroit. The unfortunate part is Frank [Gehry] is the only person that's doing it in architecture. Everybody else is doing it in aerospace and automotive and it's a very mechanical process. So these guys are in a class trying to get a tailpipe from the engine compartment out the back of the car. …While it's teaching the keystrokes of the system, it's not applying itself directly to the knowledge base of architecture (Manager at GenCo).

A similar FrameCo example also illustrates the critical nature of anchoring in the familiar. When new IT cannot be anchored into the familiar – designing and constructing at the scale of a building rather than a car – outsourcing might become a more feasible option. Alternatively, when there is no strong familiarity to a particular system a user may be able to transition much more easily. GenCo found that for one of their employees, a newly graduated engineer, struggled much less during the transition. “He didn't have eight years of paper blue line spec book… you brought a guy out there…with no preconceived notions of how it's supposed to be. So we just told him this is the way it is” (GenCo Manager).

A more general type of novelty encountered was the radical design that Gehry was proposing. Here we see the anchoring process play out differently as <sup>fi</sup>rms react to Gehry's proposed designs by trying to reconcile these through their traditional means of representation (i.e., 2D) and practices (e.g., means and methods). For example, FrameCo <sup>fi</sup>rst attempted to come up with new means and methods for Gehry's radical design by anchoring in their existing representational technologies and practices. This type of anchoring surfaced de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies to handle the level of complexity through using 2D CAD and in their practices. One of the powerful features of 3D CAD is to convey realistic visualizations. Realistic visualization serves as an anchor in the familiar even though it represents a new feature with respect to 2D CAD. In the following example, the CreteCo manager discusses how he appeals to the 3D CAD to help explain a dif<sup>fi</sup>cul part of the project to his supervisor:

“There was maybe one or two, there was a couple instances where there was something that I couldn't even describe. I couldn't put it on paper and I didn't have enough paper to explain it. …So I went up on top deck and grabbed Don, our carpenter foreman. I said, ‘man, you gotta look at this. While this illustrates the capacity of 3D CAD to provide both the detail and whole perspectives in isometric fashion, it does not necessarily follow that they will be able to easily reconcile what they see with what they know how to do. In tackling this challenge, the FrameCo manager discussed (see FrameCo's comment in Table 5) how they tried to “bring it back to what they're [carpenters in the <sup>fi</sup>eld] used to” (i.e., 2D, binders of paper) so that they could carry out their framing practices. Much of this, however, was not the same information that they would pull from 2D-based drawings. The information consisted of 3D coordinate measures to guide the layout and assembly processes. In this way, they anchored in physical representations that could convey information from 3D and aid them in carrying out innovative framing practices.

The above examples, including Table 5, illustrate what we mean by anchoring in familiar representations or practices when encountering novelty associated with Gehry's radical design. This may mean trying to use 2D CAD, or trying to employ a particular construction method to determine that it is inadequate. In dealing with the novelty of new 3D CAD representations, anchoring may happen when learning how to operate a new IT artifact within the context that is familiar (e.g., construction), or not having another artifact already embedded to begin with. In these examples anchoring helped to identify and con<sup>fi</sup>rm inadequacies of applying existing methods or representations to handle the complexity as well as to support more speci<sup>fi</sup>c experimentation to test out new construction strategies.

## 5.3. Theme III: Experimentation with 3D CAD

Firms were able to develop trust in the use of 3D CAD from their experience with the system as they learned from applying it to design and construction activities. These activities helped to identify challenges related to the interdependencies with the need to create new knowledge, their work practices, and 3D CAD. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed different ways that each <sup>fi</sup>rm experimented and gained trust in using 3D CAD. An underlying premise across these trust-building activities involved iterative cycles where the <sup>fi</sup>rms experimented with the new technology, then tested this activity in order to learn about the new artifact and verify trustworthiness. We refer to given instances of these cycles as prototypes. The following table gives examples of trust-building prototyping related activity of the <sup>fi</sup>rms (Table 6).

The <sup>fi</sup>rms used prototypes and experimented during the building process to gain more trust in their use of 3D CAD. The result of these experiences was learning about the capabilities of the IT artifact in relation to their existing practices and knowledge. An important episode that strengthened GenCo's trust in 3D CAD happened early in the construction process when they encountered what appeared to be a mistake in how the concrete and drywall element interfered with one another. The following excerpts from a GenCo manager explain how this apparent mistake led to an increased trust in their use of CATIA:

While there was a problem to solve there and it was ultimately solved, the byproduct of that was everybody drawing an increased level of confidence in the process, and this was not far into the process.

The anomaly in CATIA was simply a con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the concrete and drywall that the architects or engineers had not identi<sup>fi</sup>ed previously. Yet, it served as reinforcement of the fact that they had successfully linked the representation from CATIA into their work practices.

Working through details and encountering mistakes and challenges also helped CreteCo and FrameCo to increase their trust in using 3D CAD. The process of incorporating 3D CAD into work practices of carpenters who are building the concrete forms was no minor transition. The example further illustrates how trusting 3D CAD is not necessarily the result of an individual solely using and working within the features of a technology. Rather, the use and application of 3D CAD depends upon the use from one specialty to another in which the information forms the basis of the coordination. Another illustration of this can be seen in how CreteCo prepared formwork for concrete, which involves assembling standardized and reusable forms that will contain the concrete as it cures. In this project formwork had to be extensively customized. The process involved the formwork engineer designing the speci<sup>fi</sup>cations for the forms and sending these 3D-based speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and accompanying 3D pictures to carpenters in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. While the engineer had trust in his ability to design detailed drawings based on the architect's 3D information he did not have the same level of trust that he could actually use these detailed drawings in the construction of formwork. For example, the formwork engineer remarked that carpenters would say, “‘Hey, you know you can't bend a three-quarter inch sheet of plywood that way, you can only bend it this way and this way and so forth.’ And you don't know that in AutoCAD or anything when you are drawing it you just hope for the best” (Formwork engineer).

Carpenters also learned to interpret and understand the 3D drawings and associated terminology so that they could share any problems with the formwork engineer when following the shop drawings. “They learned from the drawings and they learned the lingo and the communication on it. So they could call up and say, ‘hey, you know you're so many degrees off here from your coordinate.’ For awhile there we actually developed a new terminology or a new communication process on the job where we educated them, and they educated us” (Formwork engineer). Typically, the formwork engineer would simply send information on to the carpenters without questioning the constructability of what he has designed. The engineer does not need to question whether what he is preparing is constructible in the physical world because the designer typically draws upon knowledge from previous projects. This scenario con<sup>fi</sup>rms our earlier remarks in Section 3 that conceptual design does not face the same material, physical constraints that the carpenters encounter. In parallel, for the carpenters to provide effective feedback to the engineer regarding any discrepancies between the representation and material demands they must understand and engage in a new communication process with new terms that will allow the engineer to make changes accordingly. Insightfully, the formwork engineer recognized that this shift was not only increasing the information <sup>fl</sup>ow and frequency, but was actually a completely new vocabulary to convey the ideas from the design to the <sup>fi</sup>eld and back.

For FrameCo, developing trust in using 3D CAD meant working through the CATIA designs and bringing them back to a form that the rest of FrameCo's crew could understand. The manager describes how this process worked:

“It was really something, as I would go to California, I'd spend 4 or 5 days, I'd come back and I'd have this whole group of guys on the job just starving for information, and just dragging me over to the computer. And this is what you did. We'd email the CATIA work I did back to the job so that they could see what I did. And then they'd tell me what I did wrong, then what I did right. And then we'd bring that back here and do all this AutoCAD work and then they would try to take the AutoCAD work and relate it back to what was in CATIA. It was really dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. The problem was near the end, we had a system down that was quick and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient” (FrameCo).

The use of CATIA became a trusted part of FrameCo's practices that changed how they went about measuring, laying out, and assembling the framing system.

In all three <sup>fi</sup>rms, the early stages in using 3D CAD were <sup>fi</sup>rst enabled by motivation that was derived from early recognition of potential de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies or through anchoring changes into existing representations and practices, which further revealed inconsistencies and de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies. The above examples illustrate that even after they began using 3D there was further development of their level of trust in the application of 3D CAD through experimenting and prototyping.

## 6. Summary and discussion: the process of embedding new IT artifacts

Our interest was how an innovative IT artifact (i.e., 3D CAD) might become suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently embedded for innovative knowledge creation when a long-standing IT artifact (i.e., 2D CAD) was already deeply embedded in work practice. Several assumptions shaped how we approached this topic.

First, the design and production of innovative and frame-breaking projects nearly always requires the creation of some new knowledge. This is particularly apparent when innovation in one specialty leads to disruptions in another interdependent specialty (Carlile, 2002).

Second, the creation of new knowledge is generally associated with innovative IT artifacts that expand the distributed cognitive capability for innovation within the designing, producing, and/or coordinating aspects of such projects (Boland et al., 1994). Yet, the introduction of these new IT artifacts is not a plug-n-play type of process and is ongoing long after implementation, adoption, and adaptation. We argue that the development of new knowledge in conjunction with new IT artifacts requires that these artifacts become embedded into the practices of the design team. Super<sup>fi</sup>cial use may not afford the level of innovation required for the design.

Third, complicating this process of embedding new IT artifacts is that they frequently need to co-exist with existing IT artifacts that are incumbent to the practice (e.g., 2D CAD). This point emphasizes the increasingly common transition from a <sup>fi</sup>rst generation digitization to a second or third, rather than from the <sup>fi</sup>rst generation of digital change (e.g., automation of manual and paper-based systems) (Table 8).

Fourth, in contrast to conceptual design processes that focus on syntactic description rather than a material, physical output, design implementers (i.e., the builders) are likely to have more deeply embedded tools that they use unquestioningly. Thus, these tools and artifacts are taken-for-granted and their linkages within a practice or trade are deeply embedded, and by the studied accounts are intertwined in physical, social, and cognitive ways. Thus, the integration of complex 3D CAD, a distinctive representational system with sophisticated features, presents challenges given how 2D CAD is embedded into existing construction means and methods.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from these three <sup>fi</sup>rms, illustrate how using 3D CAD affected their practices in the way that they were required to carry out the measurement and material assembly. 3D CAD did not cause the material changes, but the linkages between the syntax of 3D CAD and the builders means and methods of utilizing that syntax needed to change to accommodate the overall process and to build the building.

We inductively identi<sup>fi</sup>ed aspects of embedding 3D CAD from the context of radical, innovative architectural design where the producers of design – the contractors and trades – had to grapple with moving from 2D CAD to an embedded use of 3D CAD. We drew upon interviews of experienced and knowledgeable participants from several <sup>fi</sup>rms as evidence of both the embedding process and associated innovative outcomes. The observed results gained through the interviews, recognition through industry innovation awards, and our own physical observation of the <sup>fi</sup>nal product revealed a substantial amount of producer innovation.

In our analysis, we found that builders had to struggle with how to reconcile Gehry's radical design concepts and 3D representations with their responsibilities of physically shaping, measuring, laying out, and assembling the building materials and components (i.e., their means and methods). While each <sup>fi</sup>rm, in different ways, engaged in the reconciliation of Gehry's radical designs, technological representation, and building practices, there was also some commonality in their patterns. Through these reconciliation practices, 3D CAD became embedded into the practices of the builders by the end of the project. The themes we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed represent the common stages for these <sup>fi</sup>rms over the course of the project. The following table summarizes our four stages, including the analytic themes and purposes from the introduction and reaction of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ of the project to the construction.

As an initial process model, we propose that the transition from old to new IT artifact over time proceeds temporally as depicted in Fig. 5. We also depict the feedback from con<sup>fi</sup>dence back to motivation to indicate our anticipation that this is also a reciprocal type of process.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst theme – motivating the new artifact – points to strategic motivation of the artifact into <sup>fi</sup>rms that may not be too eager to embed the new technology at <sup>fi</sup>rst. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant departure from architectural design helps the <sup>fi</sup>rms to appreciate the need for 3D CAD even when 2D CAD is already embedded. Motivation helps to make the mismatches between problem and embedded artifacts more salient, thus, opening up the possibilities for other artifacts. The principle here is that to appreciate a new external artifact, new problems must be, at a minimum, understood with the given capacity of the existing cognitive schema.

The second theme – anchoring in the familiar – describes the link to the existing artifact, often through failure in linking the new problem to the old artifact, to appreciate the array of artifacts in relation to each other. This anchoring goes beyond the “recognizable components” (Germonprez, Hovorka, & Collopy, 2007) of the interface of the 3D artifact itself, but to the interpretation of the system overall. On one level reminiscent of social representations theory (Gal & Berente. 2008: Moscovici. 1988). the contractors make sense of the new artifacts and its applications by likening it to the defunct DOS operating system, or by framing it as an automotive application. These metaphors and sensemaking strategies materially affected the approach the <sup>fi</sup>rms took in appropriating the technology. Beyond interpretation, the <sup>fi</sup>rms physically anchored the new artifact to the old as there was continuous translation and interplay between 2D CAD and CATIA.

Additionally, these illustrative cases help to remind us that artifacts are typically not embedded into “Green<sup>fi</sup>eld” situations; rather, they must be understood in relation to the existing environment and artifacts. The process of incorporating new objects into practices may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult in environments where existing objects are as deeply rooted in the socio-cognitive network, as 2D drawings are in AEC. Resolution, in this case did take place via intervention between the architect, construction manger and related subcontractors. This tension seems to be iterative both in the long-term – from project to project – as well as in the short-term — during the project. These iterative processes help to align interdependent, cognitive aspects in ways that enable new knowledge in response to innovative designs.

The third theme – experimenting using 3D CAD – points out that although the motivation may be present, lack of trust may prevent embedding into the distributed cognitive system. This trust is not solely dependent upon the system quality, visual appeal, or navigability of the system as in an e-commerce research related to trust in IT (Vance, Elie-Dit-Cosaque, & Straub, 2008), but rather on how the object will interface with existing practices and knowledge. For sub-contractors, gaining trust requires further testing or simulating (Baba & Nobeoka, 1998), invoking the existing material practices of their trade with the new IT artifact. Prototyping and mock-ups further develop trust and reliance in 3D CAD. This type of trust in an IT artifact does not lead to blind reliance, but leads to more re<sup>fl</sup>ective use of the artifact. Learning to trust objects was an important element in helping people to situate how and when to draw upon objects. This presents a nuanced perspective of how the concept of trust might relate to IT artifacts that differs in context and scope to trust in IT in e-commerce settings (Vance et al., 2008: Wang & Benbasat. 2005). Our view suggests that the increase of trust in using 3D CAD is more aptly explored more deeply as IT-in-use (Orlikowski, 2000). The latter is evidenced by the lack of continually applying traditional processes and the lack of more rapidly relying on the IT artifact as the project proceeds. Further evidence of this would be beyond the scope of this paper, but it would need to determine how 3D CAD is applied in a different context by those that appear to have increased levels of trust in 3D CAD.

The fourth theme – confidence – suggests that new IT artifacts can gain a level of embeddedness in which there is little or no questioning as to which artifact is most appropriate for a particular context. Again, we did not see this as blind reliance. Rather, the de-coupling of means and methods from the longstanding use of 2D CAD, and the re-coupling of means and methods with 3D CAD created a more re<sup>fl</sup>ective practitioner. Con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the use of 3D CAD became contextual given the nature of problem to be solved or the innovation challenge at hand. The participants expressed on several occasions both the indispensible nature of 3D CAD for the Gehry project while noting that the industry was not ready for such a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant overhaul. Interestingly, several participants noted that the transition from 2D to 3D would be of greater impact and more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult than the transition from paper-based drawings to automated 2D CAD drawings because it was recognized as “a different way of thinking” (Gehry project architect). An interesting theoretical aspect was the speed at which con<sup>fi</sup>dence increased from early trusting stages. Luhmann (1988, p. 98) describes that, “the distinction between con<sup>fi</sup>dence and trust depends on our ability to distinguish between dangers and risks”. “If vou do not consider alternatives...vou are in a situation of confidence. ...If you choose one action in preference to others in spite of the possibility of being disappointed by the action of others, you de<sup>fi</sup>ne the situation as one of trust” (Luhmann, 1988, p. 97, emphasis added). From these perspectives, some participants moved to a more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient state of having to consider alternatives by agreeing up front to the use of 3D CAD (e.g., FrameCo) while others seemed more ambivalent until later (e.g., CreteCo). However, con<sup>fi</sup>dence in 3D CAD was clearly not present after that point. Con<sup>fi</sup>dence emerged through the processes of embedding we have described. In this way con<sup>fi</sup>dence was that 3D CAD was the right tool, while the details of this played out in their practices was fraught with many more types of alternative situations in which they would put themselves in positions of risk.

## 6.1. Adopting, adapting, and assimilating IT artifacts

We see the contribution of an embedding view of IT artifacts as complementary to prior work on IT adoption, adaptation, and assimilation. Literature on acceptance (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003) which tends to focus on a binary use/no use dependent variable is the starting point for understanding the embeddedness of an artifact into the particulars of its context. Technologies, once accepted, evolve over time and become more or less embedded in particular work practices (Kuutti, 1996). Technology acceptance research focuses primarily on administrative tools (with few exceptions, see Lee, Kozar, & Larsen, 2003), not the technologies that support emergent knowledge processes such as design (Markus. Maichrzak, & Gasser. 2002). Discussions of technology adaptation, on the other hand, do address the tools that enable knowledge creation (for example, computer-aided software engineering systems (Orlikowski, 1993; Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994); and virtual engineering and collaboration tools (Argyres, 1999; Majchrzak, Rice, Malhotra, King, & Ba, 2000; Malhotra, Majchrzak, Carman, & Lott, 2001), and the way that such technologies are appropriated over time. However, while these studies recognize that new IT artifacts are appropriated through a mutual adaptation process between the IT artifact and existing practices over time (Leonard-Barton, 1988; Orlikowski, 1996), the possibility of more generalizable patterns of this activity often goes undetailed. Further, these studies typically do not address the ways in which new technologies come to inhabit spaces populated with existing technologies, stakeholders, and interests that are embedded in practice. With this in mind, we were able to emphasize both the functional relations between the novel design, 3D capabilities, and means and methods. We also illustrated how that unfolds in practice when an existing IT artifact is already embedded and must (effectively) be displaced.

It is also interesting to note how a view of embeddedness helps to extend Beaudry and Pinsonneault's (2005) application of the coping model for user adaptation, which focuses on how users respond to new or changed IT in both affective and practical ways. We view the nature of IT artifact embedding as extending their model over a longer horizon and within a different context. In contrast to their model, we inductively identify processes (anchoring, motivation, experimenting, and con<sup>fi</sup>dence) that are more speci<sup>fi</sup>c than the three-stage model of appraisal (primary and secondary), adaptation strategies, and outcomes. While it is bevond the scope of the current paper, our initial comparisons lead us to believe that these are quite complimentary, with the coping model providing a higher-level overview with the embedding model delving more into the iterative processes appropriate for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context over a longer time horizon. In particular, the processes of embedding are developed within the context of an incumbent IT artifact, which is deeply embedded into the practices of the contractor and trades. The embedding process model also identi<sup>fi</sup>es strategies that <sup>fi</sup>rms use to motivate others they are collaborating with and why they are doing so (e.g., withholding information to motivate, encouraging experimentation, and prototyping). This enriches Beaudry and Pinsonneault's appraisal processes beyond determining whether a technology is a threat/ opportunity and if there is high/low control. We also point out that it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to separate the context and need for the use of IT (e.g., radically designed shapes that can only be represented in 3D CAD) and the IT artifact itself. In fact, our reading of the interviews point to <sup>fi</sup>rms that were not at all comfortable with the radical design or use of 3D CAD, but still saw this as an opportunity to improve their reputation and skills. Further, it may be, as we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in our data, there is a fair amount of dynamic, reciprocal activity within the appraisal activity in advance of reaching a more stable coping outcome.

Our process model of embedding also extends the IT assimilation literature, which attends to the diffusion of IT innovations (e.g., Fichman, 2000; Gallivan, 2001), focusing largely upon organizational appropriation and value creation (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999). Because this perspective builds on the more general diffusion of innovations perspective (Rogers, 1983) it looks to identify patterns across entire organizations. Accordingly, this body of work emphasizes general characteristics of the innovation, organization, and environment (Fichman, 2000). Swanson (2004) notes that while much of the IT assimilation literature does recognize explicitly or implicitly that it is the nature of using the IT innovation that leads to assimilation, the theoretical and empirical bases are far from developed (Swanson, 2004, p. 268). In this regard, we feel justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in focusing on the process of embedding as distinctive from assimilation, although both share a seemingly common end — the degree or extent to which an IT innovation becomes deeply intertwined into practices through use. Swanson (2004) indicates that the use perspective, though implicitly and explicitly recognized, has not been developed theoretically or empirically. One area of contrast is that as firms learn by doing they assimilate first through experimentation and then through routinization. Routinization then becomes the “basis for performance improvements in task execution, likely to be necessary to achieving the innovation's promise” (Swanson 2004, 280). In contrast we did not follow the premise that routinization would needed to be achieved prior to achieving the promise of 3D CAD. In fact, because of the innovative nature of the project some aspects were beginning to be routinized – e.g., how to extract and convey data from CATIA, while other aspects – e.g., the application of CATIA to construction practices were far from routinized. This calls attention to the importance of the domain of radical innovation as it relates to routinization and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that can be extracted prior to routinization.

We also found experimentation to be critical in embedding 3D CAD into practices in leading to adaptation of the innovation to the enterprise (Leonard-Barton 1988: Tyre and Orlikowski 1994). Yet experimentation also includes reconciling between the existing, embedded IT artifact (i.e., 2D CAD) as well. We build on the importance of experimentation by noting that it also involves ongoing comparisons using existing IT artifacts and how these experiments link to the actual work practices in this domain.

Ultimately, we believe that the processes of embedding extends several scholarly conversations around the process of how IT artifacts move from preliminary use to becoming “ready-to-hand” (Winograd & Flores, 1986) when there is already another IT artifact embedded in practice. IT artifacts are essential to the object world (Berente, Baxter, & Lyytinen, forthcoming, 2010; Bucciarelli, 1992) of the designer, and our study shows that embedding new artifacts into the cognitive schema is not unproblematic for design implementers, especially when dealing with innovative designs. The fact that these implementers had to reconcile cognitive and physical connections between a new representational technology and a radical design demonstrates the need to continue studying how IT artifacts are embedded, co-exist, or are displaced within contexts of innovation

## 7. Conclusions and limitations

Our study outlines dimensions associated with embedding new IT artifacts for the purpose of knowledge creation. This characterization of the embedding process goes beyond the binary use/no use situations of adoption (Lee et al., 2003; Venkatesh et al., 2003), by extending the IT adaptation literature (Majchrzak et al., 2000; Orlikowski, 1996; Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994). It has been long recognized that the artifacts used in a practice become deeply embedded within the routines and daily activities. We endeavored to both highlight the functional reasons why this takes place (i.e., representational information tied to means and methods), and the necessary struggle that emerges when there are incumbent, embedded IT artifacts. We identify four themes associated with embedding a new artifact, while replacing a similar artifact: (1) motivate the new IT artifact; (2) anchor the new in the old; (3) experiment to build trust; (4) con<sup>fi</sup>dence. We developed our view inductively by exploring three embedded cases in a multiyear construction project. Consequently, while we believe this provides a highly accurate portrayal, we are limited in our ability to generalize effectively outside of this domain. Our observations are limited to Frank Gehry's unusual and highly innovative design practice, and future work should continue to develop and identify processes associated with embedding new IT artifacts in the face of already embedded IT artifacts.

By developing a process model of IT embeddedness, we show how new IT artifacts are introduced in relation to existing artifacts and existing practices, emphasizing the idea that in many contemporary situations IT artifacts are not being introduced into “Green<sup>fi</sup>eld” sites where the transition is from a <sup>fi</sup>rst generation of manual and paper-based processes to digitization and automation. Given that many organizations are working on second or third generation IT implementations, it becomes increasingly important to understand the differences between technological automation and the promise of practiceenhancing technologies. Because innovation and demand for knowledge creation are often associated with the introduction of cutting-edge IT artifacts, it is also important to understand how these tensions relate to one another. As some of our data illustrate there may be various dimensions to consider as <sup>fi</sup>rms make tradeoffs and cope with these simultaneous demands. Some <sup>fi</sup>rms may see opportunities associated with innovation and prestige of working on cutting-edge projects and therefore persist more at working with IT artifacts that prove challenging to adapt and embed, but ultimately enable the necessary knowledge creation. For practitioners, this can be particularly helpful to identify the relation between the functional logics associated with existing IT artifacts and new IT artifacts within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c work practice.
