# Managerial problem-solving in the adoption of Radio Frequency Identification Technologies

Robert F. Otondo, Allison W. Pearson, Rodney A. Pearson, John C. Shaw and Jung P. Shim

Department of Management & Information Systems, College of Business, Mississippi State University, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Robert F. Otondo, Department of Management & Information Systems, Mail Stop 9581, College of Business, Mississippi State University, U.S.A. Tel: (662) 325-1962; Fax: (662) 325-8651; E-mail: rotondo@cobilan.msstate.edu

## Abstract

This study explores the ways in which managers evaluate Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) and participate in its adoption. Using in-depth qualitative focus group and published trade journal interviews, our study contributes to research on managing RFID projects in organizations by identifying salient problems and relating them to sensemaking and influencing activities across problem types and manager experience with RFID. We also identify potential dangers in applying past knowledge, cognitive patterns, and experience about information and communication technologies (ICT) to problems that arise in RFID projects. Our study provides analytic generalizability for future RFID and ICT research. European Journal of Information Systems (2009) 18, 553–569. doi:10.1057/ejis.2009.39; published online 3 November 2009

Keywords: Radio Frequency Identification (RFID); information and communication technology (ICT); adoption of technology; problem-solving; qualitative approach

## Introduction

Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) is a new technology offering increased productivity and efficiency. It has been adopted in many industries, including logistics (Chang et al., 2008), retailing (Uhrich et al., 2008), and publishing (Loebbecke and Huyskens, 2008). Managers involved in RFID adoption projects face numerous problems, including those found in Teo et al.’s (2006) extensive study of business-to-business (B2B) e-commerce inhibitors (e.g., lack of interoperability and top management support). Other problems include communication and knowledge exploitation to support adoption practices (Hovorka and Larsen, 2006; Mantzana et al., 2007; Peppard, 2007), human relations and planning/control (Nolan, 1973), aligning technology with the information system (IS) group’s mission, strategy, and policies (Bakos and Treacy, 1986; Lim et al., 2005), cost/benefit and utility analysis (Ahituv, 1980), as well as ‘resistance to change, lack of standards, and lack of third party network support’ (Emmelhainz, 1988, p. 60).

The above problems are compounded in RFID adoption because this technology is very costly, largely unproven, and often misunderstood (Lee and Shim, 2007). Even modest RFID systems generate gigabytes of spatial and temporal data per day – a good portion of which is incomplete – thus creating data cleansing, storage, and modeling problems (Derakhshan et al., 2007). RFID adoption requires managers to work more closely with supply chain partners to solve problems in sharing costs, managing data, agreeing on standards, and retrofitting hardware and software (Riggins and Slaughter, 2006; Wu et al., 2006; Curtin et al., 2007; Goswani et al., 2008).

Managing these tradeoffs is a practical concern for organizations considering RFID, and a theoretical concern for researchers investigating the adoption of RFID and other information and communication technologies (ICT). This more complex adoption landscape means IS researchers must go beyond current foundations of innovation diffusion research (e.g., Rogers, 1983; Marcus, 1987; Valente, 1993). They must expand the breadth of ICT adoption management research, which has largely focused on managers’ personal characteristics (e.g., job tenure and positional power; Sharma and Rai, 2003), or conceptualized managers’ roles in terms of commitment (e.g., Benbasat and Barki, 2007) or encouragement and influence (e.g., Hirschheim, 2007; Schwarz and Chin, 2007; Venkatesh et al., 2007). IS adoption research has also focused more on success factors than on inhibitors (Teo et al., 2006), which constrains our understanding of managerial performance in RFID and ICT adoption.

Problem-solving is a useful approach to adoption management research because it helps researchers conceptualize managers as active strategic- and goal-oriented thinkers whose work is highly cognitive, requiring intense thought, planning, and reflection (Altier, 1999). Using this approach, we describe how managers come to understand and ‘make sense’ of RFID and what cognitive patterns they exhibit. We report findings from focus groups and published trade journal interviews about managerial problem-solving in RFID adoption, demonstrate how those efforts differ across problem types and manager experience with RFID, and identify cognitive patterns in their RFID adoption efforts. Our qualitative approach provides analytic generalizability to other ICTs, industries, and contexts. The paper is organized as follows. First, the ICT adoption literature is reviewed to explore advantages of problem-solving and closely related approaches. Second, our research methodology is described. Finally, the study’s findings and theoretical implications are discussed.

## Theoretical framework

A problem has been defined as ‘an undesirable situation which may be solvable by some agent although probably with some difficulty’ (Agre, 1982, p. 122). Problems help managers ‘allocate attention to unsatisfactory states of affairs’, and problem-solving is ‘what one does when attending to such situations’ (Smith, 1988, p. 1491). Managerial problem-solving has been used successfully in many contexts similar to RFID adoption (e.g., product development; Lovelace et al., 2001).

Although managers are widely recognized as problem solvers, the managerial problem-solving approach has received little attention in IS research. With few exceptions (e.g., Teo et al., 2006), extensive studies of inhibitors are rare in the IS literature. Managerial problem-solving has been used in ICT requirements determination and systems analysis (e.g., Byrd et al., 1992; Schenk et $a l . ,$ 1998; Janz and Prasarnphanich, 2003; Browne and Pitts, 2004; Pitts and Browne, 2004), ICT investment analysis (Boonstra, 2003), and as a domain resource for constructing decision support systems (Courtney and Paradice,

1993). However, to the best of our knowledge, the problem-solving framework has been little used to research managerial behaviors in ICT or RFID adoption.

There are several reasons why managerial problemsolving is applicable to RFID adoption research. First, and foremost, RFID project managers are managers. Managerial problem-solving research helps us re-conceptualize managers in ways that match their responsibilities. For example, managers must not only identify problems, but prioritize them and judiciously allocate scarce resources to them (Pounds, 1969).

Second, RFID entails many business redesign, technical integration, governance, human resource, data management, and market structure decisions (Curtin et al., 2007; Derakhshan et al., 2007). Research on how managers intervene in such adoption decisions is limited, as is our understanding of how those interventions affect ICT adoption and use (Venkatesh and Bala, 2008). A problemsolving approach provides a rich foundation for investigating how managers intervene in these decisions when evaluating complex RFID technologies and participating in difficult RFID adoption processes. Problemsolving research draws upon related literature on sensemaking activities and cognitive structures to help explain how managers interact with organizational stakeholders to develop, modify, and disseminate ‘meaningful frameworks’ and relevant schemas (Gioia and Chittipeddi, 1991, p. 442). In addition, sensemaking and influencing processes help explain ‘influence processes that serve to create and legitimate the meaning of the change’ (Gioia et al., 1994, p. 364). Sensemaking facilitates the study of a variety of IT adoption dimensions, including strategic risk positioning (Scott and Barrett, 2005), the ongoing changes in mental frameworks, how individuals influence and are influenced by their environments, and even identity construction (Seligman, 2006). Successful sensemaking provides positive cues to employees and other stakeholders (Simon, 1996), which can facilitate adoption efforts.

Cognitive schemas can facilitate problem-solving research in RFID adoption because they ‘evoke large clusters of memorial knowledge, providing meaningful interpretations and generating useful inferences’ (Smith, 1989, p. 972). Schemas thus serve as ‘an initial frame of reference for action and perception’ (Weick, 1979, p. 154). One type of schema, problem categories, is useful because ‘experts often solve problems by categorizing them as specific problems types, thereby accessing large amounts of relevant knowledge’ (Smith, 1995, p. 679). In RFID adoption, schemas can be useful in understanding how managers put aside their individual preferences to achieve consensus (Riggins and Slaughter, 2006). They also help explain variation in managerial behavior, such as in distinguishing problem-solving capabilities of novices and experts (Schenk et al., 1998) or in explaining cognitive biases or judgmental errors (Walsh, 1995; Bazerman, 2006). Bendoly et al. (2007) concluded more work is necessary to distinguish tactical/operational and strategic elements in managerial decisions during RFID adoption – in essence, key managerial schemas have not yet been identified.

A third reason why problem-solving is applicable to RFID adoption is because it accommodates ‘complex sequences of coordinated acts’ (Eagly and Chaiken, 1993, p. 183). In RFID adoption, the problem-solving framework can help identify observable phenomena in which problems are addressed, sensemaking and influencing efforts are enacted, and cognitive patterns are applied.

## Research question

The purpose of our study is to investigate how managers solve problems and ‘make sense’ of complex RFID projects. Our interests can be formalized in the following research questions: (1) What problems do managers face when considering RFID? (2) How do managers make sense of these problems? and (3) What cognitive patterns emerge as managers make sense of RFID?

## Research methodology

We operationalized problem-solving as statements dealing with problem categories or associated rules, heuristics, and knowledge. We used Smith’s (1995) classification of managerial problem categories (MPCs), a two-level taxonomy of 14 main categories and 30 subordinate categories. Examples are provided in Table 1. We then used Gioia et al.’s (1994) sensemaking/influencing taxonomy to identify these activities and their relationships to cognitive patterns and problem categories.

As with many qualitative studies, we employed theoretical (i.e., purposive) sampling rather than random sampling (Corbin and Strauss, 2008). Data came from two sources. The first consisted of two focus groups on RFID that were conducted after a 3-h workshop on RFID. Workshops included lectures and a tour of an RFIDenabled warehouse. Both workshops were prepared and led by professional instructors from an RFID training company. Our research team conducted the focus groups, following guidelines from Ericsson and Simon (1993), Krippendorff (2004), and Browne and Rogich (2001).

Following Karahanna et al. (1999, p. 191), the focus group participants were classified as potential adopters (i.e., ‘individuals who had knowledge of [an ICT] but had not yet started using [that ICT]’; p. 191). Potential adopters were drawn from the furniture industry, as stipulated by the study’s sponsor, the U.S. Small Business Administration. Twenty-one individuals participated in the focus groups. Eighty-one percent of the participants were male. Participants ranged in age from 28 to 64, with an average age of 44.0 years. Fifty-six percent held an undergraduate degree, while 6% held a master’s degree. Participants’ average experience in the furniture industry was 11.0 years. Two-thirds of the participants agreed with the statement they were knowledgeable about computers, but almost 70% stated they were not knowledgeable about RFID. Our participants included IT, warehouse, operation level, and general managers. Three focus group participants had been involved in RFID pilot studies. Since that experience was counter to Karahanna et al.’s (1999) definition of potential adopters, we excluded their comments from our data.

Data were also gathered from interviews published in ICT and RFID trade journal web sites. Keywords such as ‘RFID’, ‘manager’, and ‘CIO’ were entered into the Googlet web site to locate articles. Other keywords such as ‘interview’ and ‘said’ identified published interviews.

Following Karahanna et al.’s (1999) reference to ‘the stages following the adoption decision’ as ‘post-adoption’ (p. 185), we classified individuals who had participated in a pilot study or subsequent implementation phase as post-adopters. Accordingly, we also used keywords such as ‘pilot study’ and ‘rollout’ to identify post-adopter interviews.

We initially identified 110 articles drawn from trade journals such as RFID Journal and CIO Magazine and from academic/practitioner journals such as MISQ Executive. One article was drawn from the Federal Register, and several articles were drawn from government practitioner journals such as Government Computer News. Several articles described surveys of managers (e.g., Kash, 2006). A closer inspection of the sample narrowed the pool to 102 articles. Examples of managers interviewed in our trade journal sample are displayed in Table 2.

Three graduate students coded the focus group transcripts and published interviews. Their training included discussions of articles about MPCs (Smith, 1995) and the use of a coding manual created by the research team based on content and protocol analysis (e.g., Ericsson and Simon, 1993; Krippendorff, 2004).

The coders agreed unanimously on 844 codings. A random sample of the coders’ final codings was checked by a panel of four expert professors, two from the management discipline and two from the IS discipline. The kappa statistic between the graduate students’ consensus codings and experts’ checks was 0.67, with a 95% confidence interval of 70.08. Shrout (1998) classifies 0.67 as exhibiting ‘moderate’ strength of agreement (p. 308). A closer inspection of the kappa matrix revealed only eight of the graduate students’ actual codings were judged ‘false’ by the experts (i.e., 95.4% agreement on actual codings). These statistics suggest the coders judgments were sufficiently valid for further analysis.

## Preliminary analysis

We assessed the thickness of our data set (Geertz, 1973) by examining the extent to which material corresponding to MPCs originated from across our potential and post-adopters. Six of the 14 major MPCs originated from one-third or more of our potential adopters, and four of them originated from more than 40% of post-adopters. The implementation/change and policies/procedures/ systems categories were the most broadly identified MPCs, originating from 61% of the potential adopters and 66.7% of the post-adopters. The next most broadly identified MPC was evaluation, originating from 56% of potential adopters and 59.8% of post-adopters, followed by authority and control from 44% of potential adopters and strategy and planning from 58.8% of post-adopters.

Potential and post-adopters provided rich descriptions. For example, statements classified as MPC 6.0 (strategy/ planning) included planning the implementation, building an infrastructure with supply chain members, documenting changes to the implementation plan, and changing business processes. Sub-categories showed similar variation. Statements categorized as MPC 8.1 (cost-benefit comparisons) addressed how RFID can reduce expenses (Roberti, 2004) and how views on costbenefit comparisons change over time (Gilmore, 2007). The richness of the trade literature was also seen in the lack of replication in comments by individuals who were interviewed in multiple trade journal articles. For example, Simon Langford, Director of RFID and Transportation Systems at Wal-Mart, discussed different problems (e.g., over-engineering for redundancy, addressing missed goals, and avoiding overcomplicated pilot tests) in the four articles in which he appeared in our sample.

## Salient problems in RFID adoption

Coding counts and frequencies for all 44 MPCs from our potential and post-adopter data are displayed in Appendix. These statistics were consolidated into their respective major problem categories (Tables 3a and b). These counts must be interpreted cautiously because they can be artificially inflated. For example, an individual’s discussion of a problem can trigger the recall and discussion of related or cross-indexed problems in that person’s – or another’s – long-term memory (Ericsson and Simon, 1993; Krippendorff, 2004). On the other hand, some important topics may not have been recalled during focus groups or not reported in our trade journal sample.

Tables 3a and b give a picture of an MPC’s salience to managers. The breadth of MPCs found in our study is similar to that found in Teo et al. (2006). We found managers faced other problems, including inadequate guidance from organizational missions, inconsistent application of policies and procedures (Murphy, 2005), problems of authority and control (Loebbecke, 2004), unclear contracts or responsibilities (Hoselton, 2005), and even problems dealing with individual agents (CIO.com, 2003; Donoghue, 2005; RFID Online Summit, 2006).

Tables 3a and b show differences between potential and post-adopters, respectively. Post-adopters discussed implementation/change, evaluation, and strategy/planning problems more frequently than potential adopters, while potential adopters discussed inter-agent relations and authority/control problems more frequently than post-adopters. This suggests post-adopters are more concerned with strategic-level problems, while potential adopters with operation-level problems. These findings are consistent with the strategic change literature and proposed RFID research agendas (Schendel and Hofer, 1979; Leontiades, 1980; Curtin et al., 2007).

## Managerial sensemaking in RFID adoption

Differences between potential and post-adopters’ participation in RFID adoption was further explored by examining how managers ‘make sense’ of RFID. This phase was prompted by IS research suggesting RFID adoption is more complex than traditional ICT adoptions (Curtin et al., 2007) and that some types of sensemaking behaviors are a factor in RFID implementation (Gogan et al., 2007).

Two important activities in managerial problem-solving are sensemaking and influencing. Sensemaking involves ‘meaning construction and reconstruction by the involved parties as they [attempt] to develop a meaningful framework for understanding’ (Gioia and Chittipeddi, 1991, p. 442). Influencing behaviors are employed when managers attempt to persuade ‘the sensemaking and meaning construction of others toward a preferred redefinition of organizational reality’ (Gioia and Chittipeddi, 1991, p. 442).

While conceptually distinct, sensemaking and influencing are ‘inextricably intertwined’ and often difficult to distinguish because powerholders such as managers ‘seldom flaunt their influence ability’ (Gioia et al., 1994, pp. 380, 365). Distinguishing the two is easier during change efforts because influence activities become manifested in symbolic representations (Gioia et al., 1994). We expected managers to manifest these behaviors overtly in RFID adoption because it is a change effort with high potential, costs, and risks.

We reviewed statements from the five common MPCs in Tables 3a and b for evidence of sensemaking and influencing using Gioia et al.’s (1994) taxonomy. Their sensemaking codes include projection (i.e., ‘speculating, forecasting, [or] hypothesizing about the future’), defining (‘explaining, or stating the meaning of a concept or event’), and bounding (‘identification of limitations and constraints to action’) (p. 369). Their influencing codes include accommodation (‘a willingness to oblige and adapt to external forces’) and framing (‘statements intended to affect interpretation and acceptance of recommendations’) (p. 369). Results are displayed in Tables 4, 5, and 6.

## Sensemaking and influence in RFID problem-solving

Tables 4, 5, and 6 show interesting differences in sensemaking and influencing behaviors across post-adopters and potential adopters. Compared to post-adopters, potential adopters engaged in a higher proportion of projection (i.e., speculation, forecasting, and hypothesizing) across all of the five problem categories in Tables 4, 5, and 6 – about 41% of all their sensemaking activities. This is probably due to potential adopters’ limited knowledge of RFID and their frequent discussions on RFID’s potential for improving business processes. On the other hand, only 8% of all post-adopters’ sensemaking statements were classified as speculation. Post-adopters were more likely to engage in ambiguity, defining, and bounding forms of sensemaking (i.e., 28%, 23%, and 18%, respectively). This finding suggests sensemaking and influencing are more complex in post-adopters than in potential adopters.

One difference in the above trend was the higher proportion of speculation in strategy/planning problems of potential adopters than post-adopters. Although postadopters were less likely than potential adopters to speculate (i.e., 8.2% of total sensemaking activities vs 41.3%, respectively), when they did speculate they were much more likely to speculate about strategy/planning (i.e., 50% [11/22] vs 11% [5/45], respectively; bracketed ratios use counts from Table 4). This finding is consistent with research that strategy/planning problems will emerge as technical adoption problems are solved (e.g., Curtin et al., 2007).

Some sensemaking frequencies in the policies/procedures/systems problem category in Table 4 deserve further exploration. Post-adopters were more likely to engage in ambiguity sensemaking than potential adopters (i.e., 28 vs 20%, respectively). However, as a percentage of their ambiguity sensemaking statements, potential adopters were far more likely to address policies/procedures/systems problems (i.e., 73% [16/22] vs 5% [4/74]). Similarly, as a percentage of their bounding statements, post-adopters were far more likely to address policies/procedures/systems problems than potential adopters (i.e., 22% [11/49] vs 8% [1/13]). Actual statements suggest both differences arise from concerns about underlying ICT systems, not policies or procedures. In this MPC, potential adopters’ ambiguity statements often involved confusion about RFID technology, while the trade literature’s bounding statements typically described environmental factors that corrupt RFID signals. The ambiguity frequencies support assertions that technical problems will emerge before managerial and organizational issues. The high frequency of bounding statements in the trade literature concerning environmental disturbances to RFID signals may be an effort to combat overly optimistic beliefs about RFID; that is, the frequencies suggest potential adopters may be more likely to suffer positive illusion biases than post-adopters (Bazerman, 2006).

Our analysis of influencing statements in Table 5 contained several surprises. Perhaps the most unexpected finding was that 77% of potential adopters’ influencing statements involved just one behavior: framing. Postadopters’ influencing statements were more varied: 36% were categorized as accommodation, 38% as framing, and 11% as enabling. As with sensemaking, this finding suggests post-adopters influencing activities are more complex than potential adopters.

Examples of influencing behaviors are instructive. One potential adopter turned to his manager and asked, ‘Don’t you think [RFID] would be a tremendous help to us on our cycle counting if we got it set up now?’ This participant was clearly attempting to influence the other on a procedural problem (i.e., MPC 2.0), but did it tactfully through a question so as not to flaunt his influencing ability. An unusual example of unsuccessful influence involved an RFID vendor who refused to conduct a pilot test for a customer because he thought it ‘wouldn’t work’. The customer then contacted a rival vendor who gladly set up the trial – which failed (ITBusiness Edge, 2007).

## Negative cases of sensemaking and influencing

Included in our sensemaking and influencing codings were a number of ‘negative cases’ (Glaser and Strauss, 1967). Many companies were criticized for poor speculation, (e.g., ‘initial expectations were not realistically set’ Schuman, 2006, p. 1). Critics faulted Wal-Mart for ‘arrogance’ in their attitude toward their suppliers (Schuman, 2006, p. 1), though others acknowledged Wal-Mart later learned RFID’s potential ‘is limited by business partner’s willingness to participate’ (Weier, 2007). Several critics accused Wal-Mart’s key suppliers of manipulating public perception when announcing ‘how delighted they are to be part of this new technology’ while privately viewing RFID as ‘a significant hindrance to their business’ (ITBusiness Edge, 2007). The U.S. Department of State and Department of Defense were also criticized for their influence tactics in RFID adoption.

We also found warnings by respected post-adopters about differences in RFID and traditional ICT adoption efforts. For example, Dick Cantwell of Gillette advised:

We’re inventing something that no one knows what it will look like. This isn’t looking in the rear view mirror and rolling out a new enterprise system, or a new warehouse management system based on incrementally improving what we have. We’re inventing a new way of doing business. (Gilmore, 2007)

Similar warnings came from Tim Marsh, Senior Manager at Pfizer Global Package Technology, and Carolyn Walton, VP of IS at Wal-Mart. Mr. Marsh argued ‘You can’t just plug in a non-line-of-sight technology and expect ROI y . You’re still going to have to change your business process’ (RFID Update, 2007). Ms. Walton warned, ‘Do not let your management assume it’s the only time you are going to request funding. RFID is not a flash in the pan’ (Boucher Ferguson, 2006). These warnings were often associated with sensemaking/influencing activities and problem categories. Mr. Cantwell’s statement involved ambiguities in strategy/planning problems. Mr. Marsh’s statement explained how to implement RFID successfully $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ defining sensemaking with a strategic problem). Ms. Walton’s statement used symbolism (i.e., ‘a flash in the pan’) to frame securing managerial commitment as a long-term process.

The warnings raise important questions about the origins of erroneous cognitive patterns. Accordingly, when addressing our third research question we looked for evidence for both positive and potentially harmful cognitive patterns (e.g., cognitive biases, Bazerman, 2006; mistaken analogical reasoning, Halasz and Moran, 1982).

## Cognitive patterns in RFID adoption

Data in Tables 4, 5, and 6 summarize differences in sensemaking and influencing levels across potential and post-adopters. However, the transcripts and interviews also provide evidence about the quality of managerial sensemaking. In particular, they show managers engage in both beneficial and potentially harmful cognitive patterns during RFID adoption. The latter finding is particularly important because it shows faulty managerial judgment and cognition are still problematic, even after decades of research in this area $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ Bazerman, 2006).

We begin this section by discussing two types of cognitive patterns uncovered in our study. The first involved the application of past experience and knowledge to the identification of potential problems in RFID adoption; the second, to the identification of potential RFID-based solutions to organizational problems. Cognitive patterns in the two sets exhibit several characteristics of schemas described in the literature. Schemas act as ‘templates for the perception, encoding, storage, and retrieval of information’ (Stopa and Waters, 2005, p. 45) and serve as ‘an initial frame of reference for action and perception’ (Weick, 1979, p. 154). Schemas bestow several benefits (e.g., organizing memory, interpreting situations, and making a problem ‘familiar’; Smith, 1995, p. 972). However, research also suggests they can constrain managers’ problem-solving and sensemaking capabilities by limiting their ability to envision possible solutions to a related problem (Barr et al., 1992).

An important characteristic of schemas is stability over time (Beck et al., 1979). While our data did not allow us to ascertain the stability of the cognitive patterns we detected, research suggests knowledge structures related to problem categories are stable and can thus be classified as schema (Smith, 1995). Therefore, we use cognitive pattern as a general term to describe ways of thinking that guide perception, information encoding, knowledge retrieval, and sensemaking, but restrict schema to MPC that most likely exhibit long-term stability.

## Identifying problems created by RFID adoption

We found evidence that managers used schemas when envisioning problems created by RFID adoption. Numerous examples of problem recognition and problemsolving knowledge were found in our trade journal sample, though they were often encoded as advice or condensed into guidelines and heuristics (Table 7). They often involved related sets of knowledge. For example, the first guideline in Table 7 contains two separate but related pieces of knowledge (i.e., ‘Create a project plan’ and ‘share the budget’) that was applied to problems of strategy and planning (MPC 6.0).

Potential adopters supplied similar evidence. The following example concerns a participant’s plans for addressing workers’ resistance to change [MPC 7.3]:

We’re already very, very lean having gone through a big downsizing, and I heard [another participant] reference that if he had [RFID], he could eventually cut back two employees. Speaking from my peers and some of the guys I’ve worked with now, I’m lucky to have a veteran crew and mainly the ones that got to stay with our organization are in distribution. They are very gun shy about anything new that may cost jobs. You have to be upfront and explain things to them, about the pros and how this is going to help them and be honest if it’s going to do that, you know.

The participant is recalling a schema relating knowledge about problems associated with employee job security perceptions to potential response tactics. The schema was modified to fit the RFID context, guiding the participant in his speculative sensemaking efforts to solving the problem of mitigating resistance to change (i.e., explaining ‘the pros and how [RFID] is going to help employees). The participant also frames his response to influence the perceptions of other participants, arguing for the value of good employees (i.e., ‘I’m lucky to have a veteran crew’) and the importance of respecting them (i.e., ‘You have to be upfront and explain things to them and be honest y’). This example is somewhat different from Venkatesh and Bala’s (2008) advice because the manager is creating realistic but perhaps unfavorable employee perceptions, which he believes in the long run may be the most appropriate course of action.

There are still people out there looking for frames that are made out of hardwood’. That is, CNC adoption helped them acquire many – but not all customers of non-adopters of CNC technology.

The participant then speculated the same scenario might apply to RFID:

Participant: We saw [CNC] coming 15 years ago, so I would say technology is why we’re [successful] now. If it wasn’t for the technology y [we] would still be back over here making hardwood frame lumber parts like we used to do 15 years ago. So it’s kind of a two-way street. Time, the computers, the technology, RFID, it’s all, it’s kind of like it’s falling in place as we go from one year to the next year. All of this technology is coming to us at the right time, as a matter of fact.

The participant re-frames the discussion in a positive way by arguing that technology adoption ‘is why we’re there now’. His use of the metaphorical phrase ‘it’s kind of a two-way street’ is symbolic sensemaking that acknowledged RFID adoption presented an uncomfortable trade-off between enhanced competitiveness and employee job cuts, which were discussed just before his story.

## Potentially harmful cognitive patterns in RFID adoption problem-solving

There was evidence of faulty cognitive patterns as well. The trade literature contained a number of warnings from RFID experts about applying previous ICT knowledge and experience to RFID adoption (e.g., Dick Cantwell of Gillette in Gilmore, 2007; Carol Walton of Wal-Mart in Boucher Ferguson, 2006). A few potential adopters provided negative anecdotal evidence, but it was nonetheless illuminating. A common finding was that potential adopters identified or speculated about the judgmental errors of others. particularly their managers

For example, one potential adopter told of having to contend with old, malfunctioning wireless barcode scanners:

I have guys on my scan team that everyday who say, ‘Well, the scanner said this but I know I scanned that.’ And if I go try to duplicate their error I can’t, not every time. But I’ve seen it where they scan a piece into a location and it will show up electronically in some other location even though physically it’s here y .

He then described upper management’s refusal to believe these stories and provide continuing support for this aging system:

I try to tell [upper management] these scanners are three years old and they’re wearing out. The wireless parts come loose. The applications, the software is old, whatever. They’ll fight me tooth and nail about they don’t want to buy y . Again, people who are in upper management who don’t want to spend money [are] saying that it’s not the scanners themselves or the guns themselves, it’s the people using the guns.

We found about a half-dozen similar stories. Yet in spite of the participants’ ability to identify or speculate on their managers’ cognitive errors, they were sometimes unable to recognize their own. For example, we saw examples of overconfidence bias (Bazerman, 2006). One example involved the participant’s belief in RFID’s capability to solve the problem of misread product codes and serial numbers. The participant described how these mistakes adversely affected the organization and its employees, and how RFID could help:

Participant: y we do in-home servicing of [furniture] and there are some very frustrating aspects of our business. If our [technicians] write down the [serial] number wrong, they have to turn around and actually go back to the consumer’s home to write a number down correctly so that we can take care of our customer’s problems through a warranty claim. If there was just an RFID tag, which would be attached to the piece, [the technicians could] scan it, and, BAM, they’ve got all of the data they need. I could see it assisting us AFTER delivery as well.

Interviewer: So you think that the service people might support it?

Participant: Oh, yeah. Oooh, yeah! [Emphatic]

The participant’s comments suggest he evoked a cognitive pattern relating knowledge about data input quality with task efficiency and customer satisfaction and applied it to RFID adoption. The phrase ‘I could see it assisting us’ suggests the manager is engaging in speculative sensemaking. However, his response, ‘Oh yeah. Oooh, yeah!’ was much more emphatic than most other responses. The danger is that overconfidence in RFID’s potential might compromise the participant’s sensemaking and problem-solving activities (e.g., constrain his bounding sensemaking regarding important problems).

One of the most common biases we saw was the ‘confirmation trap’, in which ‘individuals tend to seek confirmatory information for what they think is true and fail to search for disconfirmatory evidence’ Bazerman, 2006, p. 40). There were a few instances in which participants sought confirming evidence, as when one participant turned to his manager and asked, ‘Don’t you think it would be a tremendous help to us on our cycle counting if we got [RFID] set up now?’ However, the majority of examples were different than expected: instead of seeking confirmatory information, participants would provide such evidence for another participant, and do so spontaneously. One such example is from a discussion about how RFID could improve shipping accuracy:

Participant N: y from the perspective of actually shipping out y you’d get a black eye if you send them the wrong products when they’re expecting their products. Some customers, they may have already promoted this product and they’re ready to sell it and they have customers sitting at the door ready to buy this product y .

Participant D: One thing [Participant N] mentioned, too, about a benefit he saw was that nobody cares what went wrong with you if that person in New York gets the wrong [furniture]. I’ve had, we’ve had phone calls, ‘I’m having a wedding party this weekend and you better get me the right one here in two days!

We found that confirmation biases occurred when RFID was considered as either a problem solver or a problem creator. The above example concerns RFID as a problem solver (i.e., it solves the problem of sending wrong products). Our second example concerns RFID as a problem creator (e.g., it creates cost-benefit comparison problems, which in turn creates problems in securing commitment):

Participant L: But, if I called my boss tomorrow and said, ‘I’ve just been to the greatest seminar yesterday. We need to implement this technology’, he’s going to say, ‘How many bodies does this save?

Participant H: Yeah, that’s what they associate costs with y but [Participant L] is right. If you go in and say ‘Hey, I want to spend \$250,000 on a system’, they’re going to look at you and say ‘How much? Where are my cost savings?’ Number one: cost savings.

Again, this example is not about upper managers’ cost concerns, but about participants providing confirmatory evidence to each other.

We did find a few instances in which participants provided disconfirming evidence, along with several dozen instances in which participants prefaced their speculations with probabilistic words such as ‘could’ or ‘might’. Nonetheless, the danger is that confirmation traps may constrain debates about RFID’s advantages, disadvantages, and limitations (e.g., introduce detrimental framing or other influencing about relevant problems). Our results therefore suggest that while poor cognitive patterns are not ubiquitous, they may occur often enough to create difficulties during RFID adoption.

## Discussion and conclusions

We began our study by looking for salient problems, sensemaking activities, and cognitive patterns in the management of RFID projects. As expected, our results show that managers report a wide breadth of problems, though the vast majority – 80% – fall into about a halfdozen categories. Post-adopters discussed strategy/planning problems far more often than potential adopters, which is consistent with the strategic change literature and proposed RFID research agendas.

Our study described interesting characteristics about relationships between problem-solving and sensemaking in RFID adoption. Potential and post-adopters exhibited marked differences in their use of specific sensemaking and influencing activities. Post-adopters engaged in a wider range of sensemaking and influencing activities than potential adopters, which suggests adopters engage in more complex sensemaking and influencing as they move through the RFID adoption process.

Our study also uncovered evidence of both positive and negative cognitive patterns in RFID adoption. On the positive side, we found that managers evoke cognitive patterns and apply them to problems created during RFID adoption through sensemaking and influencing activities. We found similar evidence when they tried to ‘make sense’ of how RFID can solve organizational problems. On the other hand, we found evidence that managers can exhibit poor cognitive patterns in their sensemaking and problem-solving efforts. Potential adopters were often quick to suggest potential cognitive errors of their managers and subordinates, but were less forthcoming about their own. These findings, together with related cautionary statements from the trade literature, suggest judgmental error is still an important topic for future RFID adoption research despite the decades of cognitive research in managerial decision making.

Results from our study provide direction for future research. First, we show cognitive error is still an important topic in IS adoption. More research is needed to better understand the antecedents of beneficial and potentially harmful cognitive patterns, and how that research can be parlayed to improve IS practice. Aguinis et al. (forthcoming) offer thoughtful advice about achieving the latter goal. Second, and relatedly, more studies are needed to explain how managers transition from strategic-to-operational problem-solving and from limited-to-complex sensemaking and influencing. Other research examining how managers mitigate initial cognitive biases over time (e.g., over-confidence) is also needed. These transitions can then be examined for their associations with RFID and ICT adoption outcomes. It may well be that managers who do not make these transitions successfully may have a reduced ability to engage in meaningful sensemaking and problem-solving, or are more likely to engage in faulty judgment and cognition. Third, the example of the manager who advocated for the creation of realistic employee perceptions – even if those perceptions were unfavorable toward

RFID – points to an important dilemma in adoption intervention and ethics research.

Other opportunities for future research involve RFID’s unique technical aspects, the ability of RFID-enabled systems to generate tremendous amounts of data, and the organizational difficulties inherent in getting multiple supply chain partners to share costs and benefits, agree on standards, and conform to deployment strategies. Other unanswered questions include: What is it about RFID that makes some cognitive patterns useful for given problems and others not? How do cognitive patterns diffuse through the organization and supply chain? To what extent do cognitive biases affect selection, diffu sion, and application of cognitive patterns during RFID adoption? How does cognitive pattern choice affect RFID implementations and their impacts on organizations and supply chains? Additional research should investigate relationships between managers’ perceptions, participation, and problem-solving approach with their organizational level and scope of responsibility. Other problemsolving activities such as search strategies, reasoning, and question-asking behaviors remain to be investigated in the RFID adoption context.

Future research that more fully assesses cognitive patterns and schemas can deepen our understanding of managerial sensemaking, influencing, and problem-solving in RFID and ICT contexts. Additional research is also needed in other industries, technologies, and contexts to test the generalizability of our findings. We encourage other researchers to use and/or modify our methods in their future research efforts.

## Implications

This study has several theoretical implications. Our finding that managers recognized that RFID can solve organizational problems after adoption – but also generate a host of problems during adoption – suggests MPC related variables can serve as independent or dependent variables in ICT adoption research. For example, using MPCs as dependent variables could address Benbasat and Barki’s (2007, p. 212) concern that ‘study after study has reiterated the importance of [perceived usefulness], with very little research effort going into investigating what actually makes a system useful’ (e.g., is ICT useful if it solves problems?). Researchers should also investigate the generalizability of our finding that a large percentage of problems fall within a limited number of problem categories.

Our finding that post-adopters are more likely to engage in a wider range of sensemaking than potential adopters is consistent with previous research showing differences between experts and novices (e.g., Schenk et al., 1998; Browne and Pitts, 2004). Additional research is needed to explain how these differences come about – and how they do not – and their effects on adoption outcomes.

Our results provide a framework for additional research that explores specific relationships between cognitive patterns, sensemaking, problem-solving, and adoption success. For example, in their study of overconfidence bias in poorly performing students, Ehrlinger et al. (2008) suggest encouraging ‘a mindset that leads to greater excitement about learning and, by extension greater selfinsight’ (p. 119). This advice can be adapted to RFID and ICT adoption contexts to study how learning and selfinsight (e.g., examining and evaluating relevant schema) are associated with adoption outcomes, and how they can be encouraged during adoption processes.

Our research has practical implications as well. Managers must recognize that RFID’s unique capabilities and requirements means that their previous knowledge and experience with ICT (i.e., their schemas) may not always be applicable to RFID. Managers can use the trade literature, much of which can be accessed easily through the web, to become knowledgeable about RFID’s potential and risks. However, managers must take care to use reliable printed and web-based resources.
