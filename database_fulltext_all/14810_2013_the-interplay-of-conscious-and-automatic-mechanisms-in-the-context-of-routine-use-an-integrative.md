## Abstract

Researchers have been closely studying how information technology services became a routine part of our lives. Studies have found that users who routinely use online services either consciously develop loyalty or automatically develop a habit. But many studies now mix the elements of conscious and automatic use despite the great differences in these phenomena. This study proposes a new theoretical framework of how the conscious and automatic mechanisms simultaneously, but differentially, regulate postadoption phenomena in the context of online information services. An empirical test confirms that these two mechanisms have highly discernible patterns that researchers and managers alike must heed.

Keywords: postadoption behavior; loyalty; habit; word-of-mouth; IT usage

## 1. Introduction

Online services have matured over the past decade, and many have become deeply ingrained into the everyday lifestyle and decisions of online consumers [87]. These online services have embedded themselves into our daily routine of collecting news, checking e-mail, and more. Transformation of intermittent users into routine users benefits online service providers. Routine users are lucrative revenue sources for providers because frequent interaction equates to more opportunities for revenue from subscriptions, advertising, and purchases [108]. Furthermore, the positive word-of-mouth spread by each user brings in even more revenue in the long run than his or her purchases [64]. Consequently, modern online services need to cultivate longterm patterns of routine use and advocacy to ensure their growth. Researchers studying the use of online services have examined how to increase precisely these behaviors of continued use and word-of-mouth referrals [18, 61].

But a study of routine use of telecom services shows that strategies to increase long-term usage and word-of-mouth referrals may produce conflicting results [64]. This is because the most tenured users of routine services are not the most active word-of-mouth referrers of those services. This discrepancy means that consumers who visit daily and use a specific online news website are less active than others in referring others to their website. Trying to understand such discrepancies between usage and word-of-mouth raises the question of whether different cognitive mechanisms underlie these different behaviors. And this line of questioning leads to recognition of even deeper problems in our understanding of the continued use of routine online services in term of a theoretical perspective.

A common premise underlying many studies of continued use is that users make conscious, rational choices regarding their patronage of information technology (IT). These studies have examined what factors might influence users in their immediate choice of an IT application, as reflected by their usage intentions [18, 60, 68, 103] and intentions to spread word-of-mouth [27, 61]. The conscious, rational decisions that users make come through formal appraisals of IT that inculcate loyal, stable relationships with their IT [33, 41, 71].

But other researchers have recently found that when users repeatedly choose to use the same IT services, their decisions can become more routine and habitual [52, 110]. This routinization of behavior

#

shows that another stable, but more automatic, mechanism of continued IT use is also operative. According to this stream of literature, habit is the main driving force of the automatic mechanism [60, 66].

These two research streams actually complement each other despite their seeming invocation of conflicting mechanisms in explaining the formation of stable patterns of IT use. When the intention to use IT is involved, the literature that supports the conscious and rational mechanism (e.g., [18]) is based on the initial and continued stage of IT use. However, the literature that suggests the automatic mechanism [60] relies on the habitual stage of IT use. Moreover, in the literature the intention to spread word-of-mouth is only explained in terms of the conscious mechanism (e.g., [61]). Accordingly, researchers in information systems (IS) are beginning to recognize both conscious and automatic mechanisms and to jointly use factors representing the characteristics of these mechanisms to predict why users form stable patterns of IT use [67, 110]. Apparently, long-term behavior with IT is partly determined by conscious decisions to remain loyal and partly by habituated intentions. However, in drawing on these two mechanisms (conscious and automatic), we quickly realize that in the extant IT literature, our understanding of these parallel mechanisms has three major shortcomings. Relevant studies in information systems on these two mechanisms (1) have not clearly distinguished that the mechanisms arise from different circumstances, (2) have not considered that the mechanisms produce different stability factors of IT use, and (3) have not investigated whether such stability factors of IT use might have different effects on intentions. Because they did not simultaneously measure the necessary factors of both mechanisms, researchers may have confused what is spurious with what is important. Therefore, this study sought to investigate whether the conscious and automatic mechanisms of routine IT usage should be clearly theoretically and empirically demarcated.

The proposed automatic and conscious mechanisms, respectively, closely reflect Chaiken’s [24] psychological theory of heuristic and systematic information processing modes. In his systematic mode, recipients of information exert conscious and rational efforts to process the information to reach a decision about a given task. In contrast, the heuristic mode de-emphasizes cognitive information processing; it emphasizes instead that recipients of information rely on simple rules or heuristics based on learned knowledge [114]. In extending this view to the IS domain, the conscious mechanism is based on the mode of systematic information processing, and the automatic mechanism is based on the mode of heuristic information processing. The next section will deal with these two mechanisms in more detail.

We use the difference between the heuristic and systematic modes to argue that habitual IT use and word-of-mouth actually arise out of the operation of two different mechanisms – automatic and conscious, respectively. In addition to practical reasons (increasing revenue from existing and new customers), we chose the usage intention and word-of-mouth to highlight the divergent effects of processing through conscious versus automatic mechanisms. Word-of-mouth is the giving of a subjective opinion to another and not regularly done. These characteristics of word-of-mouth behavior are closely tied to the conscious mechanism, which is used to increase the accuracy of subjective judgment. In contrast, usage of routine services ought to follow the automatic mechanism, which is used when knowledge structures remain constant and the behavior reoccurs often enough that reengaging the conscious mechanism each time might be prohibitively expensive in terms of mental effort. Therefore, we suggest that different antecedent factors (past use versus satisfaction) and central stability factors (habit versus loyalty) should be linked and hypothesized.

A proposed model of our research hypotheses was tested empirically in the context of online information services, which could include such websites as those for news, health, and finances. Online information services are generally characterized as a high-opportunity environment with frequent interactions in which rigorous evaluation and decision-making can quickly become burdensome. Under these circumstances, and unlike in transaction-based settings, we expected the automatic mechanism to play a strong role in online information services, thus allowing us to fully compare both mechanisms of IT use. In general, we expected the findings of this study to clarify the interplay of the loyalty- and habit-related mechanisms in shaping online consumer behavior in postadoption stages.

## 2. Theoretical Background

Psychology formally recognizes the two dissimilar mechanisms of conscious versus automatic use as the product of two different modes of information processing [24]. One is a systematic process that coincides with the conscious and rational mechanism. The other is a heuristic process that coincides with the automatic mechanism. Different circumstances trigger these dual processes, which are believed to shape decisions

#

differently [114]. Systematic processing of information requires the cognitive capacity to comprehensively analyze and integrate information before arriving at a decision. Such systematic processing requires effort and cannot be performed rapidly or under duress. In contrast, heuristic processing arrives at decisions through simple rules formulated from experience. The idea of these dual processes was echoed by Petty and Cacioppo [84, 85] in explaining how two distinct routes, namely, a central one versus a peripheral one, could be taken when facing a persuasive argument or presentation. One route is based on the rational consideration of arguments central to an issue. The other is based on peripheral cues. For example, when arguments are presented about a certain product, in some cases a systematic processing mode is triggered to analyze information directly relevant to the central issues. In other cases, instead of going through conscious and rational analyses of the given arguments, peripheral cues such as who presented the argument and when they presented the argument are triggered to decide for or against the product. Implicit in this concept is the idea that a stimulus triggers the decision to use one route and not the other. We use the term conscious mechanism to emphasize a conscious and rational cognitive process that coincides with systematic processing. Likewise, the term automatic mechanism is used to emphasize how the routinization of a repeating process comes to dominate the behaviors that follow.

These theoretical distinctions from psychology should be applied to the long-term behavior of users of IT. We are concerned with the lack of clear distinctions between conscious and automatic mechanisms in IS, even though they have been distinguished in the psychology literature. Our first concern regarding how they are modeled is that the extant literature treats the antecedents of these stability mechanisms as interchangeable. Some studies suggest that satisfactory evaluations of IT and routine past use of IT both lead to habit [67]. Other studies suggest that satisfaction and past use both increase conscious feelings of loyalty [94]. But our study asserts that satisfactory evaluations should primarily increase loyalty because it is a conscious state-of-mind, whereas routine past actions are more likely to lead to a state of habituation. Therefore, a more accurate assessment is needed of how stable states of IT use arise and thus, whether distinct stability mechanisms exist.

Looking beyond conscious versus automatic states, a similar ambiguity occurs regarding the outcomes of loyalty and habit. Some studies find that habit exerts such a major role in shaping usage intentions that it can overshadow conscious evaluations of IT [60, 110]. Understandably, some intentions might become habitual in routine contexts. But it is doubtful that habit would also play such a dominant role in determining important intentions that require conscious decision making, such as spreading word-ofmouth. Therefore, this study looks carefully at the difference in the effects of loyalty and habit.

Furthermore, antecedent factors such as satisfaction and past use are often believed to directly influence intentions and behavior [58, 61, 69]. But it is unknown whether such overriding effects stay in line with the two mechanisms or if they create strong cross-effects between them. For example, satisfaction could directly influence usage intentions [18] or word-of mouth intentions [61], but which outcome would be influenced most when past use is controlled for? We need to study such differential effects to understand whether the conscious and automatic mechanisms are indeed distinct, as prior studies often assume [66].

To address these concerns, our study clarifies within an IT context the distinctions between the antecedent factors (past use versus satisfaction), central stability factors (habit versus loyalty), and behavioral outcomes (usage intention and word-of-mouth) of these two different mechanisms. Specifically, drawing on Chaiken’s [24] model of systematic versus heuristic processes, we propose a theoretical framework of how the conscious and automatic mechanisms regulate postadoption phenomena. Consequently, we propose three major research questions: (1) how does the antecedent factor of each mechanism primarily influence the stability factor of its own mechanism over the other mechanism; (2) how do loyalty and habit differ in determining their respective outcomes; and (3) how do the determinants of loyalty and habit relate differently to their outcomes?

We built an empirically testable model using theory drawn from prior literature (see Figure 1). Our model is conceptually based on Chaiken’s [24] model of systematic versus heuristic processes. Our modeling of each of the two mechanisms, however, draws on Oliver’s [78] loyalty model and James’ [50] theory of habit, both of which, respectively, coincide with our systematic and heuristic views. Oliver’s [78] model highlights the conscious mechanism in which loyalty partially mediates the impact of satisfaction on behavioral

outcomes. James’ [50] theory of habit describes the automatic mechanism in which past use affects habit that in turn determines behavioral outcomes. By taking either the loyalty model or the theory of habit, IS researchers (e.g., [60, 98]) have found antecedents or outcomes relevant to one of the stability factors (loyalty or habit) without distinguishing between them. On the other hand, by integrating the loyalty model and the theory of habit, while distinguishing between the two mechanisms, we can propose a testable model that presents a conceptual framework of how the two mechanisms differ in their antecedents, intervening variables, and outcomes.

## 3.1. Differential Effects of the Antecedents on the Stability Factors

Oliver’s [78] loyalty model presents a theoretical framework that is used in the information systems literature to explain the development of loyalty [61]. According to Oliver [78], satisfaction is a necessary condition for the formation of loyalty because consumers rarely commit to something that does not convey either a tangible or intangible benefit. Satisfaction is known to capture the conscious appraisals and prior expectations of service quality [7]. In particular, satisfaction with information technology requires an assessment of the various dimensions of the quality of information delivered and of the quality of the system that delivers it [109]. Such conscious appraisals require cognitive capacity to analyze and integrate information [24], and so satisfaction is developed from the systematic processing of information. Oliver’s [78] loyalty model goes on to assert that loyalty is a transformation of satisfaction into a psychological state of attachment to the service and that this attachment is relatively stable against short-term fluctuations in service performance. In this sense, loyalty refers to one’s “deeply held commitment” to the service ([79], p. 34). This loyal state develops in a series of phases in which each phase requires additional considerations, such as past, recent, or vicarious knowledge of the service provider. Thus, loyalty requires further systematic processing of information. Several researchers draw on the loyalty model in their attempts to explain consumer behavior. Specifically, Taylor and Hunter [98] showed in their study of IT use that satisfaction affected loyalty and loyalty determined behavioral outcomes (i.e., usage intention and word-of-mouth). Similarly, in the context of Business-To-Consumer (B2C) services, Hennig-Thurau et al. [44] found that satisfaction determined commitment (i.e., loyalty), and both factors positively affected behavioral outcomes (i.e., continuance intention and word-of-mouth).

Meanwhile, the concept of habit dates to the late nineteenth century, when William James [50] in his Principles of Psychology devoted a chapter to it. In essence, James’ [50] theory posits that repeated performance transforms a person’s “nervous” system in such a way that the same action can be performed with less conscious attention. According to this theory, past behavior leads to the formation of habit; habit in turn results in an automatic response to the same stimuli. Thus, habit represents the automatic mechanism by which behavior is activated. The notion of habit has been receiving much attention from IS researchers as a key factor that regulates postadoption outcomes [52, 59, 66, 104].

According to the literature, loyalty and habit represent different aspects of one’s stability tendencies [48, 50, 78]. Loyalty and habit stimulate their formative processes in highly discernible patterns because the systematic and heuristic processing of information represents two different mechanisms. Thus, we posit that satisfaction will have a strong effect on loyalty because both primarily arise from systematic processing of information. In contrast satisfaction should have a much more diminutive effect on habit, which represents the vastly different automatic stability tendency.

H1. The influence of satisfaction on loyalty will be stronger than the influence of satisfaction on habit (i.e., satisfaction → loyalty > satisfaction → habit).

Many IS studies, however, simply equated past use with habit; accordingly, instead of being examined directly, habit was indirectly inferred from the effect of past use on behavioral consequences [9, 59, 60, 63]. Such an equation of habit with past use seems reasonable under most circumstances because, all things being equal, increased past use strengthens habit. Yet, in a strict sense, past use, which refers to the extent of past

#

use of a service, differs from the concept of habit, which refers to a state of behavioral inertia established by a mental goal-action link [1, 52, 66]. Specifically, within an unstable environment, repeat performance cannot establish a mental link; thus, in such a case, past behavior does not necessarily transform into habit [52]. We note that Verplanken and Orbell [107] seem to argue otherwise: "Because habits develop and gain strength by satisfactory repetition of behavior […], it is not unreasonable to accept a measure of past behavioral frequency as a measure of habit strength.” However, even such a line of argument concedes that past behavioral frequency is the critical antecedent of habit. Thus, the distinction between past use and habit is important [67].

However, a clearer case can be made that past use is a strong predictor of habit. In the same way past experience helps build simple rules to trigger the heuristic processing of information [24], past use can help build habit. Unlike loyalty, habit occurs only in the unconscious realm as a form of hard-wired mental script. As previously mentioned, this ingrained script is strengthened with the repetition of the same behavior (i.e., mechanical processes), but it is rarely formed based on deliberate decision-making (i.e., conscious processes) [52]. Thus, habit is considered more a function of behavioral frequency than of thoughtful evaluation. But it is important to note that the relationship between past behavior and habit may vary according to the contextual characteristics in which behavior occurs. According to Ouellette and Wood [81], for example, behavioral opportunity moderates the role of past behavior in the formation of habit. In particular, conscious decision-making dominates in a low-opportunity context in which behaviors are rarely performed (e.g., once or twice a year), and past behavior can hardly be transformed into habit. In contrast, past behavior is easily transformed into habit in a high-opportunity context in which a behavior can be performed frequently (e.g., at least once or twice a week). For example, in the context of online news use, Kim et al. [60] showed that the relationship between past use and habit was substantial (r = 0.91, p < 0.001). Thus, past behavior is likely to have a strong relationship with habit, especially in the context of online information services.

Unlike habit, loyalty likely does not derive very strongly from past behavior. Drawing on the notion of loyalty espoused by Jacoby and Kyner [49], Oliver [78] also conceptualizes loyalty as the outcome of psychological processes that involve cognitive and affective evaluations. Overall, a consensus has been

reached that loyalty is formed from conscious judgments, not by mindless repetition of the same behavior [88, 96]. For example, Jacoby and Kyner [49] stated that loyalty “is a function of psychological (decision-making, evaluative) processes” (p. 2), and thus it “differs from simple repeat purchasing behavior” (p. 5). Thus, we expect a minor effect of past use on loyalty because a “deeply held commitment” will not be formed simply by repeated use of an online service [78, 79]. Chaiken’s theory of the heuristic and systematic mechanisms supports this line of reasoning because past experience has a stronger relationship with the heuristic mechanism than with the systematic mechanism. We see no reason why satisfaction must continue its role in habit over time, and so we control for the effect of satisfaction on habit but expect its effect to be generally minimal. Thus, we hypothesize that past use is the more important of the two antecedents in determining habit:

H2. The influence of past use on habit will be stronger than the influence of past use on loyalty (i.e., past use → habit > past use → loyalty).

## 3.2. Differential Effects of the Stability Factors on Their Outcomes

Usage intention (e.g., revenue from existing customers) and word-of-mouth (e.g., revenue from recruiting new customers) are known as two key indicators of the success of online firms [44, 60, 71, 89, 98].<sup>1</sup> Usage intention refers to an individual’s expected volume of use of a service. Unless the initial use of a service leads to repeated use in the postadoption stages, the future of an online firm could be at risk. In this sense, the frequency of a consumer’s use of a service has significant implications for its profitability [68, 103]. Online information services are more reliant on a steady stream of repeat customers than online transaction services. Thus, in a routine context such as online information services, usage intention – which strongly correlates with actual use at the postadoption stages [59, 60] – is an important outcome variable.

Word-of-mouth refers to the extent to which an individual intends to recommend, or say positive things about, a service. In this study, we considered the valence of word-of-mouth rather than its volume, because the valence of word-of-mouth is most frequently used in the literature of IT use at the postadoption

#

stages and for customer relationships [5, 44, 61, 96, 98]. Although word-of-mouth does not relate directly to consumers’ use of an online service, it indirectly—and perhaps more profoundly—helps to boost website traffic by recruiting potential customers. Thus the literature has long considered that word-of-mouth plays an important role in facilitating the diffusion of innovations [5, 93]. Word-of-mouth is also recognized as an important user behavior that is characteristic of loyal users of online services [61]. In contrast, users in constrained relationships with limited alternatives are less likely to engage in word-of-mouth. Word-of-mouth captures the conscious outcomes of rational, affective, and emotional appraisals [27]. These appraisals require the cognitive capacity to analyze information (systematic mechanism), and so they are the hallmark of a range of conscious decision-making. The practice of spreading word-of-mouth might be even more amplified in online contexts because the Internet makes it easy for a supportive customer to reach potential customers. For example, a number of news websites offer functionality that easily allows one to share articles and send feedback. As such, word-of-mouth is deemed an important postadoption outcome that is gaining attention in IS research.

Taken together, usage intention and word-of-mouth are of great importance to online information services, and thus, a theoretical framework is required that explains, compares, and contrasts the processes underlying these behavioral consequences [41, 44, 98]. Because loyalty and habit represent different aspects of the tendencies associated with stable use, similar to the systematic and heuristic processing of information, their effects on outcomes are unlikely to be identical. But few IS studies have attempted to distinguish between loyalty-driven and habit-driven consequences. Instead, some studies attribute repeated use of an online service to loyalty [57, 41], but others believe that habit is the cause [60, 66]. Thus, research to clarify the underlying mechanism that typically causes repeat use is important [49, 89].

As discussed earlier, conscious processing mainly guides subsequent use in low-opportunity contexts (e.g., buying a big-ticket item online). Routinely used online services (e.g., online news services) are highopportunity contexts that users visit much more frequently. Making conscious choices in such highopportunity contexts can quickly become burdensome. However, with routine usage of an online service, users develop a readily available reservoir of past behavior from which they can draw [24, 114], because past

#

experience formulates simple rules in the heuristic processing of information. In routine contexts, it would seem much easier to simply choose a service based on prior practice than to devote time and effort to a highly conscious decision that might be mentally taxing. Thus, routinely used online information services are a high-opportunity context in which we expect habit to be the stronger driver of repeat use.

Although users in routine online contexts are likely to rely on habit rather than on any conscious decision, we must consider whether they also form any conscious intentions about future usage behavior. Self-perception theory [15] holds that habitual behavior does not require formation of behavioral intention; instead, when external investigators ask respondents about their behavioral intentions, respondents are said to infer their intentions from their past habitual patterns. Ouellette and Wood [81] also reinforce that finding; they argue that “intents typically are positively correlated with habits” because of the self-perception process (p. 56). And Verplanken et al. [106] also found that the intention to use a car was strongly related to habit. These findings suggest that users at a routinely used online information services are likely to base their future usage intentions on habitual usage patterns that represent their best guess about subsequent use.

But the habitual use of an online service does not necessarily correlate to all intentions regarding that service. In contrast to forming usage intentions, which is a private act, committing to word-of-mouth is a highly interpersonal act that exposes one’s decisions to scrutiny by others [89]. Because word-of-mouth involves risk, consumers are likely to carefully evaluate their attachment to a service instead of automatically supporting their prior personal habits. Thus, based on our understanding of the systematic-heuristic mechanisms, we expect that habit built on the heuristic processing of information will be primarily associated with usage intention rather than with word-of-mouth, which requires the systematic processing of information in arriving at a decision of whether or not to recommend a service. Thus, we hypothesize that the effect of habit on word-of-mouth will be much weaker than the effect of habit on usage intention. H3. The influence of habit on usage intention will be stronger than the influence of habit on word-of-mouth (i.e., habit → usage intention > habit → word-of-mouth).

The marketing literature (e.g., [99]) recognizes that brand loyalty significantly mediates how users perceive brand-related advertisements. We have argued that loyalty arises from the systematic mechanism of

#

processing experience. Because a positive usage experience is one of the most important contributing factors in its creation [94], loyalty serves as an elaborator to help a person quickly access and process the already formulated conscious mechanism; this differs from the actions of the automatic mechanism. But instantly reactivating the conscious mechanism differs from skipping the rational process altogether and automatically triggering habits [60]. Unlike habit, we expect the activation of loyalty to strongly influence behavioral outcomes that are thoughtfully and systematically appraised.

Word-of-mouth is a personal endorsement of a service [89] and as such, one of the most important declarations one can make to achieve mutual benefits with a preferred service provider and establish a longterm commitment [61]. This commitment supports and strengthens the relational bond with the service provider. Word-of-mouth is based on subjective confidence and is primarily enhanced by the systematic processing of information [24, 114]. We believe that loyalty, which is one’s deeply held affective commitment [14, 79], serves as a critical factor in stimulating word-of-mouth for online services.

In contrast to the situation with usage intentions and behavior, ample empirical evidence exists to show that loyalty, or commitment, outweighs any other variable as a determinant of word-of-mouth [96, 98]. For example, Taylor and Hunter [98] showed that the correlation between loyalty and word-of-mouth is close to 0.60, which is a relatively large effect size [30]. In addition, Srinivasan et al. [96] found that loyalty toward an online service provider has a strong relationship with word-of-mouth ( = 0.55, p <0.001). These findings suggest that loyal customers tend to engage in activities that advocate and support the company in both offline and online environments [89].

We further posit that loyalty is likely to be more strongly related to word-of-mouth than usage intentions in the context of online information services. Expressing one’s opinions to peers is a lowopportunity, risky venture that would require careful deliberation formulated by the systematic processing of information. In contrast, intentions to use a routine service involve making high-opportunity, low-risk decisions that are more conducive to a heuristic approach such as following one’s habits. Thus, we hypothesize that in a routine online service context, loyalty should be much more closely related to word-ofmouth than to usage intentions.

H4. The influence of loyalty on word-of-mouth will be stronger than the influence of loyalty on usage intention (i.e., loyalty → word-of-mouth > loyalty → usage intention).

## 3.3. Differential Effects of the Antecedents on the Outcomes: Overriding Effects

Earlier we discussed how the two stability factors determine such postadoption outcomes as usage intention and word-of-mouth. Although we expect loyalty and habit to play central roles in regulating wordof-mouth and usage intention, our two antecedent factors are also likely to affect online consumer behavior at the postadoption stage. Considerable research suggests that satisfaction can directly influence word-of-mouth [44, 45, 112] and that past use could influence usage intentions [11, 81]. These findings do not necessarily indicate that satisfaction and past use overshadow the stability factors in determining postadoption outcomes; however, they do suggest that satisfaction and past use have overriding effects on the outcome variables over and above the stability factors. Thus, it is important to examine whether satisfaction and past use affect usage intention and word-of-mouth even when the intervening variables (i.e., habit and loyalty) are controlled for. Of particular interest is whether the overriding relationships will also follow the patterns of differential effects implied by the systematic and heuristic mechanisms we have followed to this point.

The literature substantiates that satisfaction is an important predictor of word-of-mouth [5, 45]. Given that the act of recommendation depends to some degree on the merit of the service in question, a relationship between satisfaction and word-of-mouth is unsurprising. Several studies show that the effect of satisfaction on word-of-mouth is quite strong. For example, Yang and Peterson [112] found that a customer’s satisfaction with online banking strongly affected his or her willingness to say positive things about it ( = 0.51, p <0.001). Moreover, in a study by Hennig-Thurau et al. [44], satisfaction was shown to have a strong effect on word-of-mouth even after taking loyalty into account. Thus, it is reasonable to argue that satisfaction has a strong overriding effect on word-of-mouth.

However, the impact of a conscious factor on usage intention is expected to be weak in routine contexts. Keep in mind that in a routine context, usage intention represents one’s best guess about actual future usage. In such a case, usage intention is not necessarily one’s conscious goal but instead serves simply as a proxy for actual usage. Ouellette and Wood [81] believe that the automatic mechanism, instead of the

#

conscious mechanism, drives actual behavior in high-opportunity contexts. Thus, although conscious factors such as beliefs and attitudes may affect usage intention, their effects are unlikely to be strong in routine contexts. For example, in the study by Bagozzi et al. [13] mentioned earlier, the impact of attitudes on intention (standardized estimate = 0.21, p <0.01) appears weaker than the impact of past behavior on intention (standardized estimate = 0.49, p <0.001). Along the same line, Kim and Malhotra [59] reported relatively small effects of conscious evaluations on usage intention (standardized estimates < 0.19). In addition, Kim et al. [60] found that in the context of online news use, conscious evaluations had relatively little impact on usage intention (standardized estimates < 0.19). As such, it is reasonable to argue that the effect of satisfaction on usage intention will not be strong in the context of online information services. H5. The influence of satisfaction on word-of-mouth will be stronger than the influence of satisfaction on usage intention (i.e., satisfaction → word-of-mouth > satisfaction → usage intention).

A strong relationship between past behavior and behavioral intention has been repeatedly reported in marketing and in social psychology [13, 40, 100, 106]. This connection is also supported by the heuristic processing of information that explains a strong relationship between past experience and decisions to act [114]. The self-perception theory discussed earlier [15] and the systematic-heuristic modes [24] can explain the strong effect of past behavior on behavioral intention. More specifically, in a high-opportunity context, people tend to employ “quick and dirty” heuristics to reduce the mental effort required for deliberate judgments [83]. Obviously, past behavior constitutes valuable information in predicting future behavior [101, 114]. Thus, instead of making decisions analytically, people sometimes simply rely on past behavior to infer behavioral intention. This self-perception process has also been shown in the IS domain. For example, in the context of coupon use, Bagozzi et al. [13] found that past behavior had a considerable influence on behavioral intention (standardized estimate = 0.59, p <0.001). In addition, in IS research, Kim and Malhotra [59] showed that past use of an online portal significantly affected usage intention (standardized estimate = 0.49, p <0.001). Similarly, Kim et al. [60] showed in the context of online news use that past use was a strong predictor of usage intention (standardized estimate = 0.45, p <0.001, calibration sample group; standardized estimate = 0.61, p <0.001, validation sample group). These findings indicate that the effect of past use on behavioral intention is quite strong, at least in a routine context.

However, a strong effect of past use on word-of-mouth is not expected. Mere behavioral frequency does not necessarily mean a consumer is favorably disposed toward an online service; instead, this frequency may simply indicate how often he or she has had an opportunity to use it. Of course, past use may be positively correlated with word-of-mouth. However, such a correlation is likely to be spurious because satisfied or loyal customers are more likely to be repeat customers. Consequently, once satisfaction and loyalty are controlled for, past use should have a minimal effect on word-of-mouth. Altogether, given the strong theoretical link between past use and usage intention and the lack of strong theoretical justification for a link between past use and word-of-mouth, we posit that the overriding effects of past use are more likely to ameliorate usage intentions than they are word-of-mouth.

H6. The influence of past use on usage intention will be stronger than the influence of past use on word-ofmouth (i.e., past use → usage intention > past use → word-of-mouth).

## 3.4. Control Variables

To statistically control the potential noise that may result from missing variables, this study incorporated several control variables that may be pertinent in the context of online information services. As depicted in Figure 1, the control variables consist of two types: beliefs in the technology acceptance model (TAM) and demographic characteristics.

TAM beliefs include perceived usefulness and perceived ease-of-use. Perceived usefulness refers to the degree to which an individual perceives it effective to use an online information service. In contrast, perceived ease-of-use is defined as the degree to which an individual perceives it easy to use an online information service [34]. These TAM variables have been found to be important in regulating consumers’ reactions to online services. For example, Koufaris [62] showed in the context of online shopping that perceived usefulness affected behavioral intention. In addition, Gefen et al. [43] also found in a study of online shopping that perceived usefulness and perceived ease-of-use determined behavioral intention. Thus, to avoid misspecification of the model, this study incorporates the TAM variables as control variables.

#

In addition, the proposed model includes four demographic variables: age, gender, Web experience, and Web usage. Venkatesh et al. [105] showed that age and gender are important variables in a study of IT use; thus, the same variables are likely to be significant in explaining the use of an online information service [45]. Moreover, an individual’s usage of and experience with the Web are said to characterize his or her behavioral pattern on the Internet [77]. Therefore, Web experience and Web usage are taken into account to accurately assess the true relationships between the research variables that are the focus of this study. Overall, this study takes into account these control variables to enhance the robustness of the findings about the hypothesized relationships and to shed more light on consumers’ postadoption reactions to online services that the loyalty- and habit-related mechanisms cannot explain.

## 4. Methodology and Data Analysis

## 4.1. Data Collection

Actual users of online information services were the target population of a field study conducted to collect data necessary to test the research model and hypotheses. A sample frame was drawn from panel members of a market research firm as a way to reduce sampling-frame error that can result from a restricted focus on a particular website(s) (e.g., CNN.com) or types of respondents (e.g., college students, adults in a certain geographic area). The research company randomly selected 1,500 members from a nationwide panel pool.

An e-mail invitation, including a link to a Web-based survey questionnaire, was sent to each of the selected members to solicit survey participation. Each invitation also included a secret code for the potential respondent to use for later identification. The subjects were asked to list three information websites (e.g., Yahoo!, CNN.com, ESPN.com, iVillage.com) that they had visited. Then, they were asked to answer questions regarding one of the three websites; the questions were randomly assigned by the Web-based survey system. This procedure was used to reduce any bias that might result from self-selection of a website and also as a way to enhance the generalizability of the findings.

#

The online survey ran for one week; during this period, follow-up invitations were sent to nonrespondents, but not more than twice. As a result, 412 responses were collected, representing a response rate of 27.5%. However, 20 responses were deleted because more than half of the questions were unanswered. After this deletion, 392 usable responses remained, yielding an effective response rate of 26.1%. No significant difference was found between respondents and nonrespondents in terms of age and gender. The mean age was 35 years, and 52% were female. About two thirds of the subjects had at least some college education (67%). On average, the subjects had used the Web for six years, and they spent 20 hours a week on the Web. Appendix B summarizes the demographic profiles of the respondents in terms of age, gender, education, Web experience, and Web usage.

## 4.2. Survey Instrument

In this study, research variables were measured mainly through existing scales that have been demonstrated to be reliable and valid. Appendix C lists the specific items in each scale. The two TAM variables, perceived usefulness and perceived ease-of-use, were measured through the items used in a study of Web usage by Agarwal and Karahanna [2]. The original items were directed toward the Web in general but were modified in our study so as to be specific to a website. The perceived usefulness scale contained three items and the perceived ease-of-use scale four items. They were measured on 7-point scales anchored with “strongly disagree” (1) and “strongly agree” (7). Meanwhile, the satisfaction scale was modified from Spreng et al. [95]. It was measured using three items on 7-point semantic differential scales anchored as: frustrated/contented, displeased/pleased, and terrible/delighted. The adverb “very” was deleted from the original “displeased/pleased” item because the other satisfaction items were not associated with such an adverb [32]. Past use was measured using the continuous-open scale [9, 31] on which respondents were asked to specify the frequency of past use in answer to the following question: “In the course of the past 4 weeks, how many times have you visited this website?”

A scale for loyalty was modified from the scales developed by Beatty and Kahle [14] and Wulf et al. [111]. Care was taken to include only attitudinal items that differed from their behavioral consequences, such as usage intention and recommendation. In addition, the scale was developed to be directed toward a certain website rather than toward a product or traditional store, which was the case in the original studies. The scale consisted of three items; specifically, the first two items were borrowed from Beatty and Kahle [14], and the third was borrowed from Wulf et al. [111]. Each of the loyalty items was measured on a 7-point scale anchored with “strongly disagree” (1) and “strongly agree” (7). The habit scale was developed based on the definition of the construct and on existing scales from the consumer behavior literature [14, 80]. Attempts were made to ensure that the scale would tap the automatic, semiconscious nature of habit (e.g., “out of force of habit,” “without a deliberate plan beforehand,” etc.). This scale was measured using four items, each of which is assessed by a 7-point scale anchored with “strongly disagree” (1) and “strongly agree” (7).

This study used Srinivasan et al.’s [96] word-of-mouth scale. Two items were included in the study, each of which is assessed by a 7-point scale anchored with “strongly disagree” (1) and “strongly agree” (7). Usage intention was measured through a 2-item scale that was developed in this study with special emphasis on capturing the frequency dimension [97]. The first item related to intention toward system usage in the next four weeks. It was measured on a 7-point scale anchored with “infrequently” (1) and “frequently” (7). The second item asked respondents to choose one of the eight frequency categories that matched how often they intended to visit the website during the next four weeks. Finally, four demographic variables were also measured. Those variables were age, gender, Web experience, and Web usage. Age was measured by asking respondents to enter their age. For gender, male and female were coded as 1 and 2, respectively. To measure Web experience, respondents were asked to enter their years of experience with the Web. Web usage was measured by asking respondents to enter the average number of hours they use the Web per week.

## 5. Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine how conscious- and automatic-related mechanisms regulate postadoption phenomena in the context of online information services. The findings of this study show that as expected, two different mechanisms controlled postadoption phenomena: (1) the conscious mechanism as reflected in the relationships between satisfaction, loyalty, and word-of-mouth, and (2) the automatic mechanism as represented by the relationships between past use, habit, and usage intention. Simply put, the causal linkages between the variables related to loyalty were generally stronger than their associations with the habit-related variables, whereas the relationships between the habit-related variables were stronger than their causal connections with the loyalty-related variables. These findings suggest that although loyalty and habit are commonly known as secondhand stability characteristics acquired from past interactions with a particular service, the stability factors differ strikingly in their formative processes and behavioral consequences. As a whole, this research contributes to the IS literature by clarifying the interplay of the

conscious and automatic stability factors in regulating consumers’ postadoption reactions to online information services.

## 5.1. Theoretical Contributions

## 5.1.1. Formation of Habit

A major principle of James’ habit theory [50] is that repeated performance strengthens habit. Thus, the validity of his habit theory hinges on an empirical association between past behavior and habit. In the IS domain, the effect of past behavior on habit was shown in an empirical study by Limayem et al. [67]. Nevertheless, Limayem et al.’s [67] study leaves numerous unresolved questions about the nature of habit. For example, Limayem et al. [67] reported that the effect of satisfaction on habit (standardized estimate = $0 . 2 3 , \mathrm { t } = 4 . 3 5 )$ was considerably stronger than the effect of past use on habit (standardized estimate = 0.12, t $= 1 . 8 8 )$ . Surprisingly, these findings imply that satisfaction exceeds past use in importance in the formation of habit. Moreover, although Limayem et al. [67] measured perceived usefulness in their study, it is important to note that they did not control for its effect on habit. But our results show that perceived usefulness has a strong positive effect on habit that should be accounted for. Interestingly enough, a reanalysis of the correlation matrix reported in the study by Limayem et al. [67] indicates that the relationship between past use and habit becomes nonsignificant when perceived usefulness is controlled for. This finding is diametrically opposed to the habit theory, which predicts a significant relationship between past behavior and habit.

The present study is the first in the IS domain to demonstrate that past use is the most important predictor of habit, at least in the context of online information services. Specifically, as shown in Table 3, past use is significantly stronger than satisfaction as a predictor of habit. A separate analysis also found that the effect of past use on habit $( \gamma = 0 . 3 4 )$ is significantly greater than the effect of perceived usefulness on habit (γ $= 0 . 2 0 ) \ ( \mathbf { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ . These findings form the first pieces of empirical evidence that past use is indeed a critical component in the formation of habit, at least in a high-opportunity context like the use of online information services. These findings support the main premise of the habit theory. We note, however, that Limayem et al. [67] did not find a strong link between past use and habit and surmise that the absence of this strong link could be because habits had not necessarily risen in their more general context of Internet use. However, our context of online information services allows us to see a more stable context in which habit is well formed. Our results allow us to go beyond the link between conscious factors and habit found by Limayem et al. [67] to demonstrate that past use greatly overshadows conscious factors in the formation of habit.

## 5.1.2. Formation of Loyalty

Reichheld [89] notes that repeat purchases do not necessarily indicate a level of loyalty because repeat customers “may be trapped by inertia, indifference, or exit barriers erected by the company or circumstance” (p. 48). Therefore, for a better understanding of online consumer behavior, it is important to examine the essence of loyalty and how loyalty differs from repeat behavior and other conscious factors (e.g., perceived usefulness, satisfaction). In the IS domain, the concept of loyalty has been a focal construct in examining online consumer behavior [41, 57, 69]. However, few studies have undertaken to examine if past use has a direct impact on loyalty after conscious factors are controlled for. This study is among the first to systematically investigate the nature of loyalty as a function of past use as well as a function of other conscious factors. One of the new findings of this study is that past use positively affects loyalty even after conscious factors are controlled for. Nevertheless, a more important finding here is that past use is not a dominant source of loyalty; this helps explain why repeat customers are not necessarily transformed into loyal customers. Although Hypothesis 1 (satisfaction → loyalty > satisfaction → habit) did not find significant support, the satisfaction-loyalty relationship $( \gamma = 0 . 1 8 , \mathbf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ exceeded the satisfaction-habit relationship $( \gamma = 0 . 0 7 , \mathbf { p } = \mathrm { n s } )$ in absolute terms. This study shows that although repeated use strengthens loyalty, simple repetition is not a primary cause of loyalty; rather, as indicated in the classification scheme proposed in this study, conscious processing is the main driver in the formation of loyalty. This finding aligns with much literature on the satisfaction-loyalty relationship (e.g., [44, 61, 96, 98]).

## 5.1.3. Usage Intention

Loyalty has long been known as a major determinant of usage intention [44, 98], and a number of prominent studies in IS have validated this relationship [35, 41, 57]. In line with prior research, this study also finds a relatively high correlation between loyalty and usage intention $( \mathrm { r } = 0 . 4 9 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ (Table 1). However, the results of our structural model revealed that once habit was controlled for, the effect of loyalty on usage intention became not significant in our routine context of online information services. Our study is the first to show that in routine settings, loyalty does not directly drive usage intentions. Instead, habit seemed the primary reason why users intended to continue routinely using their online information services. This finding is surprising because prior empirical studies in IS research that have considered both the conscious and automatic mechanisms, such as Limayem et al. [67] and Limayem and Hirt [66], have assumed that habit can have no influence on usage intentions. However, a reanalysis of the data reported in both those studies reveals that habit would have had a significant impact on behavioral intention, but neither of them explicitly modeled such a relationship.<sup>4</sup> The present study is meaningful because of its theoretical explanation of the habit-intention relationship and empirical demonstration that the hypothesized path is statistically significant.

## 5.1.4. Word-of-mouth

IS research has long focused on an examination of IT use that is narrowly conceptualized and operationalized as behavioral frequency or duration. Nevertheless, numerous researchers are beginning to suggest that whereas IT use is an important outcome variable, more research should be directed toward understanding outcomes that transcend mere usage [3, 20]. An especially interesting question that arises from this new stream of research is whether the antecedents of IT use could remain effective as the antecedents of other outcomes [16]. Habit is a relatively new construct in IS research, but it has been consistently shown to be an important antecedent of IT use. However, few studies in the IS field in particular and in other disciplines in general have tried to assess the effectiveness of habit in explaining other behavioral outcomes. This study is one of the first attempts to introduce a new type of outcome, more specifically, word-of-mouth, to IS habit research. Word-of-mouth is known to be critical to the long-term survival of online firms as a way to recruit new customers. Thus, it is important to know whether habit has as strong an effect on word-ofmouth as it has on IT use. The findings of this study are highly consistent with the research hypotheses that the effect of habit on word-of-mouth is small, if any, and significantly smaller than the effect of habit on

usage intention. Overall, a major contribution of this study is to demonstrate that word-of-mouth is largely regulated by conscious factors and unaffected by habit.

## 5.2. Practical Implications

Loyal customers may visit their favorite websites often, but repeat customers are not necessarily loyal customers. Despite this difference, this distinction is rarely made in practice. In this sense, one of the interesting findings from this study is that repeat use may simply occur out of habit without formation of a commitment. However, habit is known to be fragile in the face of a short-term service breakdown or the entrance of a competitor; on the other hand, loyalty is robust in terms of its resistance to situational changes and thus, contributes more significantly to the long-term profitability of online firms [48, 65, 91, 102]. Consistent with the literature, the present study demonstrates that people who say positive things to others about an online information service are mostly loyal customers, not necessarily repeat customers or habitual customers. Thus, the findings of this study indicate that the popularity of routinely used online services should not be mistaken as a sign of stable, long-term patronage. This is because the visitation intentions of users in routine contexts seem to be primarily driven by fragile habits. That is, to better understand their customers and effectively compete with other online businesses, practitioners should be able to distinguish between “spurious loyalty” and “true loyalty” [89].

Reichheld [89] argued that word-of-mouth would be better than satisfaction as a predictor of a firm’s performance in the competitive market. But recent studies show that although word-of-mouth is an important metric in predicting a firm’s long-term profitability, other variables also deserve attention. For example, Keiningham et al. [55] found satisfaction as significant as word-of-mouth, if not more so, as an indicator of a firm’s revenue growth. In addition, Morgan and Rego [76] showed satisfaction outperformed word-of-mouth as a predictor of business performance. Although the present study does not relate business performance to customers’ perceptions or intentions, it demonstrates that satisfaction plays an important role in determining postadoption outcomes such as word-of-mouth and usage intention. These findings suggest that unless customers are satisfied, it is difficult to elicit customer patronage, e.g., word-of-mouth and usage intention, that will ultimately lead to the success of a firm. Thus, practitioners are advised that regardless of its

#

significance as a direct predictor of a firm’s profitability, customer satisfaction should be carefully managed for better business performance.

Together, the two stability mechanisms of IT use offer a new lens for managers to use in devising strategies to retain and manage users over the long-term. Although the research on how the two mechanisms relate to stickiness and switching is nascent, a few interesting strategies can be considered. First, our results suggest that managers should first determine whether their users believe they are using a service routinely. Unlike users in transaction-oriented settings, users in routine settings seem to be using services automatically without great deliberation, and they could readily switch if circumstances change. This implies that habitual usage is directly related to the current revenue of an online information service provider, but this revenue is fragile without stickiness (e.g. satisfaction). On the other hand, users who are delighted with a service and appear to be consciously loyal might not become frequent users if usage does not become habitual. One ramification of infrequent use is that services that rely on advertising revenue rather than subscription fees might not be able to recover their costs from otherwise loyal users. Finally, managers who are seeking to start a word-of-mouth campaign – such as a viral marketing effort – should also realize that a large user-base that mainly uses their service out of habit might fail to spread word-of-mouth and thus fail to attract enough new customers. Therefore, managers should seek to understand how to weave between the two mechanisms to successfully implement their chosen strategy, be it to build a stable user-base resistant to competition, to attract active users who generate visitation revenue, or to capitalize on the delight of users as a way to trigger a viral word-of-mouth effect. But equally important, we encourage managers to appreciate the risks of overexploiting one mechanism at the cost of the other.

## 5.3. Limitations and Further Research

Several limitations of this study should be noted. One relates to the particular context considered in this study, i.e., online information services. As mentioned earlier, the model and research hypotheses in this article were developed with the unique characteristics of online information services in mind. Specifically, we theorize that in this particular high-opportunity context, habit is easily formed and exerts a strong effect on subsequent use. However, this online information service domain may differ from other contexts. For

#

example, in the context of online transaction services in which a person considers buying a big-ticket item, habit is unlikely to be important. Thus, the findings of this study may not be generalizable to other contexts.

We also have noted the failure of two of our differential hypotheses related to the strength of the relationships between satisfaction and loyalty because we found that these relationships were statistically comparable to the weak relationships between satisfaction and habit and between past use and loyalty. We need to consider, then, how our studies should measure the conscious appraisals of users. Although we originally believed that satisfaction would suffice, perceived usefulness might instead capture elements of conscious appraisal that satisfaction does not. Therefore, researchers should reconsider how to capture conscious appraisals in their settings and whether these appraisals can be parsimoniously captured by a single factor or through a combination of factors.

Another limitation of this study relates to the use of intention measures, instead of actual behavior, as a way to reflect postadoption outcomes. The use of intention variables as proxies for behavioral consequences is consistent with the literature in marketing [36, 96, 113] as well as in IS [41, 57]. Moreover, the literature shows that consumers’ perceptions and intentions (e.g., satisfaction, loyalty, word-of-mouth, repurchase intention, etc.) indeed correlate strongly with a firm’s rate of revenue growth [55, 89]. Thus, although actual behavior is not explicitly examined, this study—which offers insight into the formation of intentions at the postadoption stages—seems meaningful in its own right. However, care should be taken in relating the study’s findings to actual postadoptive behaviors or to business-level metrics.

This study may also omit some important factors. Although the proposed model (Figure 1) was found to fit the data well and to explain a large amount of the variance in the outcomes, this model may be further complemented with additional variables. For example, the notion of trust is known to be one of the key determinants of customer loyalty [41, 71, 90]. However, the model did not take into account the trust construct because (1) the trust concept is said to be “only relevant” when potential risk is associated with a transaction [26, 37, 75] and (2) individuals’ use of online information services usually, though not always, involves considerably less risk than online shopping in B2C e-commerce. Nevertheless, the findings of this

#

study should be considered tentative until further assessment of its inclusiveness of all appropriate variables and of the effects of any found missing from it are fully understood.

A problem related to the single-item scale of past use needs to be mentioned. Although past use was associated with only one indicator in this study, actual behavior has often been measured in a similar manner in both psychology and IS research [9, 100, 104]. Furthermore, to compensate for any concern the single-item scale might arouse, this study used a standard procedure to explicitly specify the measurement errors of the past use indicator [92]. As stated earlier, the measurement error was set to 0.80, which estimates our measurement of past use as being less than perfect and likely attenuates exogenous paths from it. We feel this conservative approach justifies the reasonableness of the use of the single-item past use scale; nevertheless, our findings should be interpreted with this modification in mind.

The current study opens up numerous directions for further research. For example, online consumers’ judgments, stability characteristics, and behavioral consequences are likely to change over time. As such, a longitudinal investigation into postadoption phenomena has the potential to reveal important new information about online consumer behavior. For example, the loyalty model suggests that whereas satisfaction changes widely with respect to short-term performance, loyalty is “an attained state of enduring preference to the point of determined defense” ([79], p. 42). Given that loyalty becomes less transient as a person has more experience with the service, the effect of previous loyalty on subsequent loyalty is likely to increase over time, all things being equal. In a similar vein, habit is said to become more stable with an increase in experience [50]. Thus, the relationship between previous habit and subsequent habit is expected to increase over time. Interestingly, these predictions discussed earlier also imply that over time word-of-mouth and usage intention will be more a function of loyalty and habit, but loyalty and habit will be less a function of satisfaction and past use once previous loyalty and previous habit are taken into account. In this way, a number of interesting propositions can be examined in a longitudinal study, and its findings would be expected to offer valuable insight into online consumer behavior that cannot be easily gained from a crosssectional study.

#

It is interesting to examine how the emergence of attractive alternatives (e.g., through promotions, discounts, or referrals) changes the roles that loyalty and habit play in postadoption behavior. True loyalty is somewhat irrational; accordingly, loyal customers tend to turn away “from competitive firms and other innovators that argue that the loyalist’s consumable is no longer the most efficient, lowest priced, of the highest quality, and so forth” ([79], p. 35). Meanwhile, if habitual behavior is perceived as no longer consistent with a customers’ ultimate goal, an individual can instantly change his or her way of doing things [52]. Therefore, with the introduction of a new choice, habitual users are expected to switch easily to a new service, whereas loyal customers will strongly resist changing. As online markets are increasingly more competitive, the results of this line of research will be of special interest to practitioners.

Finally, our model allowed for an association between loyalty and habit, but we did not posit any specific direction of causality. Some studies suggest that habit causes loyalty [69]; others suggest that loyaltydriven behavior leads to habit [14], and still others believe that habitual inertia moderates satisfaction to loyalty [8]. Although the relationship between a latent variable and its measured variables and manifest in structure equation models is causal [38, 51], cross-sectional survey data is “better interpreted as yielding evidence for functional relationships or alternatively relationships as far as they go but not sufficiently strong enough to suggest causality to the degree that experiments do.” ([10], p. 210). Thus, it is not definitively certain how the relationship between the two stability factors should be modeled. Once the overall nature of the conscious and automatic mechanisms is unraveled further, a more careful examination of the relationship between loyalty and habit should ensue.

## 5.4. Conclusions

Individuals’ firsthand evaluations such as perceived usefulness have long been the center of research endeavors in the IS domain. However, contemporary research highlights that especially at the postadoption stages, secondhand characteristics acquired through ongoing interactions with a service heavily outweigh simple immediate judgments as determinants of behavioral consequences. We examined two such secondhand characteristics, namely, loyalty and habit. Our findings show that although the secondhand factors perform similarly in making people stay with a service, these factors have basic differences in their

#

formative and outcome processes. This article contributes to the IS literature by offering a conceptual tool to systematically analyze the nature of the stability factors in an integrative and comparative manner. Continued use is believed to give rise to a variety of secondhand stability characteristics that include not only loyalty and habit but also some other factors such as lock-in and even addiction. It is hoped that this study will inspire further research that will yield an even better understanding of how such stability characteristics simultaneously and differentially shape postadoption phenomena.

## Appendix C: Research Constructs and Measures

Perceived Usefulness (PU) Using the website enhances my effectiveness. Using the website enhances my productivity. Using the website improves my performance.

Perceived Ease-of-Use (PEOU) Learning to operate the website is easy for me. I find it easy to get the website to do what I want it to do. It is easy for me to become skillful at using the website. I find the website easy to use.

User Satisfaction (SAT): seven-point semantic scale. Frustrated /Contented Displeased /Pleased Terrible/Delighted

Past Use (PUSE)

Loyalty (LOY) I consider myself to be highly loyal to this website. I am willing “to go to the extra mile” to remain a customer of this website. I feel loyal towards this website.

Habit (HAB) Using this website is something I do as a matter of habit. I often use this website out of force of habit. To me, using this website is routine without a deliberate plan beforehand. Using this website has become so natural for me I am often not aware I am doing it.

Word-of-Mouth (WOM) I will say positive things about this website to other people. I will recommend this website to anyone who seeks my advice.
