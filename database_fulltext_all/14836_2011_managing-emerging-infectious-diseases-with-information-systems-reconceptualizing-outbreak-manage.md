# Managing Emerging Infectious Diseases with Information Systems: Reconceptualizing Outbreak Management Through the Lens of Loose Coupling

Yi-Da Chen, Susan A. Brown

Department of Management Information Systems, Eller College of Management, University of Arizona, Tucson, Arizona 85721 {ydchenb@email.arizona.edu, suebrown@email.arizona.edu}

Paul Jen-Hwa Hu

ncreasing global connectivity makes emerging infectious diseases (EID) more threatening than ever before. IVarious information systems (IS) projects have been undertaken to enhance public health capacity for detecting EID in a timely manner and disseminating important public health information to concerned parties. While those initiatives seemed to offer promising solutions, public health researchers and practitioners raised concerns about their overall effectiveness. In this paper, we argue that the concerns about current public health IS projects are partially rooted in the lack of a comprehensive framework that captures the complexity of EID management to inform and evaluate the development of public health IS. We leverage loose coupling to analyze news coverage and contact tracing data from 479 patients associated with the severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) outbreak in Taiwan. From this analysis, we develop a framework for outbreak management. Our proposed framework identifies two types of causal circles—coupling and decoupling circles—between the central public health administration and the local capacity for detecting unusual patient cases. These two circles are triggered by important information-centric activities in public health practices and can have significant influence on the effectiveness of EID management. We derive seven design guidelines from the framework and our analysis of the SARS outbreak in Taiwan to inform the development of public health IS. We leverage the guidelines to evaluate current public health initiatives. By doing so, we identify limitations of existing public health IS, highlight the direction future development should consider, and discuss implications for research and public health policy.

Key words: public health information systems; emerging infectious disease; outbreak management; loose coupling; SARS outbreak

## 1. Introduction

They have said it before and were wrong. In February, officials in China’s Guangdong province told locals that an epidemic of a new and deadly flu-like disease was over. Within weeks, though, there was panic in neighboring Vietnam and Hong Kong as the new disease, dubbed Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS), spread across Asia. (The Economist 2003)

The pandemic of SARS in 2003 caused great concern over the capacity of central agencies to manage emerging infectious diseases (EID). This new, flu-like illness traversed southern China undetected for three months and then spread globally via Hong Kong through international flights (Peiris et al. 2003). Soon after it was identified, governments in heavily affected areas adopted World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines and implemented control measures to halt the disease. But SARS was not contained. It repeatedly broke through the barricades and came back with stronger and more deadly attacks. China and Hong Kong experienced several waves of massive SARS infections. Taiwan, Toronto, and Singapore reported unexpected large-scale hospital outbreaks after initial proclamations of successful containment (Chu et al. 2005, McDonald et al. 2004). The perception that public health officials were unable to effectively control the illness in these areas caused not only enormous social panic but also great economic turbulence. Asia lost an estimated \$60 billion in revenues because of the pandemic, particularly in the sectors of airlines and tourism (The Economist 2004).

The failure of central agencies to detect and manage SARS was generally attributed to weak disease surveillance mechanisms (Heymann and Rodier 2004). It was believed that if officials had received more information indicating EID outbreaks sooner, important public health decisions could have been made to control the disease faster. To strengthen the collection and exchange of health care-related data in national public health systems, governments significantly increased funding and accelerated the adoption of information systems (IS). For example, in 2004, the Department of Health and Human Services granted 50 states \$849 million to improve EID preparedness; about 30% of the funds were used for information technology (Loonsk et al. 2006). At the core of such endeavors was the development of syndromic surveillance systems aimed at heightening central situation awareness and responsiveness to novel infectious diseases. By electronically receiving surrogate data that may reveal the signs of EID sooner than formal clinical diagnosis, such systems are posited to be capable of issuing early alerts regarding unusual disease events and providing public health officials with nearly real-time access to the needed data to facilitate competent decision making (Chen et al. 2010).

While various syndromic surveillance systems were being developed and implemented, public health practitioners and researchers raised concerns about whether directing subtle environmental signals to public health agencies, as a means of central control, could truly transform existing surveillance mechanisms for responding to EID. Theoretically, surveillance alerts generated by the system are designed to increase central public health agencies’ awareness of unusual local events, so that effective actions can be taken in a timely manner. The reality, however, is that public health practitioners experience difficulty in dissecting the meaning behind frequent surveillance alerts and often feel overwhelmed by the volume of the alerts. As reflected in one practitioner’s criticism after completing a followup investigation of 200 warnings issued by BioSense in a two-month period, “[n]o actual epidemic or at least none of public health importance were discovered” (Fearnley 2008, p. 1625). Without knowing what kinds of alerts merit public health attention, public health personnel have to rely on tedious, labor-intensive field investigations to understand why the system triggered those signals and then determine a proper response to the alert (Duchin 2003, Fearnley 2008). In the meantime, local front-line clinicians complained about being ignored by the system, claiming that they are often the ones who initiate the clinical investigation and make central agencies aware of unusual illnesses (Mostashari and Hartman 2003). This claim is particularly evident in a striking account of SARS detection. The first sign of SARS was, in fact, traced back to a warning generated by the Public Health Intelligence Network, an Internet-based electronic surveillance system. But public health officials remained unaware of the illness and believed it was a seasonal flu until clinicians in Vietnam noticed something unusual and prompted WHO to launch an investigation, more than three months later (Heymann and Rodier 2004). This example challenges the notion that the obstacle to detecting and managing novel infectious diseases can simply be removed by tightening disease surveillance mechanisms via centralized surveillance systems. If that were the case, it would not have taken months for central officials to realize that an anomaly had been spotted.

In this study, we argue that the major difficulty of recognizing EID outbreaks early lies with the high degree of clinical ambiguity that EID evoke. According to Weick (1995), individuals experience ambiguity when “an ongoing stream 0 0 0 supports several different interpretations at the same time” (pp. 91–92). Ambiguity can be evoked by inconsistent presentation of features, poor understanding of cause-effect relationships, or lack of time and attention in a situation (Weick 1995). As a result, decision makers are not able to assign those situations into mutually exclusive categories for reaction (March 1994) and are inclined to interpret the situation solely based on their existing knowledge and past experience (Mehan and Wood 1985). When a new infectious disease emerges, its symptoms may appear similar to other wellknown diseases, such as SARS exhibiting seasonal flu symptoms. This similarity can cause ambiguity in clinical diagnosis. Without knowing the illness or its existence, public health officials and clinicians have a tendency to diagnose it as a disease with which they are familiar, a critical factor that makes early detection of EID difficult. After EID identification, the ambiguity can still persist and lead to misdiagnosis if its clinical presentations are inconsistent or if reliable diagnostic testing is unavailable. Under conditions of ambiguity, simply providing a large amount of existing data to decision makers is not helpful because they do not know what information is relevant to their decisions (Weick 1995). Instead, individuals need personal empowerment to exercise judgment and face-to-face interactions that enable discussion, investigation, and experimentation (Orton and Weick 1990, Weick 1995). Consequently, sensitive surveillance alerts alone are not sufficient to respond to EID. A key challenge to successful EID management is determining how public health officials can simultaneously organize local clinicians via central coordination and empower them with autonomy to notice and investigate subtle but important differences in patient symptoms.

The simultaneous need for coordination and autonomy can be best captured via the loose coupling theory proposed by Orton and Weick (1988). Loose coupling is most appropriate because it (1) describes how autonomous actors, such as local clinicians in our case, are preserved in the organization as sensors for responding to a rapidly changing environment (Weick 1976), a factor crucial to effective disease surveillance and (2) specifies a unique causal relationship between central control and local judgment that affects the organization of personnel involved in managing ambiguous situations, such as EID containment. Using loose coupling as our theoretical lens, we conduct a case study on the Taiwan SARS outbreak. SARS evoked one of the most ambiguous clinical situations that public health workers have ever encountered, therefore qualifying as an extreme case for a case study (Yin 2009). Furthermore, this outbreak exhibited an unexpected transition from successful disease containment to a sudden unexpected series of hospital outbreaks; this pattern of events can help to identify coordination issues in the response to EID. We believe an understanding of outbreak management through the lens of loose coupling can identify important system requirements for successful EID containment, and thus contribute to the design and development of public health IS.

The reminder of this paper is organized as follows. First, we review the traditional approach to outbreak management—a central control framework— and discuss how existing IS initiatives address its deficiency in managing EID but still have limitations. We then introduce loose coupling as our guiding theory for analyzing the case study—the SARS outbreak in Taiwan. After discussing the case, we present a framework for understanding outbreak management developed through the lens of loose coupling. Based on the framework, we present a set of guidelines for designing public health IS. We follow this with an assessment of current public health IS based on the proposed framework and design guidelines, and discuss key implications for research, practice, and policy.

## 2. Outbreak Management and Loose Coupling

## 2.1. Traditional Approach to Outbreak Management: Central Control Framework

The concept of outbreak management has long been developed from a central point of view. It revolves around how public health agencies can employ various activities, including surveillance and investigation, to discover and control infected clusters in the population. To detect potential disease outbreaks, central agencies request geographically dispersed clinicians and laboratories to report suspect patient cases that meet the criteria of public health importance (Tulchinsky and Varavikova 2000). By regularly reviewing and analyzing surveillance reports, public health officials identify significant public health events and conduct investigations to gather and clarify the epidemiological features of patients. Then, the central agencies are able to promptly distribute useful information to concerned parties and organize local clinicians for containing the infected clusters from propagation (Tyler and Dicker 1997). This centralized control view of outbreak management is illustrated in Figure 1.

According to this hierarchical, central control framework, current public health mechanisms can break down at several decision points and leave central agencies unaware of the emergence of a novel infectious disease. First, when infected patients appear in the clinic for medical attention, a clinician interprets their symptoms or diagnostic test results based on prior knowledge and experience (Bravata et al. 2002). The clinician may misdiagnose the patient as having other similar, well-known diseases and fail in local surveillance (Point T1). Second, after receiving surveillance reports from clinicians, public health agencies typically follow a series of evaluation procedures. To determine whether a reported case is of public health importance, they perform statistical analyses for verification, such as comparing the report with other sources of surveillance data or checking historical trends in terms of time, place, and person (Bravata et al. 2002, Tyler and Dicker 1997). A surveillance report that reveals the signs of an EID may be dismissed because of significance or plausibility issues (Point T2). Third, after the agencies decide to conduct investigations, the limited cases on hand may not show any epidemiological linkage that indicates the existence of an outbreak, leading the investigator to incorrectly conclude that those cases do not merit public health attention (Point T3). Last, after an outbreak is detected, who should be informed through public health alerts becomes a critical decision (Bravata et al. 2002). Public health agencies may not have a complete contact list of clinicians, and thus fail to fully disseminate the important public health information (Point T4).

To strengthen central control capacity for responding to EID and address these deficiencies, various public health projects have been initiated. Table 1 summarizes the initiatives of the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) Public Health Information Network (PHIN),<sup>1</sup> a national organization that coordinates the development of public health IS. According to our review, the activities of upward surveillance reporting (Point T1) and downward information dissemination (Point T4) are emphasized via a number of data or information exchange projects. These projects are aimed at increasing the availability of electronic health care data for surveillance (Point T1) and facilitating the sharing and distribution of important public health information (Point T4). At the center of these projects is a national syndromic surveillance system—BioSense—that integrates different sources of data to facilitate central decision making. It complements the current mechanism by creating a new surveillance channel (Point T1) through surrogate data, such as patients’ chief complaints and laboratory test orders (Bradley et al. 2005). Furthermore, it employs statistical models to automatically evaluate incoming surveillance streams for EID alerts (Point T2) and provides advanced spatial-temporal analytical tools to support central investigation and monitoring (Point T3) (Chen et al. 2010).

While these IS initiatives seemed to adequately address the breakdowns in the central control framework, public health practitioners and researchers expressed concerns as to whether this collection could actually remove the obstacles to responding to EID (Fearnley 2008, Mostashari and Hartman 2003). The first issue regards the omission of local clinicians from the scope of the syndromic surveillance system. According to an analysis investigating the determinants of successful EID detection, 72% of the disease outbreaks were in effect detected through cliniciangenerated surveillance reports and specimens (Dato et al. 2004). But the current IS projects are not focused on increasing clinicians’ engagement in disease surveillance, but rather on delegating the role to sophisticated detection algorithms. To highlight this issue, public health practitioners urged public health agencies to recognize local clinicians as essential partners in EID management not only because clinicians often initiate the first phase of investigation, but they also actively participate in the subsequent treatment containment (Gerberding et al. 2002).

At the same time, while environmental signals were directed to central agencies for decision making, public health officials were experiencing confusion over frequent surveillance alerts and characterized them as “nondiagnostic” (Duchin 2003, Mostashari and Hartman 2003). Based on signal detection theory, early outbreak identification inherits a certain degree of ambiguity and the sensitivity of a detection method is often at the cost of specificity (Wagner et al. 2001). To be sensitive enough to catch rare unusual EID events, low accuracy on system alerts is somewhat inevitable. As ambiguity escalates to a national level, public health personnel simply could not dissect the meaning behind a large volume of unclear signals. They needed field investigations to figure out why surveillance alerts were triggered and asked for protocols to determine which alerts truly merit public health attention (Duchin 2003, Henning 2004). As a result, current IS-related public health initiatives were criticized for not fundamentally addressing the challenges posed by EID, but rather simply “repurposing existing public health infrastructures” (Fearnley 2008, p. 1629).

## 2.2. Outbreak Management Agencies as a Loosely Coupled Organization

Part of the problem with current public health IS projects stems from the assumption that managing EID is the same as routine disease surveillance. The traditional view of outbreak management (or the central control framework) in Figure 1 reflects how public health agencies organize different activities to create daily defense mechanisms. It centers on wellestablished organization structures and rules in the public health system, and therefore is much more suitable for the situation where means-ends connections for disease surveillance are certain. Following the central control framework, IS initiatives naturally seek to tighten data flow based on the existing structures and link information with a set of well-defined decisions. The problem is that the novelty of external stimuli makes a difference in the ways that organizations behave. According to the theory of bounded rationality, individuals and organizations normally rely on static repertoires of performance programs for daily actions and decisions (March and Simon 1985). However, when an external stimulus is novel, such as EID, problem-solving activities (e.g., discussion, investigation, or experiment) are needed to explore proper action programs for response. During such an exploration process, the linkage between problems and solutions, or the means-ends connection, is ambiguous. Using IS to provide more data is not helpful because decision makers simply cannot see the connection between the information and the decisions (March 1987, Weick 1995). To resolve ambiguity, autonomous investigators are needed. Interacting via face-to-face mechanisms, investigators can identify their own interesting relevant data during the search process and help reconstruct a meaningful set of factual premises for decision making (March 1987, Orton and Weick 1988). Accordingly, empowering local personnel to exercise judgment and conduct investigations is a factor critical for EID containment efforts.

It is important to note that increasing central capability for maintaining organizational stability is still indispensible for managing a large-scale disease outbreak under ambiguity. As March and Simon (1985) argue, organization structure is critical for organizations to confront and deal with environmental changes. “[I]n order for an organization to behave adaptively, it needs some stable regulations and procedures that it can employ in carrying out its adaptive practices” (March and Simon 1985, p. 150). This argument is reflected in central personnel’s request for “protocols” or rules to handle unfamiliar ambiguous surveillance signals. How to meet these two opposing requirements at the same time—increasing individual autonomy in judgment while enhancing central control capacity for stability—and use IS to facilitate this dual process becomes the key to effective EID management. To analyze these simultaneous opposing organizational tensions, we adopt the lens of loose coupling.

The concept of loose coupling reconciles two contrary perspectives in contemporary organizations: open and closed systems. The open system perspective interprets the division of functional departments as an indeterminate evolution process for adapting to external competition and environments. The closed-system perspective, on the other hand, sees the organization as a rational entity with determined alignment of technology, information, and personnel to fulfill its static mission (Scott and Davis 2006). Loose coupling suggests that an organization has pressures toward both decoupling (decoupling mechanism) and coupling (coupling mechanism) and can be simultaneously open and closed (Orton and Weick 1988, 1990). The decoupling mechanism increases individuals’ judgment for responding to changing environments, while the coupling mechanism tightens control for organizational efficiency. As a result, elements in loose coupling are distinctive but responsive, having a high level of autonomy and acting as sensors (Weick 1976). Local clinicians involved in outbreak management thus form a loosely coupled organization with public health officials; they remain autonomous in clinical judgment (Chiasson and Davidson 2004, Kohli and Kettinger 2004), while following central control guidelines as instructed by the public health agency for the same purpose of disease containment.

Orton and Weick (1988) approach the idea of organization from a dynamic point of view, suggesting that an organization is at least composed of (1) individual elements that hold different perceptions or interpretations of a situation and (2) a source of order that consolidates individual differences for agreement and preference. As a result, the coupling of organizational elements is, in effect, dynamic and constantly seeking a balance between independence and coordination. “[E]lements may appear or disappear and may merge or become separated in response to need deprivations within the individual, group, and/or organization” (Weick 1976, p. 5). Orton and Weick (1988) posit that the looseness of coupling is determined by the certainty of meansends connections for accomplishing tasks. When the means-ends connection is uncertain, such as solving a complex clinical problem, a looser coupling is expected, so that individuals can experiment or exercise judgment more frequently, making them more capable of adapting to unexpected events. The increasing exercise of judgment helps to reconstruct factual premises on which the means-ends connection was built and, in turn, reduces its uncertainty. In essence, the means-ends connection forms a unique causal loop with the frequency and quality of individual judgment, mediated by the looseness of coupling. A loosely coupled organization retains agreement on preferred ends but allows individuals to disagree on the means-ends connection, developing collective judgment to promote wise choice of factual premises (Orton and Weick 1988).

The guiding loose coupling framework for outbreak management is presented in Figure 2. Following Orton and Weick’s propositions (1988), we suggest that the effectiveness of central control guidelines for managing EID affects the looseness of coupling among the central and local personnel involved. When the central guidelines are effective (i.e., the certainty of means-ends connection is high), the coupling of personnel is tightened (Arrow 1) and typical cases are efficiently managed. But the tightened coupling inevitably decreases local sensitivity and makes unusual cases difficult to discover (Arrow 2). When the guidelines are ineffective, the coupling is loosened (Arrow 1) through self-regulation. Local clinicians make their own interpretations more freely (Arrow 2) and adapt to local situations. The variation in the frequency and quality of local surveillance, in turn, influences the effectiveness of central control guidelines (Arrow 3), which further affects the coupling of central and local personnel (Arrow 1). Through this circular relationship, central public health agencies can maneuver to maintain a balance between decoupling and coupling mechanisms.

Loose coupling provides useful guidance for conceptualizing the complexity of managing EID. However, the theory does not specify the essential information-centric activities (e.g., data collection, data analysis, or decision making) that may come into play in the causal loop. Such information-centric activities can provide important implications for the design of public health IS. By analyzing the SARS outbreak in Taiwan, we aim to augment the framework with important information-centric activities and enable a comprehensive evaluation of current public health IS initiatives.

## 3. Research Method

## 3.1. Research Setting

This study followed a single-case, mixed-methods design. The Taiwan SARS outbreak qualifies as an extreme or unique case whose occurrence is rare and which alone is worth documenting and analyzing (Yin 2009). First, SARS was one of the most ambiguous clinical situations that health care workers (HCW) have ever experienced. It emerged in the Guangdong Province of China in November 2002—an influenza season—and caused influenza-like symptoms in its early stage, such as fever, sore throat, and cough (Peiris et al. 2003). While it was difficult to differentiate SARS from seasonal influenza merely through patients’ early symptoms, no point-of-care diagnostic tool or vaccine was available for clarification or public health intervention. Clinicians had to rely on epidemiological cues (i.e., travel history to SARS affected areas and close contact with a SARS patient) for early identification or waited for patients’ later development of lung infiltrate for assessment (McDonald et al. 2004). However, a number of reports pointed out that SARS patients might have atypical clinical presentations when they had chronic conditions (CDC 2003, Fisher et al. 2003), a situation that further increased the ambiguity in the effort of SARS containment. Therefore, this single-case study can provide insights into the very nature of outbreak management characterized by deep clinical ambiguity, and allow us to examine the sufficiency of current IS initiatives in response to EID.

A second important aspect of the SARS outbreak in Taiwan is that it exhibited an unexpected transition from successful containment to a series of devastating hospital outbreaks (Chu et al. 2005, Ho and Su 2004). In the early phase of SARS control, commonly defined as the importation phase, clusters of local transmission were triggered by a few individuals who had traveled to mainland China or Hong Kong. These clusters of patients were successfully identified and managed, and no mortality was reported. But after mid-April, a municipal hospital in Taipei (hereafter referred to as Hospital A<sup>2</sup>) reported having a SARS hospital outbreak. Six other hospitals subsequently reported incidents of hospital-acquired or nosocomial infection that were all traced back to Hospital A (Chen et al. 2004c, Chu et al. 2005). In this phase of hospital outbreaks, the number of SARS cases soared dramatically from the low 30s to more than 400 (Yip et al. 2007). By investigating key factors that triggered such a transition in this case, we can identify important information-centric activities and IS design guidelines for reducing the potential for such similar outbreaks to happen again.

## 3.2. Data Collection and Analysis

To comprehensively reconstruct outbreak scenarios in the Taiwan SARS outbreak, two types of data sources were used in this study: (1) the documentation of the outbreak that was published in public health-related journals and (2) a set of contact investigation data collected through interviews with SARS patients. We searched online journal databases for documentation with three keywords—Taiwan, SARS, and outbreak— and reviewed the articles for relevance. A total of 30 articles were found and 14 of them appeared in CDC associated publications, including Emerging Infectious Diseases and Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report. From SARS-related documentation, we identified events associated with traditional outbreak management mechanisms, such as disease surveillance and contact investigation, and events pertaining to the implementation of control measures. Identified events were then compiled into a chronology and carefully reviewed for potential causal relationships that map to our loose coupling framework. An abbreviated version of the chronology is provided in the online supplement, Appendix A.<sup>3</sup>

The contact investigation data includes detailed contact history of 479 patients who were confirmed positive for SARS coronavirus (SARS-CoV). In this data set, each patient is described by a paragraph of free text recorded during an interview. It lists the patient’s potential contacts of infection and subsequent hospital visit or admission history. An example of the translated free text is as follows:

The patient went to Guangdong China for business between 2/8 and 2/21. He came back to Taiwan and had fever on 2/25. He developed dry cough 2 days later and went to Clinic A for medical care. He went to Clinic B and was transferred to Hospital C. His chest X-ray showed pulmonary edema. He was treated in an intensive-care unit and his symptoms improved.

We coded the interview data and created contact categories as shown in Table 2. The details of the coding procedure are described in the online supplement, Appendix B. The coded contacts fall into two main categories: personal (patient to patient) and geographical (patient to location). A personal contact indicates a personal relationship or a recognizable close contact with a SARS patient, including family member, roommate, colleague, and close contact (e.g., a HCW who took care of SARS patients and became infected). A geographical contact shows where the patient had been 14 days before onset of symptoms, including overseas travel, hospital visit (e.g., visiting ill family or friend in a hospital, or a hospital visit not related to SARS), and hospital workplace (SARS infected HCWs’ affiliation). We also included SARSrelated hospital admission history in the geographical contact to track potential patients who propagated SARS among hospitals. A patient may have multiple records pertaining to a contact subcategory. For example, one patient had six family members who also were infected with SARS during the outbreak. Altogether, our data set has a total of 1,783 coded contacts.

## 4.1. Effective Central Guidelines Tightening

Coordination for Managing Typical Cases The Taiwan SARS outbreak began with a businessman who went to Guangdong Province, China, from February 5th to the 21st, 2003 (Chen et al. 2005a, Twu et al. 2003). He developed a fever and dry cough shortly after his return and was hospitalized in Hospital C on March 8th. At the time of admission, he was diagnosed with pneumonia and treated with the usual clinical precautions. On March 14th, his wife also developed a fever and pneumonia but showed atypical clinical presentation, a situation that confused but also alerted clinicians. During a collegial discussion, one of the clinicians mentioned a WHO alert that indicated an atypical pneumonia was recently found in Guangdong, China. To clarify the possibility of SARS infection, clinicians performed a contact investigation and learned of the businessman’s recent trip to Guangdong. Both patients were immediately isolated in negative pressure rooms with full precautions and reported as suspect SARS patients to the public health agency in Taiwan on the same day. A laboratory test later confirmed the wife as positive for SARS-CoV (Twu et al. 2003).

Epidemiological cues as identified in the WHO SARS control guidelines—travel history to affected areas and close contact with a probable patient—were closely followed for devising control measures (e.g., temperature monitoring of inbound flight passengers and home quarantine of close contacts), as well as for identifying potential SARS patients. When febrile patients came for screening, clinicians would inquire about their recent travels and contacts to estimate the probability of SARS infection (Chu et al. 2005,

Twu et al. 2003). Several patient clusters initiated by international travelers were successfully detected and managed. In mid-April 2003, the Taiwanese government proclaimed that the SARS infection was under control and restricted to “imported cases,” a statement that confirmed the effectiveness of the epidemiological cues in SARS containment.

## 4.2. Low Local Sensitivity Triggering Hospital Outbreaks

An unexpected event occurred when a female febrile patient went to Hospital A for medical attention on April 9th. She showed lung infiltrate on the chest X-ray, a radiographic presentation associated with SARS, but lacked significant travel or contact history according to the SARS control guidelines (Jang et al. 2004). As a result, clinicians did not consider her as a potential SARS patient and no clinical precautions were taken during her stay in the emergency room. The patient was later transferred to Hospital S. After admission, clinicians in Hospital S soon noticed her progressive changes on the chest X-ray. After a number of diagnostic tests and collegial discussions, they decided to report the patient to the public health agency as a suspect SARS patient despite her lack of travel or contact history. But the public health agency dismissed the case because it did not match the instructed patterns and no followup investigation was taken. On April 16th, her laboratory results confirmed positive for SARS-CoV (Jang et al. 2004).

On April 12th, a laundry worker in Hospital A, who often spent time in the emergency room, developed a fever and diarrhea (Chen et al. 2005a). Because he did not have any travel history and clinicians were not aware of any SARS patient appearing in the hospital, the worker did not receive special attention and he remained on duty and interacted with people as usual. On April 18th, his symptoms deteriorated and the chest X-ray showed infiltrate. He was immediately isolated in a negative pressure room and reported as a suspect SARS patient. On April 22nd, Hospital A reported a SARS outbreak that involved a number of HCWs and was segregated on April 24th (Chen et al. 2005b, Yip et al. 2007).

Figure 3 shows a social network movie that illustrates how the outbreak in Hospital A spread to nearby hospitals (Hospitals B and C). Although Hospital A called back all clinical staff for quarantine on April 24th, outpatients and visitors who had been infected before that date were not aggressively traced and monitored. They became “unrecognized SARS patients” who reported no significant contact history as required by the central guidelines (Ho and Su 2004). On April 27th, Hospital C found that a 73-year-old patient who had been kept in a regular ward for five days developed lung infiltrate. After immediate investigation, clinicians discovered that this patient had been to Hospital A on April 16th, but no agency informed them about the connection (Chen et al. 2004c). By the time of discovery, a new cluster of SARS patients was already initiated by the patient. Shortly after, prior hospital visits were incorporated as an additional epidemiological cue for SARS patient detection. On May 8th, through another contact investigation, Hospital C found that a suspect SARS patient could be traced back to its own emergency room (Chen et al. 2004c). The hospital reported to the public health agency for the possibility of a hospital outbreak and suspended its emergency service on May 12th.

## 4.4. Central Coupling Measures Balancing the Decoupling Mechanism

Since the epidemiological cues—travel history to affected areas and close contact with a probable patient—were no longer effective for identifying potential SARS patients, fever became the last reliable indicator with which both public health officials and local clinicians agreed. In late May, the Taiwanese public health agency implemented a series of nationwide fever-oriented measures to tighten the SARS containment efforts. Those measures, such as restricting over-the-counter access to antipyretics, deploying fever screening facilities in public entrances, using fever clinics and hotlines for triage, and launching a temperature monitoring campaign in schools and workplaces, served to effectively locate febrile patients for SARS screening and subsequent monitoring (Ho and Su 2004, Kaydos-Daniels et al. 2004). Meanwhile, hospitals also implemented their own localized measures to lessen the impact of incoming febrile patients on nosocomial infection, such as deploying medical questionnaires and temperature checking at all entrance points, creating separate entrances for febrile patients, mandating hand hygiene and surgical mask wearing, and prohibiting nonmedical visitors (Ho et al. 2003, McDonald et al. 2004).

By performing another VAR, we found that hospital admission played a positive role in resolving the vicious circle between personal contact and hospital infection and subsequently eased the SARS outbreak (see the online supplement, Appendix D, for details).

## 5.1. Dynamics of Loose Coupling in Outbreak Management

To provide a theoretical interpretation on the role of loose coupling in outbreak management, we leverage the work of Orton and Weick (1988). Their theory suggests a circular relationship among the effectiveness of central control guidelines, the looseness of coupling among personnel, and the local capacity for detecting unusual cases, as shown in Figure 2. By analyzing the Taiwan SARS outbreak, we further identified important information-centric activities in public health practices that trigger coupling and decoupling circles within the circular relationship. These informationcentric activities and their associated circles subsequently determined the effectiveness of the efforts for SARS containment in our case study. Our proposed framework for outbreak management is depicted in Figure 6.

To clarify the applicability of our framework, we define clinical ambiguity as a situation that differentiates outbreak management of EID from that of well-known diseases. Clinical diagnosis typically involves a certain degree of ambiguity. Frequent faceto-face discussions with colleagues for clarification are thus an essential part of health care work practices (Ash et al. 2004, Kohli and Kettinger 2004). When dealing with well-known infectious diseases, clinicians can reduce ambiguity by looking for distinct clinical features and employing available diagnostic tests. 5.1.1. Central Coupling Circle. Since clinical cues (i.e., patient symptoms or syndromes) yield inconsistent results, epidemiological cues are required and included in central control guidelines to reduce clinical ambiguity. They are identified from contact investigation data and reveal significant epidemiological connections consistently found among patients in terms of person, place, and time. Hence, such cues describe how the disease was disseminating through major pathways and constitute key indicators of potentially infected populations. By publishing epidemiological cues, public health officials can tighten the coordination of central and local personnel by concentrating public health resources, including personnel attention, on important areas. According to Orton and Weick (1988, 1990), such focused attention reduces the looseness of coupling. By only examining specific relations in the system, a smaller meaningful set of factual premises on which means-ends connections are built can be obtained to temporarily reduce ambiguity. In our case study, by following the epidemiological cues published by the WHO (i.e., travel history to SARS affected areas and close contact with a SARS patient), the public health agency in Taiwan was able to swiftly develop effective control measures right after the first SARS patient was detected. Local clinicians were also instantly instructed regarding how to identify potential SARS patients with the cues. Based on this, we offer the following design guideline.

<sup>Design</sup> <sup>Guideline</sup> <sup>C1.</sup> Public health IS aimed at EID containment should facilitate the induction of meaningful epidemiological cues from contact investigation data to focus attention on key areas of disease containment and tighten the coordination of central and local personnel.

From epidemiological cues, central public health agencies also develop a set of basic rules or action plans for structuring disease surveillance activities. For example, the WHO announced a set of case definitions of SARS based on epidemiological cues (WHO 2003). Febrile patients would be considered as suspect SARS cases if they had exposures via close contact, travel history, or a resident in affected areas. These basic action plans tighten the coupling of local clinicians in managing typical cases, limiting their perceptions and sensemaking in complex ambiguous environments. March and Simon (1985) suggest that because of bounded rationality, an organization needs to have some stable regulations and procedures in place. Therefore, an organization does not have to deal with all the complexity of a problem at one time and can gradually carry out its adapting practices with stability. In the Taiwan SARS outbreak, clinicians were enabled by the WHO case definitions to promptly recognize typical SARS patients without being trapped in a high level of clinical ambiguity in the early phase of the outbreak. Accordingly, we offer the following design guideline.

<sup>Design</sup> <sup>Guideline</sup> <sup>C2.</sup> Public health IS aimed at EID containment should facilitate the creation and dissemination of basic action plans to structure the activities of disease surveillance and detect typical patient cases.

By following basic action plans, local clinicians standardize their patient inquiry to efficiently differentiate potential cases from a large number of general patients. As reflected in our case study, clinicians relied heavily on the inquiry of patients’ travel and contact history to rule out the possibility of SARS infection. Orton and Weick (1990) point out that the use of a standardized procedure to process and filter incoming information has two effects on tightening a loosely coupled organization. First, it helps to reduce the impact of complex environments on the organization, so that the organization will not overreact to external stimuli. Second, it allows the organization to maintain its internal integrity and consistency. The result of standardized clinical inquiry is that the quality of disease surveillance is controlled. The number of upward-bound surveillance reports does not overwhelm the information processing capacity of central agencies, thus enabling surveillance reports to be systematically reviewed and followed up to ensure the overall effectiveness of central control guidelines. Thus we offer the following design guideline.

<sup>Design</sup> <sup>Guideline</sup> <sup>C3.</sup> Public health IS aimed at EID containment should support and facilitate standardized clinical inquiry and surveillance reporting to maintain the consistency of the outbreak management organization, control the quality of surveillance reporting, and ensure the overall effectiveness of central control guidelines.

The information-centric activities discussed above (i.e., providing epidemiological cues, creating basic action plans, and standardizing medical inquiry) form a coupling circle that effectively organizes public health officials and local clinicians for responding to an EID in the early phase of the outbreak. However, the sole presence of this coupling circle can lead to gradual deprivation of local sensitivity to atypical cases. March (1987) notes that individual actors tend to follow rules that encode valuable historical lessons to resolve complex situations rationally. But often those rules reflect an incomplete understanding of a problem rather than their capability to solve the problem. In our case, when the epidemiological cues suggested by the WHO were consistently shown to be effective, they inevitably became entrenched rules embedded in the practices of SARS containment. The reporting and reviewing of SARS surveillance cases became uniform and routine, gradually masking the true complexity of the underlying situation. This is reflected in the convergent trend of the reported and confirmed cases before the Hospital A outbreak in Figure 4. The outbreak management organization unexpectedly shifted from loose coupling to tight coupling, trading local judgment for efficient coordination. Therefore, a decoupling circle capable of continuously sensing the changes in external environments is needed to counter the negative effects of the coupling circle.

5.1.2. Local Decoupling Circle. In our case study, we also observed a decoupling circle that local clinicians attempted to trigger via collegial discussion and clinical investigation in the early phase of the outbreak. However, without the proper support of public health IS, the decoupling circle was weak and intermittent, which allowed the coupling circle to dominate. In health care practices, collegial discussion and clinical investigation are used to reduce clinical ambiguity and promote face-to-face debate, evidence-based clarification, and clinical experimentation. In Orton and Weick’s original framework (1988), collegial discussion is identified as an essential activity that loosens the coupling between central administration and local personnel. It encourages individuals to exercise judgment and express different interpretations of the focal situation. Through a decision scheme of majority rule, collegial discussion carefully constructs a set of more reliable factual premises that would lead to better judgment (Orton and Weick 1988). Kohli and Kettinger (2004) also identify collegial discussion in clinical practice as an important information-centric activity for creating mutually shared sense of rational action among clinicians. As presented in our case study, without the discussion that prompted the mentioning of the WHO SARS alert, the right factual premises for the diagnosis of the first SARS patient could not have been constructed. Following collegial discussion, investigation enables individuals to examine the validity of received wisdom or rules through face-to-face clarification and clinical experimentation. Investigation enlarges small cues, so that tiny pieces of information can be pooled for sensemaking (Weick 1995). In the case of the female native patient, clinicians in Hospital S carefully explored possible explanations for the patient’s lung infiltrate with diagnostic tests and successfully identified a patient case that clearly fell outside of the established epidemiological cues. By promoting collegial discussion and clinical investigation, public health agencies are able to increase the detection capability for unusual cases, and accordingly revise central control guidelines in a timely manner. Therefore we offer the following design guideline.

<sup>Design</sup> <sup>Guideline</sup> <sup>D1.</sup> Public health IS aimed at EID containment should promote and facilitate local collegial discussion and clinical investigation to improve the detection of novel patient cases, and increase the effectiveness of central control guidelines.

One apparent discontinuity in the decoupling circle was the dismissal of the case of the native female patient. Compared to other SARS surveillance reports that uniformly demonstrated legitimate epidemiological cues, the case of the female patient was an outlier easily mistaken as having no public health importance. But in highly dynamic and complex environments, outliers may actually reflect an insufficient understanding of the world and reveal areas where knowledge is fragmented (Taleb 2007). The result of this dismissal was that the central agency did not perform a followup investigation that could have revealed her visit to Hospital A. Consequently, Hospital A was not informed of this confirmed case and made aware of the potential connection between the female patient and the laundry worker. This led to a costly delay in recognizing the laundry worker as a SARS case. To avoid similar incorrect dismissals of important outliers, public health personnel need a more complete picture of the external environment that can be obtained via diverse, but related, public health-related data. According to Taleb (2007), ignoring important outliers is a logical confusion of “absence of evidence” with “evidence of absence.” Weick (1995) also notes that plausibility is a more important factor than accuracy in determining whether a piece of information is accepted. Accurate information can be disregarded because the data used to reason or make sense of the information is selective or incomplete. Accordingly, we offer the following design guideline.

<sup>Design</sup> <sup>Guideline</sup> <sup>D2.</sup> Public health IS aimed at EID containment should facilitate the recognition of important outliers by providing a more complete picture of external environments through various data sources, so that public health personnel can see potential connections among surveillance cases for evidence and inform local clinicians accordingly to increase situation awareness.

Another important interruption in the decoupling circle is the inactivity of the central public health agency for gathering and disseminating important events or information to local clinicians. As implied by the vicious circle between personal contact and hospital infection found in our VAR results, hospitals in a region are, in effect, loosely coupled through the people and communities they serve. Public health events in one hospital can eventually have an impact on other hospitals, causing a cascade effect like the one depicted in Figure 3. Hence, timely distribution of important local events to clinicians can raise clinicians’ situation awareness and help them to make sense of a situation in their hospital caused by events in a neighboring hospital. In turn, this can trigger collegial discussion and clinical investigation critical for disease surveillance. In our case, for example, clinicians in Hospital C did not recognize the 73- year-old patient as a potential SARS case until they learned about his prior visit to Hospital A. Without having such local information to clarify the situation, clinicians would have kept normalizing the patient’s diagnosis with other well-known diseases and delayed detection. But hospitals are loosely coupled and often have limited formal communication channels to exchange information in a timely manner. They rely on central public health agencies for serving the vital role of communication hub. Based on the above discussion, we offer the following design guideline.

<sup>Design</sup> <sup>Guideline</sup> <sup>D3.</sup> Public health IS aimed at EID containment should facilitate the timely gathering and dissemination of important local information to local clinicians to increase their situation awareness and trigger collegial discussion and proper clinical investigation.

5.1.3. Balance Between Coupling and Decoupling Circles. The effectiveness of a loosely coupled organization depends on balancing the coupling and decoupling circles. If there is only a coupling circle, “the system would eventually become rigid and unable to support itself through adaption to changing environments” (Orton and Weick 1988, p. 32). With the sole presence of a decoupling circle, “the system would eventually degenerate to uncoupling and cease to be a system” (Orton and Weick 1988, pp. 31–32). To maintain the interdependency of system elements while preserving their autonomy in judgment, a balance between these two circles must be achieved (Orton and Weick 1988). Our case study of the Taiwan SARS outbreak clearly demonstrated the effect of such a balance on the efforts of EID containment. When the coupling circle triggered by the SARS control guidelines dominated in the early phase, local capacity for detecting unusual cases diminished significantly, leading to an unexpected hospital outbreak and subsequent confusion. But when the intense media coverage of the outbreak raised high situation awareness and fueled the decoupling circle, local sensitivity to potential SARS patients soared up to a degree that appeared to overwhelm the central agency’s capacity for digesting surveillance data and reports. The outbreak management system essentially collapsed at this point. The balance of these two circles was eventually reached after the central agency further refined the basic action plan for evaluating febrile patients. Local hospitals responded by using standardized medical inquiry procedures, such as administering patient questionnaires and implementing temperature checking at the entrance point, to loosen their coupling with external environments. As a result, local sensitivity eased and the outbreak was contained. Corresponding changes in Figure 4 support these observations.

While such a balance is easy to identify from a retrospective point of view, it represents a constant struggle in the loosely coupled organization (Orton and Weick 1988). We argue that this balance can be managed through constant, consistent centrally coordinated investigation. Investigation has two effects on resolving clinical ambiguity. First, it helps to recognize the outliers that would normally be ignored. March (1987) argues that because the relationship between information and decision is no longer clear in ambiguous situations (i.e., the uncertainty of means-ends connection is high), the close articulation between information gathering and decision-making activities in the organization has to be loosened. Independent investigation can trigger the loosening of the close articulation. It allows autonomous investigators to develop their own ideas of interesting relevant information and feed previously ignored, but potentially important, clues back to decision makers (March 1987). Second, investigation is, in essence, a problem-solving activity. As March and Simon (1985) indicate, when facing novel stimuli, problem-solving activity is needed to explore and develop one or more appropriate action programs. Through constant, consistent centrally coordinated investigation, a set of meaningful epidemiological cues can be systematically constructed and refined to reduce clinical ambiguity, and thus increase the effectiveness of central control guidelines. In our case study, the 73-year-old patient in Hospital $C ,$ who did not meet the initial SARS case definitions, was recognized via a contact investigation and prompted a revision of the SARS control guidelines (i.e., prior medical visits was additionally included in the case definitions). Therefore we offer the following design guideline.

<sup>Design</sup> <sup>Guideline</sup> <sup>B1.</sup> Public health IS aimed at EID containment should loosen the close articulation between data gathering and decision-making activities and emphasize the support of centrally coordinated investigation to increase the recognition of important outliers, discover meaningful epidemiological cues, and balance the coupling and decoupling circles.

## 5.2. Assessing Public Health IS with Loose

Coupling Framework and Design Guidelines The central control framework (Figure 1) and proposed loose coupling framework are both based on contemporary public health practices to explain how various activities are organized to orchestrate public health defense mechanisms. With a shift in focus from routine surveillance to epidemic situations that involve a high degree of ambiguity, our framework reveals several aspects of the current public health system that may seem trivial in routine surveillance, but are important for managing EID. First, our framework emphasizes the role of local clinicians in detecting unusual patient cases and identifies several information-centric activities that would affect clinician judgment, and subsequently the capacity for EID surveillance. Second, unlike the central control framework that considers public health activities (e.g., evaluating surveillance reports and performing disease investigation) as a means for exerting control and influence, we focus on how central agencies can leverage those activities to enhance clinicians’ engagement in outbreak management and transform their insightful observations into effective central leadership. Finally, the loose coupling framework emphasizes the importance of centrally coordinated investigations in resolving ambiguity, especially in recognizing outliers and validating containment strategies. Despite these differences, the suggested loose coupling framework is not intended to replace the central control framework; rather, it supplements the traditional view of outbreak management by highlighting the underlying implicit causal relationships in the current public health system that help to confront and resolve deep clinical ambiguity.

While CDC PHIN initiatives seem to adequately address the deficiencies of routine surveillance indicated by the central control framework (Table 1), they are not sufficient to manage EID from the lens of loose coupling. By following March’s proposition (1987) that a loose articulation between information and decision activities is needed for reducing ambiguity, we suggest seamless alignment of a collection of IS rather than development of a single system that meets all seven design guidelines. As shown in Table 5, the entire collection of PHIN IS projects does not fully meet the design guidelines. One major shortfall across all systems is in the absence of support for local clinicians’ activities. As reflected in the central control framework in Figure 1, clinicians are portrayed as making diagnoses individually and independently. The only interactions of local clinicians with the public health system are through their sending surveillance reports and receiving public health alerts. As a result, public health initiatives for the local side are aimed at creating advanced information channels to increase the timeliness of these interactions. For example, BioSense will allow electronic submissions of laboratory surveillance results by hospitals. Another PHIN project—Electronic Health Record Alerting Solution—is designed to automatically match patient’s symptoms with existing public health alerts and dispatch matched alerts directly to clinicians via electronic medical record systems.

In the loose coupling framework, local clinicians are not entirely autonomous or independent when making their decisions. Collegial discussion and clinical investigation play a profound role in influencing clinicians’ decision making. The central control framework fails to recognize the importance of these two local activities in EID surveillance. As a result, there are no public health projects aimed at supporting these two activities; thus, there is an absence of public health IS in this area (Design Guideline D1). In addition, the content of public health alerts is also suggested to have an impact on clinicians’ behavior and judgment. Alerts with specific instructions can induce standardized medical inquiry in a highly ambiguous condition and thus yield more consistent diagnostic results (Design Guideline C2). Inconclusive, story-like content can raise clinicians’ suspicions and lead to collegial discussion and clinical investigation (Design Guideline D3). Consequently, the process to generate the message content—how to compose basic action plans and where to gather important local information—is more essential than the creation of advanced information channels that expedite the delivery process. Electronic Health Record Alerting Solution, for instance, can have totally different implications for EID containment (coupling versus decoupling) if it is used to distribute decision rules for diagnosis rather than vague event warnings. Without addressing where and how the disseminated information would come from, several PHIN projects (i.e., CDC Alert Service, Cascading Alert, Epi-X, and Electronic Health Record Alerting Solution) only partially meet Design Guidelines C2 and D3.

Since BioSense constitutes the backbone of the PHIN IS initiatives for enhancing central control, we further use it as an example to discuss some other design issues identified by our loose coupling framework. As the CDC stated, the future of BioSense is to “implement a fully functional national system capable of giving decision makers a clear picture of the nation’s health” (CDC 2009, p. 4). It provides the capacity to access various sources of health care data and supports investigation through advanced visualization tools, two essential elements in Design Guidelines D2 (the recognition of outliers) and B1 (the balance of the coupling and decoupling circles). However, there are two design issues preventing BioSense from fully satisfying these two guidelines, thus undermining its role in ensuring the effectiveness of central control guidelines.

First, although it collects a variety of health care data, BioSense does not reconstruct a complete picture of external environments from those data. Based on a basic mechanism of syndromic surveillance, received health care data are processed as a string of simple numeric signals to detect unusual bursts or patterns (Henning 2004). That is, BioSense does not develop or emphasize the capacity to comprehensively connect various public health data on the basis of infected patients and their relationships. Patients personal and relationship data reside in a separate system, Outbreak Management System. As a result, public health personnel are not able to explore the data seamlessly to see and establish potential connections among infected patients (Design Guideline D2), a factor critical for recognizing outliers. They still rely on field investigation to clarify (interpret) the meaning of syndromic alerts and identify unusual patient cases (Duchin 2003).

Second, BioSense is aimed at integrating data gathering with decision making, a partial violation of Design Guideline B1. As March (1987) points out, in ambiguous situations where the means-ends connections are not clear, “[a]n information system designed to link information with a set of well-defined decisions will not necessarily be useful in decision making” (p. 157). In our case study, the decline of reported SARS cases, which was typically interpreted as a sign of ease in public health practices, misled the Taiwanese government to conclude that the disease was under control on the eve of the Hospital A outbreak. Therefore, public health data that are well structured with conventional ways of thinking may yield an inaccurate depiction of external environments in ambiguous situations and mislead decision making. Furthermore, this integration also confuses public health personnel and raises user resistance. From an organizational point of view, decision-making practices are often closely aligned with established norms. When BioSense targets decision making, it would inevitably be evaluated by practitioners according to public health practices, which are based on statistical thinking that looks for regularity (Fearnley 2008). Consequently, many of the BioSense users criticize its surveillance alerts for indicating rare unusual bioterrorism attacks as having “no public health importance” and consider its signals as “nondiagnostic” (Fearnley 2008, Mostashari and Hartman 2003). To resolve this user confusion, a potential way is to focus BioSense on a centrally coordinated investigation as suggested by Design Guideline B1. Subsequently, its functionality to support an epidemic investigation can be more properly developed to help public health personnel see potential linkages in terms of person, place, and time. This idea has also been discussed among public health practitioners. As Mostashari and Hartman (2003) note, alert clinicians will always play the central role in disease surveillance. However, the use of syndromic surveillance can enhance diagnostic examination for individuals who otherwise would not have been tested, and helps to determine the size, location, and tempo of an outbreak.

Overall, current public health IS initiatives focus on the development of syndromic surveillance systems to detect outbreaks and the creation of information channels to receive and disseminate public health information. However, without considering the ambiguity involved in EID management, those initiatives are fragmented in functionality and are not capable of maintaining the balance between the coupling and decoupling circles. We urge public health officials to reevaluate their information needs for managing EID in light of the loose coupling framework, and develop public health IS to enhance the effectiveness of outbreak containment and management.

## 5.3. Implications and Directions for Future Research

There are two key contributions of our work. First, is the development of a conceptual framework anchored in loose coupling, to explain how the central coupling circle that tightens coordination, and the local decoupling circle that increases local judgment, jointly influence the effectiveness of outbreak management. Such a framework is particularly important today, given the increasing threat of EID and novel bioterrorism attacks that can spread rapidly and unnoticeably across dispersed geographical boundaries. Second, our work contributes to the literature on the role of IS in strengthening public health capacity to respond to EID. We identify important information-centric activities that trigger the coupling or decoupling circles and derive seven design guidelines from the interaction of those information-centric activities to provide guidance on the design and development of public health IS.

For IS research, we complement the work of Kohli and Kettinger (2004) by adopting another theoretical lens to understand how health care IS can be properly deployed to trigger changes in clinical practice and achieve managerial goals. Kohli and Kettinger (2004) show that if administrators can improve the perceived legitimacy of communication as facilitated by IS, clinicians can be properly informated via centrally provided information and their discussions governing the behavior of medical staff can be incorporated into the process of administrative control. In the current study, we further suggest that to align health care practice with administrative goals through IS requires simultaneously facilitating two types of informationcentric activities: standardizing medical inquiry with centrally provided information (coupling circle) and promoting collegial discussion and clinical investigation (decoupling circle). By recognizing the effects of these different activities, the lessons learned with respect to public health initiatives can be transferred to hospitals to help them develop better IS infrastructure and enhance their capability for managing two simultaneous opposing pressures—decreasing profitability and increasing scrutiny (Kohli et al. 2001).

The current work also provides a complementary view on how health care organizations can deal with the unique “dual” administration structure of central administration and medical personnel (Chiasson and Davidson 2004) that increases the difficulty of developing and implementing health care IS. To explain how IS can balance this dual structure, previous research has taken a political or institutional approach. For example, Robey and Boudreau (1999) discuss how IS is deployed as a political means to transform Britain’s health care practices from solely focusing on patient care to emphasizing efficiency. By adopting our proposed framework, this dual structure can be interpreted as the coexistence of central coupling mechanisms for tightening control, and local decoupling mechanisms for responding to a complex environment. A balance between these two mechanisms must be achieved to preserve the strengths of loose coupling. While Robey and Boudreau (1999) suggest that IS can exert disciplinary power to control social action, our loose coupling framework suggests that health care IS solely targeted at exerting power via administrative control may unintentionally decrease local autonomy in judgment. As illustrated in our study, epidemiological cues capable of enhancing coordination in surveillance could gradually deprive local actors of their sensitivity to unexpectedness. Future research could further examine from a loose coupling perspective how IS can influence or balance the coupling and decoupling mechanisms in health care or other organizational settings.

We encourage further efforts to extend the proposed loose coupling framework. Our development of this framework is based on a single-case study of the Taiwan SARS outbreak that involves an extremely high level of clinical ambiguity (i.e., clinical cues yielding inconsistent results, no point-of-care diagnostic tool or vaccine available). Different levels of clinical ambiguity could change the interactions among the identified information-centric activities. For example, H1N1 no longer represents a situation with high clinical ambiguity. It can, however, provide a contrasting set of data to aid in understanding the role of IS in reducing the spread of a known pathogen. It is possible that the point at which the coupling and decoupling circles are in balance may shift as ambiguity increases or decreases. Accordingly, the role of IS in supporting the balance is likely to be different. Future research could clarify this issue and provide further insights into outbreak management and the role of IS in the process, for both EID and known infectious diseases.

Finally, the derived design guidelines provide a high-level description of how IS can help to enhance the effectiveness of outbreak management by balancing the coupling and decoupling circles. We present a high-level conceptualization and analysis to provide architectural guidance, rather than system infrastructure specifications. However, detailed specifications are needed to fully realize the benefits of the framework. When developing such specifications, it is essential to consider the variety of personnel participating in public health practices (Ash et al. 2004). From the perspective of health care IS adoption research (e.g., Chiasson and Davidson 2004, Davidson and Heslinga 2007, Hu et al. 1999), future research should explore the implementation-level design features that will not only enable the balance between the coupling and decoupling circles, but will also enhance the usability of the systems by a wide variety of users.

## 5.4. Implications for Practice and Policy

Our analysis and findings also have important implications for IS development in public health, as well as public health policy. We derive seven design guidelines from the case study of the Taiwan SARS outbreak to provide direction for designing and employing IS to strengthen EID management efforts. Our guidelines stress the importance of balancing coupling and decoupling pressures within the outbreak management process to confront the uncertainty and ambiguity that EID evoke. The practical implications for the development of public health IS are discussed in detail in §5.2.

With respect to implications for public health policies, we urge policymakers to recognize the crucial role that local clinicians play in EID containment. Since congress passed the public health security and bioterrorism preparedness and responses act in 2002, strengthening the central capacity for EID surveillance and response has been the focus of public health policies. Billions of dollars have been awarded to state and local agencies for improving their communication with other agencies and clinicians (Lister 2005). While numerous public health IS projects were initiated to fulfill central agencies’ needs for information or data exchange and dissemination, organizational plans to coordinate local clinicians and increase their engagement in EID detection, however, are mostly absent. According to our analysis, collecting surveillance reports and distributing public health alerts in a timely manner do not guarantee the success of outbreak management under ambiguity. The key is public health agencies’ ability to transform pieces of clinicians’ observations of and insights about unusual patient cases into effective central guidance through effective coordination. Achieving this requires not only a strong public health IS infrastructure to facilitate clinicians’ local information activities but also long-term planning to shift the central agency’s focus from disease surveillance to coordinated investigation. Public health policy is the only way to make this happen. We echo a call made by public health practitioners that clinicians and public health agencies are essential partners (Gerberding et al. 2002) and suggest that policymakers initiate the process to bring clinicians more actively into the picture.

## 6. Conclusion

The novelty of EID evokes a high level of ambiguity that requires clinicians’ autonomy in judgment to resolve and respond. We argue that to properly support the activities of EID containment with public health IS, an overarching framework that captures the complexity inherent in the process is needed. We adopt Orton and Weick’s loose coupling lens (1988) to develop such a framework. We analyze news reports and patient contact data from 479 patients involved in the SARS outbreak in Taiwan to derive a loose coupling framework for outbreak management that highlights the importance of two types of causal circles—coupling and decoupling circles— that operate between central control guidelines and local detection capacity via important informationcentric activities. Our proposed framework is used to recommend a set of seven guidelines for the design of public health IS projects. We use the guidelines to evaluate existing public health IS projects, analyze their limitations, identify directions for future design and development efforts, and discuss policy implications.
