# Managing online sales with posted price and open-bid auctions

Hila Etzion ⁎, Scott Moore

Ross School of Business, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI 48109, USA

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 21 July 2011 Received in revised form 1 November 2012 Accepted 4 December 2012 Available online 14 December 2012

Keywords: Online auctions Multichannel retailing e-Commerce Simulation

## a b s t r a c t

We study the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of selling consumer goods online using posted price and open ascending-bid uniform-price auction simultaneously. We develop a model of consumer behavior when faced with the choice between the two channels. The model is simulated in order to identify the best designs of the dual channel regime and compare its performance with that of the only posted price regime. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the best designs of dual channels with open-bid auctions differ from those of dual channels with sealed-bid auctions previously studied. In addition, when optimally designed, the dual channel regime outperforms the posted price regime.

## 1. Introduction

The two most commonly used selling mechanisms in modern markets are posted price and auction. With a posted price, the seller declares a <sup>fi</sup>xed price and only consumers with valuations higher than the posted price can buy the item(s). With an auction, the seller sets the auction lot size and its length, the consumers bid for the item(s), and the price is determined by the consumers' valuations and the resulting bids. The problem of optimally selecting one of these two mechanisms for selling one unit of a product has been well addressed in the literature [6,10,17].

With the proliferation of the Internet, it became feasible for sellers to utilize more innovative selling strategies, employing the above two selling mechanisms either in parallel or sequentially. For example, B-2-C retailers such as CompUSA and Sam's Club use posted prices for sales of new products on their websites, and in their brick and mortar stores; however, they also utilize online auctions for selling open-box and refurbished items, and for liquidating inventory of discontinued products. Thus, posted prices are used to sell new products of which the seller has a large number of units, while auctions are used once the number of units for sale becomes limited. In another example, C-2-C (Consumer-to-Consumer) websites, such as eBay, let individuals with items for sale choose among three selling mechanisms: an auction, a posted price, and an auction with a buy-now option. In the latter, the seller sets the auction quantity, the auction length, and in addition lists a buy-now price. If a consumer chooses to pay the buy-now price then he obtains an item immediately for that price, and the auction's quantity is reduced by the unit purchased (or if the auction had only one unit, the auction ends).

While auctions with a buy-now option are commonly used by sellers on C-2-C websites, they are not used by B-2-C retailers on their websites. Unlike most sellers on C-2-C websites, who have a limited number of units for sale, online retailers usually have large quantities of each new product, or they can order from their suppliers enough units to satisfy demand. Auctions by their nature require the rationing of supply, which would not be credible when selling mass-produced products. For these reasons, it is not likely that retailers would use auctions, or auctions with a buy-now price, as their sole online selling mechanism for products with large supply. However, it is not clear why B-2-C retailers are not using online auctions parallel to the posted price channel. When running an auction parallel to a posted price channel, there can still be an “unlimited” number of units available for sale via the posted price channel, and the seller does not need to set a <sup>fi</sup>xed quota of units for sale in advance.<sup>1</sup>

B-2-C retailers such as Sam's Club, which already have an online page dedicated for using auctions to liquidate inventory, can easily start running auctions for products that they sale via posted price. However, employing auctions and posted price in parallel would not always increase the retailer's revenue. While an auction creates a venue for selling to consumers who do not value a product as much as the posted price, and thus has the potential of increasing revenue, it may also attract consumers who otherwise would have bought at the posted price. When the auction channel reduces sales for the posted price, we say that it cannibalizes the posted price channel. In addition, if most auction winners are consumers who would have paid the posted price in the absence of the auctions, then offering auctions parallel to the posted price would decrease revenue. Thus, the seller should choose a dual-channel design that limits cannibalization of posted price sales.

Previous research exhibits that a dual channel strategy which employs posted price and sealed-bid auction in parallel can yield higher revenue than using only posted prices [5,7,8,16]. However, in reality we still do not see retailers using the dual channel strategy. Can it be that the reason lies in the fact that in practice such online auctions would be conducted in the open ascending-bid format rather than in the sealed-bid format? Perhaps dual channels with open-bid auctions do not perform as well as dual channels with sealed-bid auctions?<sup>2</sup>This is the <sup>fi</sup>rst paper to study the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of employing posted price and open ascending-bid auctions in parallel when selling a product with large (unlimited) supply, and to compare the performance of this dual channel strategy to that of the single channel of only posted price. We consider the posted price channel's pro<sup>fi</sup>t as the benchmark, rather than the pro<sup>fi</sup>t from only auction, because this is the commonly used selling strategy by B-2-C online retailers.

In the model presented, the seller sets the auction's length, the auction quantity and the posted price. Next, consumers who have stochastic arrival times and heterogeneous valuations self-select whether to participate in the auction, buy for the posted price, or leave the market. In addition, due to the open-bid auction format, each bidder might need to make this decision several times during the auction. Since it is impossible to derive optimal or equilibrium bidding strategies, we propose two heuristics with which consumers can choose whether to bid or buy. With the <sup>fi</sup>rst heuristic, consumers who value the item more than the posted price use a pessimistic approach toward the auction. When implementing this heuristic in our model we are underestimating both the bidding activity by these consumers and the resulting cannibalization of the posted price channel. With the second heuristic, consumers who value the product more than the posted price use an optimistic approach toward the auction, leading to a myopic bidding strategy. When implementing this heuristic in our model, we are overestimating auction participation by these consumers, and the cannibalization of the posted price channel. We investigate the performance of the dual channel under these two alternative models of consumer behavior by simulating a market in which the seller and consumers behave according to our analytical model. A simulation is necessary because it is impossible to derive analytically the seller's expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t from operating the open-bid auction and the posted price in parallel. The simulation tool simply allows us to estimate the expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t for our analytical model for any set of parameter values.

Our main result is that, when designed correctly (i.e., when auction quantity, auction length and posted price are set jointly to maximize revenue), the dual channel of open ascending-bid auction and posted price indeed outperforms the single channel of only posted price. The increase in pro<sup>fi</sup>t is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly larger when consumers with valuation higher than the posted price are reluctant to bid (that is, they use a heuristic which underestimates their expected utility from bidding). However, even when such consumers choose to bid more than they should, using a heuristic which overestimates their expected utility from bidding, the dual channel outperforms the single channel. Thus, even when we overestimate the cannibalization of the posted price channel, and as a result underestimate the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of the dual channel, the dual channel increases pro<sup>fi</sup>tability if the seller designs it correctly. In contrast, if the two channels are managed independently of each other, then the dual channel still outperforms the only posted price regime when cannibalization risk is low, but does worse than the only posted price regime when the cannibalization risk is high. This demonstrates the importance of managing the two channels jointly rather than separating operations to two independent departments.

Our results for the optimal design of the dual channel depend on which heuristic consumers use to choose between the two channels. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when consumers who value the product higher than the posted price are more likely to bid, the seller should set a smaller auction lot size and a lower posted price than when these consumers are less likely to bid. However, in either case the auction should be long. The fact that even when consumers have low opportunity cost the auction should be long stands in contrast to previous results for the optimal designs of dual channels with sealed-bid auctions [7]. Sellers should be aware that the optimal design of dual channels with open-bid auctions differs from that found for dual channels with sealed-bid auctions.

The paper structure is as follows. In Section 2, we review the related literature and explain the contribution of our paper. In Section 3, we present the model of the dual channel, along with a detailed description of the auction mechanism. In Section 4, we consider how consumers self-select between the two channels, and suggest two heuristics on how consumers evaluate their expected utility from bidding. In Section 5, we describe how the analytical models are implemented in a simulation. Section 6 presents the simulation results, and examines the dual channel best designs. We compare the revenues from the different selling regimes in Section 7, discuss the results and conclude in Sections 8 and 9.

## 2. Contribution and related literature

There are two streams of related research. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stream examines the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of auctions with a buy-it-now option. The second stream examines the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of using posted price and sealed-bid auctions in parallel. Here we review these two streams and clarify the contribution of the current paper.

## 2.1. Auctions with a buy-now price

A relatively new selling mechanism, commonly observed on C-2-C websites such as eBay, is an auction with a buy-it-now option. In an auction with a buy-it-now option the seller sets the auction quantity, the auction length, and a reserve price, as he would in a regular auction; but, in addition he posts a <sup>fi</sup>xed price, referred to as the buy-now price, at which consumers may purchase the auctioned items. There are two types of buy-now prices. A temporary buy-price is available only until the <sup>fi</sup>rst bid is submitted. Once a bid that exceeds the seller's reserve price is submitted, the buy-price disappears and in fact the seller is running a regular auction. In contrast, a permanent buy-price is available until the end of the auction or until all units that were initially dedicated to the auction are bought for the buy-price.

In an auction with a buy-now price (permanent or temporary), all units are initially offered in the auction, but purchases for the buy-now price reduce the auctioned quantity. This is a major point of difference between an auction with a buy-now price and the dual channel regime of posted price and auctions presented in this paper. In the dual channel regime, auctioned units are dedicated to the auction and the posted price channel has its own (unlimited) supply. The differences between the two selling strategies are summarized in Table 1.

The literature about auctions with buy-now price examines whether the seller should add a buy-now price to the auction and, if so, what is the optimal buy-now price. The models of auction with a permanent buy-price [4,11] consider only one-unit auctions. In addi tion, because the number of bidders is deterministic, all bidders are present at the beginning of the auction, and no costs are related to the length of the auction, the auction length is not being modeled. Thus, the only decision variable is the buy-price. Finally, in all the papers that examine auction with a buy price, the revenue from the auction with buy-price is compared only to the revenue from a regular auction. Using only posted price is not considered. Table 2 summarizes the common features of models of auction with a permanent buy-now price, and contrasts them with the dual channel model presented in this paper.

Mathews 2003 [12] examines an auction with a buy-now price when consumers have heterogeneous arrival times (i.e., a stochastic arrival process) and are impatient. However, Mathews models a temporary buy-price, which is available only until the <sup>fi</sup>rst bid is submitted, and thus his setup is not similar to the dual channel's setup in which the posted price is available during the entire auction. In addition, Mathews considers only one-unit auctions and <sup>fi</sup>xes the auction length at 1. Thus, again, the only decision variable is the buy-price. He concludes that when the seller is impatient and the buyers are not, or the opposite, the seller can increase the expected revenue by setting a buy price that is executed with positive probability.

We conclude that the existing literature on auctions with a buy-now price cannot predict the performance of the suggested dual channel strategy when considering a seller with effectively unlimited supply. In addition, it cannot be used to determine the optimal design of such dual channels (i.e., the optimal auction length, posted price and auction's lot size) or to determine whether the dual channel outperforms the single channel of only posted price.

## 2.2. Dual channels with posted price and sealed-bid auction

The second related research stream examines dual channels with posted price and sealed-bid auctions, showing that if 1) consumers incur a cost when delaying the purchase of the item, and 2) highvaluation consumers incur a higher delay cost than low-valuation consumers, then such dual channels can outperform the single channel of only posted price.

Etzion et al. [7,8] model a monopolist, with unlimited product supply, selling identical items using a sequence of sealed-bid auctions and a posted price simultaneously. They model the facts that consumers arrive at different stages of the online auction and that their expected utility from bidding may depend on their arrival time. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they assume that consumers who can obtain the item immediately via the posted price incur an opportunity cost when they choose to participate in the auction and thus delay their use of the item, but consumers who value the item less than the posted price do not incur such opportunity cost when choosing to bid. Etzion et al. <sup>fi</sup>nd the optimal design of the dual channel for different arrival rates and consumers' delay sensitivity, and show that in most cases the dual channel outperforms the single posted price channel. Caldentey and Vulcano [5], reach similar results when the seller has a <sup>fi</sup>xed number of units to sale and consumer discount utility when they wait.

Sun [16] considers a seller offering a sealed-bid auction and posted price in parallel when consumers discount their utility from an auction due to monitoring and waiting costs. He shows that if high-valuation consumers discount more than low-valuation consumers, the dual channel strategy yields higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t than the only posted price. In contrast to

Etzion et al. [8], however, Sun assumes unavailability of the product at the end of the planning cycle (i.e., at the end of the auction). Next, we discuss why it is important to study dual channels with open-bid auctions separately, and why it is not clear whether results derived for dual channels with sealed-bid auctions apply to dual channels with open-bid auctions.

In a sealed-bid auction, consumers cannot observe the bids submitted by others and therefore have the same information available to them at each moment of the auction; bidders bid only once and then have to wait until the auction ends to learn whether they won. However, in reality most online auctions are conducted in the open ascending-bid auction format, where bidders can observe the lowest winning bid at every moment of the auction. Thus, consumers have more information available to them when choosing between the posted price and auction participation than in the dual channel with sealed-bid auction, and the information available changes as the auction progresses. In addition, when participating in an open-bid auction, a consumer is informed if she is outbid before the auction ends, in which case she can choose to buy the item for the posted price. This is not the case with a sealed-bid auction, where once the consumer submits a bid she must wait until the end of the auction to obtain an item.

Intuition predicts that it would be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for the seller to segment the market and prevent cannibalization of posted-price sales when more information is made available to consumers, i.e. when the auction is conducted in the open ascending-bid format. In a dual-channel with an open-bid auction, a consumer that submits a bid knows that she will be given an opportunity to buy the item for the posted price before the auction ends if at any point of time the auction's price goes above her willingness to bid. This would not be the case in a sealed-bid auction. Thus, consumers with valuation higher than the posted price might be less cautious about joining an open-bid auction than about joining a sealed-bid auction, leading to higher cannibalization of posted price sales with the open-bid auction format. For this reason it is unclear whether a dual channel with open-bid auction can outperform the single channel of posted price, even though this was previously shown for a dual-channel with sealed-bid auctions.

Finally, auction theory shows that a sealed-bid (q+1)-price auction and an ascending open-bid uniform price (i.e., all winners pay the same price – the highest losing bid) auction yield the same revenue to the seller when consumers are risk neutral and the number of bidders is exogenous (i.e., the same number of bidders in both cases). However, the Revenue Equivalence theorem [13] cannot be applied when considering a dual channel with open-bid auction and a dual channel with sealed-bid auction. Even when the total number of consumers arriving to the seller's website is the same in both settings, the number of bidders would not necessarily be the same because of the endogenous auction participation decision. In the presence of an alternative venue for obtaining the product (the posted price channel), the number of bidders will depend on the auction format, and thus different auction formats would lead to different dual channel revenues. We conclude that it is important to study the performance of dual channels with open-bid auctions and one cannot predict whether such dual channels are pro<sup>fi</sup>table based on models with sealed-bid auctions.

## 3. The model

We model an online seller with unlimited product supply,<sup>3</sup>who offers identical items using the two selling mechanisms: posted price and open ascending-bid uniform-price auction (we give a detailed description of the auction mechanism in Section 3.1). The auctions have a <sup>fi</sup>xed duration and are then repeated, and auction units are dedicated to the auction. The two selling mechanisms are operated in parallel, and thus a consumer that arrives to the website can choose whether to buy the item for the posted price or bid in an ongoing auction. The seller's objective is to maximize revenue per unit time. The seller chooses the auction duration T, the quantity to auction $q ,$ and the posted price $p .$ Without loss of generality, we assume that the marginal cost of each unit is zero. If this is not so, consumers' valuations of the product can be taken net of the marginal cost. The seller publicly declares a reserve price, R, and a bid increment, h. In the current paper, we do not treat the reserve price, R, and the bid increment, h, as decision variables, but examine only the optimal choice of $T , q ,$ and $p .$ For a thorough discussion of the effect of the bid increment level on an auctioneer's pro<sup>fi</sup>t, the reader is referred to Bapna et al., 2000, 2003 [2,3].

Risk-neutral consumers visit the website according to a Poisson process with rate λ, and each consumer is interested in obtaining one unit of the good. Consumers have independent private values for the good. We assume that each consumer's valuation, V, is independently drawn from the uniform distribution with support set v; v . In addition, the seller's choice of posted price, p, splits consumers into two segments: 1) low-valuation (LV) consumers, with valuation lower than the posted price, and 2) high-valuation (HV) consumers, with valuation higher than the posted price. While LV consumers have no alternative venue to obtain the item (with positive utility) but the auction, HV consumers can always obtain the item instantly via the posted price channel. Thus, for HV consumers bidding in the auction involves an opportunity cost as they are delaying their use of the item. We assume that this opportunity cost is linear in the delay time. For LV consumers, bidding does not involve such opportunity cost. Finally, for simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation purposes, we assume that consumers participate at most in one auction. Thus, a consumer who is being outbid and does not <sup>fi</sup>nd it optimal to raise his bid, either leaves the market or buys the item for the posted price. He does not wait for the next auction. ## 3.1. The auction mechanism

We model a multi-unit open ascending-bid auction. The seller offers $q \left( q \geq 1 \right)$ units in the auction, and the winners are the bidders with the q highest bids. We assume a uniform price auction, so that all winners pay the same price, which equals the value of the lowest winning bid at the end of the auction. As is shown later, when bidders use proxy bidding, the lowest winning bid equals the highest losing bid plus at most the bid increment.

Consumers can observe information regarding bidding activity and use this information to decide whether to join the auction and how much to bid. In addition, unlike in a sealed-bid auction, consumers are being informed during the auction in case their bid is no longer among the q highest bids, and can choose whether or not to submit a higher bid. Thus, each bidder might submit multiple ascending value bids, and some bidders might leave the auction before it ends.

At each point of time during the auction, the winning-bids-list (WBL) consists of the q highest bids submitted so far if there have been at least q unique active bidders, and of all bids submitted so far otherwise. We denote the lowest-winning-bid (LWB) at time t, which is the lowest bid on the WBL, by $B _ { t } ,$ , and the lowest-required-bid (LRB) to enter the WBL at time t by RB(t). In accordance with auction rules observed on many auction websites (for example, the auction rules on eBay.com), we model the LRB as given in Eq. (1), where h is the minimum bid increment set by the seller. Once there are q bids in the WBL, the LRB equals the LWB plus the bidding increment.

A consumer that arrives to the website and wants to join the auction has to submit a bid higher or equal to the LRB. If there are already q bidders with bids in the WBL, then the submission of the new bid by the $( q + 1 ) ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ bidder will push another bidder (with a bid value that equals the LWB) out of the WBL. This latter bidder will then need to decide whether to raise his bid and stay an active bidder in the auction, buy the item for the posted price, or leave the market. If he raises his bid and re-enters the WBL, the process repeats itself as another bidder is pushed out of the WBL. The bidding activity caused by the arrival of the new bidder ends when one bidder (the bidder that was last pushed out of the WBL) does not raise his bid; instead he either buys the item for the posted price (V≥p) or leaves the market with no item $( V { < } p )$ . Once the bidding activity that was triggered by the new arrival ends, there is no bidding activity until the next time a new consumer arrives to the website and decides to submit a bid. Next we discuss the role of sniping and proxy bidding in our model setup.

## 3.1.1. Sniping

We assume that bidders always get a chance to submit a higher bid when being pushed out of the WBL, and the process of submitting a bid is instantaneous.<sup>4</sup>That is, even if a new bidder joins the auction at its very end and his bid pushes another bidder's bid out of the WBL, that latter bidder gets a chance to react (i.e., a chance to submit a new bid), and so does any other bidder that is being outbid in the resulting process. Hence, sniping, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the submission of a bid close to the conclusion of an auction so that other bidders may not have time to react by raising their bids, is not feasible in our setup.

With regards to bidders' incentives to delay bids to the end of the auction, our setup resembles that of a soft-end open-bid auction in which if a new bid is submitted near the conclusion of the auction, the auction is extended by a predetermined amount of time, which allows other bidders to adjust their bids if so desired. Roth and Ockenfels [15] show that in soft-end auctions, such as those conducted on Amazon.com, sniping is not prevalent. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they <sup>fi</sup>nd that only 3% of bidders submitted bids in the last 10 min. In addition, Ockenfels and Roth [14] show that even in <sup>fi</sup>xed-end open-bid auctions not everyone snipes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they show that on average only 14% of bidders submit a bid in the last 10 min of an auction on eBay (eBay auctions have a <sup>fi</sup>xed ending time). By considering a model in which bidders do not have incentive to delay bidding to the auction's end, we differentiate the open-bid auction format from the sealed-bid auction format.

## 3.1.2. Proxy bidding

We assume that the seller's website supports automatic bidding, commonly referred to as proxy bidding. The consumer can enter a proxy bid, which is the maximum amount he is willing to pay for the item in the auction. This maximum amount is not observed by other bidders. The auction mechanism then places bids on behalf of the bidder: it will bid as much as necessary to make sure that the bidder remains in the WBL, but not more than the proxy bid submitted by the bidder.

Proxy bidding is the default bidding strategy used on eBay for one-unit auctions. According to eBay's rules for one-unit auctions, if another bidder has placed the same (or a higher) proxy bid before you, eBay will let you know that you've been outbid so that you can place another (higher) proxy bid if you want. Thus, ties between proxy bids of the same amount are broken based on a time-priority rule. We apply similar rules in our model of the multiple-unit auction.

## 4. Consumer behavior

Consumers are risk neutral and self-select the action that maximizes their expected utility. When a consumer arrives at the website he observes the posted price, p, the auction length T, the remaining time of the auction (T-t), and the auction's lot size q. The consumer knows his own valuation for the item, V, and the distribution of valuations in the population. In addition, due to the open-bid auction format, he observes the current lowest bid, $B _ { t } .$

Consumers who value the item less than the posted price, i.e., have Vbp, choose whether to bid in the auction or leave the market. We assume that such consumers do not incur an opportunity cost if they enter the auction. The reason for their non-existing (or low) opportunity cost is the fact that they cannot obtain the item elsewhere. Consumers who value the item higher than the posted price, i.e., have V>p, choose whether to bid in the auction or buy the item for the posted price. Such consumers incur an opportunity cost if they enter the auction because by doing so they delay their use of the item. Our model captures the opportunity cost which prevails due to the existence of the alternative posted price channel. We do not model other potential auction participation costs related to auction monitoring or waiting.

In the auction setup considered, consumers do not bene<sup>fi</sup>t from delaying the submission of a bid as sniping is not feasible (see Section 3.1.1). By delaying the submission of his bid, a consumer might cause more consumers to join the auction because they observe a lower required entry bid (LRB) when they arrive, and an increase in the number of bidders can lead to a higher auction price. By delaying the submission of his bid the consumer might also lose the time-priority advantage. Finally, consumers who can buy the item for the posted price with positive utility incur an opportunity cost when delaying the purchase of the item whether or not they have an active bid in the auction. Thus, a dominant strategy for consumers who choose to bid in the auction is to submit a bid immediately.

A consumer that arrives to the website at time t, or a consumer that was pushed out of the winning-bids list at time t, determines his maximum willingness to bid at that time, denoted by M(V,t), and then joins the auction if and only if M(V,t)>RB(t). It is easy to show that submitting a proxy bid of M(V,t) dominates any other commonly used bidding strategy. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this strategy dominates the pedestrian bidding strategy [2,14], according to which the consumer bids in the smallest increment possible up to M(V,t), because by submitting a proxy of M(V,t) the consumer would win with a higher probability. In addition, submitting a proxy bid of M(V,t) dominates submitting a bid of M(V,t) immediately, because with the latter strategy the expected auction price is higher. Proxy bidding also minimizes bidding costs when submission of bids and auction monitoring are assumed to be costly. Next, we determine the consumers' willingness to bid, M(V,t).

## 4.1. Consumers' willingness to bid

The consumer's expected utility from remaining an active bidder beyond time t if the <sup>fi</sup>nal LWB at time t is x, is given by:

$$
\begin{array}{l} U (t, x) = \int_ {g = T - t} ^ {\infty} (V - x - w D (t, g)) f _ {g | x} d g \\ + \int_ {g = 0} ^ {T - t} \left(\int_ {x + h} ^ {M (V, t + g)} U (r, B _ {r}) f _ {B _ {r} | x} d B _ {r} + \int_ {M (V, t + g)} ^ {p} U ^ {A} (V) f _ {B _ {r} | x} d B _ {r} - w D (t, g)\right) f _ {g | x} d g \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

In the above equation, t is the current time, g denotes the time until the next bidding activity, and r denotes the time of the next bidding activity, i.e., $r = ( t + g )$ . Notice that the time of the next bidding activity does not necessarily equal the time of the next arrival, as some consumers choose not to join the auction. In addition, $f _ { g | x }$ is the pdf of the time until the next bidding activity, and $f _ { B _ { r } | x }$ is the <sup>j</sup>pdf of the <sup>fi</sup>nal LWB at time r (the time of the next bidding activity), conditional on the <sup>fi</sup>nal LWB at time t being x. $D ( t , g )$ is the delay in receiving the item imposed by waiting for the next bidding activity, and is given by:

$$
D (t, g) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} M i n (g, T - t) & \text {   If   } V > p \\ 0 & \text {   Otherwise   } \end{array} \right..\tag{3}
$$

Finally, $U ^ { A } ( V )$ is the consumer's alternative utility, and is given by:

$$
U ^ {A} (V) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} V - p & \text { If } V > p \\ 0 & \text { Otherwise } \end{array} \right..\tag{4}
$$

The <sup>fi</sup>rst term in Eq. (2) gives the expected utility in case there is no more bidding activity by time T, i.e., in case $t + g > T$ . The second term is the expected utility when a new bid is submitted prior to the auction's end, i.e., when $t + g \leq T .$ . At the time of the next bidding activity, r, if $B _ { r } { > } M ( V , ~ r )$ , then the consumer will leave the auction and receive the alternative utility, $U ^ { A } ( V ) .$ . If $B _ { r } { < } M ( V , r )$ , then the bidder is still an active bidder at the end of the bidding activity at time r, and his expected utility from remaining in the auction at time r is given by $U ( r , B _ { r } )$

As the expected utility from remaining an active bidder at time t is clearly non-increasing in the value of the <sup>fi</sup>nal LWB at time t, and the latter is a non-decreasing function of the consumer's own bid at time t, the highest proxy bid the individual would be willing to submit at time $t , \mathrm { M } ( V , t )$ , is given by the value M for which:

$$
U (t, M) = U ^ {A} (V).\tag{5}
$$

## 4.1.1. Low-valuation consumers

Low-valuation consumers have no other option for obtaining the item except the auction. Hence, they are not delaying the use of the item by bidding in the auction and do not associate an opportunity cost to committing to the auction. Etzion et al. [8] use a similar assumption, and Sun [16] assumes that high-valuation consumers discount their auction utility more than low-valuation consumers, leading to similar results. Therefore, for these consumers we set $D ( t , g ) = 0$ . Finally, because a low-valuation consumer does not incur a delay cost, his willingness to bid, $M ( V , t ) ,$ , is constant in time. Substituting $D ( t , { \boldsymbol { \mathbf { g } } } ) = 0 ,$ $U ^ { A } ( V ) = 0 , x = M ( V )$ , and $M ( V , r ) = M ( V )$ in Eq. (2), we get the utility from remaining an active bidder at time t if $B _ { t }$ reaches the bidder's willingness to bid, M(V):

$$
U (t, M (V)) = \int_ {g = T - t} ^ {\infty} (V - M (V)) f _ {g | M} d g\tag{6}
$$

From Eq. (6), we see that for low-valuation consumers U(t, M) equals $U ^ { A } ( V )$ at $M ( V ) = V .$ Thus, for a low-valuation consumer arriving at time t, the optimal strategy is to submit a proxy bid of $V { \mathrm { i f } } V { \geq } R B ( t )$ and leave the auction otherwise. In addition, low-valuation consumers would never increase their proxy bid if later being outbid. We conclude that $M ( V , t ) = V$ for low-valuation consumers.

## 4.1.2. High-valuation consumers

High-valuation consumers, those with $V { \geq } p ,$ choose between buying the item for the posted price and participating in the auction. All high-valuation consumers have the same $M ( V , t )$ as they all face the exact same decision problem: comparing the expected auction discount over the posted price, $\left( p - E [ p _ { a } ] \right)$ , with the expected delay cost, where neither depends on the consumer's valuation for the item. Thus, for high-valuation consumers $M ( V , t ) = M ( t ) ,$ ,<sup>5</sup>and a consumer that faces the bid–buy decision at time t submits a proxy bid of $M ( t ) { \mathrm { ~ i f ~ } } M ( t ) > R B ( t )$ and buys the item for the posted price otherwise. In addition, clearly $M ( r ) { > } M ( t )$ for every r such that $r > t$ because at time r the consumer does not need to consider the cost $w ( r - t )$ , and thus he is willing to accept a lower discount over the posted price. Ockenfels and Roth [14] were surprised to <sup>fi</sup>nd that many bidders submit multiple reservation bids in the course of the auction, i.e., they submit and later raise the reservation price they authorized for proxy bidding on their behalf. However, this <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with our model in which the consumer's willingness to bid is increasing in time due to the existence of an alternate venue for obtaining the item; a venue that imposes an opportunity cost when committing to the auction.

Substituting for $U ^ { \mathsf { A } } ( V )$ and for $D ( t , g )$ in Eq. (2), a high-valuation consumers' willingness to bid at time t, M(t), is the value of x for which:

$$
\begin{array}{l} U (t, x) = \int_ {g = T - t} ^ {\infty} (V - x - w (T - t)) f _ {g | x} d g + \int_ {g = 0} ^ {T - t} \left(\int_ {x + h} ^ {M (t + g)} U (t + g, B _ {(t + g)}) f _ {B _ {(t + g)} | x} d B _ {(t + g)} + \int_ {M (t + g)} ^ {p} (V - p) f _ {B _ {(t + g)} | x} d B _ {(t + g)} - w g\right) f _ {g | x} d g = V - p \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

On the left side of Eq. (7) we have an in<sup>fi</sup>nite recursion relationship because there is always a positive probability for another bidder arriving before the end of the auction. Thus, any solution procedure for $U ( , )$ would be computationally intensive. In addition, the right side of Eq. (7) cannot be solved for x without knowing the function $U ( , ) _ { i }$ , and we cannot have a closed form expression for $U ( , )$ without knowing $M ( . )$ . Another obstacle in solving the above equation for x is that $f _ { B _ { r } | x } ,$ <sup>j</sup>the pdf of the <sup>fi</sup>nal LWB at time r as estimated at time t when the <sup>fi</sup>nal LWB is x, depends on M(r). Thus, again, the function $M ( . )$ is needed to derive an expression required to determine $U ( t , x )$ and thus to determine M(.) to begin with. We conclude that we cannot derive the expression for the consumer's expected utility from remaining an active bidder at time t, or the consumer's willingness to bid at time t.

Since we cannot derive M(t) analytically or <sup>fi</sup>nd it numerically, we assume that high-valuation consumers use a heuristic to determine their expected utility and their maximum willingness to bid at a given time during the auction. In what follows we propose two alternative heuristics. Heuristic 1 underestimates the potential auction discount over the posted price, and thus when high-valuation consumers use it, they end up bidding less than they actually should. Heuristic 2 overestimates the potential auction discount, leading to the opposite result. Evaluating the performance of the dual channel under these two different Heuristics should give us an upper bound and a lower bound for the dual channel's pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. We expect the seller to be better off when high-valuation consumers use Heuristic 1, as he can better segment the market. Next we present the two suggested heuristics.

Heuristic 1. High-valuation consumers assume that if at time t the <sup>fi</sup>nal LWB equals M(t), their maximum willingness to bid at that time, then the LWB at the end of the next bidding activity, i.e. at time $r ,$ will be such that $B _ { r } { \geq } M ( r )$ . That is, they assume $f _ { B _ { r } | M ( t ) } = 0$ for $B _ { r }  { \mathrm { b } } M ( r )$ .

According to Heuristic 1, a high-valuation consumer assumes that if at time t she becomes indifferent between remaining in the auction and buying the item for the posted price, then even if she does stay an active bidder in the auction then the next bidding activity will lead to the same situation, namely she will be (at best) indifferent between remaining in the auction and buying for the posted price.

Heuristic 2. High-valuation consumers assume that if at time t the <sup>fi</sup>nal LWB equals $M ( t ) ,$ their maximum willingness to bid at that time, then the <sup>fi</sup>nal auction price would equal M(t).

According to Heuristic 2, a high-valuation consumer assumes that if at time t she becomes indifferent between remaining in the auction and buying the item for the posted price, then the auction price would be $M ( t )$ . Heuristic 2 leads to the myopic best-response strategy, as it gives the optimal bidding strategy when no more consumers join the auction after time t. The consumer considers only the bidding activity at time t, with the current set of bidders, and not any potential future bidding activity. This heuristic clearly leads to higher willingness to bid than when consumers do consider potential future bidding activity. Thus, at any given dual channel design, Heuristic 2 would lead to higher cannibalization of posted price sales than Heuristic 1. It is also important to emphasize that although with Heuristic 2 consumers determine their willingness to bid at a given point of time not taking into account potential future bidding activity, such bidding activity may take place and as a result these consumers might submit a higher proxy bid or buy the item for the posted price before the auction ends.

We denote by $U _ { \mathrm { i } } ( t , M )$ the expected utility when the consumer uses Heuristic i and ${ x } = M ( t ) . { { U } _ { 1 } } ( t , M )$ and $U _ { 2 } ( t , M )$ are derived from Eq. (7), and are given by Eqs. (8) and (9) respectively. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Eq. (9) is derived by substituting $\scriptstyle x = M ( t )$ and $g { > } T { - } \mathfrak { t }$ with probability 1 (which represents Heuristic 2), in the expression for $U ( t , x )$ from Eq. (7). Finally, μ, the parameter of the exponential distribution of the time until the next bidding activity,<sup>6</sup>is given in Eq. (10).

$$
\begin{array}{l} U _ {1} (t, M) = (V - M _ {w} (T - t)) \left(1 - \int_ {g = 0} ^ {(T - t)} \mu (M) e ^ {- \mu (M) g} d g\right) \\ + \int_ {g = 0} ^ {(T - t)} ([ (V - p) - w g ]) \cdot \mu (M) e ^ {- \mu (M) g} d g \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

$$
U _ {2} (t, M) = V - M - w (T - t)\tag{9}
$$

$$
\mu (M) = \lambda \operatorname * {P r} (V \geq M)\tag{10}
$$

Solving $U _ { 1 } ( t , M ) = V - p$ we <sup>fi</sup>nd that $M _ { 1 } ( t )$ , high-valuation consumers' willingness to bid when using Heuristic 1, is given implicitly by the solution of the following Equation:

$$
M _ {1} = \frac {w + p \mu (M _ {1}) - w e ^ {\lambda \operatorname* {P r} (V \geq M _ {1}) (T - t)}}{\lambda \operatorname* {P r} (V \geq M _ {1})}\tag{11}
$$

Finally, solving $U _ { 2 } ( t , M ) = V - p ,$ we obtain the maximum willingness to bid as a function of time when high-valuation consumers use Heuristic 2, given by:

$$
M _ {2} = p - w (T - t).\tag{12}
$$

## 4.2. Discussion: The open-bid auction format and consumer behavior

Our model of consumer behavior captures three unique aspects of the open-bid auction format. 1) Regardless which heuristic is used, a consumer's decision whether to join the auction depends on the bidding activity up to that point of time and, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, on the value of the lowest required bid at the time of making the decision. This would not be true if the auction were conducted in the sealed-bid format, because the consumer would make the same decision (whether to submit a bid or not) regardless of the bidding activity that took place so far. 2) When using Heuristic 1 to determine her willingness to bid, a consumer takes into consideration the fact that she might quit bidding before the auction ends. 3) Regardless which heuristic is used, some consumers might end up submitting multiple increasing proxy bids. In addition, some consumers join the auction, but end up buying the item for the posted price before the auction ends. Both would not happen if the auction was in the sealed-bid format.

## 5. The dual channel's simulation

Our objective is to <sup>fi</sup>nd good dual-channel design strategies for different combinations of consumers' arrival rate and delay cost (such a combination de<sup>fi</sup>nes the environment), and compare the dual channel's performance to that of only posted price, the benchmark selling regime used by B-2-C retailers. Ideally, this would be done by deriving the mathematical expression for the seller's expected revenue, and then using F.O.Cs to <sup>fi</sup>nd the optimal values of the design parameters (q, T, p). Or, if the F.O.C cannot be solved, the mathematical expression of the expected revenue would be used to perform a numerical investigation and identify the best designs. However, it is not possible to derive analytically the seller's expected revenue for the model described in the previous sections. This is because a consumer's willingness to bid changes over time, and the decision whether to bid or not depends on state variables which are determined by previous bidding activity (both are not true in the case of a dual channel with a sealed-bid auction). The dynamic nature of the consumer behavior does not enable us to derive the expected revenue analytically.

When there is an analytical model of consumer behavior but it is not possible to derive analytically the seller's expected revenue, the model can be simulated in order to estimate the seller's expected revenue [1]. The simulation should be run a large number of times for a given combination of parameter values, and the average revenue would be an estimator of the expected revenue. Thus, the simulation presented in this paper simply lets us analyze the stochastic model described in the previous sections. In addition, our simulation answer calls for developing tools to study complex electronic markets [9].

## 5.1. Implementing the model as a simulation

We created a program in NetLogo<sup>7</sup>that simulates the model described in the previous sections. Before running the simulation the user sets the values of the parameters (λ, w) and the values of the decision variables (q, T, and p), indicates which heuristic should be implemented, and sets the simulation's clock to 0. When the simulation starts running, and every time a bidding activity terminates, the time of the next arrival is determined (the inter-arrival time is drown from the exponential distribution with parameter $1 / \Lambda ) .$ , and a valuation is drawn from the CDE of consumer valuations If the time of the next arrival is prior to the auction's end time, T, the simulation clock moves forward to that time and an agent with the respective valuation and arrival time is created. Then the simulation calculates

M(V,t), where t is the new agent's arrival time and V is his valuation, and if $M ( V , t ) { > } R B ( t )$ the new agent joins the auction by submitting a proxy bid of M(V,t). The newly submitted proxy bid might trigger additional bidding activity at time t, causing other bidders, whose willingness to bid is updated accordingly, to raise their proxy bids in order to remain in the WBL. When the bidding activity at time t ends, the simulation draws the next arrival time and a valuation, and the process repeats itself. If the arrival time exceeds the auction's end time, the simulation clock moves forward to the end of the auction, the auction price and set of winners are determined, and the simulation ends. The proxy bids M(V,t) are calculated, depending on the Heuristic modeled, according to Eqs. (8) or (9).

At the end of each simulation run, we record the resulting revenue. In addition, based on the realization of valuations we can easily calculate the seller's revenue if he were to use only posted price, p (multiplying the number of agents with $V { \geq } p$ by p). Finally, our program can also be used to simulate a model with only auction by setting $p > { \bar { v } } .$

## 6.1. Best designs of the dual channel

Examining the results reported in Tables 5 and 6, we see that for all (λ, w) combinations,<sup>8</sup>regardless of the heuristic used by consumers, the highest average daily revenue was achieved when the auction length, T, was set to the largest value we tested, 168 h (7 days), or very close to it. Finally, when high-valuation consumers use Heuristic 1 (they bid less than they should), the posted price is higher than the optimal price in the absence of auctions, and for λ 10/day the optimal price is \$67. When high-valuation consumers use Heuristic 2 (they bid more aggressively than they should) the posted price is set to \$50, which is the optimal posted price when auctions are not offered. Thus, when the seller is concerned with cannibalization of posted price sales, he keeps the posted price at \$50, but when the risk of cannibalization is lower, increasing the posted price helps to increase the auction price. For the same reason, when cannibalization risk is high (Heuristic 2) the seller must keep the auction lot small, which discourages bidding by high-valuation consumers. When cannibalization risk is lower (Heuristic 1), the seller increases the auction lot size in order to sell more units via the auction to low-valuation consumers. Table 7 summarizes the results for the optimal designs.

Why at the best dual channel design the auction is the longest we allow? Due to the Poisson arrival process, the number of potential low-valuation bidders is non-decreasing with the auction length. Thus, as the auction becomes longer, the number of low-valuation bidders is expected to increase while the number of high valuation bidders is expected to remain constant (they will not enter the auction when the remaining time is too long). The larger number of low-valuation bidders entering the auction in its early stages may then deter some high valuation consumers from bidding later on because they will observe higher LRB on arrival. Due to the fact that the seller maximizes revenue per unit time, the optimal lot size increases as the auction becomes longer.

If longer auctions increase the dual channel's revenue, why limit T to 7 days when searching for optimal designs? Because the advantage of increasing T is diminishing, and because in reality the seller also prefers to make sales via the auction earlier rather than later (a fact that is not captured by our model), it seems reasonable to limit the auction length to 7 days. Allowing a higher limit for the auction length (and larger values of q) will not change any of the insights derived in this paper. In fact, allowing longer auctions would only increase the advantage of offering auctions parallel to the posted price.

## 6.2. The auction lot size

From Tables 5 and 6 we see that multi-unit auctions can perform well even when w is low. This result stands in contrast to the results reported in Etzion et al. [8] for optimal designs of dual channels with sealed-bid auctions. Etzion et al. <sup>fi</sup>nd that one-unit auctions are optimal when w is low (e.g., \$0.5/day), but multiple units (and longer) auctions are optimal when w is large (e.g., \$2/day).

Multiple unit auctions can perform well even when w is low in the dual channel setup presented here because an open-bid auction has a positive bid increment. To demonstrate this point, consider the following example. A seller is running an auction with 5 units parallel to posted price p. Consider the case in which 5 high-valuation consumers and 5 low-valuation consumers arrive to the website during the auction. Assume also that w is low, so that all high-valuation consumers <sup>fi</sup>nd it optimal to bid. If the auction is conducted in the sealed-bid q+1 price format, the 5 high valuation consumers would submit a sealed bid of \$p (the optimal bid in the sealed-bid auction [5,8]), and all 5 auction units will go to high-valuation consumers for a price lower than \$p (the auction price would be the highest valuation of the 5 consumers with Vbp). However, if the auction is conducted in the open-bid format, then the order of arrivals matters in determining the auction price and set of winners. For example, if in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 6 arrivals there are 3 consumers with valuations in [p-h, p] and 3 high-valuation consumers, then when the fourth and <sup>fi</sup>fth high-valuation consumers arrive to the website they see a LRB higher than p (though the current winning bid is less than p). Thus, the auction price will be lower than p, and two auction units will be won by low-valuation consumers. Since for small h values we have $2 ^ { * } p + 5 ^ { * } ( p - h ) > 5 ^ { * } p$ (the two high-valuation consumers that did not win in the auction would buy for the posted price), the dual channel yielded larger revenue than only posted price, which would not be true if the auction was conducted in the sealed-bid format. This example demonstrates why, when w is low, a dual channel with open-bid auction can outperform only posted price more often than a dual channel with a sealed-bid auction.

## 7. Revenues comparison

Clearly the dual channel can perform better when the opportunity cost per unit time incurred by high-valuation consumers is large. When w is large, the seller more frequently sells auction units to consumers who value the product less than the posted price. Especially, when w is in<sup>fi</sup>nite, the seller can achieve “strict separation”, where only low-valuation consumers participate in the auction. The revenue under strict separation provides an upper bound for the performance of the dual channel.

The dual channel at its best design, when high-valuation consumers employ Heuristic 1, performs relatively close to the best case (strict separation) if arrival rate is high. ## 8. Discussion

Our analytical model of consumers' behavior cannot be solved for the optimal or equilibrium bidding strategies. Thus, we present two heuristics that consumers may use when making the decision whether to submit a proxy bid or buy for the posted price. There is no anchor in the literature for the two heuristics, and neither of them yields an equilibrium bidding strategy or an optimal strategy. However, the heuristics represent two extreme cases that bracket responses high-valuation consumers might have. Therefore, the revenue derived when consumers employ Heuristic 1 provides an upper bound for the performance of the dual channel, while the revenue derived under Heuristic 2 provides a lower bound. In addition, many of the insights gained under the two suggested heuristics are expected to hold for other models of consumer behavior. For example, the optimality of long auctions seems to be resilient to how aggressively highvaluation consumers may bid. In addition, the <sup>fi</sup>nding that even as w approaches zero, the dual channel with open-bid auctions can slightly outperform the only posted price regime seems to be true regardless of whether high-valuation consumers over- or under- participate in the auction compared to some optimal level of participation. Finally, we showed that the dual-channel can outperform the only posted price regime even under the worst case scenario, the case in which high-valuation consumers bid up to p-w(T-t) (no rational consumer would ever submit a higher proxy bid). Thus, we expect the dual channel to outperform the posted price selling regime under any other reasonable assumptions regarding how high-valuation consumers may determine their willingness to bid.

Another important point is that the seller can offer several overlapping auctions parallel to the posted price. This strategy is often observed on eBay, where retailers offer multiple one-unit auctions at any given time. However, due to the complexity of the dual channel setup, we model only the case in which the seller offers one auction at a time. Clearly, as we enlarge the set of strategies available for the seller – its performance can only improve. Thus, the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of the dual channel with one auction at a time provides a lower bound for the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of the dual channel when the seller can run multiple auctions parallel to the posted price.

## 9. Conclusions

Two commonly used mechanisms for selling consumer goods online are posted price and auctions. Though previous research examined the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of operating these two channels in parallel when the auctions are conducted in the sealed-bid format, there is lack of understanding of the viability of such dual channel strategy with open-bid auctions. In this paper we try to address this gap in the literature. We show that the performance of the dual channel depends on how consumers choose between the two channels, and on the design chosen by the seller. However, at the optimal design, even when consumers who can afford the posted price are eager to bid, the dual channel outperforms the single channel of only posted price, with an increase in revenue of around 3%. When cannibalization risk is lower, the advantage of the dual channel is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher, with up to a 30% increase in revenue compared to the only posted price channel.

Our simulation tool allows sellers to <sup>fi</sup>nd optimal dual channel designs under different assumptions regarding how consumers may make such decisions.The effect of the reserve price on the dual channel's design and performance can be examined with our simulation tool in future research. Similarly, future research should further examine the effect of changing the bid increment on the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of the dual channel.
