# THE EVOLUTIONARY TRAJECTORIES OF PEER-PRODUCED ARTIFACTS: GROUP COMPOSITION, THE TRAJECTORIES’ EXPLORATION, AND THE QUALITY OF ARTIFACTS<sup>1</sup>

Ofer Arazy Department of Information Systems, University of Haifa, Haifa ISRAEL 3498838 {oarazy@is.haifa.ac.il}

Aron Lindberg School of Business, Stevens Institute of Technology, 1 Castle Point Terrace, Hoboken NJ 07030 U.S.A. {aron.lindberg@stevens.edu}

Mostafa Rezaei Accounting, Operations, and Information Systems, Alberta School of Business, University of Alberta, Edmonton, AB CANADA T6G 2R6 {mrezaei@ualberta.ca}

Michele Samorani Department of Information Systems and Analytics, Leavey School of Business, Santa Clara University, Santa Clara, CA 95053 U.S.A. {msamorani@scu.edu}

Members of an online community peer-produce digital artifacts by negotiating different perspectives and personal knowledge bases. These negotiations are manifested in the temporal evolution of the peer-produced artifact. In this study, we conceptualize the evolution of a digital artifact as a trajectory in a feature space. Our theoretical frame suggests that, through negotiations, contributors’ actions “pull” the trajectory and shape its movement in the feature space. We hypothesize that the type of contributors that work on a focal article influences the extent to which that article’s trajectory explores alternative positions within that space, and that the trajectory’s exploration is, in turn, associated with the artifact’s quality. To test these hypotheses, we analyzed the trajectories of wiki articles drawn from two peer-production communities, Wikipedia and Wikia, tracking the evolution of 242 paired articles for over a decade during which the articles went through 536,745 revisions. We found that the contributors who are the most likely to increase the trajectory’s exploration are those that (1) return to work on the focal artifact and (2) are unregistered members in the broader online community. Further, our results show that the trajectory’s exploration has a curvilinear association with article quality, indicating that exploration contributes positively to quality, but that the effect is reversed when exploration exceeds a certain level. The insights derived from this study highlight the value of an artifact-centric approach to increasing our understanding of the dynamics underlying peer-production.

Keywords: Peer-production, wikis, artifact trajectory, feature space, exploration, group composition, artifact quality

## Introduction

J. R. R. Tolkien was both a fantasy fiction writer, as well as an academic philologist. These two roles were tightly intertwined, thus prompting Tolkien to draw on real-life mythologies and languages in his fiction. This is also reflected in the wiki articles that cover each of his books. For example, in the Wikipedia article on his novel The Hobbit (1937), contributors continuously debate the degree and scope of influence that Tolkien’s academic work exerted on his fiction. As these positions were negotiated among contributors, the content of the article evolved. Through this process, the article’s evolutionary trajectory was shaped, and its quality affected, both rising and falling over time. This paper investigates the process by which such negotiations within peer-production groups shape the digital artifact’s evolutionary trajectory, consequently influencing the artifact’s quality. We adopt the common definition of artifact quality as fitness for purpose (Klobas 1994; Lee et al. 2002); for a digital artifact, this reflects the extent to which the artifact’s form and function addresses users’ needs (e.g., the extent to which the wiki article is accurate, comprehensive, references reliable sources, and reflects a neutral viewpoint).

Although there is great variance in the quality of peerproduced goods, evidence has accumulated, indicating that, given appropriate governance mechanisms, online communities are able to produce high quality digital artifacts (Crowston et al. 2012; Mesgari et al. 2014). Measuring the quality of such artifacts (e.g., the information quality of Wikipedia articles; Crowston, Howison et al. 2006; Dang and Ignat 2016; Sarabadani et al. 2017; Yaari et al. 2011), as well as identifying the factors that influence quality (Arazy et al. 2011; Ransbotham and Kane 2011; Stvilia et al. 2008), is an ongoing ambition of both scholars and practitioners of peerproduction.

In online production communities, such as Wikipedia, where membership is characterized by turbulent stability (Arazy et al. 2016) and work is conducted in a distributed, selforganizing manner (Kane and Ransbotham 2016a; Ren et al. 2015), peer-produced artifacts, as well as their quality, evolve over time. These artifacts can be seen as spaces where different areas of expertise, cognitive styles, personalities, and viewpoints are being negotiated (Kane et al. 2014). Throughout such evolutionary processes, the artifact is continuously reshaped.

The objective of this study is to shed light on the process by which the evolution of a peer-produced artifact is formed through artifact-centric negotiations between members of an online production community, consequently influencing the artifact’s quality. Our interest here is in examining implicit negotiations that are reflected in changes made to the artifact (Jemielniak 2016; Kane and Ransbotham 2016b). We acknowledge, however, that these negotiations may also involve explicit discussions of the shared work process, for example on community discussion forums.

As digital artifacts evolve over time, they forge trajectories throughout the feature space, exploring different positions within that space. We hypothesize that a trajectory’s exploration of the feature space is beneficial, but only up to a certain point, after which additional exploration negatively affects artifact quality. Looking back at the wiki article for The Hobbit, exploration reflects extensive content additions and deletions, or substantial reframing and reorganization of the article. The article’s trajectory, in turn, is shaped by contributors’ negotiations. We explain how the contributors to a focal artifact manipulate the article according to their particular viewpoints, thus pulling the artifact’s trajectory in different directions. We make the case that contributors’ involvement with a focal artifact, as well as their membership status within the broader community, both influence the strength and direction of these pulls. Members’ actions shape the artifact’s evolutionary trajectory, and hence its exploration is driven by the composition of contributors peer-producing an artifact.

Our empirical investigation is based on data collected from two distinct peer-production communities that employ a similar technology platform: Wikipedia and Wikia. Characterizing the space that is traversed by articles’ trajectories using a bag-of-words representation, we apply a generalized linear mixed effects model to test our hypotheses. We find that two characteristics of groups’ composition influence the artifact trajectory’s exploration across the feature space: (1) the presence of unregistered members that represent the online community’s periphery, and (2) the inclusion of regular contributors that return to work on the group’s focal artifact. Further, we identify a curvilinear relationship between the trajectory’s exploration and artifact quality. Prior studies in the area have demonstrated that it is difficult to draw simple linear connections between process-related variables and the quality of peer-produced artifacts (Arazy et al. 2011; Ransbotham and Kane 2011; Ren et al. 2015). Our study advances research in the area by demonstrating that a focus on the artifact’s evolutionary trajectory helps to explain the relationship between group composition and the quality of peer-produced artifacts. The methodology we develop for tracing the evolution of peer-produced digital artifacts within a feature space, including the newly introduced metrics for measuring the artifact’s evolutionary trajectory (i.e., exploration), open the door for future studies that would consider the artifact’s trajectory as a focal construct.

## Conceptual Development

In this section, we first introduce our conceptualization of an article’s feature space, discussing the way in which the artifact’s evolutionary trajectory explores this space. Next, building on the notions of domain schemas and cognitive entrenchment (Dane 2010), we discuss how contributors relate to the feature space and describe the ways in which their involvement in the peer-production of the focal article shapes the exploration of that article’s trajectory within the feature space.

## Article Trajectory’s Exploration of the Feature Space

The quality of an artifact is commonly understood as fitness for purpose or fitness for use (Klobas 1994; Lee et al. 2002). The way quality is conceptualized, therefore, depends on the particular purpose and use of the artifact. For example, an information-based artifact, such as a wiki article, could be evaluated along intrinsic (e.g., accuracy), contextual (e.g., completeness and timeliness), and representational (e.g., clarity and consistency) dimensions. The design of a digital artifact could be thought of as a particular combination of features that together determine the artifact’s form and function. The quality of an artifact, therefore, reflects the extent to which its design serves the artifact’s particular purpose.

In the areas of design, engineering, and computation (e.g., machine learning), an artifact’s design is often represented through positioning it within a space of design alternatives, and this type of conceptualization has been used to explain how problems are solved (Kaplan and Simon 1990) and how to design different kinds of artifacts (Simon 1996). The role of designers and engineers is to identify the position in the space that yields the highest quality (taking costs into consideration). The space of possibilities is considered a design space or a feature space, where the dimensions defining the space are the various features of an artifact (Comaniciu and Meer 2002)<sup>2</sup> and the process of surveying the space of design possibilities is referred to as search (Newell and Simon 1972). For example, the positions on a chessboard may be defined in terms of two dimensions (i.e., features; the two axes) and all the different possible combinations of pieces on a chessboard may be thought of as a feature space. The distance between two points in the space reflects the extent to which two solutions are dissimilar. Considering these ideas in relation to the peer-production of wiki artifacts (or articles, a term that we use from here and until the discussion section), each feature (e.g., a word in content-based articles such as wikis) corresponds to a dimension of the space and each point in the feature space represents a particular configuration of the article’s features (e.g., a version of a Wikipedia article).

The systematic generation and evaluation of a wide variety of design alternatives, by altering design parameters, is referred to as exploration (Gries 2004; Kalman 1960; Navinchandra 2012). As an artifact’s design evolves over time, it traverses the feature space. Work in various scholarly disciplines have discussed the evolution of designs within features spaces, including biology (Wright 1978), architecture (Gero and Radford 1988), and organization science (March 1991). Drawing on these prior works, we label the line tracing the evolution of the artifact in the feature space, from an earlier version to the next, as the artifact’s trajectory. If an artifact’s design was to evolve in a directed manner, where every feature that is added to the artifact is kept until the final version, and no features are removed, the trajectory would form a straight beginning– end line (Kleppner and Kolenkow 2013).

Research from neighboring fields suggests that systematically traversing all possibilities within a feature space is often unrealistic, thus the exploration of the feature space invites a heuristic approach. In the design of algorithms, maximal exploration of the feature space is desired, but often has to be limited due to resource constraints (e.g., computational complexity, time), thus seeking a suboptimal solution where the exploration process converges within a reasonable time span (Èrepinšek et al. 2013; Yang 2011). Similarly, the scholarly literature on human processes of exploration, such as in theories of information seeking behavior (White and Roth 2009) and design (Navinchandra 2012), suggests that exploration has to be constrained due to limited resources (cognitive or organizational). In the field of organizational learning (March 1991), exploration entails the discovery of new opportunities by searching, identifying, and garnering competencies, and is supported by organic structures, loose coupling, and improvisation (Brown and Eisenhardt 1997; Tushman and Anderson 1986). In contrast to literatures that investigate human processes that foster innovation, the approach proposed in this paper focuses on the artifact as the locus of attention, studying how the artifact’s evolutionary trajectory explores the feature space. That is, rather than focus on exploration and exploitation as processes that induce innovation, we advocate for tracing how innovative artifacts themselves evolve over time.

As a peer-produced article evolves over time, its trajectory traverses the feature space. Given that articles are continuously evolving, we focus our attention on the article at a particular point of observation (rather than an end point). The hypothetical straight line within the feature space that links the initial point of article inception (i.e., the initial version of a wiki article) to a selected point of observation reflects a trajectory of minimal exploration. In most cases, an article does not follow the shortest path; instead, the trajectory wanders in the feature space. Here we define the level of a trajectory’s exploration as the deviation from that straight line, reflecting the extent of trial-and-error in an article’s evolution. High levels of exploration are achieved when the article’s trajectory meanders to design configurations (e.g., articulations of a wiki article) that are later abandoned.

Given that it is difficult to depict a trajectory’s movement in a multidimensional space, we employ multidimensional scaling (Friedman et al. 2001), using a two-dimensional representation of the feature space, where the dimensions represent the distance to inception (X axis) and observation (Y axis) states (additional details are presented in the “Method” section). In Figure 1, we illustrate this concept using the trajectory of the Wikipedia article on The Hobbit. We show the trajectory’s movement from the upper left-hand corner (inception of the article) to the lower right-hand corner (point of observation) as being curved away from the shortest path. As the article evolves over time, the trajectory explores multiple points within the feature space.

We conceptualize the features in all of the peer-produced articles as a cumulative feature space (e.g., in Wikipedia, the space could be defined by the set of words used in articles). This allows for representing each article’s trajectory in this shared feature space. A region within the feature space represents a collection of states of similar article configurations. We refer to regions within the cumulative feature space that attract multiple articles (i.e., they represent feature configurations that recur in several articles) as central, whereas regions that are rarely traversed by article trajectories are designated as the feature space’s fringe.

## Contributors’ Negotiations Shaping the Article’s Trajectory

Peer-producing high-quality articles is an endeavor fraught with difficulties, since a commonly agreed-upon process for coordinating work, such as central planning, is largely absent (Lindberg et al. 2016). Therefore, online peer-production communities rely heavily on the article itself (Howison and Crowston 2014) as well as on dialogue (Baralou and Tsoukas 2015) for coordination. The article trajectory emerges organically from contributors’ negotiation of different perspectives (Arazy et al. 2016; Kane et al. 2014). Such perspectives on the particular topic at hand, reflecting knowledge and preferences, are referred to as domain schemas<sup>3</sup> (Dane 2010): domain knowledge that is organized in the form of schemas which contain “knowledge about a concept or type of stimulus, including its attributes and the relations among those attributes” (Fiske and Taylor 1984, p. 98). Based on how they see the world, people tend to become entrenched in their domain schemas (Dane 2010) and consequently stabilize particular behavioral patterns (McGrath and Kelly 1986), that is, in their habits (Dewey 1922).

For members of a peer-production community, this means that their contribution patterns are stable—at least to some extent—such that each member’s contributions are concentrated in a particular region in the feature space. For example, in the case of content-based products, such as an online encyclopedia, people’s domain schemas are reflected in the vocabulary they regularly use when editing articles. Contributors are, therefore, likely to edit articles in ways closely aligned with their domain schemas. When working on a focal article, contributors often pull the article’s trajectory toward the particular region of the feature space that reflects their habitual contributions. Such pulls may stem from an unintentional manifestation of the person’s preferences, skills, and expertise, or alternatively from a more deliberate attempt to shape the artifact according to the contributor’s personal vision.

In online production communities, when multiple contributors working collectively are entrenched in different domain schemas (as reflected in distinct regions of the feature space) they tend to pull the artifact in different directions. Here we use the term negotiations to refer to the process by which community members collectively shape the artifact through contributions that are anchored in their particular domain schemas. Because we recognize that negotiations may be characterized as either collaborative or contentious, depending on the particular dynamics of interactions, we use negotiations in an agnostic manner, without distinguishing between cooperative and conflictual processes.

In sum, when working on a particular artifact, members of an online production community are restricted—at least to some extent—by their domain schemas. These schemas are reflected in regular activity patterns, which correspond to regions within the feature space. When various contributors come together to peer-produce an article, each individual change to the article could be viewed as a pull of the article’s trajectory. Collectively such pulls can be viewed as negotiations. Often, these pulls are directed toward the region in the feature space that corresponds to that contributor’s regular activity patterns. Next we illustrate this process through a real-world example.

## An Extended Example: The Hobbit

Consider our opening example of Tolkien’s The Hobbit. The trajectory of this Wikipedia article was shaped by contributors’ negotiations, each moving the trajectory in a direction that is aligned with their domain schema. Of particular note is the negotiation between two contributors: Foxtrot and Moondance.<sup>4</sup> These two contributors conducted a lengthy negotiation with regard to various sources speaking to the influence of Germanic mythologies on Tolkien’s writings. The region within the feature space that reflects Foxtrot’s regular activity patterns (capturing the editor’s activity across all Wikipedia articles) is represented as a word cloud<sup>5</sup> in Figure 2a. This word cloud has several words such as paganism, germanic, norse, mythology, folklore, legend, myth, and edda, which speaks to a deep interest in the types of myths that Tolkien based much of his work on. This is reflected in a domain schema that Foxtrot applies to the source in question, which leads him to make different interpretations compared to Moondance, as well as different inferences with regard to how the wiki-article The Hobbit should be written. Moondance’s editing activity across Wikipedia, in contrast, is characterized by words such as Tolkien, notability, authors, claims, specific, based, ref, and research (i.e., the word cloud that reflects the region of the editor’s regular activity pattern), which speaks to an interest in Tolkien and his works, but emphasizes procedural knowledge (i.e., a focus on meeting an established criteria, such as notability, specificity, or grounding of claims in specific references), as opposed to the substantive forms of knowledge exhibited by Foxtrot’s edits (i.e., a focus on issues of content, such as the relationship between Tolkien’s fictional work and his academic work on mythology). Moondance’s regular activity patterns reflect a domain schema that predisposes that editor to make much narrower, rule-bound interpretations of how a particular source should be cited and used when working on the focal wiki article The Hobbit.

To illustrate how these two perspectives are negotiated, we focus our attention on a particular period in the article’s life— December 2012—during which Moondance and Foxtrot pulled the article in different directions. To help interpret the dynamics surrounding such movements and show that they are directed toward the contributors’ regions in the feature space that corresponds to the contributors’ regular activity patterns, we study the discussions in the article’s talk page<sup>6</sup> during that period. Based on their respective domain schemas, Moondance interprets the source (a book, Tolkien and the Invention of Myth: A Reader) as being narrowly about the observation that myths are represented as textual artifacts in Tolkien’s work; for example, that The Silmarillion (published in 1977) is a “translation” from a fictional book written in Elvish. Foxtrot, in contrast, claims that the source makes broader claims with regard to the influence of Germanic mythology on the work of Tolkien.

Such pulls of the article’s trajectory in different directions suggest that the directionality is likely influenced by their varying domain schemas (i.e., preferring regions in different locations within the feature space).

## Hypothesis Development

Our hypothesis development serves to show how article quality is driven by the trajectory’s exploration, which in turn is driven by several group composition constructs that imply different orientations toward the feature space. We conceptualize an article’s quality at a particular point in the article’s evolution (i.e., a particular version) as an outcome driven by the trajectory’s exploration up to that point. Similarly, when conceptualizing group composition variables, we define the composition of the group of contributors to a particular article up to the particular time (or article version) when the trajectory and article quality are observed. All of the concepts that impact artifact quality directly or indirectly are defined and exemplified in Table 1.

In the design and engineering of digital artifacts in general, problems and solutions coevolve, constraints are often negotiable, subproblems are interconnected, and solutions are not right or wrong, only better or worse (Dorst and Cross 2001; Goel and Pirolli 1992). Hence, through refinement and iteration, an artifact’s design explores the space of alternatives (Arora 2004; Cross 2006; Tversky et al. 2003). Successfully navigating the challenges of peer-production and, more broadly, effectively designing digital artifacts requires balancing concerns. One danger is to fixate on a particular design or article configuration too early and fail to identify valuable alternatives (Ball and Ormerod 1995; Jansson and Smith 1991) or make only small and iterative improvements to an article’s configuration (Tohidi et al. 2006). Conversely, too much exploration of alternatives and the design may not converge (Èrepinšek et al. 2013; Yang 2011).

The extent of the article trajectory’s exploration, as previously explained, represents the expansiveness of the trajectory’s arc throughout the feature space. This means that a wider trajectory enables a more extensive exploration of the feature space. In such an exploratory trajectory, more perspectives and alternatives are considered, more examples can be incorporated, and richer details can be featured in the article. For example, the presence of multiple contributors exploring different facets of an article may make it more complete, as they provide complementary knowledge bases that can be integrated in a given article (Faraj et al. 2011). Furthermore, negotiations between contributors with regard to ways of framing an article or the overall set of facts of an article may help to increase accuracy or enforce a neutral point of view (a central value within the Wikipedia community). These factors, in turn, act to increase the quality of the article.

Excessively large amounts of exploration across a feature space, however, are not necessarily desirable. As explorations multiply, the trajectory may be pulled in many directions at once, prompting an extremely wide trajectory. This may indicate conflict and a lack of collective focus or convergence. Indeed, Arazy et al. (2011) show that, under certain conditions, task conflict has a negative effect on the quality of Wikipedia articles. Similarly, unresolved tensions may direct the collective attention of the community away from improving the content in an objective fashion and toward internecine conflict with regard to positioning of the article in the feature space. The tensions associated with extensive pulling of the article’s trajectory also have a wider impact on the community surrounding an article, by discouraging other participants from weighing in on the article (Choi et al. 2010; Halfaker et al. 2012).

In sum, designing and peer-producing digital articles require that exploration of alternatives be balanced with a clear design focus and a directed path within the feature space (Èrepinšek et al. 2013; Laseau 2001). Considering these countervailing forces, we expect that the extent to which the trajectory of peer-produced articles explores the feature space has a curvilinear, inverted-U shaped relationship with article quality. Therefore, we state

Hypothesis 1: An article’s trajectory’s exploration has an inverted-U shaped relationship with the quality of the peerproduced article, such that a medium level of exploration is associated with the highest quality of the article, compared to low and high levels of exploration that are characterized by lower quality.

Based on their prior experiences and the attendant cognitive entrenchment in their domain schemas, the composition of a group of contributors (Arazy et al. 2011) influences the ways in which the article’s trajectory explores the feature space. Large online communities are heterogeneous and often peerproduce many different articles. The composition of the group of contributors working on a focal article varies in terms of both contributors’ (1) retention within the focal article and (2) membership status within the broader online production community. In terms of a contributor’s retention within the focal article, we distinguish between newcomers and contributors that return to work on the focal article (Ransbotham and Kane 2011). Conversely, in terms of a contributor’s membership status within the broader community, the literature differentiates between peripheral contributors that are unregistered and the registered community members (Crowston, Wei, et al. 2006; Preece and Shneiderman 2009). Registered members tend to be more active across a wide range of articles, are often interested in administrative aspects and sometimes become part of the community’s core. These two classifications of contributors are fundamentally different, and one construct does not logically imply the other. Sometimes a contributor returns to work on the focal article, yet is not a registered member in the online community. The opposite is also true: a one-time contributor that plays a marginal role in the peer-production of a particular article may still be a registered member of the broader community (Arazy et al. 2011).

In what follows, we advance two hypotheses regarding the ways in which the two group composition constructs— contributors’ retention within the focal article and their membership status within the broader community—affect the extent to which the trajectory of a peer-produced article explores the feature space.

We begin by arguing that contributors’ retention within the particular article group shapes the trajectory’s exploration. Contributors who return to a particular article and make repeated changes are likely to develop psychological ownership (Pierce et al. 2003) and exhibit high motivation with regard to shaping the article (Kane et al. 2014; Majchrzak et al. 2013; Ransbotham and Kane 2011). The more time, attention, and energy contributors spend on the focal article, the more they will feel the article is “theirs” (Pierce et al. 2001), and tend to pull that article toward the regions within the feature space that reflects their domain schema (and thus the bulk of their past contributions). Given that these returning contributors make multiple changes to the focal article, we conjecture that their aggregate pull will be stronger, compared to that of newcomers. Hence, the group’s retention of active contributors (Ransbotham and Kane 2011), namely the ratio between returning contributors and newcomers to a specific article, represents the overall strength of the pull toward various regions within the feature space. As the strength of the pull increases, it widens the arc of the trajectory’s exploration. A particular example of such a strong pull is defensive filtering, whereby Wikipedia contributors that are vested in a particular article respond to newcomers’ contributions by repeatedly pulling the article back toward the version they defend (Kane et al. 2014). To illustrate this idea, consider two articles that have gone through the same number of revisions, one with few highly active contributors and the other with many one-time contributors; we argue that the trajectory of the former article—represented through a high proportion of returning contributors—will be associated with a higher level of exploration. Put formally,

Hypothesis 2a: The group’s composition in terms of contrib utors’ retention within the focal article will affect the trajectory’s exploration, such that the greater the proportion of returning contributors to a particular article versus newcomers, the greater is the exploration of the article’s trajectory.

Beyond contributors’ retention within the focal article, contributors’ membership status (Bartel and Dutton 2001) within the broader online community may also affect the way in which they relate to the feature space. Over time, contributors commonly move from being peripheral (e.g., non-registered) users, to becoming registered members of the online community, and some may eventually take on administrative positions (e.g., in Wikipedia there is a ladder of access privileges, including administrator, bureaucrat, and steward; Arazy et al. 2014). Throughout this process, contributors change their membership status (Burke et al. 2010) and move toward the community’s core (Arazy et al. 2019; Crowston, Wei, et al. 2006; Dejean and Jullien 2015). Registered community members often have extensive work experience; they are responsible for most of the peer-produced content (Mockus et al. 2002), may become deeply embedded in the community (Granovetter 1985), and attain leadership status through either formal or informal means (Johnson et al. 2015; Preece and Shneiderman 2009). These characteristics may put the members close to the community’s core at risk of becoming cognitively entrenched (Dane 2010), and such entrenchment may decrease the likelihood of conducting wide searches of the feature space. Through socialization, core members often develop similar mental models (Klimoski and Mohammed 1994) with regard to the overall organization of the communal feature space, and thus their regions of regular activity patterns may also occupy central regions within the feature space (i.e., established or mainstream areas). Unregistered members, in contrast, tend to focus on narrow domains (Arazy et al. 2011) and the regions reflecting their regular activity patterns are more likely to occupy regions on the feature space’s fringes.

Unregistered and peripheral members often contribute novel ideas (Boudreau and Lakhani 2013), challenge the accepted worldview, and thus act to increase productive tensions. For example, Lee and Cole (2003) describe how peripheral contributors in the Linux peer-production community identified most problems with the existing software code, resulting in tensions around the code base, and Anthony et al. (2009) show that valuable contributions to Wikipedia are made by unregistered members. Employing a feature space conceptualization, we argue that registered members are more likely to pull the peer-produced article toward central regions, thus decreasing the article’s trajectory’s exploration (as opposed to unregistered members, who pull toward regions at the feature space’s fringe, and thus increase the trajectory’s exploration). The aggregate effect of more registered members contributing to a focal article is a decreased exploration in the trajectory arc. Therefore, we posit

Hypothesis 2b: The group’s composition in terms of contributors’ membership status within the broader community will affect the trajectory’s exploration, such that the greater the proportion of registered members versus unregistered members, the lesser is the exploration of the article’s trajectory.

In sum, our proposed research model describes how article quality is driven by the trajectory’s exploration, which in turn is driven by the two group composition variables: contributors’ retention within the article group and their membership status within the broader community, as illustrated in Figure 4.

## Method

We open this section by describing the study’s context and explaining the sampling approach. Next, we describe our representation of how a wiki article evolves across the feature space. We proceed to describe our operationalization of the article trajectory’s exploration. We then describe the measures for the antecedents (i.e., group composition variables) and the consequent (article quality) of such trajectories. Finally, we describe the hierarchical regression method used to analyze the data and test our hypotheses.

## Sampling Procedure

The settings for this study are two online communities engaged in the peer-production of articles using a wiki system: Wikipedia and Wikia. Wikipedia, launched in 2001, is a free, multilingual online encyclopedia that contains over 38 million articles in more than 290 languages and boasts over 100,000 active contributors. Wikia was founded in 2004 by Wikipedia alumni who were interested in articles that Wikipedia considered inappropriate (mostly in topics such as celebrity gossip, gaming, and hobbies). Wikia is an advertisement-based, commercial, online platform in the English language, which hosts various fan communities and includes more than 360,000 articles and 190,000 unique contributors.

Sampling articles from two communities that employ the same technology platform (a wiki engine) and a similar type of artifact (summaries of particular topics) allows us to control for exogenous variables and ensures that our operationalization of outcome variables uses the exact same metrics. Seeking to control for variation in the topics of articles (as peer-production patterns often differ across topical domains (Kittur et al. 2009), we developed a matched sample of paired articles appearing in both sources—Wikipedia and Wikia— covering encyclopedic entries with the same title in the two domains. Our sample was restricted to the most active area within Wikia: entertainment (Greenstein 2009), and particularly to the movie domain (including movie titles and characters). Within this domain, we identified all wiki instances that had a match on Wikipedia (e.g., The Little Mermaid). Given that our work analyzes the evolution of articles throughout their lifetime, we decided to exclude articles that are too short (we set a threshold of 100 revisions); analogously, as we aim to capture peer-production dynamics, we decided to exclude articles that were edited by too few editors (we set a threshold of 30 contributors). The resulting sample consisted of 121 matching Wikipedia–Wikia article pairs, listed in Appendix A. Using the MediaWiki action API, we gathered the entire history of these 242 articles, from their inception date up to January 1, 2016. Overall, we tracked the evolution of articles stretching over a decade, covering a total of 536,745 revisions (on average, 2,218 revisions per article) made by 134,969 distinct contributors.

## Data Sets

The APIs of Wikipedia and Wikia allowed us to retrieve all versions of a given article along with logs of discrete edits (or revisions) made to each article. However, because contributors often save their work before it is complete, creating a series of revisions, each individual revision is not a desirable unit of analysis. Prior research in the area of human– computer interaction has relied on contributors’ interaction sessions to generate metrics for understanding the performance of information resources (Goševa-Popstojanova et al. 2006)—especially in the domain of search (Donato et al. 2010; Eickhoff et al. 2014) and content personalization (Lee et al. 2000; Spiliopoulou et al. 2003). Modeling our approach after Halfaker et al. (2015), we defined the unit of measure as an editing session, which encompasses the consecutive edits made to a particular article by a single editor (i.e., a session starts with the first revision and ends at the time of the last revision). Through this transformation, we moved from

536,745 revisions to 286,009 editing sessions (246,787 in Wikipedia; 39,222 in Wikia).

## Measuring Article Quality

Here we adopt a generic definition of artifact quality as fitness for purpose or fitness for use. In the context of our framework, the article’s quality is an evaluation of the knowledgebased article at the point of observation, based on metrics that are suitable for the particular artifact type. The peer-produced article in this study is a wiki article, and, therefore, article quality is reflected in the quality of the content in the wiki article.

Our measure of article quality is only available for the Wikipedia subsample; it builds on the recent work of the Wikimedia Foundation to develop an automated tool for estimating articles’ quality. The ORES service (Objective Revision Evaluation Service)<sup>7</sup> is intended to be scalable, easily extensible, and responsive (Dang and Ignat 2016; Sarabadani et al. 2017). ORES was originally designed to help automate critical wiki-work, for example, vandalism detection and removal. ORES uses automatic machinelearning methods. ORES is trained on Wikipedia’s community-based article scoring procedure, which stresses the goals of content reliability, verifiability, and objectivity, as well as importance, and assigns quality grades to articles. Wikipedia provides a rating system for articles to determine how well a particular article has achieved these goals (from lowest to highest: Stub, Start, C Grade, B Grade, Good Article (GA), and Featured Article (FA)).<sup>8</sup> Any member can nominate an article to be rated. Once nominated, a forum reminiscent of an academic peer review is used to vet candidate articles; reviewers highlight the strengths and weaknesses of the articles. Anyone can review nominated articles for a three-week period, after which the responsible Wikipedian (a position similar to a senior editor at an academic journal) determines whether there is consensus for promotion. Unlike academic peer review, the process is open for anyone to provide comments. It is important to note that the ratings refer to a particular date (and article revision). To date, only a small portion of the articles have been rated; for example, less than 0.1% of the articles have been rated as Featured articles.<sup>9</sup> This scoring system (and particularly the Featured Article category) has been employed by researchers as a primary mechanism for estimating article quality on Wikipedia (Carillo and Okoli 2011; Kane 2011; Ransbotham and Kane 2011).

The development of ORES, and in particular the set of features employed in the machine learning algorithm (e.g., number of words in an article), was done through community consultation. The features are based on the content added/ removed/changed at each revision and the characteristics of the editor. The classifier used was Random Forest (Breiman 2001). The performance of the algorithm is excellent $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ $\mathrm { R O C - A U C } = 0 . 9 4$ ; Sarabadani et al. 2017). We used the ORES API to extract quality estimates for each Wikipedia article in our sample for each of its versions. The ORES API outputs probabilities for having the article associated with each of the quality grades. Next, the probabilities are weighted using the scheme of [Stub = 0, Start = 1, C = 2, B = $3 , \mathrm { G A } = 4 , \mathrm { F A } = 5 ]$ to produce a score between 0 and 5. The outcome variable for our study was the change in quality between the current and the previous revision.

## Representing the Feature Space and the Evolution of a Wiki Article’s Trajectory

Performing our analysis required that we develop a conceptualization for recording the trajectory of the evolution of a wiki article. We conceptualized each version of the wiki article (i.e., after each editing session) as a point in a statespace (i.e., a feature space), and the article trajectory as the sequence of versions $\nu ^ { 0 } , \nu ^ { 1 } , . . . , \nu ^ { R } ,$ , from the inception of the article $ { \boldsymbol \nu } ^ { 0 }$ (an empty article) to the most current version $\nu ^ { R }$ (our study’s cut-off date). Our analysis is conducted at the level of an article’s version, such that we measure the value of each variable at the point of the particular version.

The $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ version of an article, $\nu ^ { i } ,$ is modeled as a vector of two attributes $\nu ^ { i } = ( x , y )$ , where x is the distance between the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ version and the initial version and y is the distance between the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ version and the final version. The distance between two versions is measured as the number of words that appear in one version but not in the other (i.e., Hamming distance; Hamming 1950). Appendix B reports the details of this twoattribute representation. When visualized on a twodimensional chart, the trajectory of an article starts from the empty version $\nu ^ { 0 }$ on the top-left and ends at the version $\nu ^ { R }$ that represents the current state of observation (at the bottom-right of the chart).

## Measuring the Exploration of a Wiki Article’s Trajectory

We operationalized the level of exploration in the article’s trajectory as the distance between the current state of the article and a hypothetical line representing the direct and shortest path of an article’s evolution (i.e., this line represents a situation where content was gradually added from the earliest to the most recent version, without the insertion of irrelevant content or the removal of relevant material). To focus solely on the trajectory of knowledge-related changes, we omitted changes to the article’s trajectory resulting from malicious manipulations (for details regarding the procedure for removing vandalism see Appendix C). Building on our 2D representation of an article’s trajectory $\nu ^ { 0 } \to \nu ^ { 1 } \to \dots \to \nu ^ { R } ,$ we define exploration at the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ version as the distance between version í<sup>i</sup> and the straight line that connects $\nu ^ { 0 }$ and $\nu ^ { R } .$ The mathematical formulas for computing exploration are reported in Appendix B.<sup>10</sup> In Appendix D we report on an experiment that demonstrates that the evolution of an article’s trajectory is not random (comparing the article’s and random walk trajectories).

## Measuring Group Composition Characteristics

We now list and describe the set of variables aimed at capturing the following features of the contributors who made the edits resulting in version $\nu ^ { i } \colon$ contributors’ retention within the focal article and their membership status within the larger community. A contributor’s retention within the focal article was measured through a binary variable that distinguishes between a newcomer to the focal article $( ^ { 6 4 } 0 ^ { 9 } )$ or a contributor returning to work on that Wikipedia article (“1”). Conversely, in line with prior studies on peer-production communities (Crowston, Wei, et al. 2006), contributors’ membership status was measured through a binary variable that is based on their user ID: “0” for non-registered contributors, identified through the contributor’s IP address, as described in Arazy et al. (2011)<sup>11</sup> and “1” in the case of registered members.

For the group of contributors working on a particular article, the contributors’ retention within the focal article at version í<sup>i</sup> was calculated as the percentage of returning contributors (i.e., the percentage of contributors that, up to version , have made more than a single revisions to the focal article). Conversely, contributors’ membership status within the community was calculated as the percentage of registered contributors that have edited the focal article up to version í<sup>i</sup>. Our calculation for both of these group composition variables are in line with the organizational literature, which commonly calculates group composition variables as ratios or proportions (Harrison and Klein 2007; Horwitz and Horwitz 2007; Pelled et al. 1999).

Table 2 provides a summary of the operationalization of constructs used in our study. As we explained earlier, all variables are calculated at a particular article version, taking into account the article’s history up to that version.

## Control Variables

To control for exogenous factors, we measured and included a group of control variables that have been shown to affect peer-production patterns (Arazy et al. 2011; Ransbotham and Kane 2011; Ransbotham et al. 2012), namely the editing session’s numbering within the particular article and the article’s age (time since inception, in hours). In order to control for the effect of local variations along the article’s trajectory (which does not contribute to the trajectory’s exploration), we added a variable that captures the percentage of revisions that were tagged as “edit wars”<sup>12</sup> (Kittur et al. 2007; Viegas et al. 2007). For details on the calculation of this metric, see Appendix E.

## Data Analysis

In order to test our hypotheses, we ran two independent regression models. The first was a regression of the article’s exploration using, as explanatory variables, the group composition features (contributors’ status within the article group, contributors’ status within the community) and the control variables of the revision’s sequential number, article age, and edit-wars. The second model was a regression on article quality using the independent variable of exploration, as well as all other variables mentioned above as controls.

As previously noted, our unit of analysis was an editing session; hence, the data were nested both within the domain (Wikipedia and Wikia) and with regard to the particular article from which the editing sessions were drawn. We employed generalized linear mixed effects models, using restricted maximum likelihood estimation, which accounts for the nested structure of the data. To do this, we used the R package nlme<sup>13</sup> (Pinheiro et al. 2019), which models the predictors as fixed effects, while the nesting structure is modeled as random effects.

Individual hypotheses were tested based on the statistical significance of effects for corresponding variables. The model’s overall goodness of fit was estimated based on the metrics of log likelihood (Luke 2004), where larger values are better. Because R<sup>2</sup>, as understood in linear regression, is not available for the generalized linear mixed model, we used a pseudo-R<sup>2</sup> measure<sup>14</sup> to assess the explanatory power of the predictors (Hardin and Hilbe 2007). This pseudo-R<sup>2</sup> explains how much variance in the dependent variable is explained by the fixed factors only (i.e., our hypothesized predictors).

As a post hoc analysis, we also tested the mediating effects of both exploration and the square of exploration on the relationship between group composition variables and article quality. This was done through estimating indirect, direct, and total effects, along with confidence intervals computed using quasi-Bayesian methods, that is, Monte Carlo simulations, which is the preferred method when fitting mediation models to multilevel data (Tingley et al. 2014). We used 500 simulation runs for each model, using the R package mediation.<sup>15</sup>

## Sensitivity Analyses and Robustness Checks

We conducted various tests to ensure the robustness of our findings, verifying that the results of our regression analyses were insensitive to our operationalization choices. First, we tested for multicollinearity effects in our regression models and found no cause for concern: variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all variables was below 3.5, for each of the models (O’brien 2007), except for the variable exploration and its square, which naturally were highly correlated. Second, we verified that our choice of the study’s cutoff date did not influence our findings. We tested whether choosing an alternative cutoff with 10% or 20% fewer editing sessions per article changed the findings (e.g., for an article with 1,000 editing sessions at our original cutoff, we now analyzed the trajectory up to its 900<sup>th</sup> and 800<sup>th</sup> editing session, respectively). To test the robustness of our results, we ran the various regression models using the revised variables (at the alternative cutoff) to find that the significance and direction of all hypothesized coefficients remained unchanged. Third, we verified that alternative operationalizations of the contributors’ retention within the focal article and contributors’ membership status within the community constructs did not impact our results. Details and results from all these sensitivity analyses and robustness checks are provided in Appendix G.

## Discussion

Peer-production communities consist of multiple contributors with varying perspectives, areas of expertise, and interests. As members with different domain schemas work together, tensions may ensue. The way in which these tensions are negotiated is critical for innovation and peer-production processes (Faraj et al. 2011), because they shape the artifact<sup>16</sup> trajectory as it explores the feature space, consequently influencing the quality of the peer-produced digital artifact. While existing work on online knowledge collaboration has largely posited that these tensions are situated within the peerproduction process (Faraj et al. 2011; Kane et al. 2014), in this work we offer a complementary view by investigating tensions as they are manifested in the peer-produced artifact trajectory. Namely, in the current study, negotiations in peerproduction are viewed as part of the artifact’s exploration of the feature space. Shifting our attention to the artifact’s evolutionary trajectory allowed us to reveal novel insights about the extent to which exploration of alternative artifact designs is effective, as well as to explain some of the inconsistent prior findings regarding the relationships between group composition variables and artifact quality (Arazy et al. 2011; Ransbotham and Kane 2011).

## Tracking an Artifacts’ Evolutionary Trajectories

An important contribution of this study is the proposed feature space framework. Prior studies in the Information Systems field have called for a focus on the article’s trajectory as a central mechanism (in addition to the group members and their work process) that explains the success of peerproduction endeavors. For example Kane et al. (2014) suggest that

The artifact allowed for heedful interrelating in the sense that the artifact provided the stability and visible routines …. These different focuses of community cognition that are embodied, coalesced, and coordinated by the artifact, and its evolution suggests that future research should examine this role of the artifact in more detail (p. 3044).

Along the same lines, Arazy et al. (2016) found that the artifact served as a central point for coordination of emergent work in Wikipedia. Despite the growing recognition of the artifact-centric approach to study peer-production and the calls for investigating the trajectories of digital artifacts (Yoo et al. 2010), empirical attempts to track artifacts’ evolutionary trajectories are scarce. Tschang and Szczypula (2006) and Yu and Nickerson (2011) are two such examples, but the experiments in these studies were restricted to a few design iterations, and they only scratch the surface in terms of our understanding of how digital artifacts evolve over time.

Our systematic approach to recording digital artifacts’ evolution provides a framework for future research to help address questions about artifacts’ evolution. Toward this end, our framework records the state of a digital artifact as a point in a space that is defined through the artifact’s features. In particular, our projection of the trajectory onto a 2D space, where the position of each version of the artifact is defined in terms of the distances to both the origin and the observed state, as well as our proposed metric for representing the artifact trajectory’s exploration, have proved to be a useful tool for estimating the way in which the trajectory traverses the space of feature configurations prior to converging.

While we have chosen to study the peer production of wiki articles, the framework that we have developed could be useful for studying other text-based artifacts (such as scholarly papers, legislation, or social media) as well as other types of digital artifacts (such as software products, where the product’s functions serve as the dimensions of the feature space). Our framework, thus, enables a wealth of studies that can draw upon our methodological innovations, while moving beyond the particular context they were developed in. There are some possible methodological extension of our framework, for example to analyze the trajectories’ shape and structure using techniques such as cluster analysis. This would allow researchers to identify archetypes (or taxonomies) of different types of trajectories (Greenwood and Hinings 1993), thus paving the way for an analysis of antecedents and consequents (e.g., article quality) of various trajectory classes.

## The Curvilinear Effect of the Trajectory’s Exploration on Artifact Quality

A second important theoretical contribution of this study consists of showing that exploration has a curvilinear effect on the artifact quality: that is, more exploration in the space of possible design configurations (i.e., more exploration of the feature space by an artifact’s trajectory) leads to higher artifact quality, but only up to a certain point, thus supporting

H1. Namely, our findings suggest that increased exploration is beneficial only if the group is able to converge on a particular configuration of the artifact’s design. In the absence of such convergence, the artifact’s trajectory wanders about without a clear focus, hampering quality. To verify that excessive exploration is indeed linked to low-quality output, we produced a random series of revisions to a text article (i.e., random content entails low quality), showing that the trajectory of this random article’s evolution exhibits a particularly high degree of exploration. Details of this test are provided in Appendix D.

The positive effect of exploration of design space has been studied in the context of algorithm designs (Èrepinšek et al. 2013; Yang 2011), as well as in the context of human exploration processes, such as: information seeking behavior (White and Roth 2009), design (Navinchandra 2012), and organizational innovation processes (March 1991). All of these bodies of work suggest that to achieve optimal performance, exploration has to be constrained (due to limited resources, cognitive or organizational), thus highlighting the need to balance exploration and exploitation. Adding to this body of literature, the approach proposed in this paper focuses on the artifact as the locus of attention, studying the linkage between the exploration of an artifact’s trajectory and the artifact’s quality.

Our results regarding the effect of the trajectory’s exploration open the door for several possible research directions. For example, future research could seek to identify the tipping points at which exploration’s positive effect on artifact quality is reversed and becomes negative. Another interesting line of investigation would be to draw analogies to studies of human cognitive processes and capture the notion of a trajectory’s “exploitation” of a feature space (March 1991). Analyzing the trade-offs between exploration and exploitation might reveal novel insights about the optimal artifact trajectory (i.e., that which yields the highest quality).

## Group Members’ Contributions Shaping the Artifact’s Trajectory

The third theoretical contribution of our study pertains to the novel insights regarding the effects of group members’ behavior. We have argued that contributors pull the article trajectory toward their regular activity patterns. To provide empirical evidence for this conjecture, we conducted a secondary empirical analysis, as detailed in Appendix H. Our findings allow us to tease out some of the different characteristics of peer-production groups. First, our results indicate that contributors returning to work on a focal artifact are linked to an increase in the exploration of that artifact’s evolutionary trajectory, thus supporting H2a. This implies that commitment to and engagement with a particular artifact is an important factor for increasing the trajectory’s exploration (which, in turn, is associated with the curvilinear effect on artifact quality). Second, our results indicate that unregistered community members are associated with increased exploration of the artifact’s trajectory (corroborating H2b; see Anthony et al. 2009), whereas registered members’ may actually act to limit the trajectory’s exploration. We explain that registered members tend to adopt mainstream perspectives and stabilize their contributions in central regions within the feature space, such that their pulls toward these central regions have a relatively small effect on the artifact’s trajectory, restricting its exploration. To provide support for this line of explanation, we performed a quantitative analysis of our study’s sample, as detailed in Appendix I.

Prior research has related group composition variables to the quality of the peer-produced article. For example, Arazy et al. (2011) linked contributors’ status to article quality, and Ransbotham and Kane (2011) drew linkages between members’ retention and the quality of Wikipedia articles. Interestingly, both of these papers suggested that the effect of group composition is nonlinear. Our results indicate that the article’s trajectory is a key factor mediating the relationships between the contributor group and the quality of the peerproduced artifact. Namely, we show that group composition variables linearly affect the trajectory’s exploration, which in turn has a curvilinear effect on article quality. That being said, our analysis shows that mediation is partial, such that group composition also has a direct effect on article quality (or that possibly this effect is mediated through an exogenous variable). Explicitly, beyond the effects associated with members’ behavior (i.e., in driving the trajectory’s exploration), there may be additional reasons for why the inclusion of particular types of contributors would affect the article’s quality.

Whereas our study revealed novel insights with regard to contributors’ behavior, key questions remain with regard to the underlying mental states and motivational mechanisms. Our study discusses contributors’ domain schemas (Dane 2010), as reflected by their regular activity patterns. Future research could pay closer attention to the underlying cognitive processes, studying, for example, how these schemas are shaped as part of the peer-production process or whether schemas that are based on expertise, dispositions, or worldviews have differential effects. Expertise-based schemas, for example, often lead to entrenchment (Dane 2010), suggesting that experts are likely to exert stronger pulls on the article’s trajectory.

In addition, our study discusses the negotiations between contributors, as they each try to align the focal article with their individual perspectives. We acknowledge that we have restricted our investigation to negotiations that are implicit (i.e., only manifested in changes to the artifact; Bolici et al. 2015), but we do recognize that this process may sometimes be accompanied by explicit coordination (i.e., communication and discussion in some online space; see Espinosa et al. 2004). This suggests that there is potential in exploring both processes simultaneously, possibly scaling up some of the approaches that were used in the qualitative study by Kane et al. (2014) and using them in conjunction with the computational framework developed in the present study, along the lines proposed by Lindberg (2020).

We recognize that negotiations may reflect a cooperative process, namely be conceived as a collaborative “mutual adjustment” (Mintzberg 1992), whereby different contributors provide distinct areas of expertise. Alternatively, negotiations may result from conflicts between of self-interested individuals (Arazy et al. 2011; Arazy et al. 2013; Halfaker et al. 2009; Kittur et al. 2007; Kittur and Kraut 2010; Schroeder and Wagner 2012; Thom-Santelli et al. 2009; Viegas et al. 2007), whereby conflicting parties attempt to each use “their” language in the contentious sections and the article’s trajectory is shaped in a conflictual way (Flöck et al. 2011; Kane et al. 2014; Stewart and Gosain 2006). We call for future research that would examine the relation between the nature of artifact-centric negotiations (i.e., cooperative versus conflictual) and the pattern by which the artifact’s trajectory explores the feature space. A particularly interesting line of investigation would be to study the linkages between individuals’ explorative or exploitative behaviors and the exploration manifested in the peer-produced digital artifact.

## Practical Implications

Our research carries several practical implications for administrators of wikis, as well as for organizations that want to partner with peer-production communities to harness them as sources of innovation. At a fundamental level, we argue for an artifact-centric approach for understanding peer-production and our results point to the importance of the article trajectory’s exploration as a key predictor of the article’s quality. The quality of peer-produced articles is important, and we recommend that online communities such as Wikipedia develop tools to track articles’ trajectories and continuously monitor the level of the trajectory’s exploration. The “statistical machinery” developed for this study could be employed by community administrators, or could serve any organization that is interested in tracking the evolution of its innovations over time.

Our findings also illustrate that it is difficult to compose groups for effective performance, since certain group compositions act to increase the trajectory’s exploration, which in turn has a curvilinear effect with article quality. Hence, a key challenge to custodians of online communities lies in determining the optimal mix of contributors. The importance of group composition raises an important question: Is there a way for community administrators to channel the participation of volunteers who self-select their level and mode of participation? This has been an ongoing challenge for online production communities. Ways to steer participation include lowering entry barriers (likely to increase the participation of unregistered members; see Halfaker et al. 2012), creating mechanisms for attracting committed contributors (e.g., notifications when the article needs improvements; see Choi et al. 2010), and developing task recommendation tools that can track contributors’ activities and identify relevant tasks for them to take on (Cosley et al. 2007; Zhang et al. 2014). Wikipedia has gone beyond this to form task specific groups— WikiProjects<sup>17</sup>—where participation is much more structured (Ren et al. 2015).

Finally, our findings have also implications for firms seeking to adopt principles of self-organizing collaborative production. Key principles from the community-based peerproduction model have recently begun spilling over into traditional organizations. Many companies use wikis as a knowledge management tool (Arazy and Croitoru 2010; Majchrzak et al. 2013), particularly for developing organizational encyclopedias and knowledge sharing tools (Arazy and Gellatly 2013; Arazy et al. 2015). Such companies attempt to adopt, at least in part, the organic processes that typify peerproduction (Brainin and Arazy 2016). In addition, some technology companies participate in open source software development (Fitzgerald 2006; Xiao et al. 2018) and other firms partner with peer-production communities to leverage them as innovation partners (Baden-Fuller and Haefliger 2013). While firms usually devote considerable thought and effort to the design of traditional teams, very little, if any, thought has been given to orchestrating the composition of the “crowd.” Our findings imply that these firms need to think carefully about the types of contributors they invite to participate and the crowd-based teams they assemble.

## Boundary Conditions and Limitations

First, our research is naturally limited by the fact that we have investigated a particular form of peer-production—wiki-type systems—and that within the scope of this type of peerproduction we only looked at Wikipedia and Wikia. Granted, Wikipedia in itself is a relevant context for scholarly investigation (Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015; Kane et al. 2014; Ransbotham and Kane 2011). By studying two very different communities (Wikipedia, a not-for-profit endeavor with very broad coverage, and Wikia, a more restricted fan-based forprofit initiative), we can expect that our findings will generalize to other wiki-based knowledge collaboration systems, and perhaps more broadly to other forms of peer-production, such as crowd science (Franzoni and Sauermann 2014).

Second, whereas our word-based operationalization of the feature space employs a standard bag-of-words representation (Feldman and Sanger 2007), we acknowledge that the feature space could be represented through more sophisticated text processing methods and more advanced knowledge representations. Thus, in all likelihood, the patterns observed here serve as a lower bound for the true effects, which could potentially be observed by using more sophisticated representations.

Third, our focus has been on elucidating the nomological network of the artifact’s trajectory, both in terms of its antecedents and consequents. This means that our modeling has focused more on explaining multiple relationships across a complex set of variables, as is common in social science research (Imai et al. 2010), rather than focusing on a single relationship with the overriding goal of establishing a causal relationship, as is common in econometrics. Future research could attempt to isolate particular relationships, such as that between the artifact trajectory’s exploration and artifact quality, and focus only on estimating the causal relationship between these variables, utilizing techniques such as the instrumental variable approach (Garen 1984).

## Conclusion

While the evolution of digital knowledge artifacts is emergent, it is not without structure. Being able to record artifacts evolutionary trajectories and shed light on the factors that influence the trajectory’s arc are of paramount importance for our understanding of how digital artifacts evolve. Our study highlights the importance of contributors’ status with the focal article group and in the broader community and illustrates how group members’ negotiations drive the trajectory’s arc. Furthermore, our findings emphasize the mediating role of the trajectory’s exploration in driving artifact quality. Our research, therefore, sets us on a path toward greater understanding of how the decisions of individuals collectively shape the peer-produced digital artifact, thus influencing the artifact’s quality. We hope that our artifact-centric approach, our proposed methodology, our findings, and the future research directions we have pointed to will open the door for further inquiries into the evolutionary trajectories of digital artifacts.

## Appendix B

## Mathematical Definition of an Article’s Trajectory and its Exploration

In this appendix, we mathematically define and illustrate the state of an article and its trajectory, as well as the extent to which the trajectory explores the feature space (i.e., exploration). For illustration, we use a simple, made-up trajectory.

The state of an article is computed using the following linguistic approach. We first applied standard pre-processing steps: removing stopwords (common words such as it, the, as, etc.), punctuation, numbers, and white spaces; converting uppercase letters into lowercase, as well as stemming (Baeza-Yates and Ribeiro-Neto 1999). Next, we computed the bag-of-words (Feldman and Sanger 2007) representation of each version í<sup>i</sup> using a binary vector of length N indicating presence (1) or absence (0) of each word, where N is the size of the dictionary. Then, we projected the N-dimensional representations of each article version í<sup>i</sup> onto a two-dimensional vector $\boldsymbol s ^ { i } = ( x _ { i } , y _ { i } )$ , for $i = 0 , . . . , R ,$ where $x _ { i }$ is the Hamming distance (Hamming 1950)<sup>18</sup> between version $ { \boldsymbol \nu } ^ { i }$ and the initial version $ { \boldsymbol \nu } ^ { 0 }$ (that $\mathrm { i s } , x = h ( \nu ^ { 0 } , \nu ^ { i } ) )$ and $y _ { i }$ is the Hamming distance between version $\nu ^ { i }$ and the most recent version $\nu ^ { R }$ (that is, $y _ { i } = h ( \nu ^ { i } , \nu ^ { j } ) )$ . The Hamming distance between two versions, $ { \boldsymbol \nu } ^ { i }$ and $\nu _ { , } ^ { j }$ which we denote as $h ( \nu ^ { i } , \nu ^ { j } ) .$ , sums the number of terms that appear in í<sup>i</sup> but not in í<sup>j</sup>, and the number of terms that appear in í<sup>j</sup> but not in í<sup>i</sup>. It is possible to show a few properties of the two-dimensional trajectory: (1) $x _ { i }$ is equal to the number of terms in version $\nu ^ { i } ; ( 2 )$ if l is the number of terms in the most recent version $\nu ^ { R } ,$ , then $s ^ { i } = ( 0 , l )$ and $s ^ { R } = ( l , 0 )$ . In order to illustrate our ideas, we provide a simple example for the trajectory of a wiki article in Figure B1.

In this example, the most recent version of the wiki article, $\nu ^ { R } ,$ contains 11 terms (l - 11). Thus, $s ^ { 0 } = ( 0 , 1 1 )$ and $s ^ { R } = ( 1 1 , 0 )$ . The first editing session $\nu ^ { 0 } \to \nu ^ { 1 }$ consists of the addition of four terms (smoking, main, cause, cancer), which implies $x _ { 1 } = 4$ . However, since two of these terms (smoking and cancer) are present in $\nu ^ { R }$ and two (main and cause) are not, the Hamming distance $h ( \nu ^ { 1 } , \nu ^ { R } )$ is equal to 11, which implies $y _ { 1 } =$ 11. The second editing session $\nu ^ { 1 } \to \nu ^ { 2 }$ consists of the addition of three terms (children, die, early), which implies $x _ { 2 } = 4 + 3 = 7$ . However, none of these terms are present in $\nu ^ { R }$ , and so the Hamming distance $h ( \nu ^ { 2 } , \nu ^ { R } )$ is equal to $1 1 + 3 = 1 4$ , which implies $y _ { 2 } = 1 4$

Using our operationalization of a trajectory, it is straightforward to interpret the directions toward which the article moves. South-East movement entails the addition of words that will be present in the current version $s ^ { F } ;$ by contrast, North-West progress consists of the removal of words that will be present in the current version $s ^ { F } .$ South-West movement consists of the removal of words that will not be present in $s ^ { F } ;$ by contrast, North-East progress entails the addition of words that will not be present in $s ^ { F } .$ Interpretations of the North, East, West, and South directions represent combinations of the above. For example, Eastward movement $( \mathbf { e } . \mathbf { g } . , s ^ { 5 } \to s ^ { 6 }$ in Figure B1) consists of the addition of words, half of which will be present in $s ^ { F } .$

The trajectory’s exploration $E x _ { i }$ at a particular version s<sup>i</sup> is the distance between a version $s ^ { i } ( i = 1 , . . . , R )$ and the straight line that connects $s ^ { 0 }$ and $s ^ { R } .$ Let $u _ { i }$ be the vector from $s ^ { 0 } ~ { \bf t o } ~ s ^ { i } ( \mathrm { i . e . , } u _ { i } = s ^ { i } - s ^ { 0 } )$ , and í the vector from $s ^ { 0 } ~ \mathbf { t o } ~ s ^ { R } \left( \mathrm { i } . \mathbf { e } . , \nu = s ^ { R } - ~ s ^ { 0 } \right)$ , then the trajectory’s exploration of version i can be computed as

$$

## Appendix C

## Removal of Vandalism

One of the unintended consequences of Wikipedia’s open peer-production model is that artifacts are subject to vandalism (e.g., adding irrelevant content, removal of relevant information, insertion of links to viruses). Given our interest in constructive forms of community-based peer production, we sought to omit changes to the article’s trajectory resulting from destructive manipulations. Often, vandalism entails the deletion of the entire article’s content, bringing the article’s trajectory to its original (empty) version. However, is some cases vandalism entails the addition of entirely irrelevant text, and when such changes are sufficiently large, they are detectable. In our feature space conceptualization, vandalism temporarily pushes the article away from its current trajectory (and to a region that is rarely visited again). The community’s effective vandal-fighting processes ensure that soon after the vandalism has occurred, the content is corrected and the article returns to its natural trajectory. Our approach for detecting and removing vandalism is as follows. We generated a “smoothed” trajectory in the 2D space $\left( \tilde { x } ^ { i } , \tilde { y } ^ { i } \right) _ { i = 0 , \ldots , R }$ by computing for each version $\left( x ^ { i } , y ^ { i } \right) _ { i = 0 , . . . , R }$ the median of its 20 neighbors:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \tilde {x} ^ {i} = \text { median } \left(x ^ {i - 1 0}, \dots , x ^ {i - 1}, x ^ {i + 1}, \dots , x ^ {i + 1 0}\right) \\ \tilde {y} ^ {i} = \text { median } \left(y ^ {i - 1 0}, \dots , y ^ {i - 1}, y ^ {i + 1}, \dots , y ^ {i + 1 0}\right) \end{array}
$$

such that a large distance d between a smoothed version $\left( \tilde { x } ^ { i } , \tilde { y } ^ { i } \right)$ and the original version $\left( \boldsymbol { x } ^ { i } , \boldsymbol { y } ^ { i } \right)$ suggests that $\left( \boldsymbol { x } ^ { i } , \boldsymbol { y } ^ { i } \right)$ is likely to result from vandalism. Our procedure removed the 3% of versions of the original trajectory with the largest $d _ { i } .$ The remaining points of the original trajectory are employed in all subsequent analyses. It is important to stress that our procedure does NOT modify the original trajectory; in fact, the smoothed trajectory $\left( \tilde { \boldsymbol { x } } ^ { i } , \tilde { \boldsymbol { y } } ^ { i } \right) _ { i = 0 , . . . , R }$ is built for the only purpose of identifying the indices of those revisions that result from vandalism.

These revisions are removed from the original trajectory, which is then used for all of our analyses. Figure C1 provides an example of The Hobbit Wikipedia article’s trajectory before and after the removal of vandalism.

## Random Walk Experiment

In order to show that the trajectories of wiki articles in our sample are not random, we sought to compare an empirical trajectory against the trajectory that is produced through a random process (i.e., a random walk). We aim to demonstrate the trajectories of actual peer-produced articles and articles containing a random collection of words (and thus are of low quality) differ in terms of the level of exploration exhibited by their trajectories.

Our experiments concerning the trajectory’s random walk focus on the article that was used as our running example—Wikipedia’s The Hobbit article. We analyze the trajectory composed of the first 1,000 revisions of this article and compare it to a set of 100 random-walk trajectories made of 1,000 revisions. Before illustrating the details of the procedure that we developed to generate a random-walk trajectory, it is important to note that we sought to “level the playing field” by aligning to the maximum extent possible the random and empirical trajectories (except, of course, for the choice of words that are added/deleted in each revision), so that all exogenous factors are controlled for. In particular, we made sure that the final revision of a random-walk trajectory has the same number of words (some may be repeated) as the final revision of the actual The Hobbit article; otherwise, the scale of all of our metrics would be so different that a comparison between them would make little sense.

Our procedure starts by analyzing the first 1,000 revisions of the Hobbit article, with the goal of collecting the set of pairs of the number of words added $\mathcal { Q } = ( a d d _ { i } , r e m _ { i } ) _ { i = 1 , \dots , 1 0 0 0 }$ and removed (add ) in the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ revision; note that by “number of words” we indicate the total count of words, and by “distinct number of words,” we indicate the number of unique words. Obviously, the number of words present in the final revision is equal to

$$
f i n a l \_ n = \sum_ {i = 1, \dots , 1 0 0 0} a d d _ {i} - \sum_ {i = 1, \dots , 1 0 0 0} r e m _ {i}
$$

In The Hobbit article, the final version has $= 2 { , } 9 4 8$ words (1,567 distinct words).

A random walk was simulated by starting from an empty article, and then generating 1,000 random revisions, which results in a trajectory of 1,000 versions. To generate a random revision, we sample without replacement a pair $( a d d _ { k } , r e m _ { k } )$ from set Q, which corresponds to making a revision that adds add words and removes rem words. This procedure guarantees that after 1000 revisions, we obtain an article with exactly final\_n words (i.e., the same number of words as in The Hobbit article). The words added at each revision were sampled from the dictionary of The Hobbit article (i.e., from the set of words used in the first 1,000 revisions of The Hobbit); the deleted words at each version were randomly selected from the words present in an article’s current version. If the number of removed words rem was larger than the number of words in the current version of the article, then the revision was deemed infeasible. In this case, the pair (add , rem ) was added back to the set Q and another pair $( a d d _ { k ^ { \prime } } , r e m _ { k ^ { \prime } } )$ was randomly sampled; this resampling was repeated until a feasible revision was obtained. When a feasible revision was performed we updated the current version of the article so as to keep track of how many instances of each word were present in the article at that version.

The differences in coordinates of the starting and ending points between the actual and random trajectories are due to the fact that while both trajectories resulted in a revision with 2,948 words, the actual article (left) had 1,567 distinct words, while the random trajectory (depicted on the right) had 1,813 distinct words. Random-walk trajectories do not always result in a higher number of distinct words; in our experiments, the random-walk trajectories ended with an average of 1,551.6 distinct words.

## Appendix E

## Calculating the Edit-War Variable

A Wikipedia edit-war describes cases when editors who disagree about the content of a page repeatedly override each other's contributions.<sup>19</sup> Wikipedia’s three-revert rule (3RR) specifies that an editor must not perform more than three reverts (i.e., undo the action of another editor) on a single page within a 24-hour period.<sup>20</sup> A typical edit-war involves a situation where two contributors revert each other’s edits three times, resulting in the article trajectory moving back and forth between two different states (A ÷ B ÷ A ÷ B ÷ A). However, in reality edit wars are more nuanced. Consider a situation where one contributor adds ten words and another contributor deletes nine of these newly-added words. Such a case reflects a content-related dispute, yet it does not fit the traditional definition of an edit war, because the second contributor’s deletion does not entail a “perfect” revert.

Proposing a gradated metric for content-related disputes, we develop a metric that captures situations of “partial edit war,” by measuring the degree to which a particular article revision reflects an edit war. We begin the calculation by identifying the contributors responsible for each editing sessions, so as to avoid labeling actions made by the same contributor as an “edit war.” Thus, for a series of revisions by the same contributor we retain only the last edit of each “revision session” (i.e., a group of consecutive edits made by the same contributor).

We begin by calculating the “revert score” of each revision, measuring the degree to which a revision resemble a revert of its preceding revision. The “revert score” variable is calculated in a way that the last revision of a perfect revert session (i.e., version A ÷ version B ÷ version A) is labeled as a 1; by contrast, when a revision is aligned with the direction of its preceding revision, that revision is labeled as $0 ;$ all other situations result in a revert score between 0 and 1.

Mathematically, we compute the revert score of version i by considering the 2-dimensional projections of the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ version, $s ^ { i } ,$ and of the two previous versions $s ^ { i - 2 }$ and $s ^ { i - 1 }$ :

$$
\text { revert\_score } (i) = 1 - \frac {\left\| s ^ {i} - s ^ {i - 2} \right\|}{\left\| s ^ {i} - s ^ {i - 1} \right\| + \left\| s ^ {i - 1} - s ^ {i - 2} \right\|}
$$

where ||@|| represents the Euclidean norm of a vector. From the formula, it is clear that in case of complete revert $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , when $s ^ { i } = s ^ { i - 2 } )$ the revert score is 1, whereas in case of a movement along the same direction $( { \mathrm { i . e . } }$ , when $\left\| s ^ { i } - s ^ { i - 2 } \right\| = \left\| s ^ { i } - s ^ { i - 1 } \right\| + \left\| s ^ { i - 1 } - s ^ { i - 2 } \right\| )$ , the revert score is 0.

After assigning a revert score to all revisions of an article, we compute an edit-war metric for a particular revision, $w ( i )$ , as the average revert score of the latest three revisions:

$$
w (i) = \frac {\text { revert\_score } (i - 2) + \text { revert\_score } (i - 1) + \text { revert\_score } (i)}{3}
$$

We chose to average the last three values to keep consistent with Wikipedia’s definition of the three-revert rule $\therefore ^ { 2 1 }$ Note that $w ( i )$ is equal to 1 if there are three consecutive perfect reverts $( A \to B \to A \to B \to A )$ , 0 if the last three edits follow the same direction $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ ., they all add words without removing any), and a value between 0 and 1 otherwise

Finally, our edit war variable, $e w ( i )$ captures the amount of edit war up to revision $^ { , i , }$ and is calculated as the average value of the three edit-war indicators up to revision i. That is,

$$
e w (i) = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {i} \frac {w (j)}{i}
$$

## Appendix H

## Empirical Evidence of Contributors’ “Pulling” Behaviors

The goal of this analysis is to determine whether contributors to wikis tend to “pull” the article trajectories toward the region of the word-based feature space where they concentrate their edits. The feature space is defined in terms of the dictionary (i.e., the collection of all words) used in all articles in our dataset. Through their editing history, contributors regularly concentrate their activity within a region in that word-space, defined in terms of the words they have frequently added/deleted (across all articles in the relevant online community). By pulling the focal article’s trajectory, we refer to additions/deletions of content that move that trajectory closer to those regions in the feature space that reflect a contributor’s pattern of regular activity.

Given a focal article, our methodology lists all past contributors to that article and then builds two vector representations for each contributor; the first describing the region in the feature space where they are regularly active (based on past activity outside the focal article) and the second describing the contributor-generated trajectory movements within the focal article. We aim to show that the trajectory movements of a particular contributor within a focal article are better aligned with their region of regular activity in the word-space, compared to the alignment with other contributors’ regions of regular activity.

In particular, we employed the following procedure:

• List all contributors that have ever edited the focal article. For each contributor, we keep track of the contributor’s “entry date,” which is the timestamp of their first edit in the focal article.

• Employ this timestamp to retrieve all of the contributor’s edits outside the focal article (across all articles in the relevant community, either Wikipedia or Wikia) that were made before the contributor started editing the focal article. To make this process manageable computationally, contributors who have edited more than 1,000 articles are assumed to be software robots (“bots”) and were excluded from this analysis. To determine changes made to the article, for each revision of a contributor, we also retrieve the article’s prior revision, so that we can detect which words were added and/or removed. Altogether, we retrieved 2,637,435 edits (2,131,500 in Wikipedia and 505,935 in Wikia) across a total of 1,288,075 articles (1,037,061 in Wikipedia and 251,014 in Wikia). Due to the large number of article revisions to analyze, our procedure is computationally demanding. To be able to execute the test on all articles of the dataset, we limited our analysis to the contributors with at least two edits on the focal article (and performed a sensitivity analysis to verify that this design choice did not impact the pattern of results; see below).

• Construct the word-based feature space. Use the set of all the collected revisions to construct a bag-of-word representation of all content in these revisions. We use standard natural language processing procedures by eliminating stop words and stemming terms (Baeza-Yates and Ribeiro-Neto 1999) (we use the default English stemmer available in the Python natural language toolkit, NLTK). For computational reasons, we retain only the most frequent 100,000 terms in the dictionary.

• Generate a vector representation of each “edit” based on the bag-of-words representation of each revision. Formally, the vector representation, $, r _ { i } ,$ of the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ version indicates which words are present in a certain version; our vector representation, $e _ { i } ,$ of the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ edit has the purpose of indicating which words are added and which removed between two versions $r _ { i - 1 }$ and $r _ { i } .$ Let N be dictionary size; then, the edit e is represented with a binary vector of length 2N, where the first components have 1’s in those positions corresponding to words that were added, while the last N components have 1’s in those positions corresponding to words that were removed between versions $r _ { i - 1 }$ and $r _ { i } .$ In designing our representation, we sought to capture cases where a particular term was both added and deleted within a single edit (this is not possible when using the more succinct vector representation obtained by defining $e _ { i }$ as $r _ { i } - r _ { i - 1 } )$ .

• By analyzing the content of the contributor’s edits across all non-focal articles, we create the vector describing the contributor’s region of regular activity within the word-space (the average of all their edits’ vectors across non-focal articles).

• Collect each contributor’s revisions within the focal article, to generate the vector representing their trajectory movements (the average of all their edits’ vectors within the focal article).

• Use the Cosine metric to calculate the similarity of the target contributor’s focal article movements and the feature space region of their regular activity; we refer to this value as intra-contributor similarity. Along the same lines, we calculate the cosine metric of all contributors trajectory movements (within that same focal article) and the other contributors’ regions of regular activity; we refer to these as inter contributor similarity. To establish that contributors tend to pull the article’s trajectory toward the region of their regular activity, for each article in our dataset, we perform a t-test comparing inter- and intra-contributor Cosine values.

The results of these tests show that the intra-contributor similarity is significantly higher than the inter-contributor similarity, thus supporting our argument around contributors’ pulling behavior. In particular, out of the 121 Wikipedia articles, in 117 case intra-contributor similarity was significantly higher than the inter-contributor similarity (p < 0.01), and 4 cases did not pass the statistical significance threshold (Evil Queen (Disney), Mara Jade, Monsters Inc., and Purple Man). Out of the 121 Wikia articles, 107 tests passed the statistical significance threshold (p < 0.01). Our analysis shows that the few cases (7% of our sample) where the evidence for pulling behavior was statistically insignificant the number of contributors was small (note that this value indicated the number of editors with at least two edits in the focal article that have also edited at least another article prior to their activity in the focal article).

In addition, we performed a sensitivity analysis in order to verify that our choice to restrict the analysis to those contributors with at least two edits on the focal article. Our analysis of a subset of 18 Wikipedia articles shows that the results are almost identical (all significant effects in our primary analysis remain statistically significant in the sensitivity analysis).

## Appendix I

## Empirical Evidence of Central and Fringe Regions in the Feature Space

This appendix articulates and illustrates how some regions of the cumulative feature space are “central”, meaning that they represent regions commonly traversed by article trajectories, whereas other regions of the feature space are at the fringe, meaning that they are less frequently visited. Once we have established central and fringe regions in the feature space, we will show that registered members are more likely to concentrate their activity in central regions, when compared with the regions in the feature space where unregistered Wikipedia editors tend to concentrate their activity.

The first step of the analysis consists of identifying the feature space’s central regions. To this end, we retrieved all versions of all articles in the dataset and constructed a frequency vector of length N for the all versions of the entire set of articles, where N is the number of words in the dictionary (for computational reasons, N is capped at 100,000 as described in Appendix E). The central-region vector, $\rho ,$ was constructed by computing the relative frequency of occurrence of each term across all versions in the entire set of articles.

In order to assess whether one’s region of regular activity (reflecting their work experiences across all articles) is closer to the feature space’s central region or fringe, we retrieved the edits of each contributor across all articles. Each edit made on any article in the dataset can be represented by an “edit vector” of length $N ;$ the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ component of an edit vector can have a value of -1, 0, or +1 depending on whether the -th term was added (+1), removed (-1), or neither added nor removed (0) in that particular edit. Given all edit vectors of a certain contributor $u ,$ we construct the contributor’s “region vector” d(u) by averaging their edit vectors. In the contributor’s region vector, the -th component is a fractional number between -1 and +1, which represents the net proportion of the contributor’s edits where the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ term was added (if between 0 and +1) or removed (if between 0 and -1). A contributor’s region vector can therefore be interpreted as the region in the feature space where they tend to concentrate their activity.

We conjecture that registered Wikipedia members are those that tend to be active in the feature space’s central regions, that is registered members’ “region vectors” tend to be similar to the central-region vector $\rho .$ Conversely, non-registered members tend to concentrate their activity in non-central (or fringe) regions, that is their “region vector” tends to differ from the central-region vector ñ. We test this conjecture as follows. First, for each contributor u, we compute the cosine similarity between $D ( u )$ and $\rho .$ Then, we execute a t-test to check whether the average similarity among the registered members is larger than the average similarity among the non-registered contributors.

Our results show that on our sample of articles the average similarity between d(u) and ñ across all focal articles was 0.048 for registered members and 0.027 for non-registered members; these differences were statistically significant (p value < 10E-9). Thus, the results of our tests indicate that the regions of registered community members’ regular activity are significantly more aligned with the feature space’s central region, when compared to the regions reflecting non-registered members’ regular activity.
