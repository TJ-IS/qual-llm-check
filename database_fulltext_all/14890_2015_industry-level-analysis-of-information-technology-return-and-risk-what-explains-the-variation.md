# Industry-Level Analysis of Information Technology Return and Risk: What Explains the Variation?

FEI REN AND SANJEEV DEWAN

FEI REN is an associate professor of information systems at Guanghua School of Management, Peking University, China. She received her Ph.D. from the Paul Merage School of Business at the University of California, Irvine. Her research interests focus on the business value of information technology, information technology and business strategy, and electronic commerce. Her research articles have appeared in Information Systems Research and other journals and in conference proceedings.

SANJEEV DEWAN is professor of information systems at the Paul Merage School of Business at the University of California, Irvine. His research and teaching interests focus on the economics of information technology and on electronic commerce. He has served as a senior editor at Information Systems Research and as an associate editor at Management Science. He received his Ph.D. in business administration from the Simon School at the University of Rochester.

ABSTRACT: Motivated by the wide dispersion in the returns on the use of information technology (IT) across industries, we conduct an industry-level examination of IT return and risk, focusing on the moderating role of industry competition, regulation, and technological change. We address the following research questions: What is the impact of IT investment on the return and risk dimensions of industry financial performance? How do industry characteristics moderate the relationship between IT investment and industry performance? Our analysis of these questions finds that higher levels of industry competition are associated with higher IT productivity (contribution of IT to value-added output), lower IT profitability (contribution of IT to industry average return on assets [ROA]), and higher IT risk (contribution of IT to ex ante variability of ROA). This is consistent with the notion that competition induces riskier IT investments, despite the fact that returns tend to be competed away. Higher levels of industry regulation are associated with lower IT returns in both productivity and profitability, but also lower IT risk. Finally, a higher rate of technological change induces both higher IT returns and higher IT risk. A variety of tests indicate that our results are robust. Our results shed light on factors that drive variation in IT performance across industries, and provide useful industry-level performance benchmarks of the return and risk impacts of IT investments.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: competition, industry-level analysis, information technology investment, IT return and risk, IT value, productivity, profitability, regulation, technological change.

There is a large and growing literature on the business value of information technology (IT) investments, the majority of which is focused at the firm level, with little attention given to industry differences in IT performance. This is surprising, given the documented evidence that industry characteristics explain about 75 percent of the variation in industry average accounting rates of return [61], combined with the substantial variation in IT returns across industry sectors [51].<sup>1</sup> Accordingly, in this study we examine the impact of IT capital investments at an industry level of analysis, focusing on the role of competition, regulation, and rate of technological change—which have been highlighted in prior research as being important determinants of capital investment and industry performance [12, 52]. Whereas prior research on the business value of IT essentially treats industry characteristics as control variables [8, 10, 27], in this research we seek a comprehensive understanding of how industry characteristics affect IT performance. Our central research questions are: What is the impact of IT investment on industry productivity, profitability, and risk? And how do industry characteristics moderate the relationship between IT investment and industry performance?

It is worth noting that industry effects can be studied in a firm-level specification, as has been done to varying extents in previous studies, including Brynjolffson and Hitt [10], Lichtenberg [50], and Dewan et al. [27]. Indeed, a key benefit of the firmlevel approach is that changes in quality and variety of output can be discerned in micro firm-level data that are masked at higher levels of aggregation. However, the firm-level data sets employed in prior research are typically skewed toward the largest publicly traded firms in the economy (usually the Fortune 1000 firms), and there is uneven coverage of the range of industries in the economy. Further, prior research has documented spillover effects, so that private returns deviate from social returns [13, 15, 67]. It has also been pointed out that the measurement of spillover effects is confounded with measurement errors of inputs and outputs [67]. These challenges are mitigated at the aggregate industry level of analysis, where the data come from reliable sources such as the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) and the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) that have been thoroughly vetted and are widely used by other researchers, and the measured returns better reflect economy-wide social returns from IT investments. We also note that there is value in conducting IT performance studies at different levels of analysis (for example, the country level analysis of IT productivity by Dewan and Kraemer [23]), in order to establish broad robustness of the central empirical findings.

Some recent studies have started looking at IT value at the industry level, answering calls by Chiasson and Davidson [16] and Kauffman [47] for more studies of the macroeconomic impacts of IT. Specifically, Han et al. [39] and Cheng and Nault [15] study industry IT spillovers and find significant IT spillover effects from both the supplier [39] and customer [15] sides. Qu et al. [56] look at how industry concentration and other characteristics affect the use of IT outsourcing. In an effort to further fill the gap, we conduct an industry-level analysis of the return and risk of IT investments, focusing on the role of industry competition, regulation, and technological change. These three industry factors are well-studied in the industrial organization literature [60, 62], and are posited to be significant drivers of IT performance [53], yet they have not been explicitly investigated in the IT value literature.

This study contributes to the IT value literature in a number of ways. First, this is the first attempt to empirically measure the drivers of industry-level IT value. While some prior research has provided conceptual strategic analyses [19, 20] or singleindustry empirical results (e.g., Duliba et al. [29] for the airline industry; and Banker and Kauffman [4] for the banking industry), none of them has provided a comprehensive empirical analysis as we do. Second, this study investigates multiple facets of industry structure (competition, regulation, and technological change) and multiple dimensions of IT performance (productivity, profitability, and risk). It builds a theoretical base to understand how those industry characteristics shape industry IT return and risk. This goes well beyond the scope of prior studies that have more narrowly focused on the impact of competition on either productivity or profitability, using either an analytical [6, 57, 70] or empirical approach [45, 52]. Third, this study contributes industry-level evidence to the emerging IT risk literature (starting with Tanriverdi and Ruefli [68]), which seeks to explain variation in IT returns based in IT risk considerations [25, 26, 27].

Turning to our results, we find that a higher level of competition is positively associated with IT productivity. However, the potential rents from increased IT productivity are competed away, so that the productivity gains do not translate into profitability enhancements, echoing some prior findings [41, 64]. A new result, due to our focus on both IT risk and return, is that higher levels of competition induce investments associated with higher IT risk. Thus, competition induces riskier IT investments, despite the fact that the returns tend to be competed away. For industry regulation, we find that industries under a highly regulated environment are associated with lower IT returns (in both productivity and profitability), but also lower IT risk. A higher rate of industry technological change induces higher IT returns (in both productivity and profitability) and higher IT risk, consistent with the analysis of industry clockspeed by Mendelson and Ziegler [54]. Our results are robust to different estimation methods and specifications, and together provide useful industry-level benchmarks that can serve as a starting point for individual firms and managers seeking to quantify the return and risk implications of their IT investments.

## Theory and Hypotheses

Our overall research framework, illustrated in Figure 1, captures the impact of IT investment on industry performance, and how that impact is moderated by various industry characteristics. We consider two measures of return, along with risk. The measures of IT return are productivity and profitability, corresponding to two distinct streams of the literature: (1) studies looking at the contributions of IT to the output or productivity, using both empirical [10, 23, 24, 50] and analytical approaches [57, 70]; and (2) studies focusing on the role of IT in increasing profitability, most of which are empirical in nature [8, 11, 17, 27, 28], with Barua et al. [6] being a notable exception.

Hitt and Brynjolfsson [41] provide a useful discussion of how productivity and profitability can be related to one another in the process of economic value generation. Briefly, productivity improvement leads to value creation in the industry, due to the ability to create more output from a given level of factor inputs. Part of this new value is retained by the IT-investing industry itself (producer surplus), with the rest passing through to consumers (consumer surplus). Thus, the extent of value capture by an industry determines its profitability, which in turn depends on the level of industry competition and government regulation, as also discussed in Davern and Kauffman [22] and Chircu and Kauffman [17]. Accordingly, we examine how industry characteristics shape multiple dimensions of IT risk and return performance, as described in further detail below.

## IT Performance Measures

As discussed above we consider two measures of return, IT productivity and IT profitability, along with IT risk. IT productivity refers to the marginal contribution of IT capital investment to the output of the average industry. Technically, IT productivity is characterized by the marginal product of IT, or the increase in dollar output for an additional dollar of IT capital investment at the margin, and can be interpreted as a rate of return before costs of investment are subtracted [41].

IT profitability is a more nuanced performance measure that is related to a number of other “strategic value” concepts studied in prior literature, including competitive advantage and strategic differentiation [19, 20, 21], market share and operating profits [29], stock returns or market value [28, 41], intangible value or Tobin’s q [8], and executives’ perceptions of business value [66], among others. Our approach is based on an accounting perspective, wherein IT profitability is measured in terms of the marginal contribution of IT capital to average industry return on assets (ROA). This measure is widely accepted in prior research [2, 41, 58], and reflects the other drivers of profitability discussed above (such as competitive advantage or intangible value).

Finally, the IT risk performance measure captures the contribution of IT capital to the ex ante uncertainty of industry financial returns. In the accounting literature, financial risk is typically characterized by the variability of earnings such as ROA. In our case, we aggregate firm risk measures up to the industry level, and IT risk is imputed as the coefficient on IT capital in the regression of industry risk on IT capital (see Equation [3] below), analogous to the process of measuring IT risk at the firm level by Dewan et al. [27].

In the following subsections, we theoretically analyze how each of the three industry characteristics moderates the impact of IT investments on industrial productivity, profitability, and risk.

## Competition

In industrial organization theory, the level of competition in an industry reflects the extent of market power enjoyed by individual firms, which in turn depends on factors such as seller concentration, barriers to entry, and product differentiation [12, 60]. We posit that competition leads not only to more efficient use of IT but also to an increased level of IT investment, to increase the efficiency of other labor and capital inputs. This idea is supported by the X-efficiency theory of Liebenstein [49], according to which firms react to increased competitive intensity by trying to increase the productivity of factor inputs. This theory emphasizes the significance of nonallocative factors such as motivation and incentive in determining production efficiency. Competition creates pressure for firms to improve X-efficiency, while the lack of competition leads to slack and other inefficiencies in the organization that increase costs. Under competitive pressure, firms also adopt new and advanced information technologies, seeking new efficiencies [31, 42].

Another mechanism by which competition affects productivity is through increased innovation. Specifically, increased competitive intensity forces firms to increase the pace of innovation, seeking new opportunities for revenue growth and/ or cost savings. Indeed, the McKinsey study cited earlier [51] found the pace of innovation to be the key differentiator between leading and lagging industries in terms of productivity growth. Today, innovation is often driven by new IT investments, fueled by the sustained performance/price improvements in computing technologies [32, 44].

Turning to the impact of competition on IT profitability, theoretically there are two competing effects that work in opposing directions. First, the enhanced IT productivity increases the pool of value available for capture, which would tend to raise IT profitability. However, a higher level of industry competition causes more of the value to be competed away and transferred down the value chain to consumers. The net effect on IT profitability depends on which of these two effects dominates. Prior research has shown that for certain industries such as the financial industry, IT creates value but the gains can quickly evaporate due to hypercompetition [45, 64]. Although the moderating effect of competition on IT profitability is ambiguous on theoretical grounds, we posit a net negative effect due to the composition of our data set, wherein highly competitive industries outnumber low-competition industries by a ratio of more than six to one (see Table 1).

Turning to IT risk under competition, we believe payoffs from IT investments are more uncertain in industries with higher levels of competition. First, the added IT investment due to competitive forces tends to be less efficient on the return-risk dimension; that is, the marginal dollars of investment under increased competition will tend to offer lower return at higher risk, relative to a lower level of competition. Further, new IT innovations adopted due to competition are subject to both technological uncertainty and market uncertainty. For example, the adoption of enterprise resource planning systems and customer relationship management systems can help firms with external and internal coordination [9, 43], but their complexity and broad scope entail new sources of risk, as compared to more narrowly focused technologies [65].

The above arguments lead to the following hypotheses regarding the role of industry competition in IT return-risk performance:

Hypothesis H1a (Competition─Productivity Link): Higher industry competitiveness is associated with higher IT productivity.

Hypothesis H1b (Competition─Profitability Link): Higher industry competitiveness is associated with lower IT profitability.

Hypothesis H1c (Competition─Risk Link): Higher industry competitiveness is associated with higher IT risk.

## Regulation

We focus on regulatory effects that influence capital investments because they are most germane to our analysis. In this regard, an influential study by Averch and Johnson [3] states that firms under rate-of-return constraints (e.g., via price control) tend to overinvest in capital at the expense of other inputs, resulting in higher costs per unit and inefficient production. The resulting overinvestment in IT capital would drive down IT marginal product, due to diminishing returns. It has also been noted that regulation tends to shift capital investments away from productive technology investments, therefore adversely affecting productivity [35]. This “crowding out” effect reduces the productivity of capital investments, including those in IT. Overall, we posit a negative association between regulation and IT productivity.

Industry regulation has also been recognized as a significant factor that affects IT value capture and competitive advantage [71]. Regulation retards innovation adoption and diffusion, as suggested by Schmalensee and Willig [62] and Hall [38]. A number of studies have examined the negative impact of regulation on profitability in specific industries, including Shepherd [63] for telecommunications and Joskow [46] for health care. In a similar vein, we expect that regulation will tend to curtail innovative IT investments that could be sources of differentiation and competitive advantage, even as there is overinvestment in generic IT as a whole.

With respect to risk, we expect industry regulation to decrease IT risk. Indeed, the goal of regulatory policies to reduce the chances of market failure also serves to reduce the risk of IT capital investments. In addition, firms under regulation tend to avoid investments with uncertain payoffs. Specifically, regulations tend to restrict timing flexibility and technology choice [37, 69], so that firms tend to delay or defer irreversible investments under uncertainty, which tends to disproportionately affect IT capital investments [27]. The corresponding shift toward investments with less uncertain payoffs suggests that higher levels of regulation are associated with reduced IT risk.

Overall, we posit the following set of hypotheses for the moderating role of industry regulation:

Hypothesis H2a (Regulation─Productivity Link): Higher industry regulation is associated with lower IT productivity.

Hypothesis H2b (Regulation─Profitability Link): Higher industry regulation is associated with lower IT profitability.

Hypothesis H2c (Regulation─Risk Link): Higher industry regulation is associated with lower IT risk.

## Technological Change

Technological change refers to the introduction of better production methods and the creation of new and superior products and services [60]. It generally calls for investment in new equipment and structures to embody the latest technology into the production process—so-called investment-specific technological change, which accounts for the majority of postwar U.S. economic growth [36]. Over the past few decades, IT-enabled innovations have been the key drivers of technological change, resulting not only in a higher level of IT investments but also in a greater contribution of IT to total factor productivity [10, 24].

IT-enabled technological change within an industry can also help create competitive advantage, building barriers to entry and increasing bargaining power over suppliers and customers—resulting in enhanced profitability. In addition, firms are increasingly making IT investments to support their innovative activities; Allen [1] and Ramirez and Kraemer [59] documented the critical role of IT in improving research and development (R&D) productivity and performance. Bardhan et al. [5] also found a positive and significant interaction effect of IT and R&D on Tobin’s q. Finally, we note that holding the level of competition constant, an increase in the level of technological change would create more value available for capture, thereby increasing both IT productivity and IT profitability.

With respect to the risk effects of IT investment under technological change, the above discussion suggests larger and more advanced IT investment in industries with a higher level of technological change. Based on our arguments in previous subsection Competition regarding the riskiness of new and advanced technologies, we posit a higher risk of IT investment in environments with faster rates of technological change. Meanwhile, technological change often has a transformational impact on industry, increasing the uncertainty in costs and benefits of IT capital investments [68]. IT risk is further amplified by the faster rate of technological obsolescence during times of fast technological change. Accordingly, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis H3a (Technological Change─Productivity Link): Higher rate of industry technological change is associated with higher IT productivity.

Hypothesis H3b (Technological Change─Profitability Link): Higher rate of industry technological change is associated with higher IT profitability.

Hypothesis H3c (Technological Change─Risk Link): Higher rate of industry technological change is associated with higher IT risk.

## Empirical Model and Data

Empirical Specifications

Starting with IT productivity, we use a standard production function specification [10, 24], as follows:

$$
\operatorname{IndProd} _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \log I T _ {i t} + \alpha_ {2} \log K _ {i t} + \alpha_ {3} \log L _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

where for industry <sup>i</sup> in year $t \colon I n d P r o d _ { i t }$ is a logarithm of industry value added (VA, industry revenues less intermediate inputs), $I T _ { i t }$ is industry-level stock of IT capital investment, $K _ { i t }$ is industry-level stock of non-IT capital investment, $L _ { i t }$ is labor input, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is the idiosyncratic error term. Output and inputs are all measured in constant year 2000 U.S. dollars, using various deflators as described in detail in Dewan and

Min [24] and elsewhere; $\alpha _ { 1 }$ represents the output elasticity of IT investment. IT marginal product is given by the ratio of output elasticity to IT factor share, which in turn is the ratio of IT investment to industry value added. Since industry characteristics are not factors of production, their moderating influence is analyzed through suitably designed sample split analyses.

Our empirical specifications of IT profitability and IT risk are industry-level analogs of the corresponding firm-level specifications of Dewan and Ren [26], stated as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname{IndRisk} _ {i, t + 1} = \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} i t _ {i t} + \gamma_ {2} l _ {i t} + \gamma_ {3} r d _ {i t} + \gamma_ {4} a d _ {i t} + \gamma_ {5} (\text { Competition }) _ {i t} \\ \qquad + \gamma_ {6} (\text { Regulation }) _ {i t} + \gamma_ {7} (\text { Tech   Change }) _ {i t} + \gamma_ {8} i t _ {i t} ^ {*} (\text { Competition }) _ {i t} \\ \qquad + \gamma_ {9} i t _ {i t} ^ {*} (\text { Regulation }) _ {i t} + \gamma_ {1 0} i t _ {i t} ^ {*} (\text { Tech   Change }) _ {i t} + v _ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{3}

The dependent variables $\operatorname { I n d P r o f } _ { i , t + 1 }$ and $\mathrm { I n d R i s k } _ { i , t + 1 }$ denote accounting-based measures of industry <sup>i</sup> profitability and risk in the year $t + 1$ , computed using salesweighted aggregation of firm-level profitability and risk. To get $\operatorname { I n d P r o f } _ { i + 1 }$ , we first weight each constituent firm’s ROA in year $t + 1$ by the ratio of the firm’s annual sales to the industry total sales, and then sum up all the constituent firms’ salesweighted ${ \mathrm { R O A } } . ^ { 2 }$ To get IndR $\mathrm { i s k } _ { i , t + 1 }$ , we first calculate each constituent firm’s accounting-based risk, which is the standard deviation of the firm’s quarterly ROA over the year $t + 1 \ ( \mathrm { i . e . }$ ., four observations are used in the calculation). Again, as in the case of profitability, the firm-level risk measures are sales-weighted and summed to get the industry risk.

For the capital and expense independent variables, following Bharadwaj et al. [8], we include ratios in the regression, where the capital or expense are normalized by suitable aggregate measures drawn from the same data source, for consistent measurement. Specifically: $i t _ { i t } = I T _ { i t } / A s s e t s _ { i t }$ is the ratio of industry-level stock of IT capital investment over industry total net stock of private fixed assets for the industry <sup>i</sup> in year $t ;$ Note that we do not include industry-level non-IT capital investment in the equations because of perfect collinearity (the sum of IT and non-IT capital investments is the industry total net stock of private fixed assets); $l _ { i t } = L _ { i t } / V A _ { i t } =$ total annual industry labor compensation over industry value added; $r d _ { i t } = R D _ { i t } / S a l e s _ { i t } =$ industry total R&D spending (calculated as the sum of annual R&D expenses for all firms within industry <sup>i</sup>) over aggregate industry sales; $a d _ { i t } = A D _ { i t } / S a l e s _ { i t } { = }$ industry total advertising spending over aggregate industry sales.

The specifications include categorical variables (measured on a scale of 1, 2, or 3) for the levels of industry competition, regulation, and rate of technological change. Finally, the interaction terms between it and the three industry characteristics (with corresponding variables centered) are added to capture the moderating role of industry characteristics in shaping IT profitability and risk performance. A full description of our variables and constructs is provided in the Appendix.

## Estimation Methods

For model estimation, we use ordinary least squares with panel-corrected standard errors (OLS-PCSE) and feasible generalized least squares (FGLS). Our data set (as described below) is cross-sectional and time-series, therefore it is potentially subject to both heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation.<sup>3</sup> In this scenario FGLS is preferred to OLS, because the latter will result in incorrect standard errors [72]. However, the simulation analysis of Beck and Katz [7] indicates that the FGLS approach underestimates variability of the standard errors of the estimated coefficients, leading to overconfidence. They therefore recommend using OLS-PCSE, which retains OLS parameter estimates but replaces the OLS standard errors with panel-corrected standard errors. A recent study by Chen et al. [14] shows that OLS-PCSE is less efficient than FGLS in estimating standard errors, and suggests that researchers should show results using both methods—which is what we do. It is worth noting that Han et al. [40], in their analysis of the same industry data set that we are using, use the two methods as well. To correct for autocorrelation under either OLS-PCSE or FGLS, we use both panel-specific first-order autocorrelation (PSAR1) and common AR1.<sup>4</sup>

## Data and Descriptive Statistics

Our data set contains 59 industries for the period of 1998 to 2005. In the Appendix, we provide a detailed description of our variables and data constructs including their sources and measurements, supporting studies, and comments. For our production function analysis, the industry-level value-added, capital investment (IT and non-IT), and labor are obtained from the Bureau of Economic Analysis. Value added is equal to the industry’s gross output minus its intermediate inputs. The industry IT capital measure aggregates the net capital stock of private fixed assets that belong to the categories of computers and peripheral equipment, software and communications. Non-IT capital stock is the difference between the total net stock of private fixed assets and IT capital. The labor variable is the industry total employee compensation. To get constant dollar values, appropriate deflators for industry value added, IT capital investment, and non-IT capital investment are also obtained from the BEA. The labor deflator is obtained from Bureau of Labor Statistics. The BEA data covers 59 industries over eight years, for a total of 472 observations.<sup>5</sup> Note that this BEA data set has also been used in prior IT investment studies [15, 33, 40].<sup>6</sup> Other variables in our profitability and risk specifications (Equations [2]–[3]) are calculated using data from Compustat, aggregating firm-level variables up to the corresponding industry level.

Our measures of industry characteristics are obtained from the IBISWorld industry report, which is the largest independent provider of industry information in the United States. IBISWorld combines data from government sources—such as the U.S. Census Bureau and Bureau of Labor Statistics—with data from industry and trade associations, and fills in the gaps with industry contacts and in-house databases. The data and industry reports are widely used by the largest commercial banks, technology companies, consulting companies, and media outlets, among others. The data have not been widely used in business academic research to date, with Kauffman et al. [48] and Elliot [30] being notable exceptions.

By IBISWorld’s definition, industry competition includes both internal competition (e.g., price competition, service-based competition, branding and marketing, product enhancements, and development of new products) and external competition (i.e., competition with other industries). This characterization of competition, we believe, is more comprehensive than a single quantitative measure such as the Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI).<sup>7</sup> For industry regulation, the IBISWorld measure captures the presence or absence of regulation as well as the intensity and consequences of regulation. Examples of regulations covered by IBISWorld include licenses, quotas, tax rules, and legislation regarding mergers, monopolies, and so on. With respect to technological change, IBISWorld captures the latest technology and/or systems currently used by the industry, any recent changes, and the level of change. Technology refers to machinery and equipment, such as computerization, that enable better and more efficient production, while systems refer to intangibles, franchises, manufacturing processes, and the like, that also enable efficient production. The rate of technological change is measured by comparing the rate of change in this industry to the rate of change in other industries.

For each industry, IBISWorld reports a level of high, medium, or low for the industry characteristics, which we code as ordinal values 3, 2, and 1, respectively. Due to the relatively small sizes of the low samples for competition and technological change, we combine the low and medium categories to generate our “low” subsample for these two industry characteristics. ## IT Profitability Results

Our results for the IT profitability specification are reported in Table 7. Starting with OLS-PCSE, we find that the coefficient on IT capital is –0.051 and insignificant. Our primary interest is in the moderating role of industry characteristics, for which we find a negative and significant coefficient (–0.614, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ for the interaction term between IT and competition, consistent with our Competition—Profitability Link Hypothesis (H1b). The coefficient of the interaction term between IT investment and industry regulation is negative and significant (–0.129, $p < 0 . 1 0 )$ ), supporting the Regulation— Profitability Link Hypothesis (H2b). Finally, the coefficient on the interaction term between IT investment and technological change is positive and significant (0.118, $p < 0 . 1 0 )$ , consistent with the Technological Change—Profitability Link Hypothesis (H3b). Overall, results from FGLS estimation are consistent with those using OLS-PCSE.

## IT Risk Results

Our results for IT risk analysis are also reported in Table 7. For OLS-PCSE, we find that IT capital investment is associated with higher industry risk (the coefficient estimate is $0 . 0 3 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , which is consistent with the high-risk high-return findings documented in prior research at the firm level [27]. Looking at the interaction terms, for industry competition, we find that IT risk is significantly higher in the high competition industries as compared to the low competition subsample—the coefficient of the interaction term between IT and competition is positive and significant (0.083, $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , consistent with our Competition—Risk Link Hypothesis (H1c). The interaction term of IT with regulation is negative but insignificant under OLS-PCSE, but it is negative and significant in the FGLS specification $( - 0 . 0 2 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , providing weak support for the Regulation— Risk Link Hypothesis (H2c). Finally, the interaction term between IT investment and technological change is positive and significant (0.040, $p < 0 . 1 0 )$ , supporting the Technological Change—Risk Link Hypothesis (H3c).

## Robustness Checks and Alternative Specifications

To ensure the robustness of our empirical results, we conduct a variety of additional tests (see Table 9 for IT productivity analysis and Table 10 for IT profitability and risk analyses).<sup>8</sup> First, we use industry fixed effects as an alternative estimation method to rerun every analysis, and find that the results are consistent with those from OLS-PCSE and FGLS. The next issue we looked into was the possibility of simultaneity between IT investments and industry output, whereby industries anticipating better performance may invest more in $\Pi . ^ { 9 }$ We use panel data two-stage least squares (2SLS), following Goh and Kauffman [34] and Cheng and Nault [15], whereby one-year lagged IT capital is used as an instrumental variable.<sup>10</sup> Results as shown in Tables 9 and 10 are quite consistent with our main results.

We also consider another potential source of endogeneity, which is measurement error in the IT capital variable [72]. For this, we consider an alternative measure for IT capital, derived from the Harte Hanks IT database. Following Chwelos et al. [18],<sup>11</sup> a measure of firm-level IT capital investment is developed through a hedonic analysis of a large variety of computer hardware, networking, and peripheral assets deployed in the firms—used and validated in prior IT value research [27, 52].<sup>12</sup> The sample covers all the industries of this study, but only for the 2000–2005 period, resulting in a somewhat reduced data set. Results from using Harte Hanks IT are quite consistent with the main results in the IT productivity analysis, but are largely insignificant in the IT profitability and risk analyses. We think this is because firm IT capital is aggregated from site level and our Harte Hanks data cover only partial sites of each company, therefore the ratio of IT capital to firm total assets becomes very small, thus resulting in insignificant estimates.

Another limitation that we address in our robustness analysis has to do with the categorical nature of IBISWorld industry characteristics. For competition, in particular, an alternative continuous measure is available, the HHI, which has been widely used in prior research [15, 56, 60].<sup>13</sup> Using this measure, we find that our results (shown in Table 9 and 10) are quite consistent with our main results.

[27]), as opposed to the standard deviation of quarterly ROA over the next year. Needless to say, there is a substantial overlap in the data used for variable construction going from one year to the next, which substantially reduces the variation in the industry risk variable. ## Discussion and Conclusions

This study provides the first comprehensive analysis at the industry level of the moderating role of industry characteristics (competition, regulation, and technological change) in industry IT performance in productivity, profitability, and risk. The most interesting results are for industry competition, where we find a pattern of high IT productivity, high IT risk, but low IT profitability. A potential explanation for this finding is that firms facing competitive pressures are forced to improve their efficiency by using more and advanced IT, which increases their risk exposure. But the firms are not necessarily able to capture the value from those IT investments in the form of higher profits, due to the fact that the value is competed away. This bears some resemblance to the findings of Cheng and Nault [15], who report that industry competition causes IT spillovers that are not internalized by the IT-investing firms, due to competition. This is also not unlike the argument of Clemons and Row [21] and Josefek and Kauffman [45] that IT often becomes a strategic necessity, rather than a source of sustained competitive advantage, when competitors are able to imitate innovative IT applications and offer comparable services.

We can think of the computer manufacturing industry, a highly competitive industry, as an illustration of the asymmetric impact of competition on IT productivity versus IT profitability. This industry has seen a string of IT-enabled innovations, which have helped to drive industry productivity higher. For example, an Internet-based direct sales model improves operational efficiency, and ideas like multifunctional systems (through a combination of computer hardware, software, and telecommunications) enable higher levels of production efficiency. Not surprisingly, computer manufacturing is listed as one of the six high-productivity-growth industries in the McKinsey report [51]. However, the heavily competitive environment (due to fierce price competition and short product life cycles) hampers value capture, resulting in low profit margins. IT value passes through the product market to the customers, who are able to enjoy better products at lower prices. That is, competition in the computer manufacturing industry is associated with high IT productivity but low IT profitability.

The other two industry characteristics—regulation and technological change— affect IT productivity and profitability in the same direction. As we discussed in the theory section, low regulation and high technological change both enable higher levels of IT value creation, so that for a given level of competition, proportionately larger amounts of value are captured in the form of profits. Indeed, technological change promotes IT productivity and profitability due to the fact that better production method or processes often require IT today. However, IT risk is also higher under higher rates of technological change, due to the riskiness of new technologies and technological obsolescence.

Our results have significant managerial and policy implications. As industries continue to expand their considerable investments in IT, our results shed light on the return and risk implications of the investments. Further, the industry-level results provide a higher level view of environmental effects and useful benchmarks against which individual firms can measure their own track record of IT investment performance. More specifically, the industry-level benchmarks can be a useful first step in assessing the strategic and economic impact of IT investment for a firm; that is, first examine the broad industry-level impact of IT investment, and refine that benchmark based on how a given firm deviates from the average firm along the three industry characteristics. For example, a firm that is facing above-average competition in their industry segment should take deliberate action to counteract the force that tends to “create value but destroy profits” [41, 64] from their IT investments. Such action could involve deliberate steps to ameliorate competitive intensity in conjunction with major IT investments, through mechanisms like IT-driven differentiation, IT-enabled operational efficiency, or more effective IT risk management. On the regulation side, managers should recognize how price or margin controls affect the return and risk impacts of IT investment, and plan accordingly. This also has policy implications for regulators, especially for the impact of regulations on IT-intensive sectors such as finance, retail, or computer manufacturing. Finally, technological change can be a powerful tool that innovative firms can harness to create and capture IT value.

This work is not without limitations. First, our empirical results explain only industry-average effects, and do not reflect heterogeneities within broad industry definitions. Second, our three industry characteristics are measured on a coarse ordinal scale, which restricts our ability to detect industry effects as there is limited variation. Fortunately, our industry characteristics are ordered categorical variables, which facilitate the interpretation of results. Another measurement issue is due to the fact that some of our industry variables (such as profitability) are aggregated from firm-level data. The problem is that Compustat includes only publicly traded firms, and therefore does not cover the entire industry. Finally, the short time span of eight years in our data set precludes the inclusion of sophisticated lag structures to capture time dynamics in the data. However, our use of PCSE error correction should mitigate this issue to some extent.

Overall, this study paints a broad picture of IT investment in the economy, including its overall impact on industry financial performance and the variation in IT return and risk in different industrial environments. It adds to the emerging research on IT risk by providing new evidence at the industry level. More important, it builds a theoretical base for understanding the moderating role of key industry characteristics (industry competition, regulation, and technological change) in shaping IT return and risk. Guided by the theoretical arguments, our empirical analysis suggests different IT return and risk under different environmental scenarios. Our results in part explain the large dispersion in IT performance across industries documented in prior studies [51], and help managers to better understand how the nature of the industry drives IT capital investment and industry performance.
