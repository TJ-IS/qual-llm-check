## Abstract

In IS research, social presence is generally defined as the perceived capacity of a communication medium to convey contextual cues normally available in face‐to‐face settings. However, theorizing social presence as a property of the technology has been challenged for decades. The objective of this paper is to develop a more contemporary, interactional view of social presence. To this end, this paper develops a new conceptualization of how participants form the sense that each other is present. We characterize the development of this sense as a skilful accomplishment that entails interactants' joint construction of each other as “real.” Viewing social presence as contingent on social practice, we seek to answer the following research question: “How is social presence accomplished in virtual environments?” To explicate how virtual others are perceived as becoming socially present, that is, emotionally and psychologically “real” to someone interacting with them, we draw from Goffman's work, particularly his concepts of involvement and involvement obligation. Detailing two examples of social interaction in the virtual world Second Life, our analysis highlights the key role that this moral obligation, intrinsic to everyday social interaction, plays in virtual others becoming perceived as psychoemotionally “there.” By outlining a model of how the sense of a virtual other as “real” is produced in and through social interaction, our work contributes a sociological perspective to the construct of social presence and underscores some of the material and social conditions necessary for users to perceive virtual others as present.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Social presence, which Fulk, Schmitz, and Steinfield (1990) articulate as “the degree to which a medium facilitates awareness of the other person and interpersonal relationships during the interaction,” is frequently used to charac terize a technology's capacity to convey relational information, eg, non‐verbal cues that disclose a person's emotional and psychological state. In IS research, social presence has been found to contribute to the generation of trust in computer‐mediated settings (Gefen & Straub, 2004; Lankton, McKnight, & Tripp, 2015; Lowry, Zhang, Zhou, & Fu, 2010; Ou, Pavlou, & Davison, 2014), improved virtual team performance (Miranda & Saunders, 2003; Yoo & Alavi, 2001), and success of e‐commerce websites (Cyr, Head, Larios, & Pan, 2009; Kumar & Benbasat, 2006; Pavlou, Liang, & Xue, 2007; Zhu, Benbasat, & Jiang, 2010), recommendation agents (Hess, Fuller, & Campbell, 2009), and virtual worlds (Animesh, Pinsonneault, Yang, & Oh, 2011; Goel, Johnson, Junglas, & Ives 2011). Conversely, the absence of social presence—or the perception that others in the virtual space are not “real person[s]” (Gunawardena, 1995, p. 151)—has been associated with uninhibited behaviour such as flaming (Kiesler Zubrow, Moses, & Geller, 1985), difficulties in arriving at mutually agreeable decisions (Lewicki & Litterer, 1995), and group polarization, with participants more likely to pursue their own interests rather than concurring with those of the group (Sia, Tan, & Wei, 2002).

However, this technology‐focused view of social presence, which is apparent in even recent IS research (eg, Huang, Hong, & Burtch, 2017; Lankton et al., 2015; Ou et al., 2014), has long been criticized (eg, Baym, 1995; Walther & Burgoon, 1992). Characterized as a deficit‐oriented, cues‐filtered‐out theory (Culnan & Markus, 1987) this view of social presence fails to consider the role of people as inherently social beings who often seek to insert psychoemotional cues (eg, emoticons) into even the leanest technology, eg, text messaging (Hollingshead & Contractor, 2010). Information richness theory (Daft & Lengel, 1986), which was—like social presence theory—employed in I as a rationale for media choice, has been debunked on similar grounds (A. S. Lee, 1994; Markus, 1994).

Drawing inspiration from Ngwenyama and Lee (1997) who reconceptualize information richness theory as a process of enactment involving people in a situated context—rather than as a property intrinsic to a technologica medium—we seek to redefine the concept of social presence in IS. This seems imperative given contemporary multimedia environments that integrate text, video, and audio, thus diminishing the salience of social presence as a purely technological determinant of media choice (Hollingshead & Contractor, 2010; Montoya, Massey, Hung, & Crisp, 2009). At the same time, the preponderance of virtual interactions in contemporary life (eg, customer support via online chat and virtual meetings) makes social presence as a social accomplishment increasingly relevant (Biocca Harms, & Burgoon, 2003) to understanding how virtual environments and the phenomena in them are experienced a “real” (Kreps, 2010).

In order to reconceptualize social presence as produced in and through situated interaction—rather than as a property of the technology—our empirical study is guided by the following research question: “How is social presence accomplished in virtual environments?" To answer this question, we rely on data collected in the virtual world, Seconc Life (SL). This technological setting is well suited to our research objective in that (1) virtual worlds are social environments that rely on interaction among participants for their value (Bartle, 2003; Goel, Johnson, Junglas, & Ives, 2013) (2) presence is one of the critical success factors of virtual reality technology (eg, Nowak & Biocca, 2003; Slater & Wilbur, 1997; Witmer & Singer, 1998), and (3) questions of realness are central to user's experiences of the technology, in part due to distinction between reality and virtuality (Schields, 2003) as indicated by the platform's name, Sl which stands in contrast to. “first” or. “real” life

For our theoretical framing, we draw from Goffman's (1959, 1961, 1963, 1967, 1983, 1986) work on the microprocesses of social interaction, There already exists a respected tradition of applving portions of Goffman's work to the study of information systems in general (eg, Newman & Westrup, 2005; Subramanian, Nandhakumar, & Baptista, 2013; Young, Kuo, & Myers, 2012) and to self‐presentation within virtual environments in particular (eg, Ellison, Heino, & Gibbs, 2006; Vaast, 2007). In this paper, we add to this tradition, highlighting that Goffman's concepts of involvement and involvement obligation (Goffman, 1967, p. 114ff) have much to offer for explicating social presence as a skilful accomplishment. Since allocations of involvement have remained largely unexplored in IS research (exceptions include Brooks, 2007), our work develops these aspects of Goffman's theorizing as a conceptua scaffold for explicating the mechanism through which social interaction contributes to making virtual others “real.”

Our paper proceeds as follows: We first review the literature on social presence. This is followed by an outline of Goffman's work on social interaction extending presentation of self‐concepts to focused interaction, highlighting the role of involvement obligation in shaping social presence by participants. By analysing two empirical examples of social encounters in the virtual world SL, we then demonstrate the value of Goffman's concepts concerning involvement for accomplishing social presence in and through social interaction. Based on our abductive theorizing of these SL‐based social interactions, we develop a model for generating social presence in virtual settings. Our paper concludes with a discussion of our study's key insights, contributions, implications, and limitations.

## 2 | SOCIAL PRESENCE

The term “social presence” was originally developed by Short, Williams, and Christie (1976) as the basis for medi choice theory. They defined social presence as “the degree of salience of the other person in the interaction and the consequent salience of the interpersonal relationships” (p. 65). This definition suggests that a person is deemed to be present if they make a difference to the situated interaction (eg, what a focal actors says and does in th moment) and that the resulting relationship between the communicators matters in some way. Since relational cues (eg, head nods, eye gaze, and body position) expressed by a person are instrumental in how a focal actor behaves in a face‐to‐face setting, the key concern of social presence theory is determining what the unavailability of these cue does to communication in a technology‐mediated context (Short et al., 1976).

Hypothesizing that media can differ in affordances for social presence (eg, text‐based channels filter out nonverbal cues such as tone of voice that convey psychoemotional information as a phone does), social presence theory predicts that people will select the communication medium that is commensurate with their task needs. Specifically media low in social presence (eg, computer conferencing) are predicted to be selected for task‐related communica tion, whereas face‐to‐face situations, deemed high in social presence, are perceived as indispensable to tasks where psychoemotional information is necessary to convey the meaning of a message

However, social presence theory's hypotheses regarding media selection provide us little insight into the theory' core construct. For this, we need to explore the concept of “presence.”

## 2.1 | Presence

Presence is generally understood as the user's perception of “being there” (Biocca. 1997), where “being” connotes the user's ontological status independent of his or her beliefs and attitudes and “there” refers to an external world that exists independently of him or her. However, presence is not a unitary experience (Schubert, Friedmann, & Regenbrecht, 2001). The multidimensional nature of presence highlights that “being there” implies an illusion of nonmediation, the sense of existing within a virtual world that also exists, and the transportation of the user from the physical into the virtual world (see Appendix A for a summary). Furthermore, presence is facilitated by the user's ability to convert her intentions into actions and to become immersed in the virtual environment by allocating her attention to the things people and events in the virtual space

## 2.2 | Social presence in IS research

Short et al.'s (1976) definition of social presence has been interpreted in a variety of ways in IS research. For example, focusing on the salience-of-the-other-person-in-the-interaction dimension of Short et al.'s definition in their articula tion of social presence, Zhu et al. (2010, p. 878) highlight “the capability of a medium to allow a user to experience others as being psychologically present” in their conceptualization of social presence. Kumar and Benbasat's (2006, p 429) articulation of social presence as “the degree to which a medium allows an individual to establish a persona connection with others,” in turn, emphasizes the interpersonal relationship dimension of Short et al.'s definition. I contrast, Fulk et al.'s (1990, p. 118) articulation of social presence as “the degree to which a medium facilitate awareness of the other person and interpersonal relationships during the interaction” captures both dimensions

While all these definitions sustain Short et al.'s (1976) view of social presence as a perceived property of the technology, they operationalize it differently. Most IS research (eg, Gefen & Straub, 2004; Lowry et al., 2010) treats social presence as an individually held, perceptual indicator that is assessed in terms of such self‐report survey items as “Using <collaboration tool> to interact with others creates a warm [sociable, or personal] environment for communication” (Brown, Dennis, & Venkatesh, 2010, p. 52). Nevertheless, some studies regard it as an objective measure of a technology's material features (eg, Sia et al., 2002; Zhang, Lowry, Zhou, & Fu, 2007).

Reliance on this technology‐focused, cues‐filtered‐out interpretation and operationalization of social presence persists in IS research (eg, Huang et al., 2017) despite numerous critiques. For example, Biocca et al. (2003) point out that while social presence is a dynamic perceptual experience, Short et al.'s (1976) definition has turned into a relatively stable mental model of a communication medium's capacity to render the other present. This implies mismatch between the construct and its operationalization

Further, Hollingshead and Contractor's (2010) summary of social presence research highlights that media effects related to limited contextual cues are temporary. Over time, users in computer‐mediated settings develop the abilit to express psychoemotional content in written form (Walther & Burgoon, 1992), eg, by using emoticons and emot statements (eg, “LOL”). Additionally, in direct contradiction to its predictions regarding media choice, Walther (1995) found that some computer‐mediated groups achieved more positive levels of interpersonal communication than did face‐to‐face groups.

Offering an interaction‐oriented definition of social presence (see Appendix B for the variety of definitions of social presence) that focuses on “how persons process social information and communicate their relationships based on that information” (Walther, 1995, p. 190), Walther (1995, p. 188) conceptualizes it as the “feeling one has that the other persons are involved in a communication exchange.” Gunawardena (1995, p. 151), who takes an equally interactional perspective, articulates social presence as “the degree to which a person is perceived as a ‘real person in mediated communication.” Both of these definitions highlight the link between presence and reality that prior research on presence has established.

## 2.3 | Presence as reality

Much presence research has assumed that users feel like they are “in” a virtual setting when the attributes of a digitally simulated world are indistinguishable from those in the actual world (eg, Heeter, 1992; Slater & Wilbur, 1997). Indeed, widely adopted definitions of presence include “the perceptual illusion of nonmediation” (Lombard & Ditton, 1997) and “a psychological state in which virtual … physical objects and social actors are experienced as actual physical objects and social actors” (K. M. Lee, 2004, p. 44).

By equating a person's feelings of “being there” in a virtual setting to an experience of actual reality, presence connotes that the computer‐mediated space exists and that the user exists in it. Some research (eg, Banos et al., 2000; Flach & Holden, 1998; Mantovani & Riva, 1999) therefore subsumes users' reality attributions under the concept of presence. Schubert et al.'s (2001) factor analysis of the presence construct, which demonstrated that the virtual seeming as “real” as actuality was one of the three dimensions, provides further support for equating feeling present with feeling “real.”

As with the concept of presence, “reality” also has many definitions, each highlighting different aspects of what is understood as “real” (see Appendix C for a summary of some prevalent dimensions). “Something is real if it has an effect or makes a difference” (Fleetwood, 2004, p. 29). While this definition of reality implies that it can be situated and idiosyncratic, the consequences of subjectively experienced reality are likely to be more limited than those of socially shared, “objective” notions of reality (Chayko, 1993).

Reality is thus frequently understood as a state of concrete existence, that is, phenomena having an ontologica status independent of any individual's attitudes, beliefs, or perceptions (Searle, 2010). While natural phenomena such as the Himalaya mountains exist independently of human involvement, social phenomena such as marriage, money, and job roles rely on human activity for their existence (Fleetwood, 2004), as well as social agreement and a shared belief about how they matter (Searle, 1995). To the extent that such socially enacted phenomena are “institutiona facts” whose constitution relies on social structures (Searle, 1995), they are deemed socially (ie, intersubjectively) rea (Berger & Luckman, 1966).

There is considerable overlap between the various dimensions of social presence (Appendix B) and realit (Appendix C). For example, Goffman's (1961) definition of the real as that with which an individual is engrossed is consonant with the understanding of social presence that relies on one user's feeling that another user is cognitivel and emotionally involved in a virtual exchange (Walther, 1995).

## 2.4 | Social presence and copresence and personal presence

In IS research, social presence and copresence are at times conflated and confused (eg, Goel et al., 2013; Vance, Lowry, & Egget, 2015) as both refer to the psychological sense of “being with” remote others in a mediated environ ment (Rettie, 2005; Sivunen & Nordback, 2015). Similarly, social presence is at times subsumed under persona presence (Mennecke, Triplett, Hassall, Conde, & Heer, 2011), or vice versa (Ou et al., 2014). To delineate the relation ship between social presence and its links to perceptions of reality, we now explore past research on these three forms of presence. (We adopt Heeter's, 1992, nomenclature and refer to “presence” as “personal presence” for the remainder of this paper so as to distinguish it more clearly from social presence.)

In the context of virtual settings, copresence connotes the perception that there are others in the virtual environment who are available for interaction even though their physical bodies are in different physical locations (Slater, Howell, Steed, Pertaub, & Garau, 2000). For example, copresence may be inferred from an individual's status as currently logged in (eg, availability indicators on Skype or Facebook). Goel et al. (2013) define the ability to se and hear others' avatars, as well as the capacity to recognize another's availability for social interaction (eg, an avatar simulating typing on a keyboard suggests that the user is already in conversation with someone else) as copresence

As a multifaceted construct (see Appendix D for summary), copresence further represents a bilaterally held mutual awareness that participants in an interaction have relative to each other (Rettie, 2005). In other words, the person in the example above, whose avatar is typing, is aware that her behaviour/availability is monitorable by those around her, just as their behaviour/availability is monitorable by her. Copresence thus aids the flow of interaction b minimizing interruptions and coordinating turn taking

Social presence (see Appendix B for summary of its dimensions), in contrast, is a unilaterally held. subiective perception (Rettie, 2005) that a copresent person is also cognitively and emotionally involved in the same socia space (Walther, 1995), ie, psychoemotionally “there.” To the extent that virtual others matter, that is, are treated as “social beings with feelings rather than objects that can be ignored” (Sia et al., 2002, p. 73), they are perceived as socially present or “real person[s]” (Gunawardena, 1995)

With regard to the relationship among social, personal, and copresence, the research is inconclusive. For example, in developing their concept of embodied social presence, Mennecke et al. (2011) advance personal presence as a prerequisite of copresence and copresence as a prerequisite of social presence. This causal relationship runs counter to Goel et al. (2013), who maintain that copresence is a prerequisite for personal presence in virtuality. It is also at odds with Rettie (2005), who posits that social presence is a prerequisite for an individual's presentation of self (Goffman, 1959), a performance through which an individual enacts her personal presence

This review of the social presence literature thus highlights that prior research frequently conflates social presenc and copresence. Furthermore, even when the concepts are distinguished from one another, their relationships ar inconsistently defined. The objective of this study is to offer conceptual clarity to this literature by developing a coherent theoretical foundation for social presence as a social accomplishment. For this, we draw on Goffman's theories concerning involvement in social interaction to explain how human and technological phenomena interact in ways that can unite and amplify the production of the other as socially present (ie, “real”).

## 3 | THEORY

Since prior research on social presence fails to clarify how a perception of the virtual other as a “real person” is achieved, we turn to Goffman's theorizing about social interaction in order to develop an interactional, microprocessual perspective on generating the perception that the actual person enacting the virtual other is “there,” ie, inside the virtual environment. Although Goffman's work was primarily focused on situations of physica copresence, where participants are within range of each other's naked perceptions, he acknowledges (Goffman, 1983, p. 2) that many properties of social interaction also hold in more distributed social settings. This has been ampl demonstrated in prior research (eg, Campos‐Castillo & Hitlin, 2013; Subramanian et al., 2013; Vaast, 2007)

Given the theoretical opacity of Goffman's work, we first elucidate key constructs from his work that we have found relevant for developing an interactional view of social presence. These constructs will then serve as sensitizing devices in our analysis of our empirical material and as the basis for our abductively (Locke, Golden‐Biddle, & Feldman, 2008) developed account of the mechanism for generating social presence

## 3.1 | Involvement

Goffman's (1963, p. 36) term involvement designates a psychobiological state of mind and body, comprising both cognitive attention and emotional affect. To a certain extent, involvement is unconscious and therefore reflected in a person's bodily posture and orientation. Participants' nonverbal (ie, bodily) behaviour is both expressive and communicative. Goffman notes that because of the integrity between psychoemotional involvement and the humar body's spatial‐orientational positioning (ie, where an individual is situated in space and who/what he is facing), a person's behaviour expresses his or her involvement. Involvement reflects the degree to which the situation is “real” to him, that is, “Something in which the individual can become unselfconsciously engrossed is something that can become real to him” (Goffman, 1961, p. 80). As it circumscribes an individual's cognitive attention and affectiv mood, involvement thus parallels personal presence (eg, Schubert et al., 2001; Waterworth & Waterworth, 2006) Furthermore, expression of involvement is communicative when observed.

Observation therefore supports inferences about the observed's intent. In other words, by observing another person's spatial‐orientational positioning, one can often infer the other person's involvement and surmise their intent and their likely next action(s)

## 3.2 | Copresence

Goffman defines copresent situations as ones in which it is possible for participants to mutually monitor each other (Goffman, 1963, p. 17). Copresence merely implies that participants can see each other and that they are peripherally aware of another's presence in a shared space, like pedestrians passing each other on a sidewalk. In such cases, they are engaged in unfocused interaction, ie, “the kind of communication that occurs when one gleans information about another person present by glancing at him, if only momentarily, as he passes into and then out of one's view” (Goffman, 1963, p. 24).

Participants in unfocused interaction ordinarily monitor each other's physiological expressions of involvement as embodied in posture or spatial‐orientational position, as well as visible activity. This gives them insight into the other's predisposition to engage in focused interaction. Goffman's conceptualization of copresence is thus aligne with Riva & Mantovani's (2014, p. 20) definition of copresence as “the ability of recognizing motor intentions in other individuals” and Goel et al.'s (2013, pp. 39–40) description of copresence as “whether another person is available for interaction based on what her avatar is doing.”

## 3.3 | Focused interaction

Focused interaction, on the other hand, entails that two or more people are not only in a situation where mutua monitoring is possible but are also actively cooperating in a joint activity (eg, conversation; Goffman, 1963, p. 24) When participants become engaged in joint activity, they share a common focus of attention. Further, their respective affective involvement typically becomes aligned so that participants can be said to be jointly engrossed.

Focused interaction therefore involves participants “openly cooperating” to coordinate their actions around a single, shared focus of attention, as commonly occurs in conversation (Goffman, 1963, p. 24). Achieving this kin of coordinated activity is a gradual process, with participants progressing from unfocused interaction to focused interaction (Goffman, 1963, p. 24) through aligning their spatial‐orientational positions and synchronizing thei actions, as they mutually orient towards a shared or common focus of attention and each other (Kendon, 1985). This usually occurs in iterative series of tight feedback loops enacted by participants in the interaction

The attention that a focal actor and the other pay to each other in focused interaction is captured in the following definitions of social presence: “the ability to recognize motor and proximal intentions which allows the Self to identify the Other whose intention is directed towards him” (Riva & Mantovani, 2014, p. 23) and “the extent t which other beings (living or synthetic) … appear to react to you” (Heeter, 1992, p. 262)

## 3.4 | Involvement obligation

According to Goffman (1983), a set of “ground rules” (p. 5) shapes participants' focus and intensity of involvement during copresence and focused interaction with others. These constraints, while not always adhered to, comprise an “etiquette” about how to allocate one's attention during social interaction, which Goffman terms involvement obligation (Goffman, 1967, p. 114). He notes that involvement obligation entails participants attending to each other as well as to their shared endeavour in order to accomplish the joint task.

## 3.5 | Interlocking involvement obligation

Goffman (1986) further theorizes that during joint activity, participants' involvements (attentional focus and affective mood) become interlinked in “interlocking obligation” (p. 346). The interdependence of participants' involvement obliges them to cooperate cognitively and emotionally in a way that also “accept[s] and honor[s] the selves projected by the other participants” (Goffman. 1967, p. 105), as well as supports the other getting and staving involved in the joint activity. Goffman notes that involvement during focused interaction needs to be reciprocal and is therefore an “interlocking obligation” (p. 346). Ultimately, he views it as a “moral obligation” (Rawls, 1990, p. 68) for participants because—as interactants—the existence of each participant's social self is dependent on others validating its self‐presentation: “it is through the response that the first move [e.g., presentation of self] is confirmed as existing or having meaning” (Manning, 2008, p. 680). In other words, one's involvement obligation in joint activity ultimately entails interlocking response to the other in a way that not only satisfies task requirements but also validates that other's presentation of self. This conceptualization echoes Brickman's (1980) theory of how a situation becomes “real,” namely, by the other responding appropriately to the focal actor, who must however also act in congruence with her feelings. It also is congruent with Brooks' (2007) explication of virtual interaction, and with Moore Ducheneaut, and Nickell's (2007) concern with interplayer “accountability” in MMORPGs

This suggests that Goffman's (1959) work on the presentation of self, which has often been interpreted as primar ily about “impression management,” instead represents an effort to explain why impression management matters (Manning, 2008). As “the self ultimately depends on interaction” (Rawls, 1990, p. 70), Goffman argues that its need for continuous social interaction and others' validation renders the self fragile and insecure; it is under “constant threat of annihilation” (Rawls, 1990, p. 71). This is because social interaction exposes individuals to risk: Aside from threats to physical safety, a person risks embarrassment (Scheff, 2006) or loss of “face” (Goffman, 1967, p. 7) when th presented self is unmasked as being inconsistent with an actor's genuine thoughts and feelings.

The set of “ground rules” is consequently an implicit contract (Goffman, 1983, p. 5) regulating social encounters such that they are orderly and protective of participants' selves. This contract entails a commitment to generalized ideals of reciprocity or to providing “what we owe each other” (Manning, 2008, p. 678). Adhering to such interactional ground rules—even if the other is a stranger—can thus be seen as an act of self‐preservation, since “violating [the ground rules] would upset the interaction and could in so doing very possibly obstruct our own self interests” (Rawls, 1990, p. 71). The fact that participants' own selves depend on the orderly interaction provides incentives to abide by the implicit contract. Additionally, the implicit contract includes rules for allocating involvement in th social occasion being enacted

## 3.6 | Social occasion

Goffman identifies a social occasion as a typified context of spatially and temporally bounded social activity. As a “social affair, undertaking or event” within an environment larger than the limits of human perception (Goffman 1963, p. 18), a social occasion entails a specific material setting—such as a concert hall, a night club, or a friend's house—within which certain patterns of social interaction are normally performed. The settings are characterized by “fixed equipment” (Goffman, 1963, p. 18), ie, material objects that take on meaning relative to the social interactions occurring within or around them (eg, desks in an office). Such social gatherings are generally guided by rule about how to allocate one's involvement and may also entail an involvement contour over time.

In order then to answer the research question “How is social presence accomplished in virtual environments?” we build on this summary of Goffman's key microprocesses of social interaction. Given the conceptual parallels between these concepts and the literature on social presence and its related constructs (ie, personal and copresence as well as perceptions of reality), our theoretical scaffold promises to provide the necessary granularity to inform our abductive analysis of the empirical material

## 4 | METHOD

To answer our research question, we compare and contrast two social interactions that exhibited different degrees of social presence. Both encounters were reported by the same single user of the virtual world SL. While admittedly a small sample, we contend that the richness and detail of our analysis of these two chat‐based interactions afford key insights into the microprocesses of accomplishing social presence in and through communicative action in virtual environments.

From a larger study into the avatar‐self relationship in SL (see Appendix E for more details)—conducted betwee July 2008 and March 2010 and consisting of photodiary interviews (Latham, 2003)—data featuring Aileen Anglosmit (pseudonym of participant's avatar name) were chosen. Aileen was in her late 30s and had been in Sl. for 8 months

She was a married mother of four and a middle school teacher. Her husband, whose SL name was Gandalf Morrow,<sup>1</sup> was also active in SL

We selected Aileen as our focal participant in this study because of the completeness of the interactional record she provided. In SL, modalities for social interaction included text, video, and voice. Since Aileen never used voice or video, her chatlogs represent a relatively complete record of her virtual interactions with others, as even som embodied actions (eg, gestures) were textualized. Furthermore, the activities in which Aileen engaged were typica of SL residents (eg, Schwarz, Schwarz, Jung, Pérez, & Wiley‐Patton, 2012): She enjoyed dancing at night clubs and socializing with friends and also worked as a hostess in a dance club.

Although the pseudonym Aileen Anglosmith refers to the online persona that Aileen's user enacted through her main (non-alt) avatar, we do not fix the boundaries between the physically embodied user and the virtually embodiec avatar by naming them differently. This is because SL residents generally experienced the distinction between themselves‐as‐users and themselves‐as‐avatars as ambiguous, situated, and fluid (Schultze, 2014). Furthermore, even though our analysis demonstrates that the human user's physical embodiment (including thoughts and feelings) is significantly implicated in enacting events in the virtual world, the user's cognitive and affective involvement is entangled with the avatar's embodied performance on screen. We therefore only highlight distinctions between actual user and avatar when clear separations were evident in participants' words or actions.

The two encounters chosen for analysis differed in the degree to which Aileen experienced the virtual other as socially present, that is, “the degree to which a user feels access to the intelligence, intensions and sensory impressions of another” (Biocca, 1997). We based our assessment of the degree of social presence on Aileen's characterization of these encounters in her photodiaries and interviews. She described the Dancing with Jacob incident as “confusing,” “puzzling,” and “I just couldn't figure out what was going on.” We therefore deem this interaction as generating relatively low social presence, in that Aileen seemed unable to “read” Jacob's intentions, thoughts, and feelings.

In contrast, Aileen described the Racing Go‐Carts encounter as “one of our ‘let's just hang out and be silly’ kind of conversations,” “fun,” and “we had a great morning … catching up with [friend].” The clear direction of her emotions (eg, “fun”), which were reciprocated by other participants (ie, “we had a great morning”) to the point that their interaction could be characterized as a socially shared “kind of conversation,” led us to classifying this incident as having generated a relatively high degree of social presence.

We relied on abductive strategies to analyse our data (Locke et al., 2008). We engaged in repeated interpretive reading of the chatlogs, photodiary entries, and interviews, coupled with frequent references to Goffman's vast corpus, the application of discourse analytic techniques (Fairhurst & Putnam, 2014; Putnam & Fairhurst, 2001), and constant comparison between the two vignettes. See Appendix F for more details

In presenting our analysis, we rely on the convention of using single quotes to denote paraphrased speech and double quotes to denote verbatim speech. We also italicize the concepts from Goffman's work that we apply in theorizing the data.

## 5 | ANALYSIS OF EMPIRICAL DATA

## 5.1 | Encounter with low social presence:

## 6 | DISCUSSION

The objective of this paper has been to develop an interaction‐based understanding of social presence as a socia accomplishment. Given the decade‐old criticisms of social presence as a capability of technology (eg, Hollingshead & Contractor, 2010; Walther & Burgoon, 1992), we are advancing microsociological theory that characterizes socia presence as being generated in and through social interaction.

In response to our research question “How is social presence accomplished in virtual environments?” our abductively derived theory of social presence outlines an interactional process model consisting of three phase (Table 1). Rather than proposing a strictly linear sequence of steps, we use numbers in the outline to highlight cumu lative dependence among the phases in the process of generating social presence. It is entirely possible that multiple phases are active at the same time; however, completion of the entire process—with consequent achievement of a high degree of social presence—is contingent on thresholds being met cumulatively through successive phases

A situation becomes social when people are copresent (Phase 1), that is, when (1) the focal actor is aware of another person being colocated in a virtual space (social occasion) and (2) they are within perceptual range of mutual monitoring. This implies that the others' availability for interaction can be gauged. Importantly, a focal actor's sense o being monitorable by others depends on her subjectively held belief of personal presence, ie, a perception of “being there” in the virtual space and having the capacity to enact her intentions. In the virtual context, copresence is then achieved when a focal actor can see the availability of her friends on social media (eg, Facebook and Skype) and understands that they can see hers also. Once the focal actor initiates contact with another, some degree of socia presence is enacted.

However, based on Short et al.'s (1976) definition of social presence as the salience of the other or the relation ship in (unfocused) social interaction, it is likely that copresence prompts the focal actors' perception of the other's social presence. For example, if a love interest appears in avatar form in a virtual world location in which the foca actor currently finds herself and causes her to change her avatar appearance to make herself more appealing to the other, a highly subjective and therefore low degree of social presence is enacted (Heeter, 1992; Riva & Mantovani, 2014). In other words, the virtual other is made at least somewhat “real" by virtue of his colocatior making a difference in the focal actor's behaviour (Fleetwood, 2004).

Regardless, once people engage in focused interaction (Phase 2) through enacting a joint activity (eg, dancing and racing go‐carts), social presence is likely to increase. To the extent that participants in a social interaction share a com mon focus of attention (eg, collectively witnessing cart plunging into pool) and joint engrossment (eg, as revealed in shared laughter about the inappropriately “parked” cart), a focal actor gains access to the other's intentions, thoughts and feeling. Thus, the degree to which the other is considered socially present can be inferred from his demonstrated commitment to accomplishing the task inherent in the joint activity, ie, his fulfillment of his involvement obligation

Importantly, since the actual involvement of a user behind the virtual representation (eg, avatar) is mediated, anc scripted actions are available (eg, dance moves) that do not require the user's attention, it is difficult for a focal acto to infer with certainty the degree of the other's involvement. Social presence is thus likely to be lower in situation where actions are scripted (eg, avatar dancing without user intervention) than where the user's allocation of attentio is indispensable (eg, steering go‐carts).

Key to recognizing the other as a psychoemotionally “there” in the virtual environment is for the focal actor to receive responses from the other that are appropriate to the task focus of the joint activity. The appropriatenes of the other's response is however also determined by the rules for how individuals should allocate their attention as circumscribed by the social occasion.

As we saw in the Dancing with Jacob incident. failure to comply with rules for allocation of involvement makes it dif ficult—if not impossible—to generate social presence. Neither Jacob nor Aileen allocated much attention to the other and their joint activity, instead limiting the bulk of their shared experience to an unfocused interaction around a scripted cou ples dance and relatively unsuccessful attempts at conversation. Whatever rules for involvement allocation were in effect at the sophisticated nightclub, potentially guiding their interaction, were ignored by both. Since neither consis tently abided by the implicit social contract to allocate their affective and cognitive attention to their “joint activity, the required threshold for Aileen to recognize Jacob as having a moderate degree of social presence was not reached.

Phase 3 of the social presence mechanism is characterized by interlocking involvement obligation, highlighting the extent of interdependency between the focal actor and the other in focused interaction. Up to this phase, social presenc represents a one‐way relationship: The focal actor makes a judgement of the other's “there”‐ness and “real” personhood based on the other's behaviour. However, it is not just the other that has an involvement obligation; the focal actor als has an obligation to support the other's involvement in the joint activity and to honour his social self. Both of these respon sibilities require the focal actor to step into the other's shoes in order to identify the most effective way of giving the other what is owed (Manning, 2008). To the extent that the other validates the self that the focal actor is presenting, he is confirming that she exists as expressed in the virtual environment. What might previously have been her subjective sense of personal presence is thereby socially confirmed and made intersubjectively “real.”

For the focal actor to step into the shoes of the other and to live up to her interlocking involvement obligation, she requires knowledge of the other. Generation of high levels of social presence thus depends on the focal actor's access to the other's intentions, thoughts, and feelings (Biocca, 1997), a type of access that is significantly limited in virtual environments (Moore et al., 2007). Nevertheless, any and all insights so gained allow the focal actor to identify ways of attracting and maintaining the other's involvement in a social interaction, as well as honouring the other's social self. To the extent that the focal actor is able to see herself in the other, ie, homophily (Nowak & Biocca, 2003), she can identify with him. Then, the other becomes as “real” a person as the focal actor, corresponding to a high level of social presence (Riva & Mantovani, 2014).

In the Racing Go‐Carts incident, interlocking involvement obligation was partly facilitated by the participants pre‐established relationship. Aileen changed into “blingy” clothing for the encounter anticipating that it would evoke a certain response from Bow. In this way, she supported and guided his involvement in the interaction. However, th joint activities (eg, tour of studio and go cart racing, both of which were initiated by Bow) emerged out of the immediate situation. This suggests that interlocking involvement is achievable in first‐time interactions, provided that th focal actor has adequate insight into the other's involvement and intentions

As we can see from the Racing Go‐Carts example, accomplishing a high degree of social presence is expressed in terms of the interactants' agreement around the type of social occasion they were enacting and that their engrossment in it was shared. To the extent that their interaction was positive, there is a likelihood of building a new—or strengthening an existing—interpersonal relationship. Importantly, given the focal actors' assessment of the (physically embodied) other as “there” (interacting in the virtual space), this interpersonal relationship is deemed to be between the interactants as “real person[s]” sitting behind the virtual representations (eg, avatars)

## 7 | CONTRIBUTIONS AND IMPLICATIONS

The interactional process model of social presence we outline in Table 1 develops a coherent model of socia presence as a socially produced and skilfully coordinated accomplishment. As such, our conceptualization of social presence bridges the gap between, on the one hand, the importance of the social presence construct in virtua environments (eg, social media, virtual reality, and videoconferencing) on which contemporary organizations and society increasingly rely and, on the other hand, the inadequacy of the technology‐focused definition and operationalization of the sense of the other “being there.” Specifically, our interaction‐based theory of social presence addresses critiques such as the dynamic concept of the other's “there”‐ness being reduced to a stable measur of a technology's capability to generate the focal actor's perception of the other as a “real” (Nowak & Biocca, 2003)

Informed by the microprocesses of social interaction (Goffman, 1959, 1961, 1963, 1967, 1983, 1986) and the prior literature on social presence and its related constructs (see Appendices A–D), our abductively developed mode identifies key thresholds that must be reached before different degrees of social presence can be achieved. This punctuated process model of how social presence is generated through social interaction (Table 1) thus outlines what different “degrees” (Short et al., 1976, p. 65) of social presence look like. Future research might draw on character istics of the three degrees of social presence proposed here to assess technological features designed to contribut to making virtual spaces highly sociable.

Our punctuated process model of social presence is built upon theoretically informed distinctions between and relationships among the three types of presence. It highlights that personal presence, ie, individuals' subjectively held perceptions that they exist in and can enact their intentions in the virtual space, is a requirement for (virtual) copresence. Without this sense of “being there,” a focal actor is unable to engage in the mutual monitoring that defines copresence. Copresence, in turn, is a prerequisite for social presence, in that participants need to be mutuall aware of each other's colocatedness and availability before focused interaction can be achieved. Social presence, in turn, then validates the focal actor's self as presented within the virtual environment (eg, Vaast, 2007), thereb rendering the environment intersubjective and thus more real. The focal actor's personal presence and the other' social presence are thus reciprocally strengthened through focused interaction

We expect that our descriptions of the varying degrees to which the other is perceived as “real” can support the development of new social presence scales for measuring both the technological and social aspects of social presence Such an effort might leverage the relationships between personal, social, and copresence that our abductively developed theory presents. Currently, these concepts are neither defined nor used consistently in the IS literature; indeed, they have been frequently conflated (eg, Goel et al., 2013; Mennecke et al., 2011). Our theory helps untangle these concepts; the resultant definitional clarity should enable IS researchers to assess, at a fine‐grained level, what types of presence emerge in different technological environments as they support sociality (eg, teleconferencing vs. virtual reality).

More generally, our theoretical framework defines what it means to be social in virtual worlds in which sociality is integral to the value proposition. Given considerable IS research on social media, this definition promises to be ver impactful. Specifically, to be social is to give “what we owe each other” (Manning, 2008, p. 678). One of the most funda mental—even moral—obligations we have to one another in face-to-face interaction is to reciprocally validate each other's presentation of self, so we—as interactants—can become and stay involved in social interaction in order to achieve other goals, Since all selves depend on social interaction for their existence (Rawls. 1987), social media research would do well to pay more attention to the social interaction process as it unfolds through situated action over time

In fact, we assert that our interactional process model of social presence has major implications for understand ing some of the mental health issues now being identified as stemming from social media use, such as depression anxiety, and social media addiction (eg, Primack et al., 2017; Twenge, Joiner, Rogers, & Martin, 2018). Our mode highlights a human need for validation of self normally obtained in synchronous (whether face‐to‐face, mediated or virtual) interaction through reciprocal, interlocking involvement obligations. Since many of the most popular socia media platforms (eg, Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter) are asynchronous, they skew the process in the direction of presentation of self (ie, unfocused interaction) without easy glides into the involvement obligations of focuse interaction.

Based on our interactional process model then, it can be hypothesized that the increasing mental health problems—including low self‐esteem, addiction, and fear of missing out or FoMO (Flores, 2014)—experienced by digita natives growing up with social media might be linked to timing issues in asynchronous encounters. In such interactive settings, high degrees of social presence remain elusive as focused interaction with its reciprocal involvement obligation validating each other's presentation of self (and thus personal presence) is difficult—if not impossible—t accomplish without the joint activity of focused interaction. Indeed, the diminished social presence accomplished via asynchronous social media can help explain the social media (Silva, 2017) or Internet paradox (Kraut et al., 1998), ie, the apparent contradiction that a “social technology” reduces sociality and instead generates social isolation and loneliness.

Drawing on our empirical insight that a high degree of social presence was characterized by rapid conversationa turns—and the opposite represented by an interaction with lengthy conversational gaps—future research migh explore the implications of delays in conversational turns on social media users' mental health. For example, variations in timing of others' responses (eg, likes and retweets) to an individual's self‐presentation on social media are likely to give us insights into the causal factors of the challenges with self‐esteem, anxiety, and depression that ar becoming increasingly evident among social media users today (Primack et al., 2017; Twenge et al., 2018).

Our interactional process model of social presence also has significant implications for the design of conversationa artificial intelligence (AI) agents (eg, Siri and Echo) that are increasingly becoming confidants and therapists (eg, responding to statements like “Alexa, I am lonely” with empathy) rather than mere servants that follow commands (Shulevitz, 2018) The deployment of these chatbots in environments where intimacy and empathy are highly pertinent (eg, psychocognitive therapy and intimate relationships) is characterized as “artificial intimacy” (Turkle, 2018). Applications like therapy bots (eg, WoeBot and SimSensei) and dating games with virtual characters (eg, Mystic Messenger) are increasingly developed t simulate social interaction by replacing one of the human interactants with an autonomous agent

While some are raising serious concerns about the long‐term consequences of artificial intimacy, eg, diminished expectations from human intimacy (Essig, 2018), our model offers some additional insights. For example, while an individual's subjective sense of personal presence (ie, her feeling that she exists and has the ability to act) and her awareness of mutual monitoring (copresence) are likely to remain intact when she enters into a conversation with a robot (Phase 1), and the intelligent agent can be expected to exhibit engrossment and behaviour that is appropriate to the joint activity of conversation (Phase 2), we aver that it is in Phase 3 that the human‐robot interaction is mos likely to falter. Firstly, notions of a social contract or ground rules that regulate the social encounters so as to keep them orderly and protect the participants' sense of self seem misplaced in situations of artificial intimacy. The AI bots are programmed to respond as appropriately as their intelligence will allow, yet their responses are not motivated by self‐preservation. In other words, the autonomous agent is presumably not presenting a “self” that needs validation by the human other and therefore is not at risk of being embarrassed or losing face if things go awry in the conversation. The meaning of “giving each other what we owe” therefore becomes highly ambiguous. What do human participants owe these artificial agents, eg, common courtesies like “please” and “thank you” (Gordon, 2018)? And what do the agents and/or their designers “owe” the human participants?

Secondly, the AI agent's validation of a human's presentation of self may be ineffective, as the programmed nature of the machine is likely to mitigate the risks of the human's self‐performance. Indeed, based on its programming, a chatbot is unlikely to challenge or embarrass the human interactant. Without the unpredictability of human responses in interactions, an individual's sense of self and realness (ie, personal presence validated by others) are likely to change in unantic ipated ways, potentially becoming increasingly brittle and less capable of adapting to nonroutine kinds of social occasions

Thus, as the deployment of artificial intimacy applications increases, our model proffers the following insight for their developers: reduce the predictability of the AI agent's response to the human's self‐presentation. By increasing the human's risk of embarrassment based on the bot's response, the chances of humans developing a robust sense of self are likely to increase. However, such unpredictable bot behaviour may also lead human users to reject the autonomous agent, so experimentation is advised.

Lastly, as our discussion about joint activity suggests, the materiality of the digital environment (eg, digita artefacts such as avatar bodies and go‐carts) also plays an important role in supporting the development of socia presence. We note two ways in which virtual material mattered in producing social presence: in constituting the social occasion and as objects supporting participants' involvement in joint activity.

Consistent with Stromer‐Galley and Martey's (2009) finding that architectural contexts cue behavioural norms within a given virtual environment, we note that digital materiality plays a significant role in establishing both the bounded space and the fixed eguipment aspects of an online social occasion. The social occasion, in turn carries ground rules that guide an individual's involvement (eg, refined conversation in the Blue Note nightclub). Furthermore, virtual material that is configurable by users plays an important role in supporting others to get and sta involved in the joint activity (eg, Bow's go‐carts).

The role that digital material plays in achieving social presence through focused interaction in virtual environ ments suggests that future IS research attend to the material features of the medium in which people are interacting. While digital materiality in virtual reality might comprise clothes and furniture, in social media like Facebook, it takes the form of photographs and emoticons, among others. In addition to exploring the meaning that is attributed to such material, researchers would do well to investigate their role(s) in framing social occasions that invite focused interac tion. For example, to what extent do “thumbs up” icons help others to get and to stay involved in a Facebook thread?

## 7.1 | Limitations

These research contributions need to be qualified by our study's limitations. First, we relied on Aileen's qualitative characterization of the two incidents as indicators of the degree to which she experienced her interaction partners as socially present. Given the complexity of this multidimensional construct, and its conceptual ties to other equall multifaceted constructs (ie, personal and copresence, as well as perceptions of reality), we posit that relying on users situationally grounded descriptions has its merits. While an assessment of another's social presence would benefit from a combination of situational, behavioural, and perceptual indicators, we contend that relying on the interviewee's own, self-generated description of her perceptions as a gauge is superior to a measure derivec exclusively from perceptual survey measures. Given the multidimensional nature of social presence (see Appendix B), none of the scales currently available would have captured its diversity of meaning.

Second, in order to explore the microprocesses through and by which the virtual other is made “real." we have limitec our data analysis to two interactions reported by a single SL user. While this sample is admittedly restrictive and specific to the social and material conditions of a given, primarily recreational technology, it exhibits the requisite variety and richnes to develop this abductively derived model of social presence. The setting represents an extreme case (Eisenhardt, 1989) in which the phenomenon of interest, ie, social presence as an accomplishment, can be studied intensively and naturally.

Nevertheless, the small sample size is likely to raise questions about the generalizability of the results to other empirical settings. Since we chose the incidents we analysed for theoretical reasons, we can make no claims of across‐case generalizability. However, our study does achieve within‐case generalizability (A. S. Lee & Baskerville 2003). By developing a theory of social presence as an ongoing accomplishment (Table 1), through an extensive exploration of prior literature on social presence and its related concepts (Appendices A–D) juxtaposed wit Goffman's microsociological theory and our empirical data, we achieve generalization from empirical description to theory. As a result of our reliance on both induction and deduction, our research results promise to be both testabl and empirically valid, especially for users and designers of virtual social environments.

Third, the differences in Aileen's assessment of the other's social presence in the two examples may be confounded by the fact that Jacob was a stranger and Bow was a friend. While we acknowledge that repeated interactions with others increase the likelihood that joint engrossment in an activity can be accelerated and intensified by proactively inducing the other's involvement, there are nevertheless other means of achieving this interlocking involvement obligation among strangers. These include joint attention to material aspects of virtual spac and joint activity, as well as consistently verbalizing one's attentional focus. Future research might tease out the rol that prior knowledge of the other plays in making the other “real.”

## 8 | CONCLUSION

Given the considerable and long‐standing criticism of social presence as an objective or perceived capability of a given technology, this paper explores how Goffman's concept of involvement obligation can be used as the basis for an interactional understanding of social presence as a skilful, social accomplishment. We have abductively developed a three‐phase interactional model of social presence whereby different degrees of another actual user's “there”‐ness in a virtual environment can be generated. This model offers theoretical clarity on distinctions between and relationships among the different types of presence (ie, copresence and social presence and personal presence) as well as their link(s) to perceptions of reality.

## Larger study

The larger study—conducted between July 2008 and March 2010—from which Aileen's data were chosen, consiste of photodiary interviews (Latham, 2003) with 35 SL users who spent at least 10 hours a week on SL and who lived within driving distance of the first author.

## Photodiary interview method

Data collection proceeded as follows: The first contact with participants was a 2‐hour face‐to‐face interview that took place in a bookstore of the interviewee's choosing. Bookstores were chosen as venues for this study's initia interviews because they (1) offered free Internet access, thus allowing participants to go into SL during the interview, (2) were sufficiently public so that both the interviewer and interviewee could feel safe meeting a stranger, and (3 granted sufficient privacy—eg, coffee tables and lounge chairs—to conduct and tape‐record an interview without too many interruptions or too much background noise.

This face‐to‐face interview was followed by three weekly photodiary interviews (Latham, 2003): Over a 3‐week period. participants recorded photodiaries, composed of annotated screenshots of incidents that they regarded a particularly meaningful (see example below). These photodiaries were then emailed to the researcher ahead of time and then served as the basis for weekly, 1‐hour phone interviews. Interviewees were asked to record about five photodiary entries per week. The inclusion of photographs, which were easily produced using SL's snapshot feature provided the researcher insight into the (virtual) material conditions of a given incident. Specifics were then explored in the weekly phone interviews with the participant.

All interviews were audio‐recorded with the participant's permission. They were later transcribed. Given the consid erable time commitment required by this research (ie, approximately 8 h over a 4‐wk period), participants were paid \$150

## DATA ANALYSIS

Our analysis of the data was abductive (Locke et al., 2008), in the sense that we relied on Goffman's vast theorizing around social interaction and its role in the construction of the other's social presence as a sensitizing device (Walsham, 2006) for the generation of doubt, surprise, and mystery (Alvesson & Karreman, 2007). Abduction consists of three steps: (1) applying a theory as a sensitizing device, (2) observing a surprise in the empirical phenomenon i light of this theory, and (3) articulating a new theory that resolves the surprise (Alvesson & Karreman, 2007). We followed this process iteratively, reading and rereading our data (ie, photodiary photos and annotations, chatlogs, and interview transcripts of the two incidents) through the lens of the theory (ie, Goffman's writings on social inter actions and the role of psychoemotional involvement in it) and then rereading the theory through the lens of the data

Continuously cycling between the conceptual and empirical (Van Maanen, Sørensen, & Mitchell, 2007), we eventually identified Goffman's concept of involvement obligation as the linchpin of the microsociological process of making the virtual other real. The process of determining the centrality of involvement obligation as the fulcrum concept entailed progressively narrowing our theoretical focus on Goffman's constructs, starting initially with “situated activity systems” (Goffman, 1961), then moving to “interaction order” (Goffman, 1983) and “interaction ritual” (Goffman, 1967; Collins, 1988), before finally homing in on “involvement obligation” (Goffman, 1967).

To develop a model of the social presence mechanism (Table 1) however took many more iterations of not only engaging the data in a dialogue with our evolving theory (Alvesson & Karreman, 2007) but also comparing the two incidents. Surprises that emerged out of combining abduction with constant comparison (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) included the pervasiveness of compliments in the Racing Go‐Carts incident and the lack thereof in the Dancing wit Jacob incident. Seeking to understand the role of compliments in generating social presence, we came to recognize the significance of Goffman's theorizing of the self and the importance of having one's self‐presentation validated in order to feel personally present, ie, that the virtual environment is indistinguishable from the actual.

In interpreting the chatlogs throughout this process, we also relied on the principle of the hermeneutic circle which is central to the guidelines for interpretive research outlined by Klein and Myers (1999). This principle main tains that “human understanding is achieved by iterating between considering the interdependent meaning of parts and the whole that they form” (Klein & Myers, 1999, p. 72). Considering the whole of the interaction in terms of the social occasion that was being enacted through chat (ie, a first dance between strangers at a night club and a visit to friend's house), we then sought to understand the meaning of the individual utterances within it

To this end, we drew on concepts and techniques from discourse analysis (Putnam & Fairhurst, 2001), and conversation analysis (Atkinson & Heritage, 1984; Hutchby & Wooffitt, 2008), which focuses on the structure of language, eg, turn taking, messages/response pairs, and topic shifts. We analysed not only the meaning of the word that made up an utterance to understand how a given entry in the chatlog contributed to or detracted from the ultimate meaning of the interaction (ie, the appropriateness of a response) but also the structure of the interaction (ie, turn taking and speed and length of response).

And to complement the chat logs, we also drew on data generated during the photodiary interviews, which accorded Aileen distance from the events. This distancing encouraged reflection and collaborative sensemaking with the interviewer.
