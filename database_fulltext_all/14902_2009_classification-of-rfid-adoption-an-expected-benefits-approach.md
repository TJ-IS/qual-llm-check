# Classification of RFID adoption: An expected benefits approach

<sup>a</sup> Department of Management & Entrepreneurship, Rohrer College of Business, Rowan University, 201 Mullica Hill Road, Glassboro, NJ 08028, USA <sup>b</sup> Information Operations & Technology Management, University of Toledo, 2801 W. Bancroft Street, MS # 103, Toledo, OH 43606, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

## A B S T R A C T

While expected benefits have been seen as one of the main drivers of RFID adoption, there is little understanding of what benefits should be considered when exploring RFID adoption. Further, the relationships between expected benefits and the type of adoption has not been systematically delineated. We identified cost savings, supply chain visibility, and new process creation as the three key benefits of RFID adoption and then identified the ‘‘scale’’ and ‘‘scope’’ of RFID implementation as the key dimensions of RFID adoption. Based on these, we proposed a four-category classification for the type of RFID adoption. Finally we showed how the three expected benefits are associated with the scale and scope of adoption. In particular, the findings highlighted the significant potential of RFID when it is integrated with other technologies such as global positioning or product life cycle management systems. Under various situations, a firm may perceive different benefits and value some benefits more than the others; this combination leads the firm to choose a certain type of RFID application. The model was validated using secondary case data from seven organizations that had adopted RFID.

## 1. Introduction

The commercial use of RFIDs, which started more than 20 years ago, resulted in predictions of sweeping changes in the way that organizations tracked goods in their supply chains. Large organizations such as Wal-Mart, Proctor & Gamble, Gillette, and HP decided to use RFIDs in their supply chains leading to ripple effects for their suppliers. At the same time, RFID remains a niche technology whose benefits have eluded full use in many firms. Some have seen disappointing ROI from their RFID implementation and others have found themselves unable to adopt and take advantage of the technology. It is widely acknowledged that RFID will become the primary technology for tracking products and managing inventory, but firms adopting RFID should carefully consider its long-term benefits and impact rather than simply following competitors.Studies of RFID use have focused on issues such as the need for large investments, and technical problems, such as security and inter-operability [3]. Current literature has neither looked at the benefits of adopting RFIDs nor shown how these benefits could influence the parameters and dimensions of its adoption. We used an expected benefits approach to understanding and explaining why firms adopt RFID and the ways in which it can be introduced into the organization. We attempted to answer three questions:

(1) What are the major benefits of RFID adoption as expected by a firm?

(2) What are the different ways in which RFID is adopted? That is, what are the dimensions that characterize the organizational adoption of RFID?

(3) How are expected benefits associated with the way in which RFID is adopted?

A case study approach was used for this research because of the nascent nature of the technology and its evolutionary application aspects. Seven case studies were examined to infer the relationships between benefits and types of RFID implementation.

## 2. Literature review

RFID technology has the potential to be an enterprise wide technology with inter-organizational implications in the same vein as the Internet and networked PCs. It can bring fundamental changes to business processes and society. Its benefits include replacement of the UPC and readers with the RFID. However, RFID, when fully deployed and integrated, can integrate and help manage purchasing, production processes, inventory control, inter-organizational movement of materials and information in the supply chain, to name a few areas, not including personal and social use. The technology mainly consists of four parts: RFID tags, readers and antennas, RFID middleware, and Back-end RFID enterprise service.

Frequently, innovations in technology have shaped everchanging business dynamics, requiring careful attention. Accordingly, researchers have paid significant attention to factors that influence the organizational adoption of innovative technology [11]. Noticeably, compatibility, relative advantage, and complexity have been identified as three characteristics that determine the technologies that are adopted in practice. Building on these factors, researchers have empirically studied characteristics and factors relevant to the firm-level adoption of innovative or new technologies in different contexts.

Research has shown that, in general, different factors affect early and later adopters of innovative technology or practices. While internal and external pressures drive late adopters, the motivation to attain strategic competitiveness, which closely relates to expected benefits, impels early adopters to take initiatives to adopt new and innovative technologies. If a new technology presents potential benefits that could bring a competitive edge to a company, it would probably proceed to implement the technology although they may not see significant financial return in the near term. For instance, expected desirability had a strong impact on the intent to adopt electronic trading systems in the early stages of their use; among other factors there were mimetic, coercive, competitive pressures and organizational readiness [7].

Although competitive pressure leads many firms like suppliers of Wal-Mart to adopt the technology, benefits from its adoption have been a significant factor influencing firms in their adoption decision. An empirical study reported that retailers adopted RFID primarily because they expected benefits such as being able to offer quality customer service [13]. Thus we focused our study on the benefits that firms expected from the adoption of RFID technologies, and examined how the benefits related to ways in which the firms adopted it.

## 2.1. Expected benefits

External pressures, expected benefits, and organizational readiness have been considered to be the important factors that influence adoption of new technology. Among these, expected benefits form the most influential driver of its adoption [10]. While the presence of external pressure from competitors tends to urge organizations to adopt RFID, and consideration of organizational readiness, in many cases, constrains companies to move cautiously, expected benefits encourage firms to deliberate on and plan an adoption strategy based on their business context

Expected benefits are the anticipated advantages that an innovation can provide an organization. Expected RFID benefits thus are the recognition of the relative advantages that RFID technology can provide an organization. Benefits can be either direct or indirect. Primarily, direct benefits are operational in nature: cost savings and cycle time improvement. Indirect benefits are somewhat intangible advantages that come from innovative use of a technology, such as the creation of new business processes. Various studies have identified benefits of RFID adoption, such as better of inventory control, reduced labor costs, product transshipments reduction, inventory obsolescence reduction, and rich information exchange among participants in a supply chain (see Table 1). We selected a variety of benefits and split them into three categories: (1) cost savings through reductions in theft, labor, and inventory costs, (2) supply chain visibility, and (3) new business creation. These benefits were selected because they reflect essential direct and indirect advantages.

Cost savings drive firms to invest in RFID. One source is theft reduction. Proctor & Gamble estimated the loss by shrinkage (product theft) was 2% of revenue for consumer goods [8]. The total losses that the retail industry faced due to shoplifting and employee theft in 2005, was of the order of \$30 billion. According to the 2005 National Retail Security Survey by the National Retail Federation, losses due to theft have been 3.2% of sales for home improvement goods, 2.6% for crafts/hobbies, 2.4% for supermarkets, 2.2% for pharmacies, and 1.6% for apparel stores. Woolworth stated that, 55% of all product theft happens in the supply chain before goods reach the retail store. Effective tracking and quick authenticity check via RFID reduce such shrinkage. Another source of cost savings is in decreased labor costs. It takes 1 min and 40 s to scan 1000 items through RFID scanners and 33 min (20 times as long) using bar-code technology. There is no need for repeated search and scanning in the inventory tracking and storing processes. For instance, the Gillette Company, prior to using RFID, had to scan its razor blades 5 times during their movement from the manufacturing facility to a packaging center. Somebody had to count the number of cases on each pallet to make sure that each case held the right product. RFID eliminated this, increasing operating efficiency by 400%. It also decreased errors in manual work.

Supply chain visibility (SCV), the second expected benefit, is the firm’s ability to track the flow of goods, inventory, and information in the supply chain in a timely manner. A firm having SCV can reduce bottlenecks, out-of-stock or low inventory levels in its supply chain. It significantly lowers the uncertainty of goods in a supply chain and allows firms to track and manage the flow of inventory or products. As a result, this decreases bullwhip effects. For instance, Wal-Mart reduced its inventory stocks by 10% due to RFID adoption. Furthermore, rich information exchange among partners makes it easier for firms to coordinate production and distribution, outsourcing functions and services, and partner with suppliers and intermediaries. The accumulated information helps firms analyze the performance of suppliers and identify operations that should be improved.

The third expected benefit comes from creating a new business process or a new product. As a result of the communication associated with RFID, inventory handlers do not have to scan products manually; the process can be automated. For example, Pierrel-Ospedali, an Italian pharmaceutical company, requires sterilization of some of its products at high temperatures. Inaccurate temperatures result in the batch of bottles being destroyed. Bar-code labels, if used, are subject to damage in the high temperature environment. This increases the costs of production and leaving open the possibility of human errors and time delays. The company adopted an RFID tag and controlled the sterilization process automatically. Using an RFID application that survives the harsh environment, employees just collect the data at the start and the end of the process on autoclave. Another example is in credit card industry: Japan has actively integrated credit cards with RFID chips, allowing 35 million citizens of Tokyo to travel a number of railway lines and bus routes with a single card. Hong Kong, Singapore, India, and Thailand have similarly implemented RFID-enabled transport cards.

## 2.2. Dimensions of RFID adoption

Economies of scale and scope have been widely recognized in the context of production and manufacturing. Scale economies arise when a single technology is intensively and broadly implemented in business processes. The ‘‘scope’’ dimension refers to situations where two or more technologies are jointly used; e.g., GPS, Bluetooth, and programmable logic controllers. WiMax makes the transmission of digital information possible over distances of 25–30 miles and ZigBee coordinates communications among thousands of tiny sensors. These technologies could provide information tracking and transmission. Integrated applications enable shippers to identify where and when items are unlawfully removed from the supply chain.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Background

Most firms are in the early stages of adoption of RFID technology and are in the process of understanding relevant drivers and issues. Available information therefore relates to the initial stages of adoption. It is based on the adopting firms’ perceptions of expected future benefits and outcomes, rather than on analyses of accrued measured benefits. Furthermore, our research questions were exploratory in nature; we tried to identify and analyze expected benefits of RFID adoption, and show how those benefits lead to specific modes of adoption. It was thus essential to understand and describe the actions of the adopting firms. We found it appropriate to use a qualitative method of enquiry that would allow us to collect appropriately detailed and maybe unstructured information. Qualitative methods have been productively applied where there is a need to describe and explain the phenomenon under study and have been used for examining organizational adoption of various information technologies [2].

## 3.2. Sampling

We used a multiple case study design, collecting secondary case study data on RFID adoption in seven firms from seven industries.

Our choice of firms was predicated on four aspects. First, they must have adopted or be in the process of adopting RFID’ this, allowed us to identify the expected benefits that had led to their adoption decision. Second, RFID adoption must have occurred in one or more processes; this, helped us determine differences in scale and scope. Third, the firms were selected from different industries, so that we could study the expected outcomes and benefits in a broad range of contexts. Fourth, since the value of scale and scope could independently be ‘‘high’’ or ‘‘low’’, we expected four types of firms, depending on the relative degrees of scale and scope of adoption. The choice of seven firms allowed us to investigate at least one firm of each type, so that we could achieve theoretical replication. We focused our data collection on (a) the number of intra- and inter-organizational functions and activities in which the firms had adopted or were in the process of adopting RFID and (b) the expected benefits and outcomes that influenced the adoption. Our sources of data included information about the firms from academic and practitioner literature, as well as media reports and articles. Table 2 shows the firms.

## 3.3. Data sources and data analysis

Data were collected from secondary sources that contained information on RFID adoption at the seven firms. To ensure reliability of the data, we used peer reviewed articles and the firm’s websites, to keep abreast of trends at the firms. Data analysis was performed in two phases:

1. Within case analysis for each of the seven cases in the framework of the broad theoretical structure. We identified, for each, three broad categories of codes; those that described:

a. major expected benefits from RFID adoption,

b. the scope and scale of RFID adoption, and

c. the relationship between expected benefits and RFID adoption.

2. Cross-case analysis that compared findings across the firms, to identify a fourth category of codes: representative patterns of RFID adoption and the expected benefits of each.

The articles were first coded independently by two of the authors. Then the two sets of codes were verified against one another. Similar codes were included in the final set. Differences were resolved by the three authors in discussion and incorporated in the final set of codes.

## 4. Within-case analysis

The description of each case consisted of background information, benefits that the organization expected, and its RFID application pattern based on its scale and scope dimensions. We refrained from subjective interpretation in an attempt to obtain greater objectivity.

## 4.1. Legoland

LEGOLAND<sup>1</sup> Denmark opened in Billund Denmark in 1968 as a Theme Park with 6-million LEGO<sup>1</sup> bricks. The business focused on offering creative and fun activities to families. It had at least 1.6 million guests each season. About 1600 children are lost annually. In Spring 2005, the company adopted an RFID application developed by AeroScout<sup>1</sup>. Parents wear an RFID bracelet which gives signals on the location of their children through instant text messages on their mobile phones. This has relieved parents of anxiety and Legoland management from the direct and indirect costs of recovering children. Furthermore, the data that the tags generate are useful in visualizing and analyzing how families are using the park. It provides crucial information on park service operations (managing long lines, gauging visitors’ interests in new rides, finding the right place for new building sets, etc.). The cost of deploying the system, including readers, servers and tags is between \$100,000 and \$150,000. Legoland displays low levels of scale and scope of technology because it employs the technology within the theme park only, and it does not combine many technologies to operate the system.

## 4.2. Library in the University of Nevada, Las Vegas

By 2003, more than 300 libraries in the United States had implemented RFID technology. Among the first adopters were the libraries in the University of Nevada, Las Vegas. The technology was adopted in one main library and three branches. According to the 2004 library report, the collections total nearly 1,000,000 monographs, 8500 serials subscriptions, 12,000 serial titles accessible through aggregated databases, over electronic indexes, databases or reference sources, and 27,000 media materials. Library chores are quite repetitive (check-in and check-out, sorting for re-shelving, and inventory management). To track, identify, and control library material more efficiently, the library implemented RFID in 2004; more than 500 lost items were found, after tagging 600,000 items in the collection, this saved the library \$40,000 in replacement costs. Other results have been quicker check-in and - out, better inventory management, better monitoring of usage, and better knowledge of collection development status. Furthermore, RFID utilization has freed up staff members to work at important tasks around the library rather than repetitious work. As with Legoland, the use of RFID technology in the library system was internally focused with a well defined, narrow set of objectives. No significant application combined technologies, hence the process was narrow in scope.

## 4.3. The International Air Transport Association (IATA)

IATA, a global trade organization for air transport, represents 94% of scheduled international air traffic. It tries to improve the operations of the airline industry by helping decision makers better understand the aviation industry and its benefits. Its members consist of more than 260 airlines worldwide. The IATA has been using RFID technology to manage the huge costs of mishandled baggage. Approximately 1.7 billion bags pass through its system every year and roughly 17 million bags, are misallocated. On average, it costs the industry \$100 to cope with one mishandled bag, resulting in \$1.7 billion loss per year. In 2004, it chose RFID over the bar-code technology because of its accuracy and speed. In 2005 the IATA introduced a global standard (in the 850–950 MHz frequency band) for RFID baggage tags; this was endorsed by all IATA member airlines in November 2005. The expected benefits were that RFID would significantly reduce the number of misplaced items and that the overall savings would amount to \$1 billion a year. While Legoland and the UNLV libraries applied RFID in local areas, the IATA applied it on a worldwide scale; however, its scope was limited in functionality as RFID was used mainly for tracking purpose.

## 4.4. Wal-Mart

Wal-Mart’s low cost strategy and global supply chain have allowed it to become the world’s largest retailer, with \$344.99 billion revenue at the end of 2006. There are 3900 stores in the Unites States and 2900 abroad. Each day, more than 14 million customers shop at a Wal-Mart store and there are more than 1.3 million employs worldwide. To sustain its competitiveness Wal-Mart needs to transport and track products efficiently. With the recognition of the potential benefits that RFID could bring to the company, it required its top 100 suppliers to put tags on their products by January 2005. Although these RFID mandates intensified a love–hate relationship between the retailer and its suppliers, the implementation was successful. A study in October 2005 by the RFID Research Center at the University of Arkansas, reported a 16% reduction in ‘‘out-of-stocks’’ at Wal-Mart stores thanks to RFID operations. Wal-Mart also announced in 2004 that it would extend RFID adoption to its top 200 suppliers by January 2006 and to 300 suppliers and more stores afterwards. Rollin Ford, executive VP and CIO for Wal-Mart expected that the technology can bring multiple benefits when coupled with new work processes. Wal-Mart has focused the use of RFID on tracking products. Their moves have encouraged other retailers and companies to pay more attention to RFID. The use of RFID technology by Wal-Mart is both intense and extensive and global in range.

## 4.5. Intel

Intel Corp., the largest semiconductor manufacturer in the world, has 11 fabrication facilities and 6 assembly and test facilities around the world. Their annual sales in 2005 exceeded \$38.8 billion. Approximately 80% of PCs use Intel’s microprocessors. One of the Intel’s primary concerns is to manage \$3.5 billion of inventory in its supply chain. At any given time, it holds a month’s worth of inventory that depreciates by as much as 5%, that is, by \$175 million per month. Intel stacks products in its warehouses upon production and dispatches them to customers only after it receives an order. As a result, its inventory stays in warehouses along its supply chains. To reduce inventory level and lead time, Intel has implemented a new concept of supply chain management: Inventory in Motion. Its essential idea lies in keeping products moving in supply chains toward customers, requiring shipping products toward hubs before receiving orders and assembling the products at the hub upon order placement. Intel’s goal was to decrease its delivery time from 3 to 1 week. It expected that RFID combined with GPS technology would help achieve this goal. It uses its own RFID assisted by batterypowered GPS trans-receivers for tracking its container movement on a global scale, and plans to expand this combined application along its supply chain. As of 2004, it had cut \$22 million in the course of streamlining its inventory and anticipated more savings [1]. The use of RFID technology by Intel is multifaceted, global in scale, and pervades many aspects of the Intel’s business activities requiring both coordination across activities and interoperability with other technologies.

## 4.6. Johnson Controls

Johnson Controls (JC), located in Livermore, California, supplies a range of seats for car and truck models to New United Motor

Manufacturers Incorporated (NUMMI), a joint production facility of Toyota and General Motors, housed in Fremont, California. Since NUMMI operates its system in Just-In-Time mode, JC receives orders every hour and delivers car seats as many as 12 times a day directly to the production line of NUMMI, which requires that JC pay careful attention to delivery: its production system is designed to let JC unload car seats directly to its conveyors for immediate assembly. Human errors frequently occurred in the initial manual system causing NUMMI to be dissatisfied with JC. To reduce human errors, later chose an RFID system by Escort Memory Systems (EMS) because of concerns that the dirty and abrasive working environment could impair barcodes. JC installed an RFID system on the truck seat production line and attached writable and readable tags on the bottom of the truck seat pallets that contained recipe information. The RFID technology was integrated with a touch-screen programmable logic controller (PLC) that operators used in each work process. As the pallet went though each station, the RFID reader read the information in the RFID tag and transmitted the information to the PLC, through which operators could check whether or not the car seat required any alteration. The PLC allowed the conveyor to move down only if the car seat did not need any work in the work station or if necessary work had been executed. When tasks were completed, the PLC sent the information to a RFID writer and it added the information to the tag underneath the car seat. Then the PLC would let the conveyor move down the line. This process repeated at the final station, where the PLC verified the seat’s recipe information with the actual modification record. This combined application of RFID with the manufacturing system made a new production-control process possible. Some seats needed special modification, such as adding airbags. With the integrated RFID application, however, it was possible to test all the seats in the same line. JC reports that RFID implementation saved time and dramatically reduced human errors. While the scale of RFID usage at JC is in the manufacturing unit, the scope of technology is broad, with integrated application.

## 4.7. Metro Group

The Metro Group, a supermarket chain based in Du¨ sseldorf, Germany, is the third biggest retailer in the world. It employs more than 270,000 employees from 150 nations and over 2400 outlets are distributed in 30 countries in Europe, Africa, and Asia. Metro Group has a differentiation strategy, intending to become a technology leader through better supply chain performance and customer service. The company’s effort could be summarized in its ‘‘Future Store Initiative’’, launched on April 26, 2005 with the support of more than 60 cooperating partners such as IBM, Intel, SAP, and T-Systems. The initiative aimed at setting new technological standards for the retail industry and at fostering modernization process in commerce. To implement the initiative, the group created the Future Store in Rheinberg, Germany where the Metro Group tests innovative applications for warehouse management. The store, with an area of 1650 m<sup>2</sup>, offers a futuristic experience of innovation to customers, where RFID plays a key role. RFID is integrated with the shopping carts, informational terminal, and self-check-out counters. For instance, the RFID-enabled shopping carts can read item information and display what items a customer puts in the cart. The cart is connected to a central IS so that a customer can check it out quickly. Smart shelves can also determine how many items the shelves contain. A Smart Shelf can monitor the level of inventory on the shelf. If a product runs low or is placed incorrectly, the Smart Shelf transmits this information to employees and they restock the products. This integrated application results in a highly efficient operation. This therefore is an illustration of an ‘‘integrated’’ RFID application. In general, in a retail store setting, implementing RFID application with other technologies, consumers can have access to personalized and interactive displays, shelf-mounted wireless and changeable price displays, automated check-out and payment systems, shopperspecific personal shopping assistants and smart carts, and RFIDenabled products and shelves. Metro’s savings on delivery goods in Germany is estimated as about \$7.8 million a year. Metro is using RFID technology on a global scale and with significant integration across applications.

## 5. Cross-case analysis

There were two main across-case findings. First, we found that all the organizations we studied had varying scope and scale of adoption. That is, the scale and scope of adoption could be (independently) high or low. Second, we found that the three benefits of cost reduction, supply chain visibility and process redesign varied with the scale and scope of RFID adoption. That is, there were specific benefits for a given scope and scale of production.

## 5.1. Classification of RFID adoption

Dimensions of scale and scope can be used to construct a typology that classifies patterns of RFID adoption into four parts. Decision makers in a firm have to decide the scale and scope of their implementation of the technology. Some may limit the utilization to selected products or inventories or to a certain stage of a product life cycle, whereas others may apply it to all products across the product life cycles along with other technologies. ## 5.2. Expected benefits and RFID adoption

It is interesting to examine how each benefit or combinations of expected benefits relate to each type of RFID adoption (Table 3).

(1) Cost-savings and RFID adoption: Cost savings comes from various sources. Parents feel awful when their children disappear in an amusement park. RFID enables Legoland to save costs for finding lost children. Libraries in UNLV report that RFID implementation allows them to find lost items, saving \$40,000 per year. They also derive savings from reducing repetitious tasks for employees and from better inventory management. These organizations that use RFID for internal solo application, however, display relatively small savings compared to other organizations that apply RFID on a wider scale and scope. Wal-Mart’s 16% decrease in out-of-stocks is a huge source of savings, considering that it operates 3900 stores in the Unites States and 2900 abroad. Major cost savings comes from automatic generation of pick lists of items, eliminating the need for restocking the shelves periodically and yielding added savings in labor and time. The fact that Wal-Mart pushes its suppliers to adopt RFID presents increasing amount of savings related to increasing scale of RFID utilization. Similarly for IATA, the primary reason for RFID adoption is to decrease the costs to handle misallocated items at airports worldwide. It expects at least \$1 billion of savings a year through RFID application. All companies ultimately purport to reap cost savings. The extent of these dependi on the type of RFID adoption, but the cost savings increase as the scale and scope of RFID amplify.

(2) Supply chain visibility and RFID adoption: Supply chain visibility plays a significant role for companies that have to manage a number of suppliers in various regions; long and complicated supply chains result in long lead times and slow information flows. Firms managing scattered suppliers face problems such as the inability to see and identify the movement of inventory at the right time and the right place. The tracking ability of RFID technology can reduce stock-outs and soften bullwhip effects. For this reason, the superior traceability of RFID has attracted those firms’ interests. Intel made a decision to implement RFID and reaped \$22 million savings from its partial implementation in 2004. It is planning to manage inventories globally through GPS-equipped RFID application. Many retailers and manufacturing companies sense the imperative need to acquire high traceability to reduce inventory level and to keep inventory moving in supply chains. Wal-Mart and Metro Group exemplify those types of firms. Enhanced supply chain visibility for Metro Group helped it to achieve an 11% reduction in out-of-stocks at its RFID test sites in year 2005. Companies that manage complex supply chains will perceive high supply chain visibility to be a significant benefit from RFID utilization and seriously consider adopting the technology.

(3) New process creation and RFID adoption: RFID adoption implies new process creation in business operations. Before implementing RFID, Legoland had to announce or dispatch its crews to find a lost child. Launching RFID eliminated these processes because parents could detect their children’s location through their mobile device that received RFID communication. The new children-tracking process provided data for better operations of the park. JC, for example, combined RFID with a touch-screen PLC to automate testing procedures. Employees can now decide which production processes are required for making a particular car seat, without actually intervening in the production line, enhancing the accuracy and the speed of production and delivery. The ‘‘Inventory in Motion’’ strategy by Intel can trace its products along its global supply chain by using a combination of GPS and RFID. Once an order is placed, the RFID technology conveys products from the nearest distribution center to customers and keeps the supply chain filled and moving. Of all the companies studied, Metro Group is probably the best example of new business process creation through the integration of RFID with other technologies. The company uses RFID to implement a differentiation strategy in the retail industry. For example, the Smart Cart system reads the RFID tags attached to the products in it and conveys the information to the Check-Out system, thus saving check-out time. The Smart Cart system also conveys the tag information to the Advertisement System, which generates suggestions tailored to each customer, based on the items in the cart. Different expected benefits will thus lead organizations to different types of RFID adoption.

## 6. Conclusion

We studied cases of RFID adoption and deployment in seven organizations, ranging from firms at the top of the Fortune 100 list to a university library. The intra- and inter-case analyses documented both similarities and differences in deployment of RFID technology and we attempted to find discernible patterns. This resulted in our developing a classification scheme with categories: internal solo, multiple solo, internal integrated and multiple integrated applications.

The classification framework associated RFID applications with the benefits expected from their adoption. The extent of cost savings in each quadrant hinges on the levels of scale and scope of RFID application. Firms that emphasize increasing operational efficiency through RFID should perceive supply chain visibility as essential and the scale of economy will materialize as they implement RFID across their supply chains. But firms that expect competitive advantage through RFID would perceive new process creation to be desirable and strive to creatively and strategically take advantage of RFID integrated with other technologies.

First, we identified cost-saving, supply chain visibility, and new process creation as major expected benefits that a firm may realize from RFID implementation. Second, the framework introduced two dimensions of RFID adoption strategy: scale and scope providing four different adoption types on the basis of these dimensions. This provides some useful guidelines to managers. First, it suggests that managers may decide on a RFID application boundary with respect to the scale and scope of the application. Decision makers face difficulties in finding the right type of RFID application for their company. The classification scheme gives a picture of the different types of applications are and how they could evolve, depending on the scale and the scope that a firm defines.

Second, three major expected benefits and their relationship with application type were identified; this should help firms consider which benefits they consider most important and thus which direction they should to take in RFID adoption. In terms of limitations, the secondary data used in this research does not provide the same degree of contextual information as primary data.
