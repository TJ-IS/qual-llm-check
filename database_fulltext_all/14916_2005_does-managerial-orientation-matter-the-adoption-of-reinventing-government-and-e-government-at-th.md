# Does managerial orientation matter? The adoption of reinventing government and e-government at the municipal level\*

M. Jae Moon<sup>†,‡</sup> & Donald F. Norris<sup>§</sup>

<sup>†</sup>Department of Public Administration, Korea University, Anam-Dong 5-1, Sungbuk-gu, Seoul 136-701, Korea, email: mjmoon@korea.ac.kr, <sup>‡</sup>George Bush School of Government and Public Service, Texas A & M University, email: jmoon@bushschool.tamu.edu, and <sup>§</sup>Maryland Institute for Policy Analysis and Research, Professor of Public Policy, 427 Public Policy Building, University of Maryland, Baltimore County, Baltimore, MD 21250, USA, email: norris@umbc.edu

Abstract. This paper explores the effect of managerial innovativeness in municipal government on the adoption of e-government, and it examines the association between the adoption of e-government and its outcome. The authors posit an exploratory model: The first part of the model shows how adoption of municipal egovernment is determined by managerial innovativeness orientation, government capacity and institutional characteristics such as city size and government type. The second part suggests how e-government outcomes are associated with the adoption of e-government, government capacity and institutional characteristics. Analysing two different survey data sets of American municipal reinvention and egovernment, this study finds that managerial innovativeness orientation and city size are the most compelling determinants of municipal e-government adoption. Different levels of e-government adoption may yield different outcomes.

Keywords: e-government, reinventing government, managerial innovativeness, public management

## INTRODUCTION

Within the past seven to 10 years, local governments in the United States have adopted and implemented electronic government – or e-government – as a means of delivering government information and services (Norris et al., 2001; Ho, 2002; Moon, 2002; Holden et al., 2003; Norris & Moon, forthcoming). Local governments have been able to adopt and implement egovernment because of rapid advances in the underlying hardware and software technologies (the ‘e’) that support e-government during this period. However, adoption of e-government has been driven more by government improvement than by technological innovation. That is, local governments have adopted e-government largely because it holds great promise to improve government efficiency and effectiveness, to improve citizen service delivery and to transform government itself. Adoption has been so rapid that e-government is now standard for nearly all local governments of all sizes in the United States.

Local governments have been continuous adopters of information technologies since they became commercially available in the 1950s. Adoption has proceeded through several phases of information technology (IT), including mainframes, minicomputers, PCs, networks and currently, the world wide web and the internet. However, the nature and scope of e-government initiatives are distinct from earlier applications and management of IT in the public sector. Previously, governments adopted IT primarily to enhance managerial efficiency by improving the storage, processing, analysis and retrieval of data, and by improving work automation in both intra-agency and interagency contexts. Driven by the advent of internet technology, governments started paying more attention to external applications of IT and providing information and public services to other public agencies, businesses and citizens using the web.

Despite staffing, technical, financial and other limitations, many municipal governments have continued to pursue e-government initiatives and have made progress in basic e-government functions (Norris et al., 2001; Ho, 2002; Moon, 2002; Holden et al., 2003; Norris & Moon, forthcoming). For example, the majority of municipal governments have their own web sites to provide public information to citizens, and more than half of municipal governments have established intranet systems. Less widespread are efforts to offer online financial and service transactions and to provide opportunities for interactive political and policy participation (Holden et al., 2003; Norris & Moon, forthcoming). Some argue that e-government could change the paradigm of public service delivery at the local level (Ho, 2002), indicating a potential relationship between e-government initiatives and local managerial innovations.

Municipal e-government continues to evolve, and some studies have explored its nature and scope, and the structural characteristics (form of government, government size, etc.) that facilitate its development. (Ho, 2002; Moon, 2002; Holden et al., 2003; Norris & Moon, forthcoming). However, these studies have not effectively investigated the internal link between a culture of innovation (i.e. a managerial innovation orientation) and the development of e-government. This study will investigate the particular association between these two dimensions by examining two survey data sets: the 1997 Reinventing Government Survey of the International City/ County Management Association (ICMA) and the 2000 Electronic Government Survey of the ICMA and Public Technology, Inc (PTI). The merged data set provides unique and extensive information about the managerial reform efforts of municipal governments (from the 1997 survey) and the scope of e-government initiatives (from the 2000 survey). This study will present an exploratory theoretical framework that reflects the literature of adoption and diffusion studies, as well as e-government research. The description of data collection and variables will be followed by statistical analyses. The paper will conclude with some managerial implications, as well as some limitations and prospects of the study.

## MANAGERIAL INNOVATIONS, THE DIFFUSION OF E-GOVERNMENT AND GOVERNMENT CHARACTERISTICS

Many studies have sought to identify the factors that contribute to the diffusion of innovations (either technological, policy or managerial), producing a wide variety of theories. Surveying various diffusion models in policy research, Berry & Berry (1999) posit two categories of innovation and diffusion models: diffusion models and internal determinants models. They present four major diffusion models, including the national interaction model (learning model), the regional diffusion model, leader–laggard models and vertical influence models; they also identify internal factors (motivation, size, resources, etc.) in internal determinants models. Understanding the complexity of innovation adoption decisions and multiple determining factors, they argue that no single diffusion model best explains all cases.

Influenced by the abundant literature on innovation adoption and diffusion (Rogers, 1995), much of the IT literature in public administration has focused on the adoption of IT in the public sector. For example, many studies (Bugler & Bretschneider, 1993; Brudney & Selden, 1995; Norris & Kraemer, 1996; Norris & Demeter, 1999; Norris & Campillo, 2000; Moon, 2002; Norris & Moon, forthcoming) have found that government size is one of the primary factors related to IT adoption. Studying the effects of organizational environment (size and service demand) and organizational characteristics (professionalism, slack resource and administrative performance) on IT adoption in small city governments, Brudney & Selden (1995) find that government size and professionalism are the primary determinants of the adoption of computer technology.

Like the technology acceptance model<sup>1</sup> in the management information system literature, some studies have emphasized individual (human) factors (Northrop et al., 1994; Nedovic¢- Budic¢ & Godschalk, 1996). Northrop et al. (1994) identify three individual factors (training, friendliness of software and the user’s computer background), while Nedovic¢-Budic¢ & Godschalk (1996) suggest eight human factors at work in the adoption of geographical information system (GIS) in local governments (perceived relative advantage of the innovation, personal values and beliefs about computerized technology, computer experience, perceived complexity of the innovation, exposure to innovation, computer/GIS anxiety, attitude towards work-related change, and communication behaviour).

Although the previous literature has explored organizational and individual determinants of IT adoption, it does not address the effect of a culture of innovation (in fact, it should be part of organizational characteristics) on the adoption of IT and the development of e-government. In his comprehensive study of the diffusion of innovations, Rogers (1995) presents five categories of determining variables for the rate of adoption: perceived attributes of the innovation, type of innovation decision, communication channels, nature of the social system and extent of the change agent’s promotion efforts. Innovation culture is not Rogers’ central focus, although he briefly discusses cultural relativism and the anthropological perspective on culture.

In their extensive study of the process of technological innovation, Tornatzky & Fleischer (1990) focus on organizational structure (formalization, centralization and boundary-spanning structures), process factors (communication), as well as size and slack resources.

Surprisingly, the influence of innovation culture and orientation towards innovation are not examined much, although one may expect a primary predictor of innovation adoption to be the organization’s previous attitudes towards, or decisions about, similar or relevant innovations. Such factors can easily be overlooked when we examine the adoption of a particular innovation separately and statistically.

## Managerial innovation orientation

Local governments approach managerial and technological innovation from a variety of perspectives, and some are more receptive to innovation and new practices than others. There is a difference in the general adoptability of innovations among governments because governments have different degrees of tolerance towards risk and have different experiences (positive or negative) with innovation. In general, entrepreneurial and innovation-oriented governments tend to be more receptive to new managerial and technical approaches. In particular, governments that implement managerial innovations more actively may have a strong innovation culture, making them likely to adopt e-government initiatives more easily and with less administrative resistance. In other words, governments with a management culture that is more receptive to innovation<sup>2</sup> are more likely to perceive the value of innovations, such as egovernment, and to adopt them (Tushman & O’Reilly, 1997).

## Government capacity

Another issue that is closely tied to the adoption of e-government is the level of resources available to governments. The extent to which a government enjoys resources is closely associated with its capacity to plan and implement a particular innovation and with its willingness to take a certain level of risk. Extensive research has studied the relationship between resource availability and innovation adoption (Norris, 1984; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990; Brudney & Selden, 1995; Berry & Berry, 1999). Resources may include personnel, knowledge, financial resources or the actual technical assets (hardware and software) of the government. The propensity to adopt e-government may be influenced by many IT-related government-capacity factors, including technical and financial support. Relatively speaking, a technologically rich and resourceful organization may be less concerned about the costs of adoption because of its ability to generate higher, relevant forms of organizational slack. This view describes the resource-push perspective (Moon & deLeon, 2001), which hypothesizes a positive association between the implementation of e-government and organizational capacities, such as technical and financial capacity (Norris & Kraemer, 1996).

## City size

Size is considered to be one of the major variables in the innovation adoption literature. Often, it is presented as one of many organizational characteristics or as a proxy variable for the environmental context (Brudney & Selden, 1995). A city’s size is related to the magnitude of its government, level of resources and public services. Large city governments are more likely to adopt e-government than small ones because larger cities are under greater pressure to find alternative ways to provide public services; larger cities also have more resources (including more and better trained staff, a larger budget and often a formal and well-established IT department) to pursue alternatives. The relationship between organizational size and the probability of adopting an innovation (technical or managerial) has been widely studied (Smith & Taebel, 1985; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990; Norris & Kraemer, 1996; Norris & Demeter, 1999; Weare et al., 1999; Musso et al., 2000; Norris & Campillo, 2000; Moon & deLeon, 2001; Holden et al., 2003; Norris & Moon, forthcoming). Some of the previous literature on technology diffusion and adoption has found that larger organizations tend to adopt new technology and innovations more frequently than their smaller counterparts. Studying the development and spread of municipal web pages in California, Weare et al. (1999) and Musso et al. (2000) find that adopters are more likely to be in cities with larger, more affluent and more politically active populations than non-adopters. From an analysis of 79 municipal governments portal sites, West (2001b) finds that large municipalities are more likely to offer public services online. West (2001b) also points out that larger cities may be able to ‘spread the costs of new technology out over a larger population and tax base’ (p. 5). Overall, the literature suggests that larger governments are more likely to be early adopters of e-government than smaller ones.

## Government type

There has been much discussion of the differences between council-city manager and mayorcouncil governments in policy and managerial innovations. It has been argued that cities with professional city managers introduce managerial and technological innovations more readily into the public sphere because they have been trained to value professionalism and efficiency. Svara (1990) suggests that the co-operative nature of council-city manager governments makes those governments more receptive to managerial reform and innovation than mayorcouncil governments. Some studies (Moon & deLeon, 2001) have confirmed this argument in the managerial reform context. Interestingly, Brudney & Selden (1995) use this variable as a proxy for professionalism in their model of the adoption of computer technology, but they do not find a strong statistical significant association with the adoption of computer technology (see also Norris & Campillo, 2000; Holden et al., 2003; Norris & Moon, forthcoming). To explore this aspect, we propose the same proposition: that a municipality with a council-manager government is more likely to be an early adopter of e-government than a municipality with a mayorcouncil government.

## Outcomes of e-government

Many believe that e-government initiatives will bring managerial and organizational change because these initiatives affect both internal and external work procedures and managerial relationships. In particular, e-government initiatives are expected to help governments reduce their size, the time demands on their staffs and their operating costs, and to change staff roles, increase non-tax revenues, re-engineer business processes and make business processes more efficient. We argue that governments will experience more positive e-government outcomes as they promote e-government initiatives more actively, strategically and resourcefully (Moon, 2002; Norris & Moon, forthcoming).

## DATA COLLECTION

To examine how a government’s managerial innovation orientation affects its adoption of egovernment, we used two survey databases: the 1997 Reinventing Government Survey of the ICMA and the 2000 Electronic Government Survey of the ICMA & PTI. The 1997 survey contains many questions about reinvention programmes, and the 2000 survey has much information on the implementation of e-government.

The Reinventing Government Survey was conducted in 1997–98, with questionnaires mailed to the chief appointed administrative officers (i.e. city administrators, city managers)<sup>3</sup> of American cities with populations of 10 000 or more (2858). The number of responses was 1276, representing a response rate of 45%. The survey was designed to examine municipal reinvention by measuring actual implementation and budget allocations for municipal reinvention programmes. Additionally, the survey collected detailed information on chief administrators’ values for various reinvention programmes, as well as selected personal information. The data also contain comprehensive information on the characteristics of responding municipal governments.

The 2000 survey was designed to examine and assess local government adoption and aspects of their deployment of electronic government (web site adoption, electronic delivery of community services, interactive service delivery, the digital divide, e-procurement, etc.). The survey was sent to 2899 municipal governments of American cities with populations over 10 000, as identified by the ICMA municipal government database.<sup>4</sup> A total of 1471 municipal governments responded to the survey, resulting in a response rate of 50.7%.

The two data sets were merged by aggregating the information on each city using the identification number of the municipal government. The merged data set includes only municipal governments that responded to both surveys (739). The final usable sample size was reduced because of missing values for the variables selected in the different models explored in this study.

## VARIABLES

Selected dependent variables and independent variables are summarized in Appendices 1 and 2 respectively. Two dependent variables represent the extent of municipal e-government and the level of e-government impact. Selected explanatory variables for the extent of municipal e-government are the managerial innovation orientation, government capacity and institutional characteristics. The explanatory variables for the level of e-government impact include the extent of municipal e-government, government capacity and institutional characteristics. Both dependent and explanatory variables are discussed below in detail.

## Extent of municipal e-government

The extent of municipal e-government refers to the extent to which a municipal government adopts and implements e-government. This is represented by five variables indicating the adoption of municipal web sites and the longevity of those sites, the adoption of an intranet, the development of a strategic e-government plan and a comprehensive e-government performance index (the number of online public services). The variable representing the adoption of a municipal web site and an intranet system is binary, indicating whether a municipal government has its own web site or intranet system. The longevity of a government web site, measured in years, captures whether the government is an early or late adopter of government web sites. The binary variable representing the development of a strategic egovernment plan indicates whether a municipal government has a strategic and comprehensive e-government plan. Finally, the e-government performance index is calculated as the number of online services the municipal government provides. These services include both online financial transactions (payment of tickets, fines, taxes, license and permit fees, and recreation fees) and online public services (voter registration, property registration, applications or renewals of business licenses, applications or renewals of permits, registration for programmes and services, requests for services such as pothole repair, requests for local government records, and interactive maps). The index represents the level of municipa e-government by indicating the extent to which the municipal government offers both financia and service online services.

## Outcomes of e-government

A summative variable denoting the impact of e-government represents the expected results of e-government initiatives. This variable is equal to the number of outcomes that municipal governments have experienced. Each respondent was asked to indicate whether e-government had had the following effects: (1) reduced the number of staff; (2) changed the role of staff; (3) reduced time demands on staff; (4) increased demands on staff; (5) increased non-tax-based revenues; (6) helped to re-engineer business processes; (7) made business processes more efficient; or (8) reduced administrative costs. Therefore, the variable representing the impact of e-government is a summative variable based on eight binary variables of e-government impact. Additionally, a factor analysis of these eight items was conducted to distinguish different aspects of e-government outcomes. The factor analysis resulted in three factors. The first factor is dominated by administrative changes in the role of staff and working processes, while the second and third factors are dominated by the improvement of efficiency (reduction in staff and time demands) and revenue generation respectively.<sup>5</sup>

## Managerial innovation orientation

A variable representing the degree of managerial innovation orientation captures the extent to which a municipal government adopts various managerial innovations. Twelve managerial innovation initiatives<sup>6</sup> related to reinvention reform were used to develop the index. These initiatives represent different types of reinvention innovations that include customer-oriented, entrepreneurship-oriented, outcome/performance-oriented and contracting out-oriented programmes. The managerial innovation orientation is a proxy variable representing the culture of managerial innovations that eventually indicates the degree of government’s efforts to change old practices and move towards innovative managerial reforms. In particular, this variable captures the institutional intention (indicated by specific budget recommendations for reinvention programmes) of municipal government towards managerial reforms. Although this does not capture the actual implementation of municipal reinvention, it reflects the nature of managerial innovation orientation.<sup>7</sup> This variable is calculated as the sum of binary variables for the 12 managerial initiatives, where “1” indicates an actual budget recommendation to the city council for a reinvention programme during the last 5 years, and “0” indicates there was no budget recommendation for the programme.

## Government capacity

This variable measures the existence of perceived government technical, political and financial capacity. Because the survey data lack direct measures of government capacity, we obtained proxy variables by reversing perceived barriers to e-government initiatives faced by municipal governments. Respondents were asked to indicate various barriers they have encountered. Technical capacity was calculated based on responses to items regarding a lack of technical staff, a lack of technical expertise, a lack of information about e-government applications and a lack of technical upgrade. Financial capacity and political support were based on responses to questions about the lack of financial resources and the lack of support from elected officials respectively.

## ANALYSIS

We used multiple regression analyses to examine the impact of municipal governments’ overall managerial innovation orientation (measured as the adoption of various managerial innovations) and other institutional factors on the implementation of e-government. To evaluate the implementation of e-government, the analyses examined four dependent variables. The dependent variables were regressed against various independent variables, including managerial innovation orientation, perceived technical capacity, financial capacity, political support and institutional variables such as city size and form of government. In the models, we also considered potential interaction effects among a government’s technical, financial and political capacities. For example, financial resources for e-government may be readily available if there is great political support from elected officials, while technical capacity may be also associated with financial capacity. Accordingly, we included two variables reflecting interaction effects (political support ¥ financial capacity and financial capacity ¥ technical capacity). We used logistic regression analyses for three binary dependent variables of the adoption of an official government web site and intranet, as well as the development of a strategic e-government plan. We also conducted multiple regression analyses for the other two dependent variables (longevity of the web site and e-government performance index). As F statistics and likelihood ratios indicate, the models with five dependent variables of different aspects of e-government adoption are statistically significant. As Table 1 suggests, managerial innovation orientation and size are consistently strong predictors of advances in municipal e-government: web adoption, longevity of the web site, intranet, strategic plan for egovernment and level of e-government. The two independent variables are statistically significant in all five models tested in this study. In addition to these two variables, the adoption of a web site, the longevity of the web site and the level of e-government are positively associated with perceived technical capacity, whereas the adoption of an intranet is positively associated with perceived financial capacity and with political support from elected officials. The interaction effect between political support and financial capacity is negatively associated with the adoption of an intranet, while the variable of interaction effects between financial capacity and technical capacity is negatively associated with the adoption and longevity of a government web site, as well as the level of e-government implementation. The negative coefficients for the interaction effects between financial capacity and technical capacity indicate the effect of technical capacity on the adoption and longevity of a government web site is greater when governments lack financial resources. The negative interaction effect between political and financial capacities also suggests that the effect of financial capacity on the adoption of an intranet is greater when political support is low. Concerning institutional characteristics, city size is positively associated with five selected dependent variables of e-government adoption and implementation, while the form of government is not statistically significant in the models.

Again, the statistical results suggest that managerial innovation orientation and city size are primary determinants of the longevity of municipal web sites and e-government performance. In other words, large municipal governments with high innovation orientations tend to be more receptive to new approaches and more likely to be early adopters of e-government. With respect to government capacity, the adoption of an official government web site is largely determined by that government’s technical capacity, while financial capacity and political support appear to be important factors in the government adoption of an intranet. It suggests that the adoption of an intranet is not simply a technical issue but requires relatively substantial political support and financial resources.

We also examine how the adoption of e-government affects municipal governments. Table 2 summarizes the statistical results concerning the effects of e-government adoption, government capacity and institutional factors on the perceived outcomes of e-government. Four dependent variables for e-government outcomes are examined in this study. The first is the overall outcome of e-government, a summative variable computed based on eight binary variables representing different e-government outcomes. The other three variables represent specific e-government outcomes, i.e. administrative change, improvement of government efficiency and revenue generation, generated by the factor analysis described above. The statistical results show the overall outcome of e-government to be positively associated with various indicators of e-government, including the longevity of web sites, the presence of an intranet, a strategic e-government plan and the level of e-government. In particular, administrative change is positively associated with the longevity of web sites, the presence of a strategic e-government plan and the level of e-government. Interestingly, the adoption of an intranet is a critical factor in the improvement of efficiency. The model for revenue generation is not statistically significant; this may be because e-government is not designed to function as a revenue generator. Instead, it is designed to provide online services to improve efficiency and effectiveness.

## DISCUSSION

Overall, the statistical results suggest that cities that are more innovation-oriented are more likely to advance e-government. The results confirm that a municipal government’s managerial culture is directly related to its perspective on e-government and its receptivity to e-government innovation. Managerial innovation orientation appears to be closely associated with the establishment of a comprehensive e-government initiative. Financial and technical capacities also appear to be significant factors for the advancement of e-government in municipalities. Larger municipal governments are more likely to pursue e-government initiatives, perhaps because they have more stakeholders and, compared to small governments, must be more sensitive to citizens’ demands for more efficient government. Larger governments may also have the advantage of greater administrative resources for evaluating managerial innovations and technical solutions such as e-government. Interestingly, the form of government is not statistically significant, contrary to previous studies that found council-manager governments to be more likely to pursue e-government initiatives (Norris & Campillo, 2000; Moon, 2002; Holden et al., 2003).

Although there is a positive relationship in the correlation between the council-manager form and e-government implementation (adoption of a web site, longevity of the web site and egovernment performance index), the regression results do not support the positive association statistically. The effect of government form may be mediated by other variables, such as managerial innovation orientation. In other words, the effect of municipal government form may be diluted by its association with other variables that are positively correlated with the form of government.

Because the spread of e-government has not been matched by the accumulation of rich, rigorous data on the subject, conducting thorough empirical analyses of the field is challenging. Using descriptive statistics, previous studies have provided a preliminary picture of the current state of e-government at the local (Norris et al., 2000; West, 2001b; Ho, 2002; Moon, 2002; Holden et al., 2003; Norris & Moon, forthcoming), state (West, 2001a; Gant & Gant, 2003) and federal levels (Stowers, 2003). Some use survey data (Moon, 2002; Welch et al., 2003), while others use data collected from content analyses of public portal sites (West, 2001a; Gant & Gant, 2003; Stowers, 2003). Some have attempted to present theoretical frameworks supported with in-depth case studies (Fountain, 2001), whereas others attempt to provide a broad public administrative perspective on IT applications (Garson, 2003). Because of a lack of comprehensive data, few rigorous empirical studies on e-government have been conducted.

Along with the current literature and other scholarly efforts to investigate the impact of municipal governments’ managerial characteristics on the implementation of e-government, this study has examined the effects of managerial orientation and government capacity on the implementation of e-government initiatives. Based on a combined data set from two nationwide surveys, this study was specifically designed to explore the link between managerial innovation orientation and the development of e-government, as well as the relationship between the implementation of e-government and its outcomes at the local level.

The statistical analyses partially support the propositions about the effects of managerial innovation orientation, government capacity and institutional characteristics on the adoption and implementation of e-government. As municipal governments pursue e-government initiatives with a comprehensive strategic plan and more resources, they tend to bring about more e-government results and experience more managerial and organizational change. The adoption of an intranet is perceived to contribute to the improvement of administrative efficiency rather than to administrative changes in the role of staff and working processes.

This study also suggests that financial capacity becomes more important as governments pursue intranet systems to facilitate internal communication among government units. In other words, simple e-government implementations – such as the creation of a government web site or the establishment of one-way or two-way communication with citizens, businesses or other public agencies – do not require great financial resources. However, governments need more comprehensive and substantial technical and financial resources to sustain e-government initiatives and to deepen their commitment to e-government, for example by establishing an intranet, by offering online services and financial transactions, and by promoting the vertical and horizontal integration of e-services.

In conclusion, this study finds an association between managerial innovation orientation and the adoption of e-government at the local level. This study is exploratory in nature and has theoretical and empirical limitations; for instance, the data sets used were not designed for the purpose of this study, so that only a limited number of variables were used as surrogates for conceptual variables. Future studies need to examine the exploratory model more specifically and empirically with more direct and rigorous measures, and they should include more institutional and environmental factors. More rigorous empirical analyses will require a larger number of objective measures for governmental capacity and for e-government outcomes. In addition to a simple e-government performance index (a quantity-based indicator), future studies should include indices of the quality of online public services. We should also note that egovernment continues to evolve – particularly in the political arena – and so the political aspects of e-government, such as e-democracy, e-rule making, and e-voting, should be included in future studies.
