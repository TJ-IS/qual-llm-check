# The effects of IS resources, capabilities, and qualities on organizational performance: An integrated approach

Ja-Whan Gu \*, Ho-Won Jung

Korea University Business School, Anam-dong 5Ka, Sungbuk-gu, Seoul 136-701, Republic of Korea

A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 18 October 2011 Received in revised form 10 January 2013 Accepted 4 February 2013 Available online 11 February 2013

Keywords: Resource-based view IS capabilities IS qualities IS process Performance SEM

## A B S T R A C T

Several theories, such as the resource-based view (RBV) and the information systems (IS) success mode (ISSM), have provided inconclusive results on the impacts of IS on business processes and organizational performance. The current study reviews the effects of these theories in terms of IS resources, capabilities and qualities, and further proposes an integrated approach for examining organizational performance. Our integrated model was statistically tested by using a structural equation modeling (SEM) method. Our results provide an integrated view of the associations among theories and enhance the understanding of IS contributions to organizational performance.

## 1. Introduction

Investment in information systems (IS) has grown significantly under the widely held belief that IS are essential to a firm’s competitive survival. In 2006, American businesses spent an estimated \$1.8 trillion on IS hardware, software, and telecommunications equipment [55]. Between 1980 and 2004, private business investment in IS grew from 34% to 50% of all invested capital [55]. The impact of IS investment on performance (IS business value) has become a matter of interest to both academics and practitioners alike [64]. A significant amount of research in this area focuses on IS investments and their impact on bottom line metrics where performance includes business process performance (i.e., operational efficiency within specific business processes) and organizational performance (i.e., bottom-line impacts measured in revenue or competitiveness) to explain the so-called ‘‘productivity paradox’’ [16]. While heavy investment in IS continues, past studies report mixed findings on the effect of expenditures on organizational performance [16,17,42,90,92].

‘‘Attributing the inconclusiveness to conceptual limitations’’ [10] motivated the development of new approaches for connecting the path from IS investments to business value [33,63,67]. Some studies have drawn on theories, such as the resource-based view (RBV) [6], to investigate IS business value [69,78]. In the RBV, IS resources are a source of competitive advantage and organizational performance [10,78], and the capabilities of the IS process influence both performance and quality [32,45,89]. Others have adopted a process theory that investigates the effects of IS on intermediate business process levels and explains how IS spending improves organizational performance [7,91]. In the IS success model (ISSM) [26,86], IS qualities affect user satisfaction and use/ usefulness, which in turn have an impact on business processes and organizational performance [26,86].

To synthesize what is already known about IT business value and to guide future research by developing propositions, Melville et al. [69] integrated various strands of research into a single framework, which is an integrative model of IS business value drawing on the RBV, as the primary theory, and on microeconomics and industrial organization literature, as secondary theories. However, their theoretical model was not empirically tested.

The objective of this study is to integrate two theories (RBV and ISSM) of IS business value into an integrated model that provides a comprehensive view of the associations among these theories and enhances our understanding of IS contribution. Our integrated model utilizes some key IS constructs, such as IS resources, capabilities, and qualities, provided by Benbasat and Zmud [8]. Our model was statistically tested using a structural equation modeling (SEM) method with responses from 196 firms. We also compared our integrated model with individual models to identify the usefulness of our integrated model.

Our results show that IS capabilities and qualities have an important effect on firm performance. Such an effect differs from previous study findings. Therefore, the proposed integrative approach model increases our understanding of the IS impact on business processes and organizational performance. Furthermore, the model provides CEOs and IS managers with insights into managing IS processes and qualities. For example, if firms want to increase the value of their IS business, they should do more than just merely invest in IS but also focus on the improvements of IS processes and qualities in their organizations.

## 2. Conceptual background

This section addresses three key conceptual backgrounds of our integrated model: RBV, process capabilities, and ISSM. Our integrated model, derived from the three conceptual models, is provided in Section 3.

## 2.1. Resource-based view

Rooted in management strategy literature, the RBV argues that unique resources are the main sources of competitive advantage and organizational performance. Unique resources must have four attributes: valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable, and not substitutable [6]. Researchers have identified various resources. For instance, Barney [6] suggests that resources include assets, knowledge, capabilities, and organizational processes whereas Amit and Schoemaker [1] and Grant [34] distinguish resources from capabilities, similar to our study. Resources are stocks of available factors that are owned or controlled by the firm. Capabilities, in contrast, refer to a firm’s capacity to deploy resources using organizational processes to a desired end [34].

As part of our integrated model in Fig. 3, Fig. 1 illustrates a model of RBV and performance based on Melville et al. [69] (IS RESOURCES, COMPLEMENTARY ORGANIZATIONAL RESOURCES, and BUSINESS PROCESS PERFORMANCE) and Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [78] (IS RESOURCES, IS CAPABILITIES, and ORGANIZATIONAL PERFORMANCE). The constructs in Fig. 1 are summarized below. Note that the relationships among the constructs are explained in Section 3.1.

## 2.1.1. IS resources

Researchers in the IS field have identified several IS resources as potential sources of competitive advantage and performance. For example, Mata et al. [68] identified four attributes as resources: access to capital, proprietary technology, technical skills, and managerial skills. Ross et al. [81] addressed three IT assets: human resources (technical skills, business understanding, and problemsolving orientation), technology resources (sharable technical platforms and databases), and relationship resources (shared risk and responsibility). Bharadwaj [10] classified IS resources as human resources, including technical IS skills and managerial IS skills, IT infrastructure, and IS-enabled intangibles. Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [78] identified three IS resources: human capital (technical and business skills and firm-specific knowledge), infrastructure sophistication, and partnership. As this review illustrates, there are no significant differences among these previous studies.

Based on these previous studies, IS RESOURCES in this study are defined as a multidimensional construct that ‘‘consists of a number of interrelated attributes or dimensions and exists in multidimensional domains’’ (Law et al. This construct includes business expertise (understanding and knowledge of their firm’s business), internal and external relationships between the IS unit and the business units/IS providers, technical skills (ability to adopt new technology, develop, and operate IS) of the IS function staff, and IS infrastructure (application, data, server, and network). ## 2.1.2. Complementary organizational resources

When synergies between IS and other resources exist, the latter are referred to as complementary organizational resources [69]. Prior studies have shown that an IS advantage depends on utilizing relationships among complementary organizational resources [77]. Powell and Dent-Micallef [77] identified complementary human resources (open organization, CEO commitment, and organizational flexibility) and complementary organizational resources (business process design). Melville et al. [69] showed that complementary organizational resources are non-IS physical resources, non-IS human resources, and organizational resources, as originally defined in Barney’s [6] classification of firm resources.

Based on these previous studies, COMPLEMENTARY ORGANIZATIONAL in this study are defined as a multidimensional construct consisting of other firm resources, such as organizational openness and flexibility, CEO commitment, and business processes. Appendix A provides operational definitions of those constructs.

## 2.1.3. IS capabilities

The IS literature on RBV has viewed IS capabilities as a complex and multidimensional construct [74]. Ross et al. [81] viewed IS capabilities as abilities to perform strategically aligned planning, fast delivery, and cost-effective operation and support. Feeny and Willcocks [29] identified nine IS capabilities from three areas: business and IS vision, design of IS architecture, and delivery of IS services. Bharadwaj [10] defined IS capabilities as the firm’s abilities to acquire, deploy, and leverage IS resources to shape and support business strategies and value chain activities. Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [78] defined IS capabilities as four constructs: IS planning sophistication, systems development capability, IS support maturity, and IS operations capability.

In practice, IS capabilities have been intensively developed in process assessment models, such as the Software Process Improvement and Capability Determination (SPICE<sup>1</sup>) model developed by the ISO and the Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI) developed by the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) at Carnegie Mellon University [89]. These models defined process capability as a characterization of the ability of a process to meet current or projected business goals [44].

Consistent with previous studies, we define IS CAPABILITIES as the firm’s abilities to perform routines within the IS department, enabling delivery of IS services to the organization. IS CAPABILITIES include six IS processes: IS planning [78], business process change [35], acquisition, development, operation, and support [45]. Appendix A provides operational definitions of these dimensions.

## 2.2.1. IS qualities

IS qualities are composed of system quality, information quality, and service quality [26,19]. System quality is the quality of IS itself [25], which is measured by reliability, consistency of user interface, ease of use, and ease of learning [26,86,87,19]. Information quality is the quality of information provided by IS [25], which is measured by relevance, timeliness, accuracy, sufficiency, and the format of information generated by IS [26,86,87,19]. IS service quality is the quality of service provided by the IS unit, which has been measured using the SERVQUAL instrument [73]. The instrument includes tangibles, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy [75].

Based on the previous studies, we define IS QUALITIES as the multidimensional construct consisting of system quality, information quality, and service quality. Please refer to Appendix A for details.

## 2.2.2. Satisfaction and usefulness

Satisfaction is the net feeling of pleasure or displeasure that results from aggregating all the benefits that a person hopes to receive from the interaction with IS [25,87]. Usefulness is defined as the degree to which the stakeholder believes that using a particular system has enhanced his or her job performance [86,87]. This study follows these two definitions of satisfaction and usefulness. The constructs SATISFACTION and USEFULNESS and their multiple indicators are defined in Appendix A.

## 2.3. Performance

IS researchers have used the term performance to denote both business process-level measures and organizational-level measures [69]. There are a variety of measures to study performance.

## 2.3.1. Business process performance

Business process performance has been investigated in studies using the process theory. Some researchers have developed performance measures at the business process level, based on the perspective that the first-order effects of IS investments occur at the process level [7,70]. Porter [76] argued that the use of IS within business activities enhances the value-creating potential of the organization.

Consistent with this argument, Tallon et al. [94] developed multiple process-level measures to assess the impact of IS on business activities within the value chain. The measures were analyzed in the context of six critical activities or process areas within the value chain: process planning and support, supplier relations, production and operations, product and service enhancement, sales and marketing support, and customer relations. Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [78] identified IS support for core competencies as the extent to which IS can be used to support and enhance a firm’s core competencies, as categorized by Hamel and Heene’s [37] market access, integrity-related and functionality-related competencies.

This study defines BUSINESS PROCESS PERFORMANCE as the impact of IS on business processes, developed by Tallon et al. [94]. BUSINESS PROCESS PERFORMANCE as a multidimensional construct includes six business activities within the value chain, as defined in Appendix A.

## 2.3.2. Organizational performance

IS researchers have operationalized performance at the firm level via operations measures (such as cost reduction and productivity enhancement) and market-based measures (stock market valuation) [69]. Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [78] measured a firm’s performance using operating (profitability, productivity, and financial performance) and market-based performance. Bharadwaj [10] and Santhanam and Hartono [85] used financial performance measures (profit ratios and cost ratios) to test the usefulness of the RBV framework.

This study defines ORGANIZATIONAL PERFORMANCE as aggregate ISenabled performance impacts across all firm activities, by using metrics (revenue, cost, profitability, and competitive advantage) to capture the impacts on a firm’s bottom-line [10,62,69,97].

In the same context, IS researchers have argued that investments per se do not provide any sustained advantage because investments in IS are easily duplicated by competitors [20].

Early RBV studies examined the relationship between IS resources and organizational performance, leading to controversial results. For example, Bhatt and Grover [11] found that IS resources have a direct effect on organizational performance, whereas Powell and Dent-Micallef [77] concluded that complementary organizational resources, but not IS resources, affect organizational performance.

However, the initial RBV was incapable of explaining the relationship between IS resources and organizational performance because the distance between the cause and effect attenuates their relationship [7]. Under the process theory, this distance effect supports the assumption that outcomes may not occur even if conditions are present [66]. Thus, the following intermediate variables were inserted to explain the relationship well: IS capabilities [10,81], business process performance [69,79,91], and IS capabilities and business process performance [78].

Based on the above studies, our hypotheses regarding the RBV model are represented in Fig. 3 and are stated as follows:

H1.1. There is a positive relationship between IS RESOURCES and IS CAPABILITIES.

H1.2. There is a positive relationship between Complementary Organizational Resources and IS Capabilities.

H4. There is a positive relationship between Business Process Performance and Organizational Performance.

The constructs of the IS process capabilities model are overlapped with the constructs in the RBV and ISSM. A basic proposition of the process capabilities model, including the ISO/IEC 15504 and CMMI, is that higher process capabilities improve business process performance and organizational performance through increased project performance and product quality [89].

The relationship between IScapabilities and performance has been examined from the project level to the organizational level. For example, studies adopting the RBV [34,78,81] found that business process performance is dependent on IS capabilities. Many studies [28,39,47,49] found that higher IS capabilities improve project performance in the software industry. CMMI empirical studies also reported that organizations have achieved higher productivity, cost performance, schedule performance, and business process performance through CMMI-based process improvements [32]. Dawson and Watson [24] contended that the capabilities of IS units have a measurable impact on the organization’s financial performance.

Empirical studies on the relationship between IS capabilities and IS qualities found a positive correlation. For example, Harter et al. [39] and Harter and Slaughter [38] identified that higher levels of IS capability measured by the CMM improve the product quality in software product development. CMMI empirical studies [32] also support the relationship. Thus, IS capabilities also influence IS quality.

Based on the above studies, we propose the following hypotheses as shown in the research model of Fig. 3.

H2.1. There is a positive relationship between IS CAPABILITIES and BUSINESS PROCESS PERFORMANCE.

H2.2. There is a positive relationship between IS CAPABILITIES and IS QUALITIES.

## 3.2. IS success model and performance

The ISSM introduced by DeLone and McLean [25] states that system quality and information quality affect user satisfaction and use, which in turn affect organizational impact through individual impact. This model has been empirically tested and supported by a number of studies. For example, system quality and information quality lead to usefulness and user satisfaction, while increases in usefulness increase user satisfaction [87].

An updated model of DeLone and McLean [26] consists of constructs, such as IS qualities (system quality, information quality, and service quality), that affect intention to use/use (usefulness) and user satisfaction. All of the constructs affect net benefits (business process performance and organizational performance).

Based on these previous studies, our research model includes the ISSM, as shown in Fig. 3, leading to the following hypotheses:

H3.1. There is a positive relationship between IS QUALITIES and SATISFACTION.

H3.2. There is a positive relationship between IS QUALITIES and USEFULNESS.

H3.3. There is a positive relationship between USEFULNESS and SATIS-FACTION.

H3.4. H3.4: There is a positive relationship between SATISFACTION and BUSINESS PROCESS PERFORMANCE

H3.5. H3.5: There is a positive relationship between USEFULNESS and BUSINESS PROCESS PERFORMANCE.

## 3.3. Control variables

The IS literature has reported control variables affecting the relationship between IS resources and performance, such as organizational structure [84], corporate culture [72], and industry [97]. Firms with a more focused objective for IS have a higher performance from IS across the value chain [94]. The potential performance from using IS could vary across industries [78]. Firms with a prospector type of business strategy have better firm performance than firms with other types of business strategies [23]. In this study, six control variables are used: IS OBJECTIVE, IS DEPENDENCY, STRATEGY TYPE, INDUSTRY TYPE, SALE REVENUE, and POSITION TYPE.

## 4. Research methods and data

## 4.1. Operationalization

Through a detailed literature review, a survey instrument was developed to represent the characteristics of the underlying constructs to ensure content validity. Content validity is the degree to which measures in an instrument reflect the content universe to which the instrument will be generalized [93]. We drew measures from those measures validated in empirical studies.

This study performed a pre-test and a pilot-test [59]. We conducted a pre-test of questionnaires with IS academics who were knowledgeable about the constructs, and provided useful comments on the initial instrument, such as content, understandability, and terminology. After revising the questionnaires, a pilot test was undertaken by IS managers of six firms in Korea, who were respondents of the target population and who were not included in the data set for analysis. Their comments were used as an input for minor revisions of the instrument. All measures in this study were measured on a five-point Likert scale with responses ranging from ‘strongly disagree (1)’ to ‘strongly agree (5).’

## 4.2. Data collection

Huber and Power [43] recommended that the most knowledgeable person be selected when only one respondent per analysis unit is questioned. We have followed this advice. The primary objective of this study is to investigate the effects of IS constructs (resources, capabilities, and qualities) on organizational performance. Thus, a survey was conducted from the period of June to July 2007, targeting IS senior managers who were most informed about both IS constructs and organizational performance [36,78].

The unit of analysis was the firm level. The primary sources of the sampling frame were drawn from the top 1000 firms in the corporate information database of the Korea Information Service, Inc. (KIS), which accumulates and services corporate information. The sample frame consisted of the company name, address, name, title, and phone number of the IS departmental directors. A total of 326 firms promised to respond. Next, the survey questionnaire was mailed to the IS department managers of these firms. A total of 229 responses were received by electronic mail or fax, resulting in a response rate of 70%. Out of these responses, 33 were discarded due to incomplete data, leaving 196 responses in the final analysis.

Table 2 lists the sample characteristics of the responses in terms of industry type, total sales revenue, and position type. Nearly half (47%) of the firms were in the manufacturing industry (Table 2a) and 83% had annual sales revenues of \$100 million and above (Table 2b). Of the company employees who responded, 71% were senior managers (i.e., executives, director, and vice director/ section chief) in the IS department (Table 2c). Therefore, this sample implies that the results of our study could hold across the various organizational settings described above [95].

## 5. Analyses and results

This section provides an assessment of the measurement model as well as the results of the hypotheses tests. The goodness-of-fit statistics of the model were examined on three bases: the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the comparative fit index (CFI), and the Tucker–Lewis index (TLI). The criteria for the RMSEA are ‘‘close fit’’ (-0.05), ‘‘adequate’’ (<0.08), or a ‘‘mediocre fit’’ (0.08–0.10) [65]; the criteria for the CFI and the TLI are ‘‘acceptable’’ (>0.9) [9] or ‘‘good’’ (0.95).

## 5.1. Assessment of measurement model

## 5.1.1. Item parceling

Our integrated model includes five second-order constructs (as seen in Appendix A), which include 24 first-order constructs. To develop a parsimonious model that is tractable, we performed item<sup>2</sup> parceling that requires unidimensionality of target constructs [61].

Unidimensionality is examined by using the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), as follows. We first performed the EFA with SAS 8.01 for each of the 24 firstorder constructs, except for the constructs SATISFACTION, USEFULNESS, and ORGANIZATIONAL PERFORMANCE in Appendix A. All of the EFA results were acceptable as greater than or equal to 0.4 of the loading [14]; all results showed a minimum Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.75 as seen in Appendix A, which is greater than the recommended value of 0.7 [22,71,93]. In turn, the CFA with AMOS 16.0 showed a satisfactory goodness-of-fit (the fit indices were omitted). The measures of each of the 24 constructs were averaged to be used as a parceled item [52]. The parceled item became a measure of secondorder constructs in the following analysis.

## 5.1.2. EFA and CFA for the integrated model

Some of our measures are parceled values; the IS capability measures are new measures extracted from the base practices of SPICE. The EFA was performed for each of the five constructs and for the constructs SATISFACTION, USEFULNESS, and ORGANIZATIONAL PERFORMANCEin Appendix A. During the EFA, five parceled items with asterisks in Appendix A were eliminated because of either a value of less than 0.4 of the loading value [14] or of over 0.5 of the cross-loading value on the other factor. Appendix A includes the EFA results.

In turn, the CFA was conducted to examine the construct validity and reliability of the measurement model. All CFIs and TLIs are greater than a value of 0.95. All RMSEAs are less than 0.1. All loadings of individual constructs were significant at p < 0.001. The test statistics of CFA are shown in Appendix B.

## 5.1.3. Convergent and discriminant validity

Finally, we conducted the CFA to test the convergent and discriminant validity [18]. Convergent validity can be assessed by reviewing the t-values to determine whether the factor loading on each indicator’s posited underlying construct factor is significant [2,40]. In Table 3, all factor loadings for indicators measuring the same construct are statistically significant, showing that all indicators effectively measure their corresponding construct and supporting convergent validity (t-values are omitted). ## 5.2. Hypotheses testing

The test of the proposed model demonstrated a reasonable fit between the data and the proposed structural model, as presented in Fig. 4(a). While the fit indices suggest an acceptable fit between the model and data, the modification indices revealed the potential for model revisions to improve the fit. In particular, the modification indices indicated that IS RESOURCES should be covariated with COMPLEMENTARY ORGANIZATIONAL RESOURCES. This covariated link could be theoretically meaningful. IS studies have argued that organizational resources (such as CEO commitment and business processes) were complementary to IS resources [69,77]. Based on previous studies, the structure of the research model was modified, as seen in Fig. 4(b), with one path that is freely estimated between IS RESOURCESand COMPLEMENTARY ORGANIZATIONAL RESOURCES.

In Fig. 4(b), the significance level was set at a = 0.05 (t > 1.96, one-tailed). Overall, all of the paths except one (H3.4: SATISFAC-TION ! BUSINESS PROCESS PERFORMANCE) were significant, supporting the corresponding hypotheses. The result of H3.4 did not have the same conclusion as those of Delone and McLean [26], Seddon and Kiew [87], and Seddon [86]. Three of the control variables (IS OBJECTIVE, IS DEPENDENCY, AND STRATEGY TYPE) had a significant effect on the business process performance and two control variables (STRATEGY TYPE AND POSITION TYPE) had a significant effect on the organizational performance. A bootstrap simulation with 1000 runs [3] shows the stability of the estimates.

The modified model in Fig. 4(b) demonstrated a good model fit and was also a significant improvement over the original model shown in Fig. 4(a). For example, the CFI and TLI values in the original model, i.e., 0.90 and 0.89, that are equal to or slightly lower than the recommended cutoff of 0.90 were improved in the modified model, i.e., 0.93 and 0.92, respectively. The RMSEA value was also improved within the range of acceptability, i.e., from 0.07 to 0.06. The improved expected cross-validation index<sup>3</sup> (ECVI) [15] of the modified model indicated its greater potential for replication in other samples than the proposed model, i.e., from 5.81 to 5.19. All of these fit indices were acceptable, suggesting that the modified model provides a good fit with the data. The squared multiple correlation (SMC) values, which are similar to the Rsquare in the least square regression analysis, are improved such that the modified model accounts for a range from 47% to 95% of the variance in the constructs.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Implication

Previous studies adopting RBV or ISSM have separately investigated IS business value and performance. This study developed an integrated model drawn on RBV and ISSM in order to improve the understanding on how IS affects organizational performance. The premise of this study is consistent with LePine and King [58] comments that ‘‘synthesizing theory to produce integrative theories may be worthwhile in helping readers develop a broad understanding of a concept or process.’’ Our contribution of the integrated model and its statistical tests can be addressed on the basis of the recommendations on the necessary ingredients of a theoretical contribution [58,21,50,98].

Fig. 4. Test results.

BUSINESS PROCESS PERFORMANCE, which was separately explained in the RBV and ISSM, can be broadly understood from an integrated perspective such that IS RESOURCES and COMPLEMENTARY ORGANIZATIONAL RESOURCES influence IS CAPABILITIES. In turn, IS CAPABILITIES directly (RBV part) and indirectly (ISSM part, i.e., through IS QUALITIES and USEFULNESS) influence BUSINESS PROCESS PERFORMANCE. This understanding may correspond to ‘‘HOW are constructs related?’’ in a theoretical contribution, i.e., causality [98], p. 491. In addition, this study partially contributes to the body of IS RBV literature by providing an empirical test of a path suggested by a theory paper [69], i.e., from IS RESOURCES and COMPLEMENTARY ORGANIZATIONAL RESOURCES via intermediate BUSINESS PROCESSES to IS Business Value (ORGANIZA-TIONAL. PERFORMANCE).

Several thousands of organizations have been assessed on the base of CMMI and SPICE around the world<sup>4</sup> [41], and assessment results for process capability have been used as a base for many important decisions, such as ‘‘actions to improve internal software processes, large-scale acquisitions, and contract monitoring’’ [48]; however, there are insufficient studies to explain those phenomena in the RBV and ISSM. Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [78] also recommended an integrated model that includes IS capabilities and ISSMs. To improve our understanding, Hypothesis 2.2 (IS CAPABILITIES and IS QUALITIES) in Fig. 3 is established and tested on the basis of both the RBV and ISSM. This relationship implies that the previous studies [30,39,38,54] are included in our integrated model. In addition, the significant relationship provides process communities with a theoretical base of process improvement efforts.

This study could also contribute to the IS RBV literature by developing a set of IS CAPABILITIES measures by using the best practices of the process assessment model, such as CMMI and SPICE. This approach will allow for the development of an IS capabilities measure that will aid in inventorying and measuring organizational IS capabilities, as suggested by Bharadwaj [10].

In practice, our questionnaires would be used as an instrument to perform self-assessments. In particular, the measures of IS capabilities, based on SPICE best practices, could be used as actionable items to assess and improve IS functional activities, such as IS planning, business process changes, IS development, and support. These approaches can show the level of capability/ maturity achieved in IS processes; they can also be used to conduct benchmarking against other companies in the same industry. Furthermore, this approach can help to improve the implemented processes and moreover, enhance business processes and organizational performance.

## 6.2. Limitations

Assumptions and limitations of this study should be made clear in interpreting our results. These assumptions and limitations are not unique to this study but are characteristics of most empirical studies. As noted, following the recommendation by Huber and Power [43] for selecting representative respondents in each measurement unit, we targeted IS senior managers assumed to be aware of both IS constructs (RESOURCES, CAPABILITIES and QUALITIES) and ORGANIZATIONAL PERFORMANCE as respondents. The selected respondents were chosen because they are in charge of managing IS constructs and periodically evaluating the effects of IS constructs on organizational performance. However, some studies [36,78,94] noted that IS executives have the tendency to overrate or exaggerate the level of performance of IS constructs. These authors, therefore, recommended caution when interpreting the results. The same recommendation is also applicable to the results of our study.

This study used perceived data from a survey to examine the impact of IS constructs on business processes and organizational performance. Although the objective measure(s) would be preferred, it is not always possible to compute the exact objective data of organizational performance influenced by IS constructs [94]. Perceptual measures can be a good alternative to an objective measure. For example, Dess and Robinson [27] suggest that subjective perception data of organizational performance might be considered in the absence of objective data. Furthermore, managerial assessments of company performance are highly correlated with objective performance indicators [27,82,96].

## 7. Concluding remarks

Currently, most firms have made efforts to manage their IS resources and improve their IS capabilities and IS qualities for the achievement of organizational performance from IS investments.

Those efforts have separately been explained by the RBV and ISSM. This study integrates two theories to increase the understanding of IS performance. Our findings enable practitioners to have a more comprehensive understanding of how IS investments influence business value. These findings are our contribution to the practitioners, and our paper can therefore be differentiated from previous studies.

As explained previously, most of the hypothesized relationships in our model have strong support from the data, except for the relationship between SATISFACTIONand BUSINESS PROCESS PERFORMANCE. Further examination may be required.
