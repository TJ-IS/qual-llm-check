# Market Perception on Cloud Computing Initiatives in Organizations: An Extended Resource-based View

## Abstract

This study aims to investigate the economic value of cloud computing from the perspective of the stock market. More specifically, we conceptualize the relationship between the cloud computing initiatives of firms and the market reaction based on resource-based and extended resource-based views. For this study, we analyze 212 firm-level cloud computing announcements between 2006 and 2011 by employing the event study methodology. Our empirical results indicate that the market positively reacts to the cloud computing initiatives of firms. Moreover, the market reactions to cloud computing initiatives depend on three key characteristics of cloud computing, namely, firm-specific, resource-specific, and vendorspecific factors.

Keywords: Cloud computing; event study; extended resource based view; firm performance; market value of firm; technology innovation.

in this new IT service model, the value of cloud computing and the factors that drive its value need to be examined.

This study investigates the business value of cloud computing from the perspective of stock market evaluation by identifying the plausible conditions under which firms can benefit from cloud computing initiatives. Specifically, we explored both resource-based view (RBV) and extended resource-based view (ERBV) in conceptualizing our research model. RBV emphasizes the role of proprietary resources within a firm in developing a sustainable competitive advantage. ERBV extends the theoretical boundary of RBV and thus posits three sources of a firm‟s competitive advantages, namely, capacity of the focal firm, networked (relational) resources, and capability of the partner, which are collectively analogous to the basic components of cloud computing business (i.e., client firm, cloud computing service, and service vendor). We first identify the critical factors related to the returns from cloud computing use in organizations by synthesizing the theoretical and methodological backgrounds. We then conduct a cross-sectional analysis to examine whether these factors are related to the increase in a firm‟s market value using 212 cloud computing announcements from 2006 to 2011. To the best of our best knowledge, this study is one of the earliest attempts to conceptually identify the critical contextual factors related to cloud computing and empirically assess its potential value.

3 RBV has been adopted as a major theoretical background for understanding the influences of

4 IT investments on business competence in the market. RBV suggests that firms can achieve a

5 competitive gain or improve operational efficiency by effectively combining IT resources

6 with their internal IT capability (Mithas et al., 2011). Therefore, IT studies adopting RBV

7 seek to conceptually explain whether IT resources contribute to a firm‟s competitive

8 advantage and how a firm utilizes its IT assets to achieve efficient performance (Wade and

9 Hulland, 2004). For instance, Andreu and Ciborra (1996) argue that organizational learning

capability strengthens the positive role of IT in developing firm competence. In accordance

with the theoretical arguments, many studies have shown that firms can improve their

performance by effectively leveraging their IT investments; such endeavor can be achieved

by developing strong IT capabilities (Ravichandran and Lertwonsatien, 2005).

However, traditional RBV does not sufficiently explain the emerging use of cloud computing in organizations because RBV assumes that the generation of competitive advantage is based on the accumulated resources owned and controlled by the firm itself (Barney, 1991). This proprietary assumption may result in an inaccurate evaluation of a firm‟s competitive advantage generated from externally delivered IT services (i.e., cloud computing services in the present study) (Lavie, 2006).

Thus, researchers extended the resource boundary to external entities to complement the limitation of traditional RBV. Compared with traditional RBV, ERBV can explain the gaining of competitive advantage in a more integrated manner. ERBV emphasizes the network aspect of interconnected firms by conceptualizing how firms can reinforce their competitive advantage under interorganizational settings (Lavie, 2006). ERBV posits that a dyadic network exists between a focal firm and a partner in the form of a strategic alliance or

any other business relationship specified by contractual agreements (Dyer and Singh, 1998). These allies interact through network resources for their own business purposes. Network resources refer to external resources that are available through a firm‟s interorganizational relationship with partners or outside vendors (Gulati, 1999). These resources are distinct from resources can provide focal firms with strategic opportunities with less risk and cost (Reuer and Koza, 2000). These studies collectively support the significant role of external resources in generating the competitive advantage of firms (Lee et al., 2001).

## 2.2. Structure of the Research Model

A dyadic network is used to structuralize our research model and specify the contextual factors for improving firm performance. In the context of cloud computing, a focal firm refers to a client firm that uses cloud computing services. Such services comprise the network resources offered by the contractual relationship between a client firm and its cloud computing service provider. The partner is analogous to a cloud computing vendor who delivers IT resources in an “as-a-service” format through its own cloud computing infrastructure. We investigate how these three components collectively contribute to the market value of a firm from the ERBV perspective. Specifically, we hypothesize the following domains to understand the association of cloud computing investments with a firm‟s increased market value: (1) client firm‟s internal characteristics (i.e., firm-specific factor); (2) features of cloud computing services as IT resources (i.e., network resourcespecific factor); and (3) service provider‟s characteristics (i.e., vendor-specific factor).<sup>1</sup>

First, the firm-specific factors are correlated to the unique characteristics of an individual firm. Previous IT studies suggest that firms are required to build the associated capabilities in extracting additional value through internally or externally delivered IT resources. These studies argue that heterogeneous IT value is eventually attributable to organizational characteristics (Tornatzky and Fleisher, 1990). Therefore, the firm-specific factors can elucidate how the market reacts to cloud computing initiatives with respect to the unique traits of firms. Two major contextual factors, namely, firm size and prior experience, are derived from the intensive review of previous studies; such factors are commonly displayed across the sample announcements.

Second, the (network) resource-specific factors refer to a set of technology-related features and potential benefits embedded in IT resources acquired from external vendors. In particular, ERBV emphasizes the rent-yielding feature (i.e., benefit factors for a firm‟s enhanced business competence) of network resources in the business relationship between focal firms and partners. Previous studies argue that the superiority of external resources from the partner side (e.g., faster implementing time, less operation cost) can potentially contribute to the performance of a focal firm with less risk and cost, consequently affecting the firm‟s market value (Lavie, 2006; Reuer and Koza, 2000). Therefore, the technological characteristics and benefits derived from service vendors should be included as major elements that result in various levels of market expectations against the announcement of cloud computing initiatives. This study identifies three cloud computing characteristics, namely, cloud service types, implementation types, and cloud computing benefits, to investigate how the technological features of cloud computing are related to the market value of adopting firms. Cloud service types refer to the three subsets of cloud computing services commercialized as on-demand services in the market. These subsets include software as a service (SaaS), platform as a service (PaaS), and infrastructure as a service (IaaS) (Sultan,

2010). Implementation types indicate the alternative ways by which firms deploy cloud computing services in their business operations. Our study examines two types of cloud computing implementation: standalone (i.e., independent use of cloud service) and integrative (e.g., integrating cloud service with existing systems) (Staten, 2008). Cloud computing computing initiatives, operational benefit (e.g., cost saving) and strategic benefit (e.g., agility and flexibility) (Armbrust et al., 2010; Marston et al., 2011).

Finally, the vendor-specific factors represent the service provider‟s overall technology and business capabilities that can influence the market reaction to cloud computing initiatives. In contrast to traditional IT operations, cloud computing allows external ndors to be involved in the provision and management of the necessary IT res urces (Hayes, 2008). These partner-related factors are used to investigate the extent to which a vendor‟s characteristics influence the client firms (i.e., increased market value of firms), particularly when the announcement contains information regarding the identity of the partner for cloud computing initiatives (Han et al., 2012). This issue is important to both the announcing firms and the investors because vendor characteristics reflect the quality of cloud service providers. In this study, the involvement of a qualified cloud service vendor sends a credible signal to the stock market regarding the high possibility of reaping business value in the future, there by mitigating the investor‟s uncertainty toward the new technology (Han et al., 2012). ERBV argues that the capability and knowledge of a partner can be used as valuable resources to increase the focal firm‟s competitive advantage (Lavie, 1996). Thus, we posit that the partnership with qualified vendors provides subscribing firms with high-quality cloud services that can efficiently support business processes and initiatives. We categorize vendors according to their financial stability specified by IPO status to capture vendor-specific factors.

## 2.3. Market Reaction to Cloud Computing Initiatives

The strategic values of cloud computing include its quickness and dynamic delivery of IT resources, thereby providing a new way of conducting business. Compared to traditional IT insourcing and outsourcing, cloud computing offers on-demand IT services that allow firms to use Internet-mediated IT infrastructure capabilities (i.e., software, hardware, and platform) with less deployment time (Gillett, 2008). Firms can rapidly procure the necessary computing power needed to deploy their new business ideas by leveraging such IT services. The reduced time lag in preparing required IT resources enable firms to rapidly initiate their business processes or strategies, thereby marketing new products or services at the right timing.

Cloud computing employs IT virtualization as a technology base to share and allocate IT resources, thereby enabling cloud service users to flexibly utilize various IT capabilities depending on their demands (Armbrust et al., 2010). Cloud computing enables firms to easily acquire additional computing power at the peak demand of resources while bringing less pressure to reduce unnecessary IT resources at lower demand (Hayes, 2008). Therefore, firms that employ cloud computing can try new business initiatives with less economic risks, which are typically associated with the irreversibility of traditional IT investment (Fichman, 2004).

Another benefit of cloud computing is that firms are provided with the latest technology with less investments in internal IT resources. For instance, cloud service enables employees to use virtual IT resources regardless of time, location, and computing device. The ubiquitous work environment enables employees to efficiently share work status within and across departments in an organization, making the business processes more agile or seamless (Cearly and Smith, 2009). Furthermore, cloud computing provides firms with a plausible technology platform that can improve their collaboration with business partners. Cloud computing also allows firms to communicate with their external partners that use the same cloud service. In this case, the burden of implementing collaboration systems and synchronizing technology specifications can be reduced. Consequently, the efficiency of business transactions across organizations can be improved, resulting in enhanced business competence for both the focal firms and the partners.

Considering the aforementioned potential of cloud computing, we recognize that the new IT provision model can provide efficiency and agility benefits or both depending on a firm‟s intention and priority to utilize the pool of cloud services. Considering the aforementioned potential of cloud computing, we recognize that the new IT provision model can provide the benefits of efficiency and agility, depending on a firm‟s intention and priority to utilize the pool of cloud services. Conceptually, the pooling of IT resources through cloud computing allows firms to use as much hardware and software capabilities that they need without wastage, resulting in more operational benefits, such as cost efficiency. Similarly, scalability in the cloud model is crucial in improving agility because firms can scale resources as needed in dynamic business environments (Marston et al., 2011; McAfee, 2011). Previous studies argue that enhanced IT flexibility supports agility, which consequently drives organizational innovation (Prahalad and Krishnan, 2008; Sambamurthy et al. 2003; Tallon and Pinsonneault, 2011). In other words, the flexibility embedded in the cloud computing model can signal either a cost-related or a strategic advantage.

As a result of eminent improvements in efficiency and flexibility, firms have increasingly opted to launch cloud computing projects. For instance, the cases of eBay and GE show large-scale cloud computing applications for major systems (e.g., cloud-based datacenter) and business functions (sales and business operations planning).<sup>2</sup> Also, the cost advantage of IT procurement provides smaller firms with a chance to huddle IT barriers against business innovation (Marston et al., 2011). The cloud-based business applications or data analytics capabilities enable the small and medium firms to deploy their business strategies with less IT infrastructure burden. As such, the cloud computing announcements may signal to investors a firm‟s business commitment to generate better cash flows by rationalizing IT cost and refining business competency.

Taken together, we expect that the announcements of cloud computing initiatives result in positive market reaction. Thus, this leads us to propose the following hypothesis:

H1: The announcement of a cloud computing initiative has a positive effect on a firm’s market value.

## 2.4. Contextual Factors of Cloud Computing Initiatives

Although the announcements of cloud computing initiatives yield positive market returns, heterogeneity in the value of cloud computing exists among firms; such heterogeneity depends on several contextual factors. In this section, we discuss how the specific contextual factors in the research model (in Figure 1) contribute to the business value (i.e., stock market returns) with respect to the cloud computing initiatives of firms.

## 2.4.1. Firm-specific Factors

Firm Size. IT literature (e.g., Lee and Xia, 2006) emphasizes the importance of firm size in assessing the value of IT in business practices. Several studies suggest that large firms generally possess a great amount of slack resources that facilitate various technological innovations and absorb potential failure risks (Tornatzky and Fleischer, 1990). Dewan et al. (1998) empirically find that firms present a more intensive level of IT investment as they become larger with extended business scope and scale. Thus, large firms may be trapped into organizational inertia, making them less agile and flexible in adopting and exploiting new technologies (Zhu and Kraemer, 2005). Such structural inertia embedded in large firms may slow down the organizational use of technological innovations and consequently retard the potential values of IT. By contrast, small firms could easily adopt new technologies because they require less communication, coordination, and procedure to obtain support (Nord and Tucker, 1987). In line with the argument, Richardson and Zmud (2002) show that, compared with large firms, small firms are more likely to sustain competitive advantages brought by IT investments.

In addition to the structural benefits for small firms, cloud computing allow small firms to establish competitive IT infrastructure with a small amount of IT budget. For small firms struggling to acquire sufficient IT resources, the value proposition of providing various Internet-mediated IT capabilities with less or no upfront investment reinforces the viability and affordability of cloud computing (Plummer, 2008; Staten, 2008). This condition causes investors to expect small firms to achieve more business values from cloud computing initiatives compared with large firms. Therefore, we expect that the market evaluates cloud computing initiatives of small firms more positively. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H2: The announcement of a cloud computing initiative has a greater positive impact on the market value of smaller firms than that of larger firms.

Prior Experience. Cloud computing provides insight into a new paradigm of IT management in organizations because it potentially transforms the design and delivery of IT resources

(Buyya et al., 2009). The new IT paradigm requires firms to refine the existing IT knowledge to fully utilize its strategic potential for better performance (Plummer, 2008). So, firms must effectively exploit the cloud computing services available in the market by having high internal IT capabilities (Fichman, 2004).

Previous IS studies have identified the internal capability in the form of firm-specific IT knowledge accumulated through prior technological experience (Nevo et al., 2007; Wade and Hulland, 2004). The experience with a certain technology allows a firm to explore differential traits embedded in the technology. Consequently, firms gain better understanding on how to exploit the feature for IT and business. Thus, prior experience with certain technologies is crucial in developing an organization‟s IT capability (Nevo et al., 2007). The theoretical arguments suggest that the prior cloud experience increases the adopting firms‟ ability to exploit the cloud computing opportunities for business purposes (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990).

Furthermore, firms gaining experience through “learning-by-doing” mechanism can add cloud computing to their internal IT capability, which they can apply to similar technologies. For example, Nevo et al. (2007) find that having prior experience with Internet business solution (IBS) helps a firm implement new forms of IBS more effectively. Thus, any announcements by firms with prior experience may encourage investors to more positively estimate future business value derived from the use of cloud computing. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3: The announcement of a cloud computing initiative has a greater positive impact on the market value of a firm with informed prior experience in cloud computing than one with no informed prior experience.

## 2.4.2. Resource-specific Factors

Cloud Computing Service Types. As a new concept of IT provision, cloud computing bears an ambidextrous feature that supports two major IT trends: business agility and IT efficiency (Marston et al., 2011). Marston et al. (2011) explain that cloud computing not only provides competitive IT resources that can be rapidly deployed and scaled but also reduces a large amount of upfront IT investments. The differentiated potentials of cloud computing can be attributed to its technical feature, which allows users to access various Internet-mediated IT resources in different hierarchical layers, including software, platforms, and hardware (Armbrust et al., 2010).

Previous studies have categorized cloud services into three major types, namely, SaaS, PaaS, and IaaS (Sultan, 2010; Vaezi, 2012). SaaS provides Internet-enabled applications for business processes. PaaS is an off-premise developing environment that supplies application development toolkits. IaaS refers to virtual computational devices (e.g., server, storage, and network) that serve business applications and platforms. The different types of cloud services within the layered architecture enable firms to flexibly compose a combination of looselycoupled IT resources on a different set of business problems (Buyya et al., 2009).

Although the classification of cloud services is meaningful, it is from the service provider‟s viewpoint. Thus, we need to reclassify cloud services from the client perspective, specifically from the perspective of client IT infrastructure. Previous studies (e.g., Kayworth et al., 2001; Chatterjee et al., 2002) have identified the key elements of IT infrastructure (i.e., physical and intellectual IT assets and IT standards) and have presented a conceptual framework that explains how these elements form enterprise systems. These studies argue that IT infrastructure could be blended into two system components: business application and shared IT service. The former (e.g., ERP, CRM, and SCM in SaaS format) aims to deliver business functionality that enables business processes, whereas the latter (e.g., shared server, database, and development tools in IaaS or PaaS format) provides a technical base that creates and operates business applications (Chatterjee et al., 2002, Kayworth et al., 2001). Thus, we classify the cloud computing types into application-oriented (i.e., SaaS) and infrastructure-oriented (i.e., Non-SaaS including IaaS and PaaS) cloud services.

#

Prior studies have explained the differentiated strategic position of these two dimensions of IT infrastructure use. For instance, Kayworth et al. (2001) maintain that hardware-related infrastructure contributes to the firm‟s IT efficiency by providing enhanced computing power. However, Kayworth et al. (2001) also emphasize that business application in the form of software can enhance the agility or flexibility of firms by restructuring the business processes and routines. In addition, other previous studies on major enterprise systems (i.e., ERP, SCM, and CRM) advocate the strategic benefits related to business agility. These studies explain that ERP enables cross-functional transaction integration, SCM provides more sophisticated planning capabilities, and CRM allows for in-depth customer understanding and sales force automation (e.g., Hayes et al. 2001).

Based on the above argument, we expect that adopting different cloud service types may cloud service (i.e., SaaS) can receive more favorable market evaluation than that of hardware and platform-oriented cloud services (i.e., IaaS and PaaS). This phenomenon is due to the fact that using a new SaaS cloud type for restructuring business operations might be a more attractive event for investors who are looking for a better cash flow (Dehning et al., 2003; Dos Santos et al., 1993). Previous studies examine the market reaction to investments in business applications and find the positive relationship between such investments and the increase in firms‟ market value (e.g., Hayes et al., 2001; Oh et al., 2006b). Therefore, SaaS cloud service is associated with higher market value compared with non-SaaS cloud services. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis;

H4: The announcement of a cloud computing initiative has a greater positive impact on the market value of a firm that uses SaaS cloud services than one that uses non-SaaS cloud services.

IT) and the integration level (i.e., the extent to which outsourced IT is integrated within the existing systems in an organization). These dimensions are regarded as significant factors for outsourcing success (Lacity et al., 1998; Lee et al., 2004). Other than IT control dimension, IT outsourcing literature proposes the three types of IT sourcing: total insourcing, selective outsourcing, and total outsourcing (Lacity et al. 1996). The categories are related with the scope of in- or out-sourcing practice and the degree of integrating outsourced IT with existing systems (Lee et al. 2004). As in e case of IT outsourcing, firms have options regarding how to utilize outsourced cloud services in the aspect of system integration. As an “off-premise” approach, cloud computing offers “readyto-use” IT capabilities to perform IT functions as independent units or by connecting to existing systems (Cearly and Smith, 2009; Staten, 2008).

We categorize cloud implementation types into standalone and integrative approaches<sup>3</sup>. In the standalone approach, most of the related business operations are processed within the cloud system. Typical examples are Web-based e-mail systems, such as Google‟s Gmail or Microsoft‟s Outlook, which are implemented for internal use but without connecting to internal systems. In the integrative approach, the selected cloud services are connected to the firm‟s existing systems so that the associated business operations are conducted across the cloud and legacy systems. An example of such approach is Salesforce.com‟s cloud-based CRM service integrated with an internal ERP system.

#

Compared with the standalone approach, the integrative approach can bring more opportunities for effectively responding to the expected risks and internalize cloud services into current IT and business practices, thereby producing more benefits (Lee et al., 2004). This finding is in agreement with the IT outsourcing literature, which shows that selective IT outsourcing is more effective than total outsourcing in terms of keeping core IT competence and improving IT coordination capability (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998). The reason is that firms can control the uncertainties involved in deploying outsourcing practices by integrating the outsourced IT resources into the existing systems and business processes (Geyskens et al., 2006). Therefore, we expect that investors shall embed more premiums on the integrative hypothesis:

H5: The announcement of a cloud computing initiative will have a greater positive impact on the market value of a firm that implements integrative cloud computing than one that implements standalone cloud computing.

Cloud Computing Benefits. Cloud computing benefits refer to the operational and strategic benefits that a firm expects from the cloud computing initiative. Previous studies on cloud computing have reported three representative potential benefits of cloud computing: cost reduction, IT management flexibility, and organizational agility (Armbrust et al., 2010; Plummer, 2008; Vaezi 2012). Cloud computing can reduce existing IT costs by shifting IT ownership from clients to service providers. The shifts in IT ownership do not require upfront IT investments; such shifts also reduce IT implementation time through instantly deployable IT resources (Cearly and Smith, 2009). Vaezi (2012) further explains that the cost savings from cloud computing are obtained in two aspects; the savings in IT investment and the savings in operational and maintenance expenses for supporting full- scale organizational IT. The elastic and scalable nature of cloud computing enables firms to use IT resources more flexibly in response to fluctuation in the resource demand (Armbrust et al., 2010). The

service scalability also increases organizational agility in response to environmental changes (Vaezi, 2012). In addition, cloud computing enables firms to utilize on-demand IT functions with the latest technology, thereby sustaining their technological agility (Staten, 2008).

Previous IS studies posit that strategic roles and the related benefits served by IT investments are closely related to business strategies for sustaining competitive advantages. Such strategies include operational efficiency, which attempts to improve internal work productivity, and strategic positioning, which aims to enhance market position with differentiated products and services (Tallon et al., 2000). Cost reduction is considered an operational benefit because it consequently yields more productive IT management with less cost. Meanwhile, both agility and flexibility are considered strategic benefits because they enable firms to rapidly initiate new services or products by allocating their resources to more business-centric tasks and by reducing the time needed to supply the required IT resources.

The market reaction to corporate IT investments is contingent on the strategic importance of the expected benefits (Dehning et al., 2003; Dos Santos et al., 1993). For instance, Dehning et al. (2003) argue that on the one hand, benefits pertaining to efficiency-oriented IT investments (e.g., cost reduction) are short-lived because the practice is relatively easy to be duplicated by competitors. Thus, the market does not positively react to these investments. On the other hand, the market‟s evaluation for IT investments focusing on the benefits for business transformation (e.g., agility and flexibility) is more favorable because such investments yield new business models and provide unique products and services that the competitors cannot imitate. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H6: The announcement of a cloud computing initiative has a greater positive impact on the market value of a firm with informed strategic benefits than one with informed operational benefits.

## 2.4.3. Vendor-specific Factors

#

Vendor Reputation. Although cloud computing shows great potential contribution to firms‟ competitive advantage, because of recency, market worries in terms of data security, service quality, and vendor reliability have emerged in relation to the perceived hazards of cloud computing services (Plummer, 2008). For instance, a firm may encounter critical risks, such as system crash and discontinuous cloud business, if the firm subscribes from unqualified vendors. The aforementioned issues on cloud computing business may cause market reaction to be contingent on vendors‟ reputation, which is related to overall ability to securely carry out qualified cloud services (Hayes et al., 2001; Oh et al., 2006a). For instance, in IT outsourcing initiatives, Oh et al. (2006a) find that stock markets assess initiatives with renowned IT vendors more favorably. Similarly, in our context, the level endor reputation plays a significant role in evaluating a firm‟s business value accrued from cloud computing initiatives.

Reputation is defined as the collective judgment of customers regarding a firm‟s quality and capabilities earned over time within a certain domain (Fombrun, 1996; Rindova et al. 2005). In line with this concept, a cloud computing vendor‟s reputation is the assessed quality or capabilities of that vendor, which is informed over time by continued appraisal of its performance (Carter and Ruefli, 2006; Fombrum, 1996). Not surprisingly, vendors with higher market reputation are generally known to yield greater potential to provide highquality services, and for sustaining the cloud computing business for a longer period (Staten 2008). This ability is brought about by the combined strengths of accumulated expertise in business and technology, robust IT infrastructure, competitive service offerings, and highly skilled human resources (Han et al. 2013).

The aforementioned discussion implies that reputation is closely related to market perception of service providers with regards reliability, dependability, and trustworthiness (Oh et al., 2006a). Thus, vendor reputation may have an effect on market reaction to client

#

firms‟ cloud computing initiatives in terms of success rate and future values of projects. Previous studies suggest the use of third-party quality certification (e.g., ISO 9001 and ISO 27001) in assessing vendors‟ overall quality and capabilities, and in verifying their market reputation (e.g., Rao, 1994; Rindova et al. 2005). Furthermore, in cloud computing context, where assessment of a vendor‟s capabilities is uncertain because of the industry‟s immaturity, a vendor‟s reputation is more likely to be influenced by third-party quality signals, such as certifications (Podolny 2005; Rao, 1994; Rindova et al. 2005). Thus, a vendors‟ acquisition of certification complements its reputation and provides positive signals to the market that it is a competitive service provider.

Considering the arguments about vendor capabilities, vendor reputation and third-party quality certification altogether, we conjecture that a market‟s reaction to a firm‟s cloud computing announcement is contingent on service providers‟ reputation, assessed through third-party quality certification; thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H7: The announcement of cloud computing initiative has a greater positive impact on the market value of firms partnering with service providers with higher market reputation than those partnering with lower market reputation.

## 2.4.4. Control Variables

In addition to the hypothesized factors, we include several control variables in our research model. First, we add a dummy variable that represents the service and non-service industries based on SIC to control industry-specific effects (Dehning et al., 2005; Im et al., 2001). Previous studies have shown that IT investments have a greater influence on the service industry than on non-service industries (Oh et al., 2006a).

We then control the effects related to announcement characteristics (i.e., source of announcement and disclosure time). Previous studies suggest that managers in eventgenerating firms have an incentive to announce the news that contain the most favorable

## 3. Research Methods

## 3.1. Data

We followed the standard procedures suggested and employed in previous event studies in gathering sample announcements related to cloud computing initiatives (Dos Santos et al., 1993; McWilliams and Siegel, 1997). The sample data were collected between January 1, 2006 and December 31, 2011rom a full text search of primary news sources within the Lexis-Nexis database, such as the PR Newswire and the Business Wire. We then prescribed search terms related to the cloud computing business that could be used in news media in describing a firm‟s cloud computing initiative. This procedure was conducted to make the search procedure more efficient. The set of keywords consisted of the three following categories: (1) key words related to the focal technology (e.g., cloud computing, cloud service, on-demand, SaaS, PaaS, and IaaS), (2) names of service providers (e.g., IBM, Google, Microsoft,

#

Amazon, and Salesforce.com), and (3) action verbs (e.g., launch, announce, invest, partner, adopt, and deploy). Our search yielded 1,028 news articles with the combination of these keywords.

A consensus procedure in line with past research was employed to determine the relevance of sample announcements to the study (Han et al. 2013). All four co-authors participated in configuring the content of each announcement and in deciding whether the announcement was relevant. We discarded the announcement, if any, of the authors who disagreed with the criterion of sample selection.

After reading each article, we excluded the announcements that contained subjects that were irrelevant to cloud computing (e.g., weather and financial market forecast), general news without firm-specific events, and announcements by private firms<sup>4</sup>. During the screening process, 339 announcements were left. The selected announcements were then filtered in the presence of confounding events, such as overlapping announcements regarding dividends, earnings, mergers and acquisition (McWilliams and Siegel, 1997). Duplicated announcements with the same or similar news were removed. This screening procedure yielded 239 cloud computing announcements. We also eliminated cloud computing announcements with extremely high or low abnormal returns (ARs) (i.e., outliers) using Cook‟s distance analysis (McWilliams and McWilliams, 2011). Finally, the outlier analysis yielded 212 announcements. Table 1 reports the detailed procedure of data collection.

The daily stock returns of the individual firms were retrieved from the Center for Research on Security Prices (CRSP) database. We used the S&P 500 index as the benchmark for the overall stock market return in estimating the normal performance of the market. For the analysis, we used an estimation period of 255 stock trading days (–256 to –2). The trading

## 3.2. Event Study on the Firm’s Market Value

The event study was applied as a primary research methodology to investigate the market reaction to a firm‟s cloud computing initiatives. Event study captures the change in a firm‟s market value when a specific business-related announcement is released. To this end, we first identify how much the normal performance of stock return for a firm would be if there had not been the announcement in the market (Subramani and Walden 2001). Such normal performance is calculated by regressing a firm‟s stock return by the overall market return. Then, the difference between expected returns from the regression formula and actual returns is computed as abnormal returns (AR) which reflect changes of market value for a firm attributable to the announcement of an event (McWilliams and Siegel 1997). Event study has emerged as a pervasive mechanism because it measures the investors‟ perceptions of intangible costs and benefits associated with IT investments (Dos Santos et al., 1993; Im et al.,

2001). With such information, we can explore the relationship between IT investments such as the adoption of cloud computing services and business performance. The key procedure in the event study analysis is calculating the abnormal returns (ARs) in response to a firm‟s cloud computing announcements. In this study, we estimate the normal return of the stock if the event had not occurred to measure the effect of a cloud computing event. The approach produced the following regression:

$$
R _ {s, t} = \alpha_ {s} + \beta_ {s} R _ {m, t} + \epsilon_ {s, t} (1)
$$

where $R _ { s , t }$ is the return of stock s on day t, $R _ { m , t }$ is the market return on day t, $\alpha _ { s }$ and $\beta _ { s }$ are the firm-dependent coefficients to be estimated, and $\varepsilon _ { s , t }$ is a random error term

AR, which indicates the difference between the expected returns based on the general market movements and the actual returns, can be expressed as follows:

$$
A R _ {s, t} = R _ {s, t} - \left(\alpha_ {s} + \beta_ {s} R _ {m, t}\right)\tag{2}
$$

where $\alpha _ { s }$ and $\beta _ { s }$ are the coefficients of the parameters obtained from ordinary least squares (OLS). ARs are simply the prediction errors of the model in Equation (1). The stock‟s AR provides an estimate of the economic worth of the event (Brown and Warner, 1980).

The standard errors of ARs used for hypothesis testing are calculated using the following formula defined by Judge et al. (1988, p 170):

$$
\operatorname{var} \bigl (A R _ {s, \tau} \bigr) = \left(S _ {s} ^ {2} \left[ 1 = \frac {1}{T} + \frac {\left(R _ {m , \tau} - \bar {R} _ {m}\right) ^ {2}}{\sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \left(R _ {m , t} - \bar {R} _ {m}\right) ^ {2}} \right]\right)\tag{3}
$$

where $S _ { s } ^ { 2 }$ $\bar { \mathcal { R } } _ { m }$ value over the prediction interval and T is the number of days in the estimation interval. Here, τ indicates the number of days within the event window, and t indicates the number of days in the estimation interval.

Once the ARs of the sample cloud investment announcements are calculated, the CAR can be computed by summing all sample ARs over a specified event window (e.g., ±1 days).

Although a longer day event window $( \mathrm { e . g . , \pm 5 }$ days or ±10 days) can be used, increasing the length of the event window is likely to capture the confounding effects (McWilliams and Siegel, 1997). Therefore, we selected a short event window (±1 day from the event date) to remove almost all of the confounding effects (McWilliams and Siegel, 1997).

Once the length of event window is specified, CAR and the variance of CAR over the event window can then be calculated as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} C A R _ {s, \tau} = \sum_ {i = - \tau} ^ {\tau} A R _ {s, i} \\ \operatorname{var} \left(C A R _ {s, \tau}\right) = \sum_ {i = - \tau} ^ {\tau} \operatorname{var} (A R _ {s, i}) \end{array} \tag {4}\tag{5}
$$

From Equations (4) and (5), we can derive the average CAR (ACAR) across all firms and the variance of CAR as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \overline {{C A R}} _ {\tau} = \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {N} C A R _ {s, \tau} (6) \\ \operatorname{var} (\overline {{C A R}} _ {\tau}) = \frac {1}{N ^ {2}} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {N} \operatorname{var} (C A R _ {s, \tau}) (7) \end{array}
$$

where N denotes the number of sample announcements in our study. For the ACAR in our sample, the daily ARs were averaged over the sample of N announcements and over the three-day event window (McWilliams and Siegel, 1997). For the ACAR in all samples, the ARs usually represent the overall effect of cloud computing investment on a firm‟s market value (Subramani and Walden, 2001). Finally, the ACAR was examined to verify its statistical significance using the t-test with a null hypothesis stating that the ACAR is equal to zero. The formula is as follows:

$$
t = \frac {\overline {{C A R}} _ {\tau}}{\sqrt {\operatorname{var} (\overline {{C A R}} _ {\tau})}} \sim t _ {(a, d f = N - 1)} (8)
$$

The model uses CAR as a dependent variable and incorporates the six contextual factors corresponding to H2 to H7 as independent variables.<sup>5</sup> The model specification used in our study is described as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} C A R _ {i} = & \text { Constants } + \beta_ {1} F S _ {i} + \beta_ {2} E X P _ {i} + \beta_ {4} C S T _ {i} + \beta_ {5} C I T _ {i} + \beta_ {6} C B _ {i} + \beta_ {7} V R _ {i} + \beta_ {8} I N D _ {i} + \beta_ {9} A S _ {i} \\ & + \beta_ {1 0} D T _ {i} + \beta_ {1 1} \Sigma Y e a r _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i} (9) \end{array}
$$

As the dependent variable, $C A R _ { i }$ refers to cumulated abnormal return around the cloud computing announcement of firm i. We used the AR over the three-day event window from day -1 to day +1.

As the independent variable, FS<sub>i</sub> denotes natural log-transformed total assets<sub>t-1</sub> one year prior to the announcement year. The current study employed total asset as a measure for firm size. We intend to implement a measure that represents the scale of firm resources to explain the contribution of cloud computing to a firm‟s market value from the RBV and ERBV perspectives. We used total asset because the measure frequently used in IS studies as a resource-related indicator (Goode and Gregor, 2009). $E X P _ { i } ,$ which represents prior experience, refers to a sample firm‟s previous cloud computing experience captured from the announcements. Such informed prior experience includes the cases of contract renewal for the existing cloud services, switch of cloud vendors or services, or extension of the contract for additional cloud services.

For cloud computing service type (i.e., CST<sub>i</sub>), we used a dummy variable to indicate whether the sample firms adopt either SaaS or non-SaaS cloud service, as discussed in Hypothesis 4. $C I T _ { i } ,$ which refers to cloud computing implementation type, was coded as 1 if firm i integrated a certain cloud service with existing systems; otherwise, it was coded as 0. $C B _ { i }$ represented cloud computing benefits coded as either operational benefit or strategic benefit by using dummy variables. The testing result of Hypothesis 1suggests that the announcements of cloud computing investments are significantly associated with positive market returns. This result empirically demonstrates that the business value of cloud computing is justifiable from the perspective of stock market evaluations. The findings show that the financial market recognizes the potential of cloud computing as an emerging IT practice that can achieve a higher IT management efficiency, which is directly and indirectly linked to better firm performance.

Furthermore, we find that the investors‟ reactions to cloud computing investments are associated with the characteristics of cloud computing initiatives. Among the firm-specific factors, investors (or shareholders) perceive the cloud computing initiatives conducted by smaller firms more favorably (Hypothesis 2). The significant result of FS in our study implies that it can be used as a valid instrument for examining the variance in investors‟ perceptions of the economic salience of technology innovations across organizations.

However, the coefficient of EXP is statistically indistinguishable from zero (Hypothesis 3). EXP‟s lack of effect on the investor‟s positive reactions can be attributed to the following reasons. First, cloud computing is a new paradigm in the use of service-oriented IT resources compared with traditional IT management, which is based on the assumption of proprietary IT ownership. Given the novelty and dynamic nature of cloud computing, investors may perceive that firms constantly acquire new skills and knowledge to efficiently manage this new IT service model (Mithas et al., 2011). Second, given that cloud computing has recently been commercialized, investors possibly do not recognize much difference in the relative capability to capitalize on the new IT service model between firms with prior cloud computing experiences and those without (Staten 2008). Accordingly, our sample period is too premature to capture the effect of prior experience in cloud computing. Third, firms may not benefit from past experience of cloud services because the services could be aligned with simpler quality assurance, such as service level agreements (SLAs). Given that SLAs consist of a set of predefined measures for monitoring service level, they may not necessarily require client firms‟ accumulated IT managing skills from prior experience (Malladi and Krishnan 2012a) The insignificant result of EXP encourages further studies to redefine construct and ensure validation through iterative empirical tests in the cloud computing context (Dehning et al., 2005).

For resource-specific factors, we find that CIT has a significant effect on the positive market values of the investing firms (Hypothesis 5). This finding suggests that the market is concerned not only with the event of the cloud computing initiative but also with the

#

application of this new technology to business operations. The positive and significant result of the integrated cloud solution implies that a firm‟s effort to integrate cloud computing with the existing systems can be regarded in the stock market as a favorable way to efficiently realize the benefits and reduce expected risks from this emerging technology.

Among the CSTs, SaaS cloud service has a significant effect on the positive market value of the investing firms (Hypothesis 4), which predicts that application-related cloud services (i.e., SaaS) can have a relatively greater effect on market returns. Investors may assess the SaaS cloud service to be more valuable based on its capability to instantly provide IT functions that are closely related to business operations. For firms aiming to gain immediate returns for their IT investments, application-related cloud services, such as SaaS, would be a relatively plausible choice compared with other cloud service types (e.g., IaaS and PaaS) because SaaS provides more adaptability, which facilitates the application of cloud computing to business processes.

With respect to CB (Hypothesis 6), a significant relationship unexpectedly exists between operational benefit and positive market returns. It indicates that the market is currently more interested in operational potential (e.g., cost-saving) in cloud computing than in strategic benefits. The result seems to be counter-intuitive because strategic benefit is expected to receive a more positive market evaluation. A possible explanation for the result is that investors have shortsighted preference for the operational benefit over the strategic benefit. Cloud service providers have emphasized the operational advantage as a major marketing point to persuade client firms (Plummer, 2008; Staten, 2008). Thus, only a few cases have successfully used cloud computing in a strategic way because the commercialization of cloud computing services has recently expanded. The market need more time to understand and trust the strategic value of cloud computing. Another possibility is that the global economic recession in recent years has motivated firms to be more

interested in cost-effective ways of using IT resources, driving them to transfer their IT to cloud computing environments. Therefore, investors tend to prioritize the implementation of cost-effective systems rather than the exploitation of the strategic benefits of cloud computing.

For vendor-specific factors, VR is not associated with positive increase in the market investors do not perceive a significant variance in potential values of cloud computing by vendor status. One possible reason is that investors have not specifically identified differentiated points (or competitiveness) among service offerings from cloud vendors due to the recency of cloud computing commercialization. Thus, investors who evaluate the market merging IT service model to effectively manage its business operations. Nonetheless, firms should consider external entities (e.g., partners and vendors) as important sources of competitive advantage in networked business environment (Lavie, 2006). In the more mature ERP market, prior studies have already identified the importance of vendor status and found its significant influence on the market value of adopting firms (e.g., Hayes et al. 2001; Oh et al. 2006a). Although validating the effect of vendor status in the context of cloud computing is still premature, researchers need to keep testing the role of vendor capabilities under diverse research settings.

## 5.2. Implications for Theory and Practice

The results have several theoretical and practical implications. From a theoretical perspective, the study contributes to both RBV and ERBV theories. Based on the two theories, we extend the boundary of the IT literature toward the new IT and business phenomenon, and link the academic attention to an emerging issue in the IT industry. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of earliest attempts to address the business value of cloud computing by adopting the ERBV perspective. Considering the growing research interest in assessing the business value of cloud computing investments, our findings support the role of IS research in

#

establishing positive evidence and building credibility for an emerging technology or IT phenomenon (Agarwal and Lucas, 2005). In this sense, the current study strengthens our understanding of the role and contribution of new IT innovations to business operations in networked business environments.

Furthermore, we broaden the theoretical knowledge by applying ERBV in conceptualizing cloud computing business and by empirically examining the association of contextual factors with market reaction to cloud computing initiatives (Williamson, 1999). With notable exceptions (e.g., Malladi and Krishnan, 2012a, 2012b; Venters and Whitley 2012), the current study is among the first to examine key contextual factors of cloud computing and to empirically validate their relationship with their economic value. A more detailed understanding of the effect of cloud computing must be obtained before significant progress can be made. Therefore, the theoretical and empirical demonstration of the effect of the contextual factors in the cloud computing context is an important theoretical contribution. In this manner, this study provides a comprehensive research guide regarding how to theorize an emerging technology and enables future research to reflect more on applying ERBV to other IT contexts.

In terms of practice, this study has several managerial implications. First, this study provides preliminary evidence regarding the benefits accrued from cloud computing initiatives. The study motivates managers to identify what firm-level characteristics can be leveraged to maximize the potentials of the new IT service model. In this sense, the conceptual model in this study helps managers configure key factors examined in terms of stock market reaction and verify if they contribute to the beneficial use of cloud computing.

Second, the results provide managers with practical basis that can be used in adopting this new IT service model to increase the efficiency of current IT management practices and facilitate further business innovations. Although we show more favorable market reaction to

#

smaller firms that initiated cloud computing, our results need not be interpreted as completely confined to smaller firms. The initiatives for larger firms can also lead to positive consequences if cloud computing is implemented based on the best practices of smaller firms, in which managers have more chances of understanding the core nature of cloud services and efficiently capitalize on these services for business purposes (Fichman, 2004).

Third, we find that cloud initiatives pertaining to cost-reduction benefits are significantly associated with positive returns. For managers, the empirical result practically reinforces the function of cloud computing as a technology platform that can save on IT operational costs. However, unlike strategic benefits from cloud computing adoption, the effects of operational benefits may not be sustainable in the long term (Dehning et al., 2003; Dos Santos et al., 1993; Im et al., 2001). Accordingly, practitioners should think beyond the cost efficiency of cloud computing because this narrow focus may overlook the true values of the new IT model in terms of agility and innovation (World Economic Forum 2010). In academic perspective, studies such as those of Marston et al. (2010) and McAfee (2011) highlight strategic benefits obtained from cloud computing. Other studies (e.g., Malladi and Krishnan, 2012a, 2012b) empirically advocate the role of cloud computing in facilitating CIO strategic focus and IT-enabled innovation. Thus, cloud service providers must validate the more persuasive potential of cloud computing to strengthen a firm‟s market competence, thus making this emerging IT service model more attractive to their clients. Hence, vendors are required to develop more diverse service offerings that can satisfy various corporate needs and to discover the best practices regarding strategic use of cloud computing in business operations.

Finally, our results also suggest that firms in the service industry achieve relatively higher benefits from cloud computing services compared with those in non-service industries. We assume that the return on cloud computing investments can be rapidly realized in a more customer-interactive and competition-intensive industry, which are the constituents of the service industry sector. Thus, managers who seek to apply cloud computing to their organizations must consider the firm and industry-specific characteristics that influence the success of the investments. These managers must also decide on the appropriate timing at which they can launch their initiatives.

## 5.3. Limitations and Future Research

Despite the aforementioned contributions, this study has several limitations, which also offer opportunities for future research. First, this study used public announcements of cloud computing initiatives as a primary data source. Although we seek to propose the theoretical relevance for the selected variables, various constructs in the research model are consequently limited to available information derived from our sample. We acknowledge that other meaningful constructs may have better explanation of the relationship between cloud computing practices and firm performance. Therefore, our research model needs to be further refined by considering organizational and technological features to capture the effect of the cloud computing adoption on business value in a more detailed manner.

Second, we are not able to address the causality between cloud computing services and market performance because of the nature of research method and data. That is, the variables used for our analysis present organizational and technological status (e.g., firm size and cloud service types) at a specific time point based on cloud computing announcements. Such crosssectional model is well-suited to understand association rather than causality. Thus, future studies should use advanced estimation techniques with longitudinal datasets to test the causality between contextual factors and business value of cloud computing.

Third, this study focuses mainly on the client side. Further studies should investigate the effect of cloud computing announcements on the market values of service providers. For example, future studies can consider various vendor related factors, such as vendor size and

#

vendor experience. Uunderstanding the client and vendor in the cloud computing context is important because a successful outcome is determined not only by the service provider or the client but also by the organizations

Finally, the study mostly draws on the technology adoption perspective to investigate the investors‟ perception toward cloud computing initiatives related to the future value of firms.

This approach is appropriate for studying the business value of an emerging technology.

However, our study does not embrace other theoretical perspectives that may be useful in understanding cloud computing investments. A possible perspective is to study the assimilation of cloud computing in organizations. From this line of research, IS researchers can identify other critical factors and gain a better understanding of what drived higher level of cloud computing utilization in organizations.

## 6. Conclusion

With the growing interest in cloud computing adoption to facilitate the strategic use of IT linked to firms‟ business competence, the evaluation of the new IT service model with respect to business value is emerging as a salient issue in IS field. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the early studies to conceptually identify key contextual factors of cloud computing and empirically assess its potential value.

After analyzing official firm-level announcements, we find that a firm‟s cloud computing initiative is associated with the positive increases in a firm‟s market value. Such increases vary in terms of firm-specific (e.g., firm size and prior experience), resource-specific (e.g., cloud computing types, implementation types, and cloud computing benefits) and vendorspecific factors (e.g., vendor stability). The results prove the economic value accrued from cloud computing adoption and provide an initial groundwork for researchers who attempt to further study the business value of cloud computing. Moreover, these results can be used as a reference for practitioners to understand whether, and in what ways, this new IT service

model contributes to a firm‟s business performance.

## Cloud computing deployments are increasing despite concerns about security

How widespread is business use of cloud computing? The results of one survey published Tuesday suggest use of both public and private clouds has real momentum in the United States, with private cloud computing appearing to be more popular.

Among 210 IT executives in U.S. businesses, roughly one-third currently uses only private cloud computing, while another one-third uses both private and public clouds. The survey, conducted in August by Harris Interactive and sponsored by Novell, indicates that 43% of IT executives with decision-making authority foresee increased use of both public and private cloud platforms in the future. Roughly 29% expect more use of private-cloud platforms, while 5% expect increased use of public clouds Another 5% have "no plans" regarding use of cloud computing, and 7% said they are not sure.

The Harris Interactive survey also sought to find out whether cloud computing deployments will occur alongside, in-stead of replacing, company-owned data centers.

When asked if the use of cloud computing will increase as current IT platforms need to be replaced, 92% of the IT execs answered either "strongly agree" or "somewhat agree." At the same time, almost 9 in 10 agree that cloud computing will occur alongside, instead of replacing, company-owned data centers.

When asked about the benefits of public cloud computing, 68% cited freedom of maintaining hardware and a lower cost of upkeep; about 60% reported both lower cost of upkeep and better resource scalability; and 37% said public-cloud computing enables "quicker IT response time."

But public cloud computing still raises significant security concerns. Among the IT executives polled, 91% said they had security concerns about public cloud computing, and almost 9 in 10 said they believed confidential data is more secure in private-cloud systems than it is in public-cloud systems. In addition, 81% cited concerns that it's harder to maintain regulatory and policy compliance in public as opposed to private clouds.

Some negative perceptions about public-cloud computing are still rampant, with three-quarters of respondents expressing the opinion that "outside vendors are not as diligent about data security as internal IT departments." But perhaps most telling is the question of where responsibilities between client and vendor lie: 60% of the IT execs said they "feel it is unclear in public cloud computing."

The Harris Interactive survey indicates that 8 in 10 of the respondents seem to be tilting toward private-cloud computing as offering "most of the advantages of public cloud computing without the security and compliance issues." Most (89%) agreed private clouds are the next logical step for organizations already using virtualization.

When asked about possible barriers to adoption of private-cloud computing, 53% said the initial cost is a barrier and half expressed security concerns as well.
