# The deep structure of organizational online networking – an actor-oriented case study

Dr Matthias Trier\* & Dr Alexander Richter<sup>†,‡</sup>

\*Copenhagen Business School, DK-2000 Frederiksberg, Denmark, email: mt.itm@cbs.dk, <sup>†</sup>University of Zurich, CH-8050 Zürich, Switzerland and <sup>‡</sup>Bundeswehr University Munich, 85577 Neubiberg, Germany, email: arichter@i<sup>fi</sup>.uzh.ch

Abstract. While research on organizational online networking recently increased signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, most studies adopt quantitative research designs with a focus on the consequences of social network con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. Very limited attention is paid to comprehensive theoretical conceptions of the complex phenomenon of organizational online networking. We address this gap by adopting a theoretical framework of the deep structure of organizational online networking with a focus on their emerging meaning for the employees. We apply and assess the framework in a qualitative case study of a large-scale implementation of a corporate socia network site (SNS) in a global organization. We reveal organizational online networking as a multi-dimensional phenomenon with multiplex relationships that are unbalanced, primarily consist of weak ties and are subject to temporal change. Further, we identify discourse drivers and information retrievers as two mutually interdependent actor roles as an explanation for uneven levels of user contributions to the SNS. Based on our analysis, we elicit abstract order principles, such as topical discourses, and identify transactive memory theory as a potent explanation of the evolving interaction structures. We <sup>fi</sup>nally discuss how the deep structure framework can contribute to future research on organizational networks.

Keywords: deep structure, online social networking, organizational online net working, social network sites, case study

## MOTIVATION

Research that relates the con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of social networks to advantages for individuals, teams or whole organizations has a long tradition and recently increased exponentially (Borgatti et al. 2009; Giles, 2012; Wu, 2013; Zhang & Venkatesh, 2013). Within this research domain, the increasing importance of electronic media for organizational communication (Fallows, 2002) attracted academics’ interest in the special role of online communication for organizational networks (Ahuja et al., 2003; Cho et al., 2005; Barak & Gluck-Ofri, 2007; Robert et al., 2008;

Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld et al., 2009; Faraj et al., 2011; Mazmanian et al., 2013). Related quantitative evidence shows that network structures matter in multiple important ways (Borgatti et al., 2009). However, extant <sup>fi</sup>ndings appear incoherent, which suggests contextual in<sup>fl</sup>uences that have so far not been systematically considered in theoretical frameworks. For example, high structural centrality of actors (Freeman, 1979) was related to knowledge contribution in electronic networks of practice (McLure Wasko & Faraj, 2005), whereas in other studies, lower levels of network centralization improved complex problem solving of groups (Bavelas, 1950). A related open question is whether egocentric development of structural holes (high levels of actor centrality) or closely knit communities (low levels of actor centrality) create individual or group-level advantages (Burt, 2001). These contrasts illustrate the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of interpreting structural networking patterns, as their meaning appears to be subject to individual actors attributions, to the context and to the researchers’ perspective.

Next to assessing structural aspects, the implications of quantitative relationship properties are inconclusive. For example, individuals may seek to achieve improved access to diverse information through weak ties or emotional support and bonding through trusted strong ties (Granovetter, 1973). Besides the preceding studies that take a relational approach, Yoo & Kanawattanachai (2001) argue that socio-cognitive theories might provide a better explanation of social networking, as individuals are driven by the need to ‘coordinate their knowledge and expertise to solve the problems they face’ (p. 188). For social network sites (SNS), the primary relevance of cognitive processes and accessing diverse information via weak ties is corroborated by a study of Robert et al. (2008). They found that ‘structural and cognitive aspects were more important to knowledge integration when teams communicated through lean digital networks’ (p. 314). In the context of knowledge sharing, such cognitive aspects of informal networking have been related to transactive memory systems (TMS) (Davison et al., 2013). TMS is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ‘specialized division of labor with respect to the encoding, storage, and retrieval of information from different substantive domains that develops during the course of many relationships’ (Wegner, 1987; Hollingshead, 2000, p. 258). In contrast to these cognitive explanations of networking and its implications, Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. (2009) report that use of enterprise SNS related to developing strong ties with colleagues, but not to accessing new expertise. Again, these incoherent <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the phenomenon of socia networking is complex, contextual and apparently not yet suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently understood. We need a more systematical grasp of the relative and situative importance of structural embeddedness, social bonding through strong relationships or socio-cognitive requirements such as topic search, learning and problem solving in this context (Richter & Riemer, 2013). One approach is to understand what exactly is hidden behind the observable surfacing network structures and the derived metrics (e.g. centrality) captured and studied in social network research.

The importance of understanding these complex inner workings of online social networks for interpreting the surfacing network structure has been pointed out in recent work: scholars warned of unreflected use of network data, as SNS might, in fact. be different from conventional social networks (Howison et al., 2011; Kane et al., 2014). Further, Trier (2008) illustrates how online interaction patterns can be subject to fast changes that often re<sup>fl</sup>ect the impact of higher-level order principles such as external or internal events. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the context of an internet community is further examined by Moser et al. (2013). The authors explore how actors and their digital interaction form underlying organizational practices of the community, which in turn shape the users’ further interactions. In an organizational context, Koch et al. (2013) emphasize that a good <sup>fi</sup>t of managerial organizational norms and values and the actors’ use of social media affects the implementation success. A more thorough investigation of the organizational context of networking and its impact is hence necessary to understand how interpretations of information systems and the emerging online social network patterns are affected.

In order to develop a solid foundation that helps researchers to address all these critica aspects of network research, we propose and apply the deep structure framework (DSF) as a systematic approach to go beyond the surfacing network structures and uncover the subjective meaning of the deeper layers of online social networking in the organization, as perceived by the participants. Our work grounds in conceptualizations of the meaning of socia networking that recently have been discussed in the context of qualitative sociological network research (Fuhse, 2009).

The detailed theorization of deeper levels that eventually bring about an observable and measurable social network con<sup>fi</sup>guration recognizes the long-standing concern of Emirbayer & Goodwin (1994) to give more emphasis to the symbolic construction of categories during the emergence of network structures and to the development of subjective meaning of networks. This qualitative perspective can be a starting point for reconciling the preceding mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings in extant (primarily quantitative) network research. For example, it allows scholars to systematically qualify the prospective importance of cognitive aspects and the topical landscape (Yoo & Kanawattanachai, 2001) as drivers of the evolution of the informa network structures. In an organizational context, it is further very likely that underlying factors facilitate and constrain observed network structures. More generally, a qualitative inquiry into the deep structure of networking can help other researchers to extend the <sup>fi</sup>ndings offered by structural network analysis (e.g. centrality and density) in a systematic way.

We aim to advance current approaches to study social networking in organizations by <sup>fi</sup>rst developing an analytic theoretical lens (Gregor, 2006) with the objective to conceptualize a suitable framework to capture the deep structure of online social networks. Motivated by Emirbayer & Goodwin (1994) and drawing from a recent approach by Fuhse (2009), we focus on the cultural constructs such as expectations, identities and categories that constitute the meaning of the online social network.

We then apply and illustrate our DSF with an empirical case study that is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst systematic inquiries into the different interdependent layers of organizational online networking with SNS. Based on our approach and the empirical case data, we are able to contribute to several relevant topics of network research: we identify how the participants of the corporate SNS assume several heterogeneous and mutually interdependent roles that can explain the emergence and evolution of online social networking. Our case analysis further sheds light on the divergent interpretations of the meaning of organizational online social networking for active and passive users. With the framework, we can better understand how emergent types of actor roles can inform the interpretation of concepts like centrality or user activity. Finally, we relate our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings to the recent insights from the research on TMS (Wegner, 1987) to explore how TMS constitutes a suitable theoretical grounding for explaining ou observed networking behaviours. With this link, we contribute to the current controversy about structural, relational or cognitive perspectives as appropriate theoretical explanations.

Our inquiry is guided by the following two research questions:

RQ 1 How can an empirical assessment of the deep structure of online social networking in organizations (and its heterogeneity or evolution) lead to better theoretical explanations of the phenomenon?

RQ 2 How can an inquiry into the deep structure inform the interpretation of observable organizational social network structures with social network analysis (SNA)?

The remainder of the article is organized as follows. In the next section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst present a conceptualization of the four levels of the analytical DSF (Fuhse, 2009) (Figure 1, Box A). In the Methodology and Case Study sections, we then empirically illustrate the four levels of online social networking in a large organization (Figure 1, Box B). We follow a sequential process, in which we point out ambiguities that motivate taking a further level of analysis into consideration. We then address our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question in the Discussion section, when we relate our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings to recent theoretical conceptualizations of TMS (Wegner, 1987) to identify TMS as the appropriate theoretical model to explain our observations (Figure 1, Box C, the link is further unfolded in a brief exhibit in the Discussion section that summarizes further support from a second targeted inquiry). We address the second research question by subsequently re<sup>fl</sup>ecting how the DSF analysis can potentially inform future quantitative SNA and metrics (Figure 1, Box D; Implications for Network Interpretation section). We conclude with outlining important implications for organizational value creation in online social networks.

## THE DEEP STRUCTURE OF SOCIAL NETWORKS

The central focus of this article is the conceptualization and illustration of the deep layers o online social networking. For a systematic development of the DSF as an analytical theory for online social networks, we draw from the meaning structure of social networks, recently proposed by Fuhse (2009). Before doing so, we unfold how the observable network structure is typically conceptualized and studied in existing research. This analysis of the ‘surface’ level of social networking provides us with an initial theoretical lens and a terminology that later helps to link individually perceived concepts, use interpretations and expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to the network structure. We further point out shortcomings where the surfacing network structure alone is not providing conclusive insights. This lack of contextual information suggests avenues for conceptualizing deeper levels of networking phenomena that are necessary to clarify and augment the interpretation in a concrete empirical setting.

## Structural analysis of networks

Most network studies of of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online contexts agree on adopting the measure of degree centrality to characterize network embeddedness (Burkhardt & Brass, 1990; Burt, 1995; Ahuja et al., 2003; Robert et al., 2008). This widespread metric quanti<sup>fi</sup>es the importance of an actor by dividing the number of direct contacts of an actor by the maximum contacts possible in the network. The underlying assumption is that the number of contacts of an actor is linked to his importance or power in the network. Related to centrality is local density, sometimes also referred to as the local clustering coef<sup>fi</sup>cient (Burt, 2001). It measures how densely connected the contacts of some focal actor ‘ego’ are, by comparing the proportion of existing links between the direct contacts of ‘ego’ with the theoretically possible links in this group. This shows if group members tend to collaborate directly with each other or via ‘ego’ as their important central hub. These definitions imply that, if centrality of some actor is high, density around that actor tends to be low and a structural hole exists (Burt, 1995). To correctly understand the optimal balance between density vs. centrality in a particular social network setting, we need to inquire about the network context and the individual meaning that central actors assign to their position or to their participation in densely connected clusters.

Structural centrality and density are also frequently related to tie strength and cognitive <sup>fi</sup>t (or low information diversity). This relationship has initially been popularized by Granovetter (1973), who found that weak ties to diverse and distant others are less redundant and make these links information rich, whereas strong ties are unlikely to bring about novel information. On the other hand, strong ties of repeated interaction are related to emotional support and trust (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Ridings et al., 2002). A further property of the relationship can be its reciprocity (also symmetry in terms of how often each actor was active). Reciprocity has for example been related to disclosing information online (Barak & Gluck-Ofri, 2007). As noted in our introduction, in this context of competing theories, a thorough analysis of the context and roles of actors is required to conclusively determine whether weak or strong and unidirectional or reciprocal ties better explain some empirical setting.

Previous research studied different general types of relationships, such as friendship, advice information exchange or trust (Wasserman & Faust, 1994). These relationship types can coexist in a social network, leading to multiplex ties between actors. For structural analysis, however, multiple simultaneous relationship types imply analytical challenges. While methods to deal with such multiplexity have been proposed, most researchers followed the recommendation of

Wasserman & Faust (1994, p. 219) to not consider the different relationship types as additive but to follow a comparative approach that compares the different link structures to uncover overlaps or disparities that then inform theory (Haythornthwaite, 2006; Howison et al., 2011). While structural comparisons may detect overlaps and complementation effects, they are not uncovering complex discursive interdependencies between different coexisting relationship types, e.g. if different time horizons or other sources of incommensurability are involved. A related issue is that sociological theories were based on an analysis of the conventional forms of social relationships (e.g. kinship or friendship). In online social networks, however, we may <sup>fi</sup>nd a multitude of new ways to be related, e.g. via sharing documents or tagging the same resource. It is not yet academically established whether the resulting network graph is actually suitable for interpretations that are informed by ‘of<sup>fl</sup>ine’ theories (Howison et al., 2011). At the same time, there is no reason to believe that these relationship types are stable over time or independent of each other (Quintane et al., 2013).

Other research studied digital networks with multiple types of actors (Kane & Alavi, 2008) in a multi modal approach. A structural lens, however, focusses on the causes or consequences of the position of different types of actors. Such analysis would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from being augmented by a systematic interpretative inquiry into different existing actor roles. As an example, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to identify experts in a community by just looking at actors’ structural patterns (Casciaro & Sousa, 2005).

In conclusion, we argue that structural research of the ‘surface’ network often requires additional qualitative inquiry into the underlying symbolic construction process to yield unambiguous results. Such an in-depth investigation can better address heterogeneity within a collective (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007, p. 667) and can uncover meaningful interdependencies of different relationship types between actors. Our conceptualization and illustration of the deep structure of online socia networking contribute to our knowledge, as there is no empirical investigation of the perceived relationship types, assigned meanings and actor identities in an SNS.

## Theoretical framework

Similar to our earlier review. Fuhse (2009, p. 67) also arrived at the conclusion that 'the level of meaning has often been ignored in network research’. He argues that too little attention was given to expectations or cultural practices embodied in interpersonal structures. To better address the interplay of meaning and structure, he developed a theoretical framework that draws from relational sociology and is inspired by White’s (1992) view of networks as phenomenological realities composed of stories and identities. In order to conceptualize the deep structure of organizational online networking, we draw from Fuhse’s classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and disaggregate a social network into four interrelated levels that enable to focus our attention on different underlying aspects of networking (Figure 1). These levels help to transcend beyond the level of the visible network to more abstract domains that determine how and why people network.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst level of the DSF is the empirically observed (‘surface’) network that we introduced in the previous section. It is mostly modelled as an analytical network graph (Figure 2, bottom) that is used to derive widely used measures such as centrality. The observed relationships may in practice differ slightly in their meaning, but for analytical purposes, homogeneity is assumed. As outlined earlier, we argue that research needs to go beyond this surfacing network structure, and Howison et al. (2011) supports this claim for the study of online relations.

For that purpose, the <sup>fi</sup>rst layer underlying the observable network structures is the transactiona level (Figure 2, left). Transactions can be exchanged messages or goods that eventually yield the observed ‘surface’ network. They take place between people and can be shaped by structura and situational constraints. Researchers can specify the nature of these transactions as reciprocal, mutual, balanced, recurring, cooperative or con<sup>fl</sup>icting.

While transactions indicate what happens, the meaning structure addresses why these transactions occur. ‘This is the level of interpersonal (or interactor) expectations that evolve in and guide transactions’ (Fuhse, 2009, p. 53). Transactions emerge from a relatively stable pattern o interpersonal expectations, which in turn may emerge from past experience. These expectations de<sup>fi</sup>ne how to make sense of the interactions and the emerging types of relationships such as friendship or collaboration. In this process, the identities of the actors are constructed. This leve of the social network features two different aspects: the individual expectations and perceptions and also the interpersonally established expectations and cultural forms.

Finally, there is an overarching fourth level with a focus on order principles that may structure the expectations and transactions. There are two theoretical types of such principles. Opportunity structures are rather devoid of meaning and affect transactions directly. One example is the bias produced by the geographic co-location of some actors. A further order principle is constituted by existing actor categories, such as age group or gender, or existing relationship models. Both are also existing forms of meaning, but they also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the meaning structure of networking.

In<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the deepest layer of the order principles, transactions and the meaning structure are tied together. Through transactions, de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of actors and relationships emerge. As expectations, embodied in these de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, they guide future transactions. This social structure of transactions and the meaning structure results in the analytical picture that is so often used as the basis of measuring quantitative network data. Further, the abstract order principles may also change over time.

The elements of the DSF can be exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed with the following brief scenario: in organizational online interaction, the hierarchical reporting relationships and the assignment of business tasks comprise contextual order principles (level 4) that act as opportunity structures, determining which employees exchange particular information across which locations. The employees develop and negotiate interpretations about the forming relationships and their roles in the overall network (level 3). This sense-making process determines interpersonal transactions, such as collective decision-making in meetings (level 2). All these effects leave their imprint in the observable network structure that researchers could, for example, elicit and quantify via observations of the actors’ participation in shared meetings (level 1).

This example suggests that the deeper layers appear of high importance, if a complex organizational context is learned and shared and people have multiplex interdependencies (e.g. in jointly developing a software system with task division). Here, analysing deep structures may identify similarities and differences of online interaction in the open internet and in an organization. For example, what is the role of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency for online social networking? At the same time, DSF analysis can prevent us from inappropriately applying theories from other contexts that are only similar on the surface level but that ground in other meaning contexts (cf. Howison et al., 2011). The DSF can also enable us to better understand perceived business bene<sup>fi</sup>ts on the individual level, but also they are assumed to accrue on the organizational level. It may lead to explanations of differences, where the same media are used in different contexts with different outcomes.

As an illustration of the merits of explicating the various levels of the DSF, and also as a <sup>fi</sup>rst inquiry into the meaning of corporate social networks, we will now employ our framework to investigate the deep structure of online social networking in a leading global engineering organization in the industry, energy and healthcare sectors.

## METHODOLOGY

For our empirical inquiry into the deep structure of organizational online social networks, we adopt an interpretative qualitative research method. As ‘every network context produces its own culture of intersubjectively shared expectations’ (Fuhse, 2009, p. 52), we focus on a single case study of a large corporate implementation of an SNS in a globally operating company.

We use multiple sources of evidence (Eisenhardt, 1989) to ensure the reliability and validity of our results. After preliminary observation of the platform. we conducted semi-structured interviews that inquired into the work context, social media experiences, preferred uses of the SNS and other perceptions and conceptions of the online interaction. Following a theoretical sampling approach (Miles & Huberman, 1994), respondents were selected in cooperation with the organization to achieve interviews that are likely to extend or replicate theory. The selected respondents were from various organizational departments from different locations in Germany (n = 9), Austria (6), Slovakia (3) and the USA (2). They represent diverse hierarchy levels and work contexts including engineering (n = 4), software development (4), headquarter functions (3), consulting (2), governance (2), business development, project business, quality management or portfolio management. One of the interviews was carried out with the main responsibility of the SNS roll-out, which re<sup>fl</sup>ected on the top management perspective. Following the principle of theoretical saturation as the criterion for stopping further inquiry (Miles & Huberman, 1994), we interviewed a total of 20 employees, all participating in the SNS. The interviews were carried out between April and August 2011.

Half of the interviews were conducted in person, and the other half over the phone (after an employee involved in the project established the necessary trust levels). The interviews took between 30 and 90 min and were recorded and then fully transcribed into text. In order to validate the empirical data, the transcript of each interview was then sent to individual participants to verify their answers to give their consent for publishing their quotes (anonymously). The transcripts were subsequently analysed using qualitative content analysis (Miles & Huberman, 1994). The process was supported by software for qualitative research (MaxQDA).

We adopted a qualitative research approach that is interpretative but guided by the abstract ‘sensitizing’ theoretical concepts (Bowen, 2006) from our DSF. Sensitizing concepts can provide ‘a general sense of reference and guidance in approaching empirical instances (Blumer, 1954, p. 7). We use them to focus our theory development on the inner workings of the elements of the DSF and on their interrelationships. In particular, we adopted an initial qualitative coding tree (Miles & Huberman, 1994) with the four very aggregated meta-codes drawn from the DSF: (1) analytical network picture; (2) transaction level; (3) meaning structure; and (4) order principles. We further consider two sub-codes for meaning structure: (3.1) relationships between users and (3.2) the identities of users. We also consider three types of order principles: (4.1) relationship models; (4.2) actor categories; and (4.3) opportunity structures (also Figure 2). Within this sensitizing theory-driven structure of meta-codes, we used an inductive open coding process, so that new codes could emerge from the data. In our case description, we present relevant quotes and their assigned theoretical code labels to illustrate the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed types of relationship or actor identities. To ensure high levels of intercoder agreement, we <sup>fi</sup>rst produced a coding guide that prescribed the length of a coding element as a complete sentence or a combination of multiple complete sentences (only) if they constitute a single semantic unit. We further allowed multiple code categories for the same text element, as the dimensions of the deep structure can coincide. Then a reduced set of 15% of the material was coded by both authors in a joint session, where we <sup>fi</sup>rst each assigned a code to a text block and then assessed any differences in our interpretation. In 90% of the cases, we found agreement in our code assignments. Moreover, the occasional differences were not due to inconsistent categorizations but related to the level of explicitness that each coder initially required to actually code a particular statement.

The data from our initial round of coding, memoing and interpretation to study the deep structure of online social networking motivated a second analysis in which we narrowed our focus to study the relative importance of relational, topical and cognitive aspects to explain the conceptions and mechanisms (structures and processes) of networking. Again, we used sensitizing concepts from related theories (Hollingshead, 2000; Yoo & Kanawattanachai, 2001) to categorize what people expressed with respect to alternative theoretical explanations of networking.

As a further source of evidence in our case analysis (Eisenhardt, 1989), we considered contextual material such as internal documents, minutes or presentations, notes from informa interviews and meetings and (anonymized) log <sup>fi</sup>le analysis of the interaction on the SNS between September 2009 and June 2010.

To support our understanding, we also studied 610 electronic messages exchanged between users on the SNS and coded them according to their use cases as explained by Richter et al. (2011). Based on the log <sup>fi</sup>les from the SNS usage, we <sup>fi</sup>nally produced graph visualizations via automatic analysis of an anonymized version of the platform’s database. The retrieved log data were formatted for network visualization and analysis with the software Commetrix. The social graph represents the level of the analytical network picture of the DSF. Because of privacy requirements and the corresponding anonymization process, we are not directly linking individuals’ interview data to their network position.

We follow a sequential case study design, i.e. we focus on case results, but we also immediately discuss remaining gaps and ambiguities of a particular level of the DSF analysis in order to sequentially assess and establish the need to analyse the next deeper level.

## CASE STUDY

The case company was founded more than 150 years ago and is a leading global engineering conglomerate in the industry, energy and healthcare sectors. More than 400 000 employees work at 1640 locations in 190 regions, including 176 research and development facilities. For this reason, it has a very global and diverse culture. We chose this company, as, at the time of the study, it was one of only few multinational companies where an SNS had reportedly been appropriated and was part of the working practices of many employees (as explained in the following). At the same time, the company representatives were interested in a study that contributes to a better understanding of how the platform connects the employees and catalyses emergent structures like informal networks.

We need to point out that in our analysis, we refrained from investigating national backgrounds, hierarchy effects or subcultures, as we noticed early on that, in our case, the networking behaviou was mainly marked by the business and communication practices that people wanted to support with the platform. We further address this effect in the section on the DSF layer of the order principles.

Information technology plays traditionally a crucial role between the divisions in the case organization. By the time of the study, the corporate objective was to become an ‘integrated technology company’. However, the company’s sectors and cross-sectorial business units are set up in a very autonomous manner. This is also re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the information technology environment for knowledge networking. The <sup>fi</sup>rst version of a platform for online interaction was already introduced in a business unit in 1998. Employees could create networks with specialized topics, and communities have emerged. The interested parties could exchange information, and at the same time, they could be found by other employees. In December 2008, it was decided to develop a new version of the platform to support social networking companywide with the aim of better using synergies and exploiting innovation potential as a result of stronger networking between the sectors.

As a central intention of the new platform, ‘networks’ should connect topics and appropriate experts. Every employee should have the possibility to create networks or join existing communities without an approval process. The network page incorporates typical functions like posting news and discussing network topics.

After a 6-month pilot phase in October 2009 with approximately 3000 users active in 400 networks, it was rolled out companywide in March 2010. At the time of the interview study, the platform had already more than 15 000 networking users globally.

## Analytical network level

Although our main focus is the systematic account of the deeper levels of networking, we begin the analysis with the observable surface network structures of the SNS as the baseline from which we set out to explore the other levels of the DSF

We were faced with the expected practical dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of structural network analysis in online contexts: in contrast to traditional media, the SNS afforded multiple ways for people to form network relationships. For example, users could develop similarity-based relationships, e.g. by joining topic groups or by using similar tags. People could further create relations by liking or commenting on other users’ contributions. Looking at the data, we further found that each of these partial networks showed only a limited view on the overall networking activity. Moreover, relationships were scattered across many disconnected networks with different modes (discussions vs. following others’ activity streams vs. messages), all nested inside different topic groups. Passive readers of such discussions were not included in this trace data. As we will see in our discussion of other levels of the DSF, these (missing) passive members also provided value to the network. Finally, considerable interaction resulted from broadcasting, where one individual simultaneously reaches out to all other members of a topic group. In line with Howison et al. (2011), these issues illustrate the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties to achieve an accurate and coherent depiction of an overall network structure from SNS trace data.

We followed the advice of Wasserman and Faust (1994, p. 219) and did not aggregate the ties to a single construct as they appeared to represent multiple underlying dimensions of the networking activity so that any aggregation would have implied strong biases and limitations. Instead, we focussed our structural analysis on the co-membership network shown in Figure 3, which appeared most complete and most representative. Our interviews (see later discussion) corroborated this methodological decision.

In the co-membership network, a link represents a topical similarity resulting from two em ployees A and B who are joining a topic community (Wasserman & Faust, 1994, p. 307). Selecting this af<sup>fi</sup>liation link type re<sup>fl</sup>ects that information is broadcasted between all members of the group via the activity stream that all have subscribed to. This renders it likely that employees are aware of each other, as authoring individuals.

The more topic groups (the members of) two organizational units share, the stronger is their link. Note that, in agreement with the organization’s privacy protection policies, Figure 3 shows the respective organizational units as nodes to protect the privacy of individual participants; each unit represents a number of employees indicated by the label. Units are of very different sizes.

Our network analysis reveals a complex pattern consisting of various clusters connected by only a few brokering organizational units that engage in multiple topics. The few nodes between clusters indicate that only a few critical participants connect different topical discussions and hence can disseminate new information from one community to another. Most participants appear to follow only one special topic of interest.

The clusters show that most topics are of interest for many organizational departments, and hence, a large number of informal cross-departmental links developed. The clusters have very different group sizes. Apparently, a few prominent shared topic domains account for the majority of cross-departmental informal interrelationships.

The analysis reveals that the network structure is largely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by actors’ topic orientation or information needs, suggesting the important role of cognitive theories in explaining organiza tional networking. In our data, most organizational units follow each other in two topic communities (median = 2 and maximum = 7), suggesting the dominance of weak relationships. The network shows only four highly central organizational units, whose star-like network patterns indicate the absence of direct connections of the contacts around them and hence imply the existence of structural holes. The majority of departments are interconnected in <sup>fi</sup>ve dense clusters, where nodes are highly connected with each other and density, as a function of existing ties divided by possible ties, is high. Conversely, dense clusters imply a very even distribution of centrality and information access as the contacts do not need to pass a central hub node to reach other group members. Dense connections can, however, also be a result of the measurement method: in co-membership networks, all participants in a group are linked with each other.

At this point, an inquiry into the transaction level of our DSF can augment our current analysis and uncover deeper levels of the observed organizational phenomenon. Further, the current level of analysis does not address whether central people are special properties or resources that explain the observed structure. The DSF layer of order principles is dedicated to identifying organizational in<sup>fl</sup>uences that may restrict people’s networking activities and are hence important moderating factors for the structural view.

Finally, the existence of a link from A to B does not tell us about how mutual expectations and perceptions actually shape the multiplex relationships and whether they can be regarded as rich or strong ties. The meaning structure level of our DSF is suitable to address these missing elements of the structural network analysis.

To shed light on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of topics, multiplex relations and the causes of centrality, we wil now continue our DSF analysis by unfolding the deeper levels of networking.

## Transaction level

The second level of the deep structure of social networking is the transaction level. This domain re lates to the question of what is being carried out to create the aforementioned observable structures.

In our interviews, a <sup>fi</sup>rst reported social action of people was that they assign themselves to one or more communities, similar to subscribing to a Facebook (‘fan’) page about a certain topic. While no text has yet been exchanged at this stage, this action is establishing passive followership between members. The data further showed that by following, the employees express interest and a general readiness to read information or to be accessible for requests (respondent 8, henceforth R8), yet some people actually do not intend to advance beyond this activity.

Respondents mentioned the low effort of following (many) others. However, some interview respondents also pointed out that people who are registered in various networks for following information updates in their selected topic <sup>fi</sup>elds may also give rise to the negative connotation that ‘networks are often just consumed’ (R5).

Employees following many topics may appear central in terms of linking different groups in the analytical network graph. However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings at the transaction level illustrate that the actors’ centrality in the network structure may be caused by very different, sometimes mutually exclusive, processes (e.g. dormant attention vs. active dissemination). Relating transaction level with structural level analysis suggests that centrality is hence not necessarily marking important network participants. Well-connected actors are central, however, in their exposure to different topics. This may be related to faster responses or higher levels of innovativeness. Further, the transaction level corroborates that the observable dense co-membership network graph with weak ties appears as an appropriate structural representation. Again, we note that topic-driven activities appear to drive network structure. The modelled links constitute dorman informal paths for raising awareness and disseminating topical information. The actors (i.e. organizational units) that have a broader topic interest eventually span several topic groups. They can contribute as boundary spanners that link departments.

Next to self-attachment to groups, active communication transactions were reported by ou respondents. In more detail, employees mentioned that they apply their expertise to answer speci<sup>fi</sup>c questions, request diverse opinions on some provided information to develop solutions that are compatible with other employees’ requirements or give their opinions regarding the usefulness of presented tasks or objectives. From these transactions, we can imply that, in our case, usage of the SNS is centred around information management, as the following quote exempli<sup>fi</sup>es:

I personally use [the SNS] to get informed about news and requests. I have pre-selected some networks which can provide great added value for my work context. I receive information, which is important for me, and then I decide, if I go more into depth in my research, or how I apply the information. (R17)

In sum, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that two types of transactions constitute the community network. Exchanges are not reciprocal or symmetric in terms of effort invested by the participants. We recognized a broadcasting character in that communications originated at one person and are received by many others. Our respondents <sup>fi</sup>nally noted that different types of transactions follow in a sequence, e.g. from exchange of general (i.e. public) information to actual collaboration (R13 and R10). Transaction-level insights help to clarify ambiguities of the structural analysis.

## Meaning structure level

The third level of the DSF concentrates the analysis on the co-constructed meaning structure of the social network. This level addresses how the actors develop and share a particular set of expectations about their participation in the network. This is further re<sup>fl</sup>ected in emerging actor identities and relationship types. The individual meaning structure as perceived by the participants can further be related to the assumptions and objectives of the main community coordinator or external people. In our case, these external (management) roles considered it a fundamental prerequisite for a thriving corporate community to achieve a maximal number of actively posting participants (R20).

As identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the preceding structural and transactional analyses, the organizational SNS does not emphasize strong reciprocal social relationships but rather fosters a dense network of weak links with the typical pattern of a few active contributors and many mostly passively following recipients. However, it is interesting to note that these unbalanced and often unidirectional relationships sustain despite the fact that the main burden for maintaining momentum is carried by only a few people whereas many others ‘consume’ (R5) the created value of an improved information access without contributing their own contents.

Focussing the DSF analysis on actors’ expectations and attributed meanings sheds light on this phenomenon: our inquiry revealed that the contributors appear to derive their acceptance of this unbalanced state from expected increased awareness of their topic agenda that they intend to push forward and disseminate across the organization:

[I use the SNS to] distribute and broadcast our information and related services, so that people recognize our existence […] We have to come to their minds when our topic is concerned. (R18)

I try to achieve that agile methods are generally accepted as a part of software engineering. (R3) People have a strong interest and that is why they are very active. They want to achieve something, they want to effect change. (R6)

This awareness-based motivation appears not to be primarily rooted in personal gains of reputation or personal centrality, as existing network theories could suggest. Rather, several actors seek to establish and nurture speci<sup>fi</sup>c topics in the organizational discourse. This is corroborated by several SNS users who mentioned that they want to increase their network’s member base (R16 and R17). Also, some people used metaphors like ‘target group’ (R8).

Next to the expectation to raise awareness, the interviews and also the results of the transactional analysis convey that other actors interpreted the SNS as an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient means of retrieving authentic and current information:

For me, it [the SNS] is hence an information medium. (R17)

It is now easier … to get authentic information … I save time with retrieving information…. (R2)

Another respondent even mentions that he follows topics and not persons (R4). For this person, the bene<sup>fi</sup>t is that information sources can be self-selected and updates are automatically received as a news via e-mail. In this interpretation of the meaning of the SNS, the broadcasting of news and the social interaction aspect of the system are strongly attenuated, while the factual information management is the primary purpose with a strong emphasis on work ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

The preceding two interpretations of the meaning of the SNS would not necessarily lead to the observable rich exchange of information and opinions. However, respondents stated that reciprocated relationships resulted from the commonly accepted normative expectation that, if people have the expertise, they should respond with helping feedback to a question. Anothe reason to respond and thereby create a reciprocated relationship is to express likely consequences of a colleague’s news or change proposals, almost like in a mutually accepted negotiation that leads to a broader scope and acceptance of planned activities. Various respondents expressed that their activities are guided by the intention to drive a discourse and evolve it in directions bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for them but accepted by a broad base of colleagues.

In our inquiry, we noticed the general expectation that virtual actor relationships are evolving in a maturity process, from initial awareness to either collaboration (R13) or generation of business leads (i.e. orders, R10). This evolution brings together the broadcasters with the retrievers, fuelled by the generally accepted culture of generalized reciprocity and broad negotiation. This implies that the meaning, and with that the type of a relationship, is not <sup>fi</sup>xed a priori but actually slowly and continuously changes over time, a property that is not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently considered in existing social network research with its practice of comparing selected link types or focus on one dimension alone.

In connection to the preceding interrelated meanings of the SNS, distinct and perceivable actor identities emerged. First, participants noted ‘a core group who might meet and collaborate on the topic and some interested bystanders who follow the topic’ (R16). Further, users identi<sup>fi</sup>ed matchmakers who try to identify and motivate experts to help answer questions (R18). Another role was the emancipated feedback provider, who evaluates change proposals and requests from others in order to advance in particular problem situations and bring in important local requirements. This identity uses the SNS to better transcend organizationa hierarchies and be heard (R1). Finally, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed specialists who are very con<sup>fi</sup>dent about their expertise and who feel eligible to settle speci<sup>fi</sup>c related requests and to correct others statements (R1).

The DSF level of the meaning structure explains why and how the observed roles emerged and develop speci<sup>fi</sup>c online relationships that characterize organizational online networks: <sup>fi</sup>rst, actors link to others in order to shape the organizational discourse in a competition for attention and recipient responses. Second, unbalanced relationships emerge with large efforts for some participants who, in turn, expect to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the discourse. On the other hand, there are participants who are interpreting the SNS as an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient information research tool. Third, mutual help was grounded in a culture of generalized reciprocity or based on defending own requirements. The described set-up leads to relationships that mainly represent a directed information transfer with many weak (mostly also directed) links and only occasional feedback from consumers of the information. Finally, the relationships are perceived rather as a communal good that produces value, increases transparency and furthermore may change its meaning over time. Stronger relationships migrate to other more con<sup>fi</sup>dential environments or physical meetings. These various relationship meanings illustrate the multiplexity and the dynamic change of the links established in SNS, which is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to condense into a simple relationship edge.

Actors who take these roles were faced with the challenge of reconciling their online networking engagement with their contextual organizational practice. Our next DSF dimension considers this tension by explicitly focussing on in<sup>fl</sup>uential order principles.

## Order principles

The fourth DSF level is concerned with order principles that in<sup>fl</sup>uence actor expectations and emerging relationship types. This level is especially relevant for understanding differences in social media adoption between the public internet and the corporate application. Important aspects of an organizational setting are the occasional physical meetings (e.g. in projects), the shared organizational culture or the notion of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in a competitive setting. These order principles may affect transactions directly or shape relationships and actor roles. A thorough understanding of this level is further required to verify implications from structura network models, especially as the organizational network is constantly reacting to external events (Trier, 2008).

In our case company, the dominant organizational impact came from existing corporate objectives and their task contexts. These motivated and constrained actors to shape and in<sup>fl</sup>uence the discourse. As one respondent notes:

My task is assembling a complete solution and I do this via the virtual group. [The SNS platform] is a system for discussing within the group but also for involving local companies. Further specialists are invited to contribute their requirements and adaptations, if they need a special solution for their countries. (R13)

Other people perceive time pressure and hence develop into a topic followership role that emphasizes ef<sup>fi</sup>cient information acquisition. Further, the interplay of dispersion across multiple locations and opportunities to meet in joint projects or community meetings is an organizational in<sup>fl</sup>uence on networking. One respondent argues that when too many people are known and easy to reach (offline). the network loses value as a means of information acguisition (R15)

The organizational requirement of task-related goal attainment caused the observed changes of the relationship meaning from awareness to professional topic development collaboration or business development (R5). Participants insisted on pursuing organizationa objectives and creating value that compensates their time investment.

I would start to ask what we really want to achieve and then I would steer it into a direction that goes beyond ‘Facebook’; an interesting mixture of expertise and informative articles. (R8)

Topic domains emerge as a major ordering principle that affects networking transactions and the resulting structures (also across departments). Participants derived their perception of group identity mainly from their topic similarity. The topics’ properties also determine whether a group is highly interconnected or whether a few people inform and update a large crowd of topic followers. Both alternative con<sup>fi</sup>gurations can be equally effective for the organizational objective.

In conclusion, we notice the important role of the organizational order principles tasks and the respective responsibilities of the individuals, topics and time pressure. Further, the objective to create value changes the nature of the online interaction and creates a need for progress and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient goal attainment. These order principles corroborate that online interaction in an organization is driven by very different mechanisms than those in the public internet, or in other words, research <sup>fi</sup>ndings from extra-organizational settings (e.g. Facebook) are likely to be limited in the applicability to organizational contexts, where important contextual factors constrain and afford the observed network effects.

## DISCUSSION

Our presented study is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical cases with a deep analysis of SNS-based online social networking in large organizations. The DSF provided a very suitable framework to systematically capture and reveal the de<sup>fi</sup>ning features of organizational online interaction at different levels. Whereas the <sup>fi</sup>rst two levels of the DSF enable a description of what is happening in the network, levels 3 and 4 shed light on its meaning for the users and hence focus on explanations for the observations. The identi<sup>fi</sup>ed complexities of the deep layers explain why theorizing based on the network structural layers alone is problematic.

One interview with the main coordinator of the SNS implementation effort (R20) enables another veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of the revealed user perspective to the expectations and requirements of top management. He referred to a management vision of creating ‘an emergent structure’ with ‘digital traces of the experts […]. Important are the connections between people, with the aim that they are aware of each other and exchange in the case of need’. Further managemen did aim at creating weak ties and did not expect or require that strong links are forged with the SNS. Rather, this is assumed to happen in other organizational contexts. Based on our data, we hence conclude that the networking processes and the emerging meaning structure of the network that we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed are consistent with the expectations of management and create the required added value also on the organizational level.

The systematic DSF account generated can now be used to address our two research questions.

We will now <sup>fi</sup>rst identify and develop relevant theoretical models of organizational online networking (research question 1) to assess if extant SNS theories that ground in studies of public social networks (Facebook, Twitter, etc.) are actually applicable to organizational contexts. Then we will re<sup>fl</sup>ect on methodical implications for interpreting observable network structures with SNA methods (research question 2).

## Theoretical drivers of online social networking in the organization

On a more general level, our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question asks which network theories provide the most appropriate explanations in organizational contexts. In particular, it can be argued that many SNS-related theories actually ground in studies of public internet use, and while on the surface, the networks might look similar, our account of the deep structure points out very idiosyncratic properties and objectives of organizational social networking applications, casting doubts on the explanatory power of extant theories.

The transactions, meanings and ordering principles identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in our case study suggest that the deep structure of online social networking in organizations is fundamentally shaped by the actors’ engagement in the consumption, dissemination and development of different topical discourses.

In an attempt to theorize the empirical observations, this <sup>fi</sup>nding strongly suggests a dominating role of a socio-cognitive disposition (Yoo & Kanawattanachai, 2001) as the appropriate explanation of organizational online networking processes.

With evidence for this disposition, we can explicate theoretical differences between online social networking in the organizational and in public internet SNS such as Facebook (Ellison et al., 2007). Extant theories that were mostly derived in studies of public internet platforms typically explained the phenomena as a result of social/relational preferences. With this different orientation identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, it is important to recognize that while social network graphs in public and corporate contexts might look similar on the surface, they re<sup>fl</sup>ect fundamentally different phenomena.

Based on our data, we theorize that the underlying reason for the observed topical orientation is the actors’ requirement of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient information management via access to diverse novel information through many weak (or even dormant) links. This ordering principle calls for professionalism and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient employment of scarce resources. People rationalize the SNS as an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient device for information retrieval or to disseminate and grow a topic.

Further, our DSE analysis uncovers the need for more differentiated theories on actor roles Contrary to the methodological assumption of one coherent and simple actor role for the ‘nodes that is typically made in structural network analysis, our organizational networking domain showed two different and interrelated roles that together shape the dynamics of the online interaction in a complex interplay. Consequently, we can conceptualize discourse drivers who primarily intend to disseminate and grow a topic. As the second main role, information retrievers are interested in accessing information effectively. The drivers are motivated by shaping the orga nizational discourse and establishing their topic agenda in ways that <sup>fi</sup>nd a broad consensus among the employees. The retrievers emphasize ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains and appreciate the added value of information that directly comes from experts. These recipients want to avoid distractions and are very careful in selecting the information they want to receive. The dependencies between these mutually supporting roles are the actual source of the network activity despite the uneven distribu tion of efforts. On a more general level, the interplay of these two interdependent roles provides a theoretical explanation of why we <sup>fi</sup>nd prospering communities, even if only very few people actively contribute. Like in a Pareto equilibrium, a smaller group of contributors competes for a large group of retrievers in order to grow their topic. In the light of this <sup>fi</sup>nding, the extant notion of ‘lurking’ (Preece et al., 2004) should hence be replaced by theoretical concepts such as ‘information retriever’ to better account for the very rational reasons for the passivity.

The observed dynamic further results in many weak relationships that are likely to have a clear direction of information <sup>fl</sup>ow. People attribute their willingness to help not necessarily to perceived obligation to return a favour. Rather, the salient cultural norm of a generalized obligation to help each other emerges as a very important factor to move from initial low intensity follower relationships (implied by the two roles) to rich interaction and the corresponding reciprocity through feedback, negotiations or answering of questions. The complex interrelationship of the two roles and cultural norms interact, resulting in interpersonal relationships that constantly change and mature over time.

The aim of exerting impact on the organizational discourse also results in a process where people search for prospective partners, then establish contacts online, develop collaborations and <sup>fi</sup>nally develop additional interaction modes to work on joint projects. The primacy of weak relationships found in the SNS can also be related to the general accessibility of the contents: people mentioned that for more con<sup>fi</sup>dential topics, they leave the platform and also they try to arrange physical meetings.

While participants noted the formation of a shared language, members rarely established strong relationships, which could give rise to theorized bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as emotional suppor and high levels of trust (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). We note the importance of organizationa context for interpreting the networking behaviour: strong relationships may result from SNS use, but platforms appear to be used in the early stages of this interpersonal process before people move, in a subsequent step, to other modes of interaction. None of the respondents expressed any egocentric structural objectives such as becoming central in a relationship network, but such a desire might still be implied for those participants who were trying to build a discourse and a larger group of interested followers. Interestingly, these people primarily appear to seek structural centrality of their topic, but not their person.

The identi<sup>fi</sup>ed dominance of (topic-driven) socio-cognitive dispositions (Yoo & Kanawattanachai, 2001) has important implications for identifying appropriate theoretical frameworks for explaining online social networking in organizations. Despite the current preponderance of social–relational theories such as social capital, our case data strongly suggest to adopt more cognitively oriented theories. In particular, we can join our empirically grounded <sup>fi</sup>ndings with insights from studies of TMS (Wegner, 1987; Hollingshead, 2000; Yoo & Kanawattanachai, 2001; Davison et al.,

2013). This theory explains networking as a process that yields a specialized division of labour with respect to the encoding, storage and retrieval of information from different substantive domains during the course of many interactions (Wegner, 1987; Hollingshead, 2000, p. 258). In this process, network members reduce topical redundancy among people by assuming responsibility for some areas of expertise but not for others and increasingly learn about and rely upon other people’s complementary domain expertise. TMS can provide a socio-cognitive explanation of the motivations and dynamics of online social networking: in an effort to establish and develop transactive memory, people build personal pro<sup>fi</sup>les, information is allocated to experts and people use the resulting network structure as a socio-cognitive mechanism to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently retrieve information.

Based on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we propose that TMS offers the most suitable theoretical foundation for researching the organizational networking domain. In order to further corroborate this required shift, we conducted an additional ex post analysis of our case data. We found comprehensive empirical evidence for the existence of TMS processes that we present in Exhibit 1.

## Exhibit 1. Transactive memory system (TMS) processes in online social networking

With respect to the TMS process of updating other people’s pro<sup>fi</sup>les, respondents mentioned that ‘it is important that you see who is doing what. Members of other departments entered the community and I got aware of their responsibilities and new conversations started’ (R13). Many employees observe people pro<sup>fi</sup>les, and one argued that this pro<sup>fi</sup>le is also ‘signaling that somebody takes responsibility for a certain topic’ (R18). Moreover, the social network site (SNS) supports the TMS process of allocating (or assuming) incoming information to the person with the respective expertise: community members found it easier to see who is responsible or what one needs to contribute with the SNS (R8). Also, respondents emphasize that with the SNS, they <sup>fi</sup>nd information much faster and directly from the authors.

Beyond just con<sup>fi</sup>rming the existence of TMS, our analysis also suggests possible extensions of this theoretical framework to better address online social networking. We noted that SNS appear to have the special property that they interconnect but also pool individuals in topical communities. Next to understanding who knows what (as a core aspect of TMS), SNS functionality also assembles the available experts and information in intermediating pools that enable users to economically acquire information without the effort of learning about or directly contacting their authors. People explicitly assumed topics by registering in the topic groups (i.e. pools), and then retrieval allocation (as the last TMS process) was supported by an ‘improved materialization of the network’ (R18). This also coincides with the observation that some respondents did not perceive the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of including actor pro<sup>fi</sup>les in their retrieval. We argue that beyond a certain group size, SNS support the establishment of a second layer in the TMS: they aid in moving away from memorizing individual topical experts by providing pools of expertise as a nexus to jointly elaborate certain topics. Not only do SNS help to organize and later identify the experts within these pools, but the transparency of the network also enables a <sup>fi</sup>rst quick access to the information on an expert group. In this more general TMS level, knowledge about personal pro<sup>fi</sup>les is not required but can be developed if one enters the pool and requires more special information or has to decide between different information offerings.

## Implications for network interpretation

Next to theorizing SNS use, our second research question set out to explore how uncovering the deep structure of a network can augment and inform quantitative structural analysis with SNA.

First, our case data highlight that the typical measures of the surface network such as centrality can be ambiguous. Valid interpretation requires knowledge about the underlying causes of the actors’ positioning. For example, in our case, a high number of contacts of an actor ( i.e. degree centrality) does not necessarily need to be a scarce resource as the community is marked by many unidirectional weak ties that are determined by the actors’ responsibilities

Furthermore, the structural network analysis typically de<sup>fi</sup>nes, a priori, one distinct type of rela tionship that is modelled. This selection would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from grounding in a deeper understanding of the networking phenomenon at hand, as our approach points to a rich underlying interdependence of different meanings and the complexity of actor relations. In fact, our empirical inquiry revealed the emerging multiplexity of relationships in online social networks: we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed helping, informing, collaborating, shaping and negotiating a discourse. While multiplexity has already been found in previous studies (Haythornthwaite, 2006), our qualitative lens can augment structura analysis by uncovering how the relationship types are changing during the discourse from awareness to collaboration or to business transactions. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that multiplexity is indeed no a simple additive phenomenon. Rather, a complex interaction and emergence of different models of relationship types shape the meaning of the online social network

Relationships were often not symmetric; i.e. the adjacent actors invest different efforts in building and maintaining the link. While we could <sup>fi</sup>nd diverging roles and associated motivations for senders and receivers that enable and sustain such asymmetric relationships, we emphasize that the ties cannot simply be assumed to be undirected or balanced. Our qualitative inquiry hence sup ported our structural analysis by con<sup>fi</sup>rming our selection of the networks of co-membership in topic communities (as presented in Figure 2) as a basis for structural representation. More generally, the proposed DSF can uncover the order principles and relationship types that mark the network.

The DSF is also able to shed light on the interpretation of central actors in our network structure: the networking patterns of many employees are apparently following an agenda that is mainly set by their obligations and responsibilities in the organization. Their task context, strategies to build awareness for certain topics, time constraints and the related pressure to add value imply that central positions in corporate SNS are considerably in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the organizational role of the actor. This dependency on agendas, as uncovered by our DSF analysis of a network, supports the structural interpretation.

We can conclude that with increasing complexity of the deeper levels of the DSF, the value of isolated social network depictions and analysis declines. This complexity is particularly salient in organizational contexts, with shared of<sup>fl</sup>ine backgrounds, time pressures, different role assignments, resource competition and hierarchies.

## LIMITATIONS

Before we present the <sup>fi</sup>nal conclusions, we want to address the limitations of our research approach. While we were aiming to maximize internal reliability by using interviews, observations and network analysis as multiple sources of evidence (Eisenhardt, 1989), it needs to be noted tha for privacy reasons, the 20 respondents we interviewed cannot directly be pinpointed in the net work graph. The external validity of our results might be reduced because we adopted a single case design. A related study at another global company corroborates the notion of growing a topical discourse but also found the important driver of connecting to co-workers on a personal leve and advancing the career (DiMicco et al., 2008). Further, our case data did not indicate substantial effects of national backgrounds, hierarchies or subcultures. However, further studies with othe companies should reassess these factors.

## CONCLUSION

In this article, we offer a systematic theoretical framework to assess the deep structure of social networking. Our qualitative case analysis of a global corporate SNS implementation is a <sup>fi</sup>rst example that demonstrates and corroborates how the DSF helps to systematically reveal rich insights about four layers of the deep structure of organizational online social networks, comprising the visual picture, transactions, meaning structure and abstract order principles. This systematic inquiry into personal conceptions and perceptions of users can guide researchers towards a more consistent understanding of organizational online social networks and their underlying contingencies by shifting the focus to cultural constructs such as expectations, actor identities and relationship categories that constitute the meaning of a particular network.

Grounded in empirical data, we identify that organizational social networks require very different theoretical explanations than internet-based public social networks. In companies, online network ing appears to be driven by a strong focus on developing topics and in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the organizational discourse in a shared context. The identi<sup>fi</sup>ed socio-cognitive and economic driving forces render the application of TMS theory more suitable than theories with a social–relational focus.

When it comes to the dynamics, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that despite few contributors and many passive retrievers, there is a Pareto equilibrium with healthy dynamics. The engine of the network is the interplay of two relevant actor identities, i.e. the discourse driver and the information retriever. Both have very different but complementary motivations to engage on the SNS. This may be an explanation of why communities prosper despite only a few active people. At the same time, it can serve as a criticism to the current negative framing of ‘lurking’ (Preece et al., 2004), which may in fact be a necessary element. More generally, these interesting role dynamics call for future research on the interplay of special roles in online social networks.

The practices evolving around the SNS appear to be in line with the de<sup>fi</sup>ned managerial objectives, such as creating mutual awareness, fostering weak ties and creating fast responses. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings help to better understand how networks are likely to form and emerge and how actors adopt certain roles, when SNS are implemented.

While it was very useful to uncover all four layers in our case study in order to arrive at a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon, it might also suf<sup>fi</sup>ce to focus on isolated elements of the DSF. For example, future SNA researchers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from assessing the unfolding relationship types and their interdependencies before conducting quantitative network analysis in order to ensure reliability. Further, order principles should be assessed to learn how to interpret network measures.

Finally, our framework is also suitable for future research on social aspects in the organization that do not lead to a visible network depiction.

## Biographies

Dr Matthias Trier is an Associate Professor at Copenhagen Business School. He studies phenomena related to elec tronic communication and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence effects in online media within and outside the organization with a mixed methods approach that blends quantitative, qualitative and network analytical methods. Example topics include the implementation/appropriation of social media, online participation, framing of electronic discourses (e.g. from a manage ment perspective), information transfer, dissemination processes or bottom-up community emergence as a part o knowledge management initiatives. One special methodo logical focus is on developing an event-driven method fo dynamic network analysis. The corresponding software Commetrix enables research into emerging structures and dynamic processes of networking among people.

Alexander Richter is a Senior Researcher at the De partment of Informatics at the University of Zurich and at the Cooperation Systems Center at Bundeswehr Univer sity Munich. In the last years, he has lead research projects on the national and European levels, aiming to understand the role of social software in the increasingly differentiated working practices of knowledge workers. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, he is interested in the impact of social platforms in emergent structures such as informal networks and new forms of leadership or innovation as well as the development ot methods to evaluate these developments and approaches to address them. For instance, he co-developed a frame work that supports the selection, implementation and eval uation of social software at companies such as Allianz, Airbus, Bayer, BMW and Bosch. He is Chairman of the Computer Supported Cooperative Work Special Interest Group in the German computer society and a mentor of a start-up in the domain of ambient assisted living
