# Risks and Controls in Internet-Enabled Reverse Auctions: Perspectives from Buyers and Suppliers

CHAITANYA SAMBHARA, ARUN RAI, MARK KEIL, AND VIJAY KASI

ABSTRACT: Internet-enabled reverse auctions (RAs) are touted as a means of making the procurement process more effective and efficient, but as is often the case with information technology (IT), there can be risks that managers need to be aware of and mitigate in order to realize the benefits. Using the Delphi methodology followed by semistructured interviews, we identify buyer and supplier perspectives on risk factors associated with RAs and the controls that can address key risk factors. We find that the risk factors map to (1) auction process governance, (2) organizational contingency, (3) buyer–supplier relationship, and (4) agency and transaction cost. Drawing on the findings, we advance an RA risk-control integrative model and formulate corresponding propositions (1) comparing buyers’ and suppliers’ perspectives on risks associated with RA use and (2) identifying controls to mitigate the risks. Our research contributes to theory by (1) surfacing that RAs introduce novel risks and that buyers and suppliers can have either similar or remarkably different views regarding particular risks, (2) showing how both buyers and suppliers can create information asymmetry and act opportunistically, and (3) uncovering the role of input controls, a type of control that has not received much attention in the literature on controls for IT initiatives, as an effective means of addressing most of the key risk factors. For practitioners, our study provides guidance about a wide range of risk factors that both parties could face when using RAs, the adverse consequences of these risk factors, and the controls that can be established to mitigate the key risk factors.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: auctions risks, Delphi methodology, interfirm governance, intrafirm governance, online auctions, online controls, principal opportunism, process governance, reverse auctions, reverse information asymmetry.

A reverse auction is a real-time, dynamic procurement mechanism used by a buyer with multiple suppliers. In reverse auctions, suppliers compete by reducing their bid price for products or services, to win the buyer’s business. The use of Internetenabled RAs (hereafter RAs) emerged in the mid-1990s and has profoundly impacted how firms procure products and services. With about \$1.4 trillion worth of goods and services procured electronically worldwide per year,<sup>1</sup> RAs have been found to significantly reduce procurement costs—by 5–15 percent [37]. The use of RAs, however, requires significant resource investment. Reverse auctions are a part of buyers’ and suppliers’ long-term planning and are strategically important for both parties [36]. Reverse auctions are important for buyers because they reduce purchasing costs and can provide an efficient means for identifying new suppliers. They are important for suppliers because they can help suppliers expand their business and generate new sources of revenue.

Despite their touted benefits [37, 60], the adoption of RAs has been uneven. While some firms have used them successfully and expanded their use [70], others have either encountered resistance [50] or have shunned them due to the long-term damage that RAs can have on interfirm relationships [54]. In numerous cases, buyers have not accrued expected savings; suppliers have incurred significant preparatory costs and yet not won a client’s business; and both parties have reported the destruction of mutual trust. As a result, the role of RAs as an effective procurement mechanism has been questioned [12]. On one hand, some studies show a trend that the use of RAs is declining (e.g., Peng and Calvi 2012 [50], and a study by the U.S. Government Accountability Office).<sup>2</sup> On the other hand, some studies show the opposite—an increase in the use of the RAs (e.g., [32, 70], and a PricewaterhouseCoopers (PWC) report on the Indian Chamber of Commerce).<sup>3</sup>

The apparent paradox of some firms increasing their use of RAs while other firms are discontinuing them motivates our study. Inspired by studies that have examined similar organizational paradoxes [7, 43], we suggest that using RAs is a strategically important business decision involving risk factors —where a risk factor is defined as the cause of an adverse consequence [3]. One or more contributing risk factors can lead to an adverse consequence [61]. These risk factors can be addressed through the use of controls. Thus, firms that identify and control RA risk factors are better equipped to realize the benefits of using RAs while firms that do not address the risk factors are more likely to suffer losses and abandon the use of RAs [38]. Against this backdrop, we suggest that achieving the effective use of RAs requires: (a) identifying the risk factors associated with using RAs, and (b) understanding the controls that mitigate key risk factors. We address the following three research questions:

Research Question 1: What are the risk factors associated with the use of RAs?

Research Question 2: How do buyers and suppliers concur or differ in their perspectives on the risk factors associated with the use of RAs?

Research Question 3: What controls can address key risk factors associated with the use of RAs?

## Background

As the first step in our process, we synthesized the literature on RAs. We first searched for variations of the key term “reverse auctions” on multiple digital libraries. We then identified the streams of literature in which the papers on RAs were published. We found that studies on RAs primarily appeared in information systems, operations, management, and marketing journals. We also searched for other practitioner articles and media reports on RAs. We developed a list of 158 papers that include academic papers, practitioner papers, and government and other sponsored reports on RAs. Out of these 158 papers, we shortlisted 29 academic papers that discuss at least one RA risk factor.<sup>4</sup> The remaining papers discuss other related issues of RAs, including the optimal design configuration of RAs to maximize cost reduction, benefits and value proposition of RA use, comparison of different bidding rules (e.g., Dutch vs. Japanese), comparison of RAs with other procurement mechanisms, and algorithms to compare suppliers. Since the goal of our study is to examine the risks of RA use and identify the controls, we focus our attention on these shortlisted papers for literature synthesis.

The papers that feature one or more RA risk factors are listed in Table 1. The research objective of the paper and the theoretical base of the paper are shown. While all these papers recognize the importance of identifying and addressing RA risk factors, the research objectives of most of them are not specifically related to uncovering or mitigating RA risk factors. Instead, the identification and discussion of RA risk factors in most of these papers is complementary to the research objectives. Moreover, only three studies [10, 13, 41] have considered both buyer and supplier perspectives. The discussion of RA risk factors in these three studies, like most of the papers listed in Table 1, is concise since their research objective is not related to risk factors. Consider Hur et al. [34] as an example of a paper that discusses RA risk factor(s) but whose objective is not related to RA risk factor(s). Hur et al. [34] discuss the risk factor of overlooking the total cost of ownership in lieu of the RA bid price. The focus of Hur et al. [34], however, is to examine how firms learn to use RAs and how they can integrate RAs into their purchasing process. As such, the paper discusses RA risk factor(s), but its focus is not on RA risk factors.

Among the papers that focus on RA risk factors, risk factors that pertain to buyer– supplier relationships are most emphasized (see [12, 35, 37, 38, 39, 58, 64]). Only one of these papers (Smeltzer and Carr [59]) takes an overarching view of RA risk factors. However, the identification and discussion of RA risk factors by Smeltzer and Carr [59] is limited to three top RA risk factors. Therefore, we did not find a study that takes a comprehensive look at RA risk factors, or undertakes a holistic assessment of risks and controls by considering both buyer and supplier perspectives.

Collectively, the papers we reviewed offer valuable guidance regarding RA risk factors. We found that together these 29 papers identified 40 risk factors, a starting point for our study. From this list, it was not clear if and how buyers and suppliers differ in their perceptions of risk factors. Which risk factors are common to buyers and suppliers, and which ones are applicable to only one group? Among the risk factors of common concern to buyers and suppliers, are any risk factors of more concern to buyers as compared to suppliers and vice versa? Research on organizational risk management (e.g., [3]) suggests that no action or business process of strategic importance is risk-free and that not all risk factors are addressable. Therefore, it is important to identify the key risk factors and focus on establishing controls to address them. Findings from prior literature, though, do not provide insights on which RA risk factors present a greater challenge and for which controls should be established.

To address these important gaps in understanding, we first conducted a Delphi study with one buyer panel and one supplier panel. The Delphi study was conducted to develop an elaborate list of RA risk factors and build a comprehensive understanding of RA risk factors from both buyer and supplier perspectives. We then conducted semistructured interviews with selected experts from both panels to identify the controls that should be established to mitigate the top-ranked risk factors. Drawing on the insights from the Delphi study and the semistructured interviews, we build a risk-control integrative model to explain how the relationship between RA risks and controls can be conceptualized. Indeed, the prior literature has contributed to our understanding of various RA risk factors. We, therefore, consolidate the findings from the Delphi study and the semistructured interviews with the findings from the literature to build propositions for our model. Our risk-control integrative model and the propositions explore two key aspects of risk and controls related to RAs: (1) How do buyers and suppliers concur and differ in their perceptions of key RA risk factors? and (2) How can controls address key RA risk factors? We draw on the theories of process governance, interfirm governance, and intrafirm governance to interpret the findings from our study and develop our propositions.

## Methodology

Delphi study: Considering the exploratory nature of the research questions, the ranking-type Delphi methodology seemed to be the most appropriate choice. The Delphi methodology has been widely used in studies that have sought the aid of domain experts to identify issues and reach consensus on relative importance [55]. For this study, two separate Delphi panels were formed, one composed of buyers and the other of suppliers.

The composition of the panels: For the buyer panel, we included a diverse group of experts with years of experience in procurement and RAs. Similarly, for the supplier panel, we recruited professionals with a wealth of experience in sales, business development, and marketing who had experience both in RA bidding and postauction negotiation. We recruited the panelists using professional connections from the Institute of Supply Management (ISM). Table A1 in Online Appendix A shows the profile of both panels. The Delphi study was conducted asynchronously over the Internet in Fall 2010.

Delphi methodology process: We followed Schmidt et al. [55] to conduct the Delphi study. The methodology for the Delhi study is shown in Figure A1 in Online Appendix A. The first phase (brainstorming) yielded a consolidated list of 34 unique risk factors for the buyer panel and 49 unique risk factors for the supplier panel. In all, 65 unique risk factors were identified, of which 18 were common to both panels. In the second phase (selection), we asked each panelist to select his or her top 20 risk factors (i.e., those that represented the most serious impediments to participating in/using RAs). We retained only those items that were selected by a significant majority of the panel. Our goal was to present 15–25 items for the panelists to rank. A range of 15–25 items is ideal because ranking fewer than 15 risk factors would not have let us identify the top common risk factors between the two panels, and ranking more than 25 risk factors would have been excessively cumbersome for the panelists [49, 55]. With this goal in mind, we kept items selected by 60 percent of panelists. In the third phase (ranking), each panelist was asked to rank the risk factor that they considered the most important as first, and so on. We used Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) to measure the degree of consensus in the panel. A Kendall’s W above 0.50 indicates consensus within a panel. After two rounds of ranking by each panel, the buyer panel yielded a Kendall’s W of 0.503, and the supplier panel yielded a Kendall’s W of 0.574 indicating that a consensus had been reached for each of the panels and that we could have a reasonable degree of confidence in the rankings [49, 55].

Follow-up interviews for controls identification: To gain an in-depth understanding of the controls needed to address the key risk factors identified in the Delphi study, we conducted semistructured interviews with a subset of our panelists in spring 2011. We identified key risk factors based on (1) those in the top five risk factors of each panel, and (2) the common risk factors featured on the top ranked list of the combined panels. Ten experts from each panel were invited for interviews, of which seven from each panel agreed to participate. The average work experience of the interviewees was 26 years (range 12–40) for the buyer panel and 28 years (range 12–43) for the supplier panel. The average number of RAs conducted by the interviewees from the buyer panel was 193 (range 50–550). Suppliers, on average, had participated in 1,030 RAs (range 500–4,000).

## Results

Classification of risk factors: Prior studies have relied on various theoretical perspectives to examine different aspects of RA use. As shown in Table 1, these theoretical perspectives include agency theory and transaction cost theory among others. The choice of the theoretical perspective in the studies was based on the aspect of RAs being examined. For example, Jap [38] examines how the governance of RAs, specifically bidding formats, can affect cost savings and impact buyer– supplier relationships. Jap [38] draws on transaction cost theory to examine supplier perceptions of the buyer, auction process governance to examine the format of the bidding process (sealed bid/open bid), and relational theory to examine the impact of the bidding format on the buyer–supplier relationship. Likewise, the choice of theoretical perspectives for our study was determined by the nature of RA risk factors.

The theoretical perspectives of our study relate to process governance (specifically auction process governance), intrafirm governance (organizational contingency), and interfirm governance (buyer–supplier relationship and agency and transaction cost) and provide a comprehensive coverage of risk factors associated with RA use. The dimensions represent constructs of the informing theoretical perspectives (e.g., opportunism is a dimension of agency and transaction cost). We had two key objectives when classifying the RA risk factors into theoretical perspectives and dimensions. Our first objective was to take a parsimonious approach where we categorized the risk factors into as few theoretical perspectives and dimensions as possible and take a high-level abstract view. Taking such an approach increases the generalizability of the insights and reduces reliance on the contextual circumstances within which a given phenomenon is being examined [66]. Our second objective was to classify the risk factors into theoretical perspectives and their dimensions that were mutually exclusive and yet collectively exhaustive. We relied on the combination of risk factor title, description, and raw data (collected during the first phase of the Delphi study) to map risk factors to theoretical perspectives and dimensions.

First, an elaborate list of theoretical perspectives and dimensions to which risk factors could be mapped was developed from the reverse auctions (papers listed in Table 1 and other papers on RAs), “forward” auctions, interfirm governance, and intrafirm governance literature. Second, the mapped theoretical perspectives and their dimensions were retained, and the unmapped theoretical perspectives and dimensions were discarded. The informing theoretical perspectives of RA risk factors that we identified are: (1) auction process governance, (2) organizational contingency, (3) buyer–supplier relationship, and (4) agency and transaction cost. Examples of dimensions include the phases of auction process governance (e.g., pre-, during-, and postauction phases), constructs of organizational contingency (e.g., participative ability), buyer–supplier relationship (e.g., interaction-based noncontractibility), and agency and transaction cost (e.g., opportunism, contractual hazards, etc.). A few risk factors were found to be counterintuitive to the known constructs for agency and transaction cost. Accordingly, we devised two new dimensions to maps these risk factors: (a) reverse information asymmetry, where an agent/supplier lacks information (whereas what has been focused on is the lack of information for the principal/buyer), and (b) principal opportunism (as opposed to agent opportunism, which has been the focus of previous studies) [33]. Although alluded to in the theory of relationship constraints [15], and other studies (e.g., [33, 40, 70]), the empirical evidence for these risk factors has been insufficient.

Drawing on process engineering research [14] the dimensions are examined according to the phase of the RA process in which they occur. Thus, the identified auction process governance risk factors span (a) pre-auction, (b) during-auction, (c) post-auction, and (d) phaseindependent risk factors. The phase-independent risk factors represent auction process governance risk factors that cannot be exclusively mapped to a particular phase.

Organizational contingency: Organizational contingency risk factors entail organization-wide adoption and management of RAs. Organizational contingency theory originated in the management literature [44] and has been widely tested, adopted, and enhanced in different streams of literature such as information systems (e.g., [62]) and operations [25]. Organizational contingency theory of information systems emphasizes the role of organizational conditions on which the success of IT-enabled mechanisms is contingent [62]. Two dimensions and three risk factors were mapped to this theoretical perspective. The first two risk factors pertain to organizational change. Implementation and use of a new IT-enabled mechanisms for a business practice is considered to be an organizational change and firms often face resistance to such changes [62, 71]. The third risk factor pertains to the participative ability of suppliers because success of an IT-enabled business exchange is also contingent upon the extent to which stakeholders are able to participate in its use [62].

Buyer–supplier relationship: The buyer–supplier relationship RA risk factors correspond to the dimension of interaction-based noncontractibility between the buyer and suppliers [48]. The relational view of firms originated in management [16] and strategy literature [53]. Two risk factors were mapped to the buyer–supplier relationship. Interaction-based noncontractibility encompasses responsiveness, trust, and flexibility, three factors critical to the longevity of interfirm relationships. The higher the degree of noncontractibility in an interfirm relationship, the less likelihood the buyer will use RAs [48].

Agency and transaction cost: The agency and transaction cost risk factors arise due to a conflict of goals between a buyer and a supplier. The agency and transaction cost perspectives are from the economics [68] and management [18] literature. A task assigner is identified as a principal, and the task assignee is an agent [17]. Prior studies that have drawn on agency theory’s principal–agent lens in a B2B context identify the buyer as a principal, and a supplier as an agent where the buyer provides specifications for products or services that a supplier sells to the buyer. Due to a significant overlap between the tenets of agency theory and the transaction cost theory such as goal incongruence, contractual hazards, self-interest, and opportunism, researchers have often drawn on both theories in conjunction (e.g., [6, 65]). The risk factors identified in our study overlap with the dimensions of agency and transaction cost theories. Six dimensions and 15 risk factors were mapped to the agency and transaction cost perspectives. We devised 2 new dimensions—reverse information asymmetry and principal opportunism—to accommodate 6 risk factors that could not be mapped to any other dimensions and were counterintuitive to the known constructs of interfirm governance.

Reverse information asymmetry arises when conditions resulting from the use of technology or the principal’s behavior impede the agent’s ability to gather critical information needed to make important business decisions. One top-ranked risk factor and four overall risk factors were mapped to this dimension. The issue of hidden information is a well-explored agency problem, but the focus has traditionally been on the principal’s perspective (i.e., information asymmetry for the principal). While some studies have called for examining the agent’s perspective (e.g., Miller and Sardais [46] note the lack of research on the agent’s perspective and call for further empirical research and theoretical elaboration), only a handful have tried to address the problem of information asymmetry for the agent (e.g., see the theory of relationship constraints [15]). Since the buyer determines to whom the business should be awarded, it has a strong power position over suppliers. Reverse auctions condition suppliers to believe that by making them compete the buyer is looking for a supplier who offers the lowest price. Only the buyer knows what factors will be considered when awarding the business, thus creating reverse information asymmetry as suppliers are aware only of the price they bid in an RA. This lack of critical information creates an illusion that a supplier needs to be the lowest bidder. Reverse auction risk factors pertaining to principal opportunism arise when the buyer (a) takes advantage of information generated through RAs that is not accessible to suppliers, and (b) uses its power position over suppliers to reduce procurement costs and extract relationship-specific rent. By using RAs, the buyer acts in its self-interest at the expense of its suppliers.

Expert interviews to identify controls: To gain insight into the controls that could be used to address the highly ranked risk factors that surfaced in our Delphi study, we interviewed 14 experts, 7 from each panel. The experts were asked to suggest controls that address (a) the top 5 risk factors identified by their panel in the ranking phase of the Delphi study, and (b) other top-ranked risk factors identified by their panel in the ranking phase that were common to both panels. Two lists, each with 11 risk factors, were developed to interview the experts from the buyer and supplier panels. The risk factors considered in the interviews are shown in bold letters in Table 2. The average duration of each interview was 40 minutes (range 25–70 minutes). All the interviews were audio recorded and transcribed.

The panelists suggested multiple control actions to address each risk factor. To the best of our knowledge, there was no prior work or guidance that had identified control actions specific to mitigating RA risk factors and then classified them into categories. Therefore, we used an approach similar to the first phase of the Delphi study where we consolidated and refined the list of suggested control actions by grouping similar items together, removing duplicates, and sharpening descriptions to improve clarity. This process yielded a consolidated list of 33 unique control actions. After examining the objective of each control action, we determined that when grouped together the 33 control actions constituted 8 unique controls. Each control, thus, has a common objective that is achieved by 1 or more control actions. Overall, the 8 controls had 3 key goals: (1) assessing the market and suppliers, (2) preparing for RA execution, and (3) monitoring performance. We found 7 controls to be precontract input controls and 1 control to be a postcontract outcome control. Input controls regulate the antecedent conditions of performance and are enforced prior to a contract [8]. The 8 controls, goal of each control, and the control type are shown in Table 3. A detailed list of the 33 control actions is provided in Online Appendix C. Our results suggest that (a) both buyers and suppliers are responsible for implementing input controls, and (b) input controls are both internal, that is, a party monitors its internal activities, and external, that is, one party monitors the other. Figure 1 in the next section maps the relationship between controls and the RA risk factor dimensions.

## Development of Theoretical Propositions

We use the findings to develop propositions as follows: (1) draw on the findings from the Delphi study along with insights from the literature (Table 2), to formulate propositions pertaining to the risks that buyers and suppliers view to be common or different [P1(A) – P4 (A)]; and (2) draw on the insights from the interviews to build propositions that pertain to the controls that can mitigate risks and advance the risk-control integrative model [P1 (B) – P4 (B)] (Figure 1). Our proposition development is at the level of select theoretical perspectives of risks (auction process governance, organizational contingency, buyer– supplier relationships, and agency and transaction cost) and the relevant control goals (assessing market and suppliers, preparing for RA execution, monitoring performance).

Pre-auction risks of greater concern to buyers: Pre-auction governance entails several activities that include identifying the products or services to be procured through the RA; listing the requirements for the products or services; identifying qualified suppliers to participate in the RA; structuring the lots for bidding; and establishing a communication link with suppliers among others [10, 36, 59]. The time spent preparing and setting up each RA is significantly more than the duration of the RA event and post-auction negotiations combined [5]. Naturally, the buyer handles most of the RA governance. The Delphi study results reveal that although suppliers also invest time preparing for an RA event where they examine the buyer’s requirements and estimate their cost structure, the number of tasks to be managed is much higher for the buyer. These findings suggest that pre-auction process governance risk factors concern buyers more than suppliers as evident from the selection and ranking of the risk factors in Table 2.

During-auction and post-auction risks of greater concern to suppliers: The steps that follow pre-auction governance are the RA event and the post-auction negotiation and contracting. The RA event can be a stressful time for suppliers, especially those vying for the business [10, 58]. Some suppliers can bid more aggressively if they have better cost structures or if they do not adhere to the buyer’s specifications. A supplier who needs to win the buyer’s business can catch “auction fever” in such instances and bid below its profit margin [35]. A supplier whose bid price is close to or below profit margin finds it difficult to maintain the offered auction price post auction. Findings show that suppliers’ top three during-auction risk factors (risk of other suppliers not adhering to specifications and underbidding; impulsive bidding leading to unrealistically low bids; and early attrition of quality suppliers due to aggressive bidding) and their top ranked post-auction risk factor (supplier unable to maintain the offered auction price) were not ranked by the buyer panel. These findings suggest that suppliers are more concerned with during- and post-auction RA risk factors than are buyers.

Phase-independent risks of concern to both buyers and suppliers: Phase-independent risk factors span multiple phases of the RA process governance or cannot be ascribed to a particular phase. These are among the most frequently discussed risk factors in the literature. The most notorious risk factor is the loss of profit margin for suppliers (see, e.g., [5, 13, 36]) followed by not factoring in the total cost of ownership [34, 58]. The format of RAs is designed to pressurize suppliers to reduce their prices. Although some studies have found evidence of buyers coercing suppliers to participate in RAs [24, 28], RAs have been largely accepted as a quick and efficient means of finding business partners. Both buyers and suppliers acknowledge that buyers sometimes tend to focus on price to the extent that they treat suppliers innovative products and services as commodities, overlook nonprice criteria, and do not factor in the total cost of ownership. Suppliers in our panel also expressed concerns about their lack of awareness of other suppliers’ cost structures, especially when suppliers compete against low-cost foreign suppliers. Overall, both buyers and suppliers consider phase-independent process governance risk factors to be important.

Accordingly, we formulate the following propositions regarding the relative emphasis placed by buyers and suppliers on the risk factors associated with the three phases of the auction process or on those that are phase-independent:

Proposition 1 (A): Buyers and suppliers differ as well as concur in their assessment of process governance risk factors of RAs, where:

1. Buyers are more concerned with pre-auction risk factors

2. Suppliers are more concerned with during-auction and post-auction process risk factors

3. Both buyers and suppliers are concerned with phase-independent risk factors.

Controlling auction process governance risk factors: While the literature has identified practices for the effective management of the RA process (e.g., garnering support from top management; developing an RA strategy; preparing the RA tool [2, 5, 39]) or procurement processes in general (e.g., development of organizational readiness for procurement innovations, [51, 52]; diversity of organizational procurement knowledge, procurement process digitization [61]), our findings from the interviews enable us to identify specific controls to mitigate auction process governance risks.

The precontract controls that emerged from the interviews address risk factors associated with auction process governance. Seen collectively, these controls have an underlying process logic that implies temporal precedence. To elaborate, the controls require the buyer to first thoroughly assess the market and its suppliers, and prepare for RA execution. The buyer should engage in pre-auction screening of suppliers and assess supplier competition to filter out suppliers who are unlikely to be competitive. Subsequently, the buyer should prepare for the auction by gaining approval and support of internal stakeholders and management to use RAs and by allocating sufficient time to prepare for RAs. The buyer should then play a facilitator role in developing supplier readiness by training suppliers on the RA tools and process. Suppliers should also prepare by validating the buyer’s requirements, and determining the lowest possible bid price at which they can achieve a sufficient profit margin and be able to maintain the quality of their products or services if they win.

It is important that the buyer determines the auction rules and incentives in advance and maintains a proper communication channel with suppliers. When ready to conduct an RA, the buyer should determine the award criteria upfront; communicate it to all the participating suppliers; adhere to the award criteria when awarding the business; and hold its procurement personnel accountable for the suppliers selected to participate. These are important steps to ensure fairness since suppliers invest valuable time and resources to participate. If not done, suppliers may lose confidence in the buyer’s integrity and the RA process. As one expert from the supplier panel said: “I have seen cases where award terms were communicated but were not followed. It hurts [the] buyer’s credibility in the market. I would much rather prefer a situation where suppliers did not win the award but believed the reverse auctions were conducted fairly.” Based on the above discussion, we formulate the following proposition:

Proposition 1 (B): Process governance risk factors of RAs can be mitigated by precontract input controls pertaining to (a) the buyer assessing the market and suppliers, and (b) the buyer and suppliers preparing for RA execution.

## Reverse Auctions Risks and Controls Related to Organizational Contingency (P2)

Organizational contingency risks of greater concern to buyers: The organizational contingency risk factors of RAs pertain to dimensions of organizational change within a buying firm and the participative ability of suppliers. Prior studies emphasize the importance of contingencies, for example, organizational structure and support in the adoption of technological innovations [67]; the influence of internal stakeholders in devising procurement strategies [42]; and reconfiguration of supply chains when switching to new ways of establishing B2B relationships, such as RAs [25]. In the context of RAs, organizational change requires a buyer to adopt and use the technology of online RAs [5, 39]; gain the support of internal stakeholders such as top management and internal clients [52]; and engage with suppliers through a different mechanism (RAs) to establish a business relationship [29]. Participative ability refers to suppliers’ lack of knowledge of the RA process. These risk factors are contingent on the buyer’s ability to manage internal and external stakeholders. We find that all three risk factors mapped to organizational contingency are in the buyers’ top-ranked list. None of these risk factors were considered to be important for ranking by suppliers. This insight leads us to the following proposition about organizational contingency risk factors of RAs:

Proposition 2 (A): Organizational contingency risk factors of RAs are of greater concern to buyers than to suppliers.

Controlling organizational contingency risk factors: The experts from both panels suggested that controls that focus on preparing for RA execution were sufficient to address the organizational contingency risk factors. Controls pertaining to developing readiness for RAs were suggested to be salient. Developing readiness for RAs by gaining the support of internal stakeholders and facilitating supplier readiness by educating them about (a) RA technology, and (b) the RA procurement process can substantially mitigate organizational contingency risk factors. In addition, providing suppliers with an incentive and effective communication regarding the auction rules can help address risk factors. The insights from the interviews lead to the following proposition regarding controls that address organizational contingency risk factors:

Proposition 2 (B): Organizational contingency risk factors of RAs can be mitigated by precontract input controls that pertain to buyers and suppliers preparing for RA execution.

## Reverse Auctions Risks and Controls Related to Buyer–Supplier Relationship (P3)

Buyer–supplier relationship risks of greater concern to suppliers: In the literature, the adverse impact of RAs on buyer–supplier relationships is one of the most wellknown and discussed supplier risk factors. In fact, numerous papers have focused solely on the impact of RA use on buyer–supplier relationships [38, 58]. For example, Jap [38] finds that even when a buyer is faithful, suppliers can build negative perceptions regarding the RA process. This negative perception then influences suppliers’ conduct in the relationship with the buyer. In particular, the interaction-based noncontractibility [48] aspect of a buyer–supplier relationship can be damaged. Thus, it is often difficult for a buyer to establish strong trusting relationships with suppliers when the relationship is established using RAs [13].

The use of RAs can weaken existing relationships and precludes the formation of strong business relationships when RAs are repeatedly used. One supplier said: “The use of reverse auctions and forced price reduction takes away our incentive to be flexible for the customer. Why would a supplier be willing to accommodate the buyer’s requirements when it is selling its products at a very lean profit margin?” Another panel member suggested: “Reverse auctions should be used selectively. The buyer suggests that it is not in need for a relationship with suppliers when it uses auctions for all of its categories.” Thus, in putting suppliers through RAs and making them compete over price, the buyer communicates that it prefers lower costs to strong business relationships. Consequently, we find that suppliers highly rank buyer–supplier relationship risk factors, while buyers do not, leading to the following proposition

Proposition 3 (A): Buyer–supplier relationship risk factors are of greater concern to suppliers than to buyers.

Controlling buyer–supplier relationship risk factors: Findings from the interviews suggest that the more experience suppliers have with RAs, the more they understand the nature of the business and the market. An expert from the supplier panel said: “To be very honest, we (suppliers) need to know that we are not alone in this world. There is tough competition out there. Though you always strive for a good relationship with buyers, it is the nature of the business that you can lose your client.” However, controls were also suggested to address the buyer–supplier relationship risk factors. Controls that prepare buyers and suppliers for RA execution could address buyer–supplier relationship risk factors. Controls pertaining to developing mutual understanding and determining auction parameters are suggested to be salient.

Suppliers have a positive impression of buyers who are willing to communicate outside of online RA tools, either by talking on the phone or meeting in person. When suppliers are given the incentive of a long-term contract and if suppliers are confident that the buyer evaluates all suppliers on merit alone, they do not consider the buyer to have acted opportunistically. Suppliers are also more trustful of a buyer who develops readiness by gaining the support of internal stakeholders and facilitates supplier readiness by training them prior to the RA event. These insights lead to the following proposition regarding controls for buyer–supplier relationship risk factors of RAs:

Proposition 3 (B): Buyer–supplier relationship risk factors can be mitigated by precontract input controls that pertain to buyers and suppliers preparing for RA execution.

## Reverse Auctions Risks and Controls Related to Agency and Transaction Cost (P4)

Small number bargaining risks of greater concern to buyers: When conducting RAs, it is important for the buyer to estimate the number of suppliers available to compete. Prior studies have suggested that a buyer should have no less than three and preferably five or more suppliers competing in an RA [5, 38, 41]. Even when there are enough suppliers to participate, a buyer should ensure that the market prices for the products or services being auctioned are somewhat elastic, that is, prices will see a downward trajectory when the demand increases [59]. Conducting RAs consumes time and money and if a buyer fails to study the market before the RA event, it can fail to achieve its objectives. Suppliers, on the other hand, fear that continuous use of RAs can drive some of them out of business. When the market is consolidated due to fewer suppliers, a condition known as small number bargaining [4], the buyer can find it difficult to generate sufficient bidding activity during an RA event. Small number bargaining risk factors preclude the buyer from obtaining lower prices. Therefore, we find that buyers are more concerned with small number bargaining risk factors of RAs than suppliers.

Contractual hazards risks of concern to both buyers and suppliers: Both agency theory and transaction cost theory contend that although there may be no perfect contract, firms can try to agree on a most optimal contract [4]. Firms can rely on their business partner’s goodwill during difficult times if the relationship is strong. However, when a relationship is established through RAs, the likelihood of a goodwill gesture by a buyer or a supplier is low [38]. A common cause of conflict in a postcontract relationship is a dispute regarding the specifications [5, 41, 59]. When in a dispute, business partners fall back on the contract to determine the appropriate price as per the agreed-upon specifications. It is better to negotiate a contract with clear specifications than to engage in a dispute after the contract is signed. Buyers ranked the contractual hazards risk factor as number one and suppliers ranked it among the top five risk factors. Thus, buyers and suppliers concur on the importance of this risk factor.

Agent opportunism risks of concern to both buyers and suppliers: The opportunistic behavior by a supplier is a frequently discussed risk factor in RA literature and is a well-studied problem in all business domains. Both agency theory and transaction cost theory address supplier opportunism risk factors. Reverse auctions negatively affect a supplier’s goodwill toward the buyer since suppliers are forced to compromise on their profit margins. Suppliers can act opportunistically by first bidding low to gain the contract and then taking advantage of loopholes in the contract to increase prices [10, 13]. While this affects the buyer, it also precludes other suppliers from earning the business. The most common form of supplier opportunism arises when a supplier compromises on the quality of the product or the quality of service and support to cut the cost and honor the offered RA bid price [5, 13].

To maintain profits, a supplier can introduce hidden costs not anticipated by the buyer. Not only does this hurt the buyer, but it is also likely to hurt the supplier’s own credibility in the long run. A buyer can use additional RAs to switch to a new supplier, especially in a favorable market, when it discovers opportunistic behavior by an incumbent supplier. A supplier, on the other hand, can be forced to look for new customers, possibly bidding in RAs again, reconfiguring its products or services as per the specifications of a new customer. Interestingly, we find that supplier opportunism concerns both buyers and suppliers.

Principal/buyer opportunism risks of concern to both buyers and suppliers: While supplier opportunism is a well-understood problem in the literature, principal opportunism is relatively underexplored. Only a handful of studies have examined principal opportunism (e.g., [13, 15, 33, 40]). These studies reveal that much as an agent can act in its self-interest, the principal too can be opportunistic when its goals conflict with those of the agent. While it can be argued that the principal opportunism risk factors identified by suppliers could be perception driven [38], we suggest that those identified by buyers are not, as these risk factors are an acknowledgment of buyers’ own actions and behaviors. Suppliers can perceive that a buyer has manipulated RAs by introducing artificially low bids, a phenomenon known as shilling [37, 38]. Our results suggest that the low richness of online RA tools as a communication medium [11, 27] can drive such perceptions among suppliers. Suppliers can only see either their rank or the bid price. The limited information causes suppliers to become suspicious of the buyer’s behavior, believing that the buyer is acting unethically or opportunistically. This lack of information and feedback can create suspicion among low bidders who later do not get invited for contract negotiations. When a nonincumbent supplier is not awarded the business, it may perceive that the buyer used the RA only to test the market and determine the current price. The supplier may suspect that the buyer has no intention of buying from other suppliers, but rather intends to use the outcome of the RA to exert pricing pressure on its incumbent suppliers. The incumbent suppliers, on the other hand, can perceive that the buyer is using the RA only to renegotiate the current price. Since it is difficult for suppliers to verify the buyer’s true intentions, these perceptions can lead to a loss of supplier trust in the buyer.

A buyer can opportunistically use RAs by inducing competition by intentionally including certain suppliers who will not be awarded the business [29]. A buyer can also be unfaithful to the RA process by accepting quotes outside of the RAs and negotiating with suppliers that were not invited to bid. A buyer acts opportunistically and in its self-interest when it denies sincere and competent suppliers the opportunity to win the business. When a buyer does not honor the award terms by awarding business to participating suppliers, it risks either their outright refusal to participate or their unwillingness to provide their best bids in future RAs. All such controversial actions by a buyer are considered to be an unethical use of RAs [5, 13, 21]. Thus, reverse auctions can act as a catalyst for buyer opportunistic behavior. Among the five top-ranked principal opportunism risk factors, the first two (a buyer manipulating RAs by introducing artificial bids; buyer using RAs with no intention of buying) were ranked highly by the supplier panel. The other three principal opportunism risk factors (buyer including suppliers who will not be awarded the business; buyer not faithful to the auction process; and buyer’s failure to honor award terms), were highly ranked by the buyer panel.

In a transactional exchange, a buyer can at best suspect or later discover opportunistic behavior by a supplier. A buyer can refuse to do business with an opportunistic supplier and can invoke its legal rights. However, in the case of buyer opportunistic behavior, the buyer is aware of its own opportunistic behavior. Therefore, not only are these risk factors an acknowledgment of the potential abuse of RAs and unethical behavior by buyers, but they can also damage a buyer’s reputation. As a result, suppliers can refuse to participate in RAs and not provide their best bids. Strikingly, the principal/buyer opportunism risk factors were identified and ranked high by both buyers and suppliers. Drawing on these insights we develop the following proposition:

Proposition 4 (A): Buyers and suppliers differ as well as concur in their assessment of RA agency and transaction cost risk factors, where:

1. Small number bargaining due to lack of competition in reverse auctions is of greater concern to buyers than to suppliers.

2. Both buyers and suppliers are concerned with contractual hazards due to inadequately specified requirements and with opportunistic behaviors by buyers or suppliers.

Controlling small number bargaining risk factors: We interviewed the experts regarding one RA risk factor that mapped to this dimension.<sup>7</sup> It was suggested that controls focused on assessing the market and suppliers could address the small number bargaining risk factor. Consistent with prior studies (e.g., [38]) suggesting five or more suppliers, experts from our panel suggested that at least six suppliers should be included, and that caution should be exercised if three or fewer suppliers elect to participate in the RA. A buyer should also request pre-bid prices to ensure that the participating suppliers are likely to quote a reasonable price during the RA.

Controlling contractual hazards risk factors: Contractual hazard risk factors arise from the inability of a party to secure its interests through a contract. The risk factor of inadequately specified requirements was mapped to this dimension. It was suggested that controls focused on preparing for RA execution could address this risk factor. In particular, the controls related to developing readiness for RAs are important. A buyer should allocate sufficient preparation time and understand its needs by communicating with internal stakeholders. The buyer should also provide sufficient time to suppliers to prepare their bids. It is important that the buyer communicates with suppliers and explains specified requirements in detail. Last, the suppliers should be given incentives to prepare bids without having to compromise on quality. Otherwise, it can be difficult for the buyer to rely on the contract to protect its interests when a supplier provides inferior products and services.

Controlling opportunistic behavior by the buyer: The experts were interviewed regarding one principal opportunism risk factor: the risk of the buyer not being faithful to the auction process. No tenable controls were proposed to address principal opportunism since RAs give the power to make informed decisions to the buyer and it is not possible for suppliers to gather or purchase information from the buyer. Moreover, the buyer is not legally bound to award business to suppliers based on the outcome of an RA. Suppliers have little to no control over how a buyer conducts its RAs and how the buyer makes award decisions. As one expert from the buyer panel put it: “It is fairly common where buyers feel empowered to deviate from the process that they may or may not have communicated with suppliers. You get into borderline [sic] of ethics and integrity issues here.”

Several experts from the buyer panel admitted to having been either directly involved or having firsthand knowledge of being unfaithful to the process. When explaining such an incident, one expert said: “We were not faithful to the auction process; we were not faithful to the suppliers.” Another expert said:

The integrity of the auctions depends on the group responsible for conducting the auctions. Yes, I have seen and been a part of such auctions. It was something I was asked to be involved in where there were separate negotiations going on while the auction was taking place. It was happening backstage. It was clear what was going on was less than ethical. There were phone calls going on during the auction where separate negotiations were taking place.

The controls suggested to address opportunistic behavior by a buyer were those focused on preparing for RA execution. Specifically, the controls related to determining auction parameters by designing auction rules. While developing readiness and mutual understanding can help, the most important control is related to adhering to the rules and ethical guidelines. In their study, Charki et al. [13] examined the issue of unethical behavior in detail and suggested that incorporating RAs into the broader context of relationship management can help address principal opportunism. In particular, the values of both (a) ethics as fairness and (b) ethics as honesty are required. Treatment of ethics as honesty alone is not sufficient.

Controlling opportunistic behavior by the supplier: We discussed two agent opportunism risk factors with the experts. The two risk factors relate to the risk that the quality of service and support will be reduced, and the quality of the product will be reduced to achieve the offered bid price. While prior studies suggest several postcontract controls to address agent opportunism (e.g., [17]), our findings suggest the need for both pre- and postcontract controls. We suggest that strong precontract input controls lead to less taxing postcontract controls. The precontract controls that focus on assessing market and suppliers and the postcontract controls that monitor performance can help address opportunistic behavior by suppliers. To enforce input controls, the buyer should evaluate and compare suppliers on their capability prior to the RA. After awarding business, the buyer can constantly monitor suppliers and hold them accountable by providing feedback based on the quality benchmarks determined prior to the RA.

Based on the above insights we build the following proposition regarding controls to address agency and transaction cost risk factors of RAs:

Proposition 4 (B): Agency and transaction cost risk factors of RAs can be mitigated by both the precontract input controls and the postcontract outcome controls, where:

1. Small number bargaining due to lack of competition can be mitigated by precontract input controls that pertain to a buyer’s assessment of the market and suppliers.

2. Contractual hazards due to inadequately specified requirements and opportunistic behavior by the buyer can both be mitigated by precontract input controls that pertain to buyers and suppliers preparing for RA execution.

3. Opportunistic behavior by suppliers can be mitigated by a buyer assessing market and suppliers prior to contract and post contract monitoring of supplier performance.

## Implications

We developed a comprehensive list of RA risk factors and the controls that can address the top-ranked risk factors. We then developed a risk-control integrative model. We built propositions that explain the interplay between RA risks and controls by drawing on the insights from (1) the similarities and differences between buyer and supplier perspectives of RA risk factors, and (2) the risk-control integrative model. We now discuss the implications of our findings for research and practice.

Theoretical elaboration of the concept of risk factors in RAs: Based on the similarities and differences between the buyer and supplier rankings of the risk factors, we identified two key insights. First, buyers and suppliers differ in their assessment of RA risk factors where buyers emphasize pre-auction governance, organizational contingency, and small number bargaining risk factors of RAs. Suppliers emphasize during-auction governance, post-auction governance, and buyer–supplier relationship risk factors of RAs. Second, buyers and suppliers concur in their assessment of RA risk factors where both emphasize phase-independent governance, contractual hazards, and opportunism risk factors of RAs. Collectively, these insights suggest that theoretically, it is meaningful to differentiate RA risk factors based on buyer and supplier perspectives and the orientation of risk factors toward auction process governance, organizational contingency, buyer–supplier relationships, and agency and transaction costs.

Precontract input controls as key risk mitigator: Our findings show the importance of establishing and enforcing stringent precontract input controls to cut the costs of postcontract controls. Pre-auction assessment of the market and suppliers can help mitigate auction process governance, reduced bargaining, and supplier opportunism risk factors. Controls that focus on preparing for RA execution are most extensive and particularly important in addressing RA risk factors. The controls that focus on preparing for RA execution can help address nearly all the RA risk factors. Postcontract supplier monitoring controls can check for supplier opportunism. Additionally, our results suggest that both buyers and suppliers are responsible for implementing input controls. The input controls are both internal (a party monitors its internal activities) and external (one party monitors the other).

Support for and extension to the theory of relationship constraints: A key premise on which the theory of relationship constraints is based is the inadequacy of agency theory in addressing principal opportunism. While IT is traditionally seen as a facilitator of information sharing and diffusion, we find that IT can also be used to induce information asymmetry by giving buyers an advantage in a transactional exchange. A buyer can use its advantageous position over suppliers to extract benefits and generate extra rent. Through these findings, we find support for the theory of relationship constraints [15] and extend it in two respects.

First, the theory of relationship constraints suggests that a party with an information advantage signals some meaningful information about itself to a disadvantaged party. The information-disadvantaged party screens the advantaged party by assessing its suitability to be a business partner. Since both parties may have some advantageous information, they can simultaneously signal and screen one another. We find that the use of RAs leads to circumstances in which the informationadvantaged party can intensely screen the disadvantaged party. When a buyer already has an established relationship with an incumbent supplier, it can use RAs to assess market price; accurately estimate the incumbent’s and other suppliers’ cost structures; and use this information advantage to reduce procurement costs. Thus, we find that RA use can induce information asymmetry in favor of the buyer, which is a situation we refer to as reverse information asymmetry.

Second, we find evidence of principal opportunism. We find that reverse information asymmetry begets principal opportunism as previously suggested by Dawson et al. [15] and other studies (e.g., [33]). The buyers who served on the panel admitted during individual interviews to have opportunistically used RAs. Thus, our findings support the idea of principal opportunism and contribute to the prior research [33, 40] by showing how principal opportunism can arise due to the use of technology for a business activity. Our findings reveal that principal opportunism primarily occurs prior to contract. Since no contractual clauses can be applied to curb opportunistic behavior at this time, it is contingent on a buyer to establish and adhere to ethical guidelines and win the trust of participating suppliers.

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

Although our study generated important insights on risks and controls in RAs by using multiple Delphi panels and in-depth interviews with experts, it is not without limitations. First, while we identify and characterize risk factors and controls, further research is needed to assess the extent to which the risk factors affect the success of RAs. Second, future research can examine the extent to which controls identified in our study can mitigate the risk factors in different buyer contexts (e.g., type of good/ service being procured) and whether buyers and suppliers differ in their perspectives on the controls for any risk factor(s). Third, we identify controls only for a select number of risk factors (i.e., the key risks). Future research can expand the search to other risk factors and identify additional controls, and examine differences in the effectiveness of controls from the perspectives of buyers and suppliers. Finally, methodologies similar to the Delphi methodology have been suggested for knowledge generation exercises (e.g., repertory grid methodology). Future research using alternative knowledge elicitation techniques may be useful in order to determine whether there are any significant differences in terms of the surfacing of risk factors or controls.

## Conclusion

Our study makes a significant contribution to our understanding of RAs by identifying the risk factors associated with RA use, surfacing the similarities and differences in buyers’ and suppliers’ perspectives of the risk factors, and identifying the controls that can effectively mitigate the risks. It advances our theoretical understanding of RAs by: (1) integrating theoretical perspectives (auction process governance, organizational contingency, buyer–supplier relationship, and agency and transaction cost) to explain the risks and the differences in suppliers’ and buyer’s assessments of them, and (2) identifying the controls that can be deployed in the pre- and postcontract periods to mitigate the risks. Our risk-control integrative model and the corresponding propositions can be evaluated and further developed in future research related to risks and controls of RAs and more broadly B2B exchanges.
