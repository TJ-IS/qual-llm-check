Abs trac t: The application of real options techniques to information security is significantly different than in the case of general information technology investments due to characteristics unique to information security. Emerging research in the economics of information security has suggested real options analysis (ROA) as a potential technique for assessing the value of information security assets, but has focused primarily on the most effective level of investment and the configuration of intrusion prevention/detection systems. In this paper, we attempt to address significant gaps in the literature by developing an integrated real options model for information security investments using Bayesian statistics that incorporates learning and postauditing in the analysis. By using the proposed model with actual data on e-mail and spam, we demonstrate that ROA with Bayesian postauditing offers a systematic valuation and risk management framework for evaluating information security spending by firms. We also discuss the managerial implications.

Key words and ph ras es : Bayesian revisions, conjugate prior distributions, economics of information security, information security investments, postaudit, real options, return on investment, ROC curves.

Given th e importanc e of c omputer and information security, investment in information security is now recognized as a critical issue by both practitioners and academics alike. Much of the recent information security research has focused on the technical aspects of reducing information security breaches. Research devoted to the economic aspects of information security is emerging and has centered on the difficulties associated with the definition and measurement of information technology (IT) security costs and benefits, the most effective level of investment, and managerial decisions pertaining to the configuration of intrusion detection and prevention [8, 15, 35]. Security vulnerabilities, increased dependence on information, and pressure from consumers and regulators require organizations to spend more on securing information assets. Increases in information security budgets require proper techniques for evaluating investment decisions pertaining to IT security. A variety of approaches for analyzing security investments have been suggested, including traditional decision analytic framework [22], systematic risk assessment [26], and the economic analysis of the optimal level of security investment based on damage and the likelihood of security violations [15].

Gordon and Loeb [15] and Gordon et al. [17] suggest using capital budgeting techniques such as net present value (NPV) or more advanced real options analysis (ROA) to evaluate information security projects. There are two important areas in capital budgeting that are useful for improving our understanding of information security investments: the evaluation of IT security investment projects and the postaudit. ROA allows managers “to apply rational interventions that favorably change the traits of investments (timing, scale, scope, etc.) as the uncertainties unfold” [2, p. 47]. ROA techniques are well suited for evaluating IT security investments due to the high-risk characteristics inherent in the changing threats to IT systems, the timing of investments that may change due to the uncertainty associated with potential breaches, and the nonrandom changes in irreversible investment costs that are dependent on the level of configuration efforts that drive labor and other costs related to prevention, detection, and recovery.

The extant IT literature has recognized the importance of using real options, but has not investigated the unique aspects of IT security [1, 3, 23, 24]. First, intrusion prevention/detection systems (IDS) use noise or signal distributions to characterize their effectiveness. In addition, the investment costs of configuration may change due to varying configuration efforts and licensing costs. There is comprehensive literature on intrusion prevention/detection, but it has not been used in the context of evaluating investments in IT security. Second, given the highly uncertain conditions governing IT security, it is economically rational to invest in stages, when an IDS performance deteriorates or a breach occurs. Thus, once an investment is made to configure an IDS, it is useful to be able to evaluate the actual performance of that system before making the next investment.

Bayesian learning and postauditing have immense value in ROA. A decision maker can update subjective initial forecasts with additional information obtained by sampling. Combining sample information with previously existing information at the evaluation phase can reduce the possibility of optimistic forecasts aimed at promoting projects. Once a decision to accept an IT security project has been made, the opportunity for a postaudit using Bayesian learning arises. The objective of the postaudit is to improve and control the decision-making process [14, 16]. In a postaudit, one can assess or monitor projects to determine whether the investments have served their intended purpose. As such, chief executive officers (CEOs) and accountants in most organizations encourage postaudits. Postaudits, though relatively new to the IT discipline, have an immense value in sequential IT security investments because they provide a control (technical and financial) mechanism as well as valuable input into future IT security investment decisions. As such, the Bayesian revision of valuation parameters is an important area of research pertaining to postaudits that would contribute to the literature in investment analysis and information security.

As we demonstrated above, two major research gaps need to be addressed regarding the theoretical and practical side of evaluating IT security investment decisions. First, more definitive research is needed on how to model real options for the IT security setting. Second, learning and the postauditing of investment decisions need to be explored in this context. Consequently, the objective of this paper is to develop a real options model for information security investments using Bayesian inferences for valuation and postauditing. As such, the intended contribution of this paper is twofold: (1) to further the real options literature by using Bayesian statistics to model learning options and the postauditing of sequential real options, an idea that can be applied to a variety of other types of sequential investment problems where there is an opportunity to sample, such as research and development (R&D) (pharmaceutical), IT platforms (positioning using pilot projects, prototypes, etc. [13], and network expansion); and (2) to further the information security investment literature through the novel application of an integrated model that combines unique IT security features. Our work extends the previous work of Bardhan et al. [1], Benaroch et al. [4], Bierman and Rao [5], Cavusoglu and Raghunathan [7], and Herath and Park [20].

The basis of this paper is a case study of investments in e-mail service security in a North American university. We use this study due to the importance of e-mail security and the availability of actual data to evaluate the model. Three strands of research— economics of information security, real options in IT, and Bayesian statistics—are relevant to this study. The economics of information security deals with optimal investment levels and the configuration of IDS useful for estimating costs and benefits.

Influential research in this area includes the work of Cavusoglu and Raghunathan [7], Cavusoglu et al. [8], Gordon and Loeb [15], Gordon et al. [17], Ulvila and Gaffney [34], and Yue and Çakanyildirim [35]. The second strand of research deals with real option analysis (valuation), which is now a part of the mainstream literature in financial economics. Bardhan et al. [1], Benaroch and Kauffman [3], Benaroch et al. [4], Dos Santos [12], Fichman [13], Kumar [24], McGrath and MacMillan [28], Taudes [32], and Taudes et al. [33] provide the seminal work in the application of real options to various problems with IT. The third strand of research relates to Bayesian statistics and the families of conjugate prior distributions useful for postauditing. Important work in this area is by Luce and Raiffa [27], Raiffa and Schlaifer [30], and Savage [31]. We apply these three strands of research to evaluate information security investment projects and the Bayesian postaudit, topics that, to the best of our knowledge, have not been investigated thoroughly before.

## Case for Security Investments in E-Mail Services

Th e North Americ an univers ity (hereafter referred to as NAU) considered in this study is a mid-sized university with over 18,000 full- and part-time students and approximately 1,200 faculty and staff. We consider the security investment decision in e-mail services at this university for our application case study. A recent CSI/FBI survey reports that viruses remain the number one threat to IT systems [18]. The 2007 e-crime watch survey indicates that viruses and other malicious codes were the most frequent types of electronic crimes (74 percent) experienced by organizations [29]. Spam and phishing e-mails also ranked high in the list of electronic crimes committed. While there are various sources for virus propagation, the 2004 ICSA survey shows that virus propagation by e-mail poses the greatest threat [6] and that e-mail vectors continue to be the primary means of spreading viruses.

In addition to the direct damage virus e-mails pose, spam e-mails also adversely affect organizations. Spam e-mails affect bandwidth consumption, thereby reducing network efficiency, employee productivity, and e-mail server storage capacity. In Table 1, we tabulate e-mail transaction data from the NAU system for a two-day and a six-month period to establish its existing configuration. These data show the severity of the e-mail security issue. As shown, there were 298 detected virus infections in a span of two days. These, along with other nonproductive e-mails such as spam and phishing, cause a significant loss of productivity as well as posing an IT security issue to the NAU.

## E-Mail Services Architecture at the NAU

Organizations may use different e-mail server architectures depending on resource availability and security levels. Figure 1 depicts the architecture used for e-mail services at the NAU. All incoming e-mails with the NAU e-mail address pass through a firewall. The firewall checks the e-mail and Web traffic for any potential virus infections. The allowed-to-pass (AT P) e-mail traffic is then diverted to the SMTP (simple mail transfer protocol) server. SMTP is used to move and store mail throughout the university network. The e-mails are stored on the SMTP server until the e-mail recipient retrieves them. Several filters are configured to identify malicious or spam e-mails. Because all organizational e-mails are filtered, the location of a recipient, whether on-site (on university premises) or off-site, does not make a difference. However, e-mails received through third-party e-mail services, including Yahoo, Gmail, Hotmail, and others, do not get scanned and, therefore, increase the vulnerability of the NAU system.

## Security Configuration Alternatives

There are many freeware filters available that are effective; however, these filters need to be configured, which requires skilled labor. Depending on the level of the security desired,<sup>2</sup> the labor hours allocated to configurations may vary. Also available are offthe-shelf products, which need relatively few, nearly negligible, numbers of hours to install. However, the cost of the product as well as the level of security it provides may differ from an in-house developed configuration. The decision makers at the NAU could have used either the traditional NPV or a real options model to evaluate their IT security investment decisions.

## Issues Unique to Information Security: Overview of Technical and Market Uncertainties

Information s ec urity projec ts h ave h igh -risk ch arac teris tics due to the changing threats to IT systems and the uncertainty associated with potential breaches. Benaroch [2] provides an excellent discussion of the three main risk categories related to IT assets: (1) firm-specific risks (monetary, project, functionality, or organizational), (2) competitive risks, and (3) market risks (environmental, systemic, and technological). In the IT security context, some of the above are directly applicable. Firm-specific risks in this context are endogenous, and may arise due to uncertainty in investment costs and resource capabilities related to IDS configuration. Exogenous risks arise from factors such as security threats and the potential for breaches.

As the following quotation indicates, it is standard in ROA to consider uncertainties in two broad categories—technical and economic:

A phased investment decision often has both economic (market) uncertainty and technological uncertainty. Technological uncertainty is largely at the start of the project, but is diminished as the company invests to learn more. Thus there are two independent sources of uncertainty—economic uncertainty increasing through time and technological uncertainty decreasing (as investments are made). [10, p. 221, emphasis added]

In the case of information security, technical (technological) uncertainties may depend on the risk characteristics of the threat, the potential for a breach, and the ability to correctly detect an intrusion before the occurrence of the breach (part endogenous and part exogenous). Technological uncertainty is affected by the firm’s level of information security spending. Information security systems, like all information systems, involve hardware and software costs as well as labor costs for configuring, tuning, and monitoring the system. The probability of detecting a breach tends to relate to the level of information security spending; that is, higher security spending that builds better systems and configurations should increase detection rates. In accordance with the existing literature, we assume that the dollar value of the benefits from information security activities increases at a decreasing rate [15]. In order to obtain the probability of detection for a given level of information security spending, the security breach function can be modeled as a decay function with an adjustment parameter. The effectiveness of information security is measured by the adjustment parameter (alpha) and can be captured by an information security expert’s assessment of how secure the system is at a given point in time.

Another aspect of technical (technological) uncertainty in information security is related predominantly to the configuration of a host-based IDS. Host-based IDS sensors may use characterization of signal distribution or noise distribution to look at and detect attacks. When evaluating an IDS, the idea is to balance the false negative error (a fraudulent event detected as authorized) and the false positive error (a normal event detected as fraudulent) to minimize information security–related costs such as investigation and damage. The complementary event to a fraudulent event being detected as authorized (false negative) is the event of detection. Thus, the probability of detection and probability of false positive errors are adjustable quality parameters in an IDS. These two quality parameters are characterized by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of the IDS [7, 34]. Given one quality parameter—either the probability of detection or the probability of a false positive—and a specific ROC curve for an IDS, security specialists can compute the other quality parameter.

The current literature on IDS configuration has considered decision-theoretic and game-theoretic approaches to finding optimal operating points and quality parameters. However, the current research, to our knowledge, has not investigated the Bayesian revision of IDS parameters in the decision theory approach. Ulvila and Gaffney [34] integrate decision tree analysis to incorporate uncertainty, extend ROC, and cost analysis methods to decide the optimal operating point of an IDS, and compare one system with another. Yue and Çakanyildirim [35] extend the Ulvila and Gaffney [34] model by optimizing both the detection and reaction decision variables of an IDS. Cavusoglu and Raghunathan [7] and Cavusoglu et al. [8] explore the optimal configuration of detection software by using both decision and game theory approaches. While the above studies provide valuable insight into different security vulnerabilities including hacking, they focus on optimal configuration rather than an ROA of IT security. Moreover, the game theory approach involves analyzing the optimal security investment problem in terms of a game between a hacker and the organization. The use of this theory is unique to intrusions where a hacker has a motive against a particular organization. In other scenarios, such as spam and e-mail virus security problems, the malicious user may not have a motive against a particular organization; these situations may be more appropriately treated as games against nature. Given this, the game-theoretic approach may not be the best for spam and virus e-mail security scenarios and decision-theoretic methods may be more appropriate. Moreover, the game theoretic approach requires firms to know intruder-specific parameters such as intruder utility and penalties, which are more difficult to obtain than the parameters required by decision-theoretic approaches such as breach probability, which can be obtained through historical logs [7]. In general, current business practices seem to favor a decision-theoretic approach to determine the optimal configuration of intrusion detection systems.

It is rational to undertake these highly uncertain security investments in stages as per ROA since firms can assess an IDS’s performance and invest if there is performance deterioration due to new threats/vulnerabilities or an actual security breach. These sequential IT security investments require modeling multiple economic uncertainties. Economic (market) uncertainties are those related to the price of traded assets (underlying risky assets) and, in the context of security investments, the movement of the market value of the security asset. In evaluating information security projects, it is not possible to find a traded asset (or a twin security such as a stock or commodity) to approximate the underlying risky asset. Security analysts can overcome this difficulty by applying the market asset disclaimer (MAD) assumption [10]. Here the traditional expected present value of cash flows (excluding investment cost) or the gross project value is used as the best unbiased estimate of the project’s market value. This value is assumed to follow a geometric Brownian motion (GBM) or a binomial lattice in a discrete setting. Technical uncertainties do not depend on market variables as defined in an ROA context and are independent of the economy [10]. Therefore, technological and economic uncertainties can be separated in the ROA of information security.

To model market uncertainty, the project volatility must be known. While a multitude of uncertainties may exist in IT security, to apply ROA, these uncertainties have to be quantifiable and reducible because it is exceedingly difficult to analyze a lattice or develop analytical models with more than two sources of uncertainty [2, 10]. To avoid this problem, real option analysts can use the consolidated approach [10, 21] described in Appendix A. Using this approach, we combine the multiple uncertainties that drive the value of an information security project via Monte Carlo analysis into a single uncertainty: the volatility of the project or standard deviation of the logarithmic annual returns of the project. This estimate of volatility is then used to model the market uncertainty of the information security project by the up-and-down movement of the binomial lattice.

IT security investments can be modeled as American-style sequential real options. The irreversibility in spending and the uncertainty associated with potential IT security breaches suggest that it may be rational to take a “wait-and-see” approach to allocating funds for information security projects [17]. This approach involves undertaking investments in stages, thereby enabling firms to exploit the upside potential of an investment opportunity through learning. In our modeling framework, instead of a one-time lump investment in information security, we recast the opportunity into a series of interrelated investment projects that are undertaken after resolving uncertainty. More specifically, the upstream IT security project i spawns a downstream project i + 1 that is undertaken contingent upon what is learned in project i. The Bayesian model facilitates these learning opportunities. Each project is treated as a separate real option with its own underlying asset and respective volatility. For exposition clarity without a loss of generality, we consider three interrelated information security opportunities with investment costs $( I _ { i } ,$ for i = 1, 2, 3)<sup>3</sup>.

In the Benaroch et al. [4] and Herath and Park [21] models, the sequential options are a European type in which the options are exercisable only at maturity and early exercise is not possible. We allow for early exercise by using American options, which are the most common type of real options [10, 11]. American options offer more flexibility in circumstances where the investment costs change, making it much cheaper to undertake security projects now rather than later. The second difference is that we model the learning options that arise from a reduction of technological uncertainty when investments in IDS are made sequentially and we show how this idea can be used in valuation and postauditing.

In general, multistage projects may be linked by both technological and market dependencies. Market dependencies are modeled by asset correlation. There is technological dependence in multistaged information security projects because intrusion detention systems are a platform type where the current IDS is built on top of the previous IDS. As we explain next, the quality parameters of an IDS capture technical dependence. In addition, when there are multiple assets (several projects), these assets may be either correlated or uncorrelated. We assume that these underlying assets are uncorrelated in order to model the multistage investments as sequential options [19].<sup>4</sup>

## Modeling Technical Uncertainty with ROC Curves

We use Cavusoglu and Raghunathan’s [7] and Ulvila and Gaffney’s [34] ROC curve approach to compare how effective different configurations of e-mail gateways (IDS) are in capturing technical uncertainty.<sup>5</sup> This approach is based on classical statistical theory where the ROC curve shows the relationship between the two classification errors in a detection system. The two classification errors are the false positive and false negative. The classification error of a false negative given by $1 - P _ { _ D } = \mathrm { P r } ( H _ { _ 0 } | H _ { _ 1 }$ is true), but this is taken care of by $P _ { D } .$

This is a simplifying assumption, but it can be relaxed as in Cavusoglu and Raghunathan [7] where a decision whether or not to investigate can be taken in both signal and no-signal states. ## Costs, Benefits, and Investment Considerations

When undertaking security investments, cost–benefit trade-offs have to be considered. Securing information costs money, sometimes substantial amounts, but other tradeoffs ranging from a matter of inconvenience and comfort to issues involving basic freedoms such as privacy may be more important.

## Cost Function

The total cost function includes the configuration-specific costs as given in Equation (1) for some level of investment $s _ { i }$ and the operating costs with annualized cost parameters. Operating costs include the following: (1) annual fixed operating costs such as hiring IT security personnel to maintain the system independent of the level of IT security capacity acquired; (2) the annual variable portion of costs for training, and the like, that depend on the level of information security investments; and (3) opportunity costs associated with loss of access as more controls are emphasized. The opportunity costs assume a quadratic term so that the total cost of information security will initially increase at a decreasing rate, and thereafter increase at an increasing rate due to the access restriction put in place by higher levels of information security. The total cost function with configuration specific costs and operating costs is given by

$$
\hat {C} _ {i} = \Big \{c P _ {D} ^ {i} \psi + d (1 - P _ {D} ^ {i}) \psi + c _ {1} P _ {F} ^ {i} (1 - \psi) \Big \} + \Bigg \{a _ {0 i} + a _ {1 i} s _ {i} + \frac {1}{2} a _ {2 i} s _ {i} ^ {2} \Bigg \}.\tag{3}
$$

## Benefit Function

The benefit function associated with information security spending can be modeled as the expected benefit of preventing a breach. The probability of a breach is often considered to be the product of threat probability and the probability of vulnerability [15]. Although information security risks are often difficult to estimate, several generic risk assessment frameworks such as DRA M (delphic risk assessment method), INFOSEC-IAM (information security assessment methodology), and OCTA VE (operationally critical threat, asset, and vulnerability evaluation), and others may provide valuable guidance.

The breach risk is modeled as a decay function, where the probability of a breach reduces as the level of security spending increases (see Figure 3). At a given security spending level $s _ { i } ,$ the probability of a breach is $\operatorname* { P r } ( o \mid s _ { i } ) = e ^ { - \infty _ { i } }$ , in which the adjustment parameter a represents an expert’s subjective assessment of the effectiveness of the system. Due to the varying risk profiles of experts and the changing security threat environment, a can vary (see Figure 3). The probability of avoiding a breach for a given level of security is $1 - e ^ { - \infty _ { i } }$ and can be equated to the probability of detection $P _ { D } ^ { \ i } = 1 - e ^ { - \infty _ { i } }$ . Since for any level of investment $s _ { i }$ one can compute the probability of detecting $P _ { D } ^ { i }$ by using the ROC relationship for a given $r _ { i } = \lambda _ { _ { F } } ^ { ~ i } / \lambda _ { _ { N } } ^ { ~ i } ,$ we can compute the probability of a false positive $P _ { F } ^ { \ i } .$ The assumption that quality parameters are a function of the resources allocated for information security fits with current practice and is a reasonable assumption.

The expected benefit of providing secure information depends on the level of detection, which in turn depends on the level of security spending. Given the probability of a breach, the benefit of preventing a breach can be calculated using the probability of avoiding the breach—the complement probability $( 1 - e ^ { - 0 . s _ { i } } )$ times the cost of an information breach if no security investment is made. Therefore, the benefit associated with preventing a breach is

$$
\hat {B} _ {i} = C _ {B} \left(1 - \operatorname * {P r} \left(o \mid s _ {i}\right)\right) = C _ {B} \left(1 - e ^ {- \alpha s _ {i}}\right).\tag{4}
$$

Because increased security spending will reduce the probability of a breach, the expected benefits will increase. Notice that the benefit function is independent of the time subscript, which indicates that the benefit associated with a certain level of investment will be the same throughout the project life. This is a simplified assumption that can be relaxed by considering $C _ { B }$ to vary with time. The annual after-tax cash flows and traditional NPV of project i are shown in Figure 2, Panel C.

In security projects, a Monte Carlo simulation can be used to estimate the costs and benefits. If firm-specific data are available, an analyst can specify the input distributions by fitting distributions to the data. Typically, it is difficult to collect data for information security investments, possibly because information security systems are unique or because a system may not be in place. In addition, even if some form of information security system is in place, complexities and costs may preclude data collection. In such instances, simulation allows an analyst to select input distributions in the absence of data by using the two-point and three-point estimation procedures [25].

## Modeling Learning Effects of Technological Uncertainty: Bayesian Approach

We develop a Bayesian model to update the technological parameters $\lambda _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \textit { i } }$ and $\lambda _ { _ { F } } ^ { i }$ of an IDS that are inputs in the real options model. We illustrate the Bayesian model for the number of normal transactions $( \lambda _ { _ { N } } ^ { i } )$ , which is similar to the Bayesian model for fraudulent transactions.

Consider the random variable of interest, which is the arrival rate of normal transactions into an IDS configuration resulting from undertaking information security project i. Under the Bayesian approach, the parameter of interest for inference (q) for an IDS is the score of normal transactions (i.e., $\mathsf { \Omega } _ { \Theta } = \lambda _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \mathrm { \Omega } i } )$ . In the sequential real options model, the first project i spawns another downstream project i + 1 that could be undertaken depending on the effectiveness of project i. Therefore, the parameters pertaining to security project i in the Bayesian setting are labeled as the prior distribution parameters (denoted by a prime). We model the arrival rate of normal transactions (or probability of normal transactions per unit time) by a Poisson process. Poisson distributions have been widely used to model the arrival of normal transactions as well as intrusions (fraudulent) per unit of time ([9, 26] among others). In statistics, the exponential distribution is used to model Poisson processes.

In the Bayesian approach, we would need to sample information involving $( { \boldsymbol { \Theta } } = \lambda _ { \boldsymbol { N } } ^ { \ i } )$ and summarize it by a sample statistic Z. Then the posterior distribution (denoted by a double prime) of $( { \boldsymbol { \Theta } } = \lambda _ { \boldsymbol { N } } ^ { \ i } )$ is given by Bayes’s theorem [30]:

$$
f ^ {\prime \prime} (\theta | z) = \frac {f ^ {\prime} (\theta) f (z | \theta)}{\int f ^ {\prime} (\theta) f (z | \theta) d \theta},
$$

where the densities $f ^ { \prime \prime } ( \theta | z ) , f ^ { \prime } ( \theta )$ , and $f ( z | \theta )$ represent the posterior and prior distributions and the likelihood function.

In practice, the above formula is difficult to apply and, thus, Bayesian statisticians have developed the concept of conjugate prior distributions [30]. Essentially, these are families of distributions that have well-developed prior to posterior relationships based on assumptions regarding populations or sampling processes. In information security, the Poisson process is the appropriate process for sampling for the arrival rate of normal (fraudulent) transactions $\lambda _ { _ { N } } ( \lambda _ { _ { F } } ) .$ <sup>7</sup> Therefore, the likelihood function is a Poisson and the natural prior and posterior conjugate is the gamma distribution [30] (see Figure 4, Panel A).

The Bayesian approach for revising the rate parameter $\lambda _ { { } _ { N } }$ based on Poisson sampling is as follows. In evaluating information security projects, the analyst would need to estimate the mean and the variance of the rate parameter. These estimates may be based on data from an existing IDS configuration or subjective estimates of the prior gamma distribution $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ ., the mean $E ( \lambda _ { \scriptscriptstyle { N } } ^ { \prime } )$ and variance $\mathrm { V a r } ( \lambda _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \prime } ) )$ . The mean and variance of the prior gamma density with shape parameter $( R _ { N } ^ { \prime } )$ and scale parameter $( t _ { \ N } ^ { \prime } )$ for the arrival rate of normal transactions are $E ( \lambda _ { _ { N } } ^ { \prime } ) = R _ { _ { N } } ^ { \prime } / t _ { _ { N } } ^ { \prime }$ and $\mathrm { V a r } ( \lambda _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \prime } ) = R _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \prime } / ( t _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \prime } ) ^ { 2 }$ (with $\lambda _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \prime } > 0 , R _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \prime } > 0$ , and $t _ { { \scriptscriptstyle N } } ^ { \prime } > 0 )$ . To revise the prior estimates, the security analyst can observe the performance of the IDS over a period of time $( T _ { N } )$ and determine the number of events $( R _ { N } )$ (i.e., the cumulative proportion of normal transactions to total incoming transactions) over the period $( T _ { N } )$ . Based on the gamma conjugate family, the posterior distribution for the mean arrival rate of normal transactions is also a gamma distribution with shape and scale parameters $R _ { \ N } ^ { \prime \prime } = { R _ { \ N } ^ { \prime } + R _ { N } }$ and $t _ { \phantom { \prime \prime } N } ^ { \prime \prime } = t _ { \phantom { \prime } N } ^ { \prime } + T _ { \phantom { \prime } N }$ The mean and variance of the posterior gamma distribution is $E ( \lambda _ { _ { N } } ^ { \prime \prime } ) = R _ { _ { N } } ^ { \prime \prime } / t _ { _ { N } } ^ { \prime \prime }$ and $\mathrm { V a r } ( \lambda _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \prime \prime } ) = R _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \prime \prime } / ( t _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { \prime \prime } ) ^ { 2 }$ . In Figure 4, Panel A, we present the prior to posterior relationships of the gamma conjugate family for normal transactions.

The Bayesian revision approach (see Figure 4, Panel B) for determining the posterior values for the rate of fraudulent transactions $( \lambda _ { _ { F } } )$ is similar to the method we detailed above for $\lambda _ { { } _ { N } }$ . Therefore, equations for the mean and variances of the prior and posterior gamma distribution of $\lambda _ { \scriptscriptstyle F }$ can be written as $E ( \lambda _ { _ { F } } ^ { \prime } ) = R _ { _ { F } } ^ { \prime } / t _ { _ { F } } ^ { \prime } , \mathrm { V a r } ( \lambda _ { _ { F } } ^ { \prime } ) = R _ { _ { F } } ^ { \prime } / ( t _ { _ { F } } ^ { \prime } ) ^ { 2 }$ $E ( \lambda _ { F } ^ { \prime \prime } ) = R _ { F } ^ { \prime \prime } / t _ { F } ^ { \prime \prime }$ , and $\mathrm { V a r } ( \lambda _ { \mathit { F } } ^ { \prime \prime } ) = R _ { \mathit { F } } ^ { \prime \prime } / ( t _ { \mathit { F } } ^ { \prime \prime } ) ^ { 2 }$ . Once the revised parameters for the arrival rate of normal and fraudulent transactions have been computed, the analyst can use those parameters to estimate the posterior value of $r _ { i }$ given by

$$
r _ {i} ^ {\prime \prime} = \frac {E (\lambda_ {F} ^ {\prime \prime})}{E (\lambda_ {N} ^ {\prime \prime})}.\tag{5}
$$

The revised value of $r _ { i } ^ { \prime \prime }$ obtained from the Bayesian model is more accurate. The value for $r _ { i } ^ { \prime \prime }$ can be used to determine the value $P _ { { _ F } } ^ { i }$ from the ROC relationship when $P _ { D } ^ { i }$ is known. In the successive application of the Bayesian model, the posterior values become the prior values for the next phase if there are no changes to the IDS system.

The Bayesian model for updating the real option input parameters $\lambda _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ^ { i }$ and $\lambda _ { _ { F } } ^ { i }$ is quite versatile. It can be applied at the real option evaluation phase or the postaudit phase as discussed below and illustrated in Figure 4, Panel C. In the real option evaluation phase, the Bayesian model can be applied to revise an analyst’s initial estimates of $\lambda _ { { } _ { N } }$ and $\lambda _ { { } _ { F } } .$ . For example, an analyst can make a subjective assessment (based on experience and judgment) of the means and variances of the rate of normal and fraudulent transactions, $E ( \mathsf { \lambda } _ { N } ^ { \prime } ) , \operatorname { V a r } ( \mathsf { \lambda } _ { N } ^ { \prime } ) , E ( \mathsf { \lambda } _ { F } ^ { \prime } )$ , and $\mathrm { V a r } ( \lambda _ { F } ^ { \prime } )$ . Low variances indicate the analyst perceives that the IT system is in a steady state (stable) with a low risk from potential threats and no sudden spikes in transaction volume expected in the near future. On the other hand, high variances can be an indication of higher risk, which in practice can be observed when there are sudden spikes in transactions as in the case of attacks. If there are a potential threats and subjective initial estimates do not reflect these threats, then the Bayesian model can be used to revise the prior estimates. The resulting posterior estimates are more accurate, and thus should be used in the evaluation phase. For downstream future IDS configurations that have yet to be implemented, the forecast values of $r _ { i }$ should be used because active sampling cannot be done.

In the postaudit phase, the Bayesian model can be used to review the initial forecasts $( r _ { i + 1 } )$ of downstream real options. That is, before undertaking project i + 1, the analyst can observe the actual transaction data resulting from implementing project i and revise the prior estimate (or forecast value) of $r _ { i + 1 }$ . Then, this posterior value $r _ { i + } ^ { \prime \prime }$ can be compared with the original forecast value $r _ { i + 1 }$ from the evaluation of project i. The real options pertaining to project i + 1 and other downstream projects should now be reevaluated to determine the optimal timing as well as whether or not the value of the real options justifies undertaking future investments. If the revised values for the variances of $\lambda _ { { } _ { N } }$ and $\lambda _ { _ { F } }$ are low and the threat of an attack is low, then the Bayesian model demonstrates that the IT system is more secure after undertaking investment project i. On the other hand, if the revised value of $r _ { i + 1 } ^ { \prime \prime }$ and variances of $\lambda _ { { } _ { N } }$ and $\lambda _ { { _ F } }$ are larger than the initial forecasts, then the IDS configuration based on project i investment is insufficient to secure the IT system. This is because for a given level of detection (based on the level of investment), a higher $r _ { i + 1 } ^ { \prime \prime }$ gives a higher false positive error and a larger variance indicates higher risks due to threats. Thus, the reevaluated real option suggests the optimal time for undertaking project i + 1. As such, the Bayesian model developed in this paper has implications for both theory and practice. We discuss these implications with a postaudit illustration of the real options model below.

## Modeling Market Uncertainty: A Consolidated Approach

The consolidated approach allows a consideration of the uncertainties of multiple input variables specific to information security such as benefit functions, investigation costs, damage costs, operating costs, and detection costs, all of which drive volatility into a single source of market uncertainty—project volatility. The estimates of uncertainties pertaining to specific input variables could be obtained from historic data or subjective estimates. Appendix A describes the consolidated approach for estimating a security project’s volatility parameter.

## The Integrated Model

We consider an American-type sequential real options model where the initial investment $( I _ { \scriptscriptstyle 1 } )$ in information security can be undertaken at any time during the first year. If the first security investment is undertaken, the manager can undertake the second information security investment $( I _ { _ 2 } )$ at any time between years 1 and 3. If both the first and second information security investments are undertaken, the manager can invest in a third platform information security investment $( I _ { 3 } )$ anytime between years 3 and 5. The call options have the following option lives: $C _ { 3 }$ is a two-period call (exercisable between years 3 and 5), $C _ { 2 }$ is a two-period call (exercisable between years 1 and 3), and $C _ { 1 }$ is a one-period call (exercisable between years 0 and 1). Hence, all of the three call options are the American type. The real call $( C _ { 3 } )$ of the furthest downstream option is a standard American call option, but $C _ { \gamma }$ and $C _ { _ 1 }$ are more complex American-type real options because they are nested within one another. In valuing such sequential options, the order of real options from an exercise point is opposite to its economic priority because the furthest downstream option will be valued first [4, 21]. We price the American-type sequential real option using the binomial approach shown in $\mathsf { A p - }$ pendix B. The value of the information security investments modeled as American-type sequential real options is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} C _ {1} = \max \bigg [ V _ {1} - I _ {1} e ^ {- k \Delta t} + C _ {2} e ^ {- r _ {f} \Delta t}, e ^ {- r _ {f} \Delta t} \left(p _ {1} \max \Big [ C _ {2} + V _ {1} ^ {+} - I _ {1}, 0 \right] \\ \qquad + (1 - p _ {1}) \max \Big [ C _ {2} + V _ {1} ^ {-} - I _ {1}, 0 \Big ] \bigg) \bigg ]. \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

## Discussion

In th is paper, we inves tig ate an org anization’s information security investment decisions using a real options model in conjunction with Bayesian learning and postauditing. In doing so, we develop an integrated real options model for evaluating information security projects and demonstrate how postauditing benefits the management of IT security investments.

## Managerial Implications

The real options model combined with Bayesian learning for valuation and postauditing developed in this paper has implications for practice. The sequential real options model incorporates learning into valuation through the Bayesian revision of configuration parameters. The benefits of using revised parameter estimates has several managerial implications, including the reduction of upward bias in estimates and the incorporation of up-to-date information. In addition, the revision of subjective priors in the evaluation phase reduces the possibility of a biased forecast by project sponsors to justify security investments and, as a result, reduces management fears regarding the eagerness of project sponsors to get their project funded.

The Bayesian postaudit also has several practical implications. The postaudit approach helps decision makers (CEOs, IT managers) understand and control the IT security investment decision-making process. First, it provides a technical control mechanism where one is able to compare the forecasts with actual IDS performance after implementation. Second, since active learning is built into the revision model, firms can gain insights useful for improving future investment decisions. The revised values for input parameters can be compared with forecasts and then used in the model to determine whether and when to undertake the next investment. Specifically, postaudits assist in the selection of future investments and are suitable for sequential IT security investments. Finally, the postaudit introduces accountability into information security spending that is useful for firms allocating scarce resources to competing investments. The Bayesian learning and postaudit approach is general and can be used in other types of investment problems (such as R&D, IT platforms, and network expansion) that allow for investing in stages and sampling using problem-specific models. One can use learning and postauditing in network expansions by trying out one or two new locations in order to get objective sample information and then, after testing demand, cost, and other parameters, deciding whether or not to expand.

The application of the integrated real options–Bayesian model in an IT security context has several practical benefits. First, it demonstrates how IT managers can integrate security-specific features properly in valuation. Second, it demonstrates how to conduct a postaudit of security investments. Third, it shows how firms can design an IT security investment decision process that looks not only at valuation aspects but also at technical and financial controls.

## Limitations and Avenues for Future Research

This paper has several limitations that provide future avenues for research. First, this paper focuses on e-mail security investment, a situation in which decision-theoretic approaches are appropriate. Future research could investigate a real options model for justifying security spending to prevent hacking by combining the current model with Cavusoglu et al.’s [8] game theory model of optimal IDS configuration. One approach could be based on the standard real options framework where the exercise decision does not directly depend on the action of the hacker. Another approach could focus on the hacker and the firm’s strategic interaction in a real options setting. Here, although a standard real options model suggests a wait-and-see approach, the fear of preventive security hacking could force firms to undertake investments early.

Second, this paper focuses on firm-specific security investments where technical dependence is more prevalent. In other instances, such as when a security software development firm markets versions of security software, the projects may exhibit market dependence in addition to technical dependencies. Other possible extensions to this research include considering resource constraints (effort and monetary) in investment decisions and developing cost functions that describe the dependence between effort and configuration parameters in order to obtain more realistic optimal configuration parameters.

Third, the difficulty in obtaining the prior estimates of mean and standard deviation may be a limitation and, in many situations, only subjective estimates may be available. Fitting exact distributions to the sample data is often difficult in practice and some subjective assessment may be needed [5]. Also, the continuous application of the Bayesian model to revise estimates when major threats occur may not be feasible for many firms due to resource and time restrictions. The advantage of the Bayesian model, however, is that it incorporates available information into the decision-making process in a systematic manner. While the purpose of the postaudit is to improve and control the decision-making process, such audits may be misinterpreted or may fail to attain the intended objective. In addition, organizational political factors and resource constraints often influence security investment decisions and have not been considered in this model. Finally, as in the case of much of the IDS literature, the consideration of a single ROC curve aggregates the different types of filtering processes. To examine multiple types of alarms, multiple ROC curves would need to be considered.

## Conclusions

In th is paper, we develop and illus trate a real options model for information security investments using Bayesian statistics to update input parameters. The model incorporates active learning and can be used with valuation and postauditing. We contribute to the real options literature by modeling learning options and the postaudit of sequential real options, processes that are relevant to a variety of investment problems. The Bayesian update of intrusion detection parameters using Poisson sampling is an important contribution to the information security literature in intrusion configuration and detection. Our integration of security-specific features in ROA through Bayesian learning and postauditing in a real-life example is a novel contribution to the field and demonstrates a systematic approach to IT security investment decision making and control in firms.

## Appendix A: Consolidated Approach for Estimating a Security Project’s Volatility Parameter

Benaroch and Kauffman [3] provide an excellent exposition of different volatility estimation schemes in the context of general IT projects. We adopt the Monte Carlo simulation-based consolidated approach of volatility estimation used by Copeland and Antikarov [10] and Herath and Park [21]. Consider a security investment opportunity with an initial outlay (I) and a series of expected cash flows $f _ { t } ( s _ { i } )$ for $t = 1 , . . . , T .$ The expected cash flow in each period is a single estimate consisting of the previously described security-related input parameters (see the fourth section). We let k represent the required rate of return for the investment opportunity—that is, the risk-adjusted discount rate estimated from the capital asset pricing model. The volatility of a security project can be obtained from the standard deviation of the one-period logarithmic return distribution given by

$$
\hat {k} = \ln \left(\frac {P W _ {1}}{P W _ {0}}\right),\tag{A1}
$$

where $P W _ { n }$ (for $n = 0 , 1 )$ is the present value of future cash flows (excluding information security investment costs) from period 0 to n. For a project with equal annual cash flows with a life of T years, the expressions for $P W _ { 0 }$ and $P W _ { 1 }$ are given as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} P W _ {0} = (1 - \tau) \left[ C _ {B} \left(1 - e ^ {- \alpha s _ {i}}\right) - \left\{c P _ {D} ^ {i} \psi + d \left(1 - P _ {D} ^ {i}\right) \psi + c _ {1} P _ {F} ^ {i} (1 - \psi) \right\} \right. \\ \left. + \left\{a _ {0 i} + a _ {1 i} s _ {i} + \frac {1}{2} a _ {2 i} s _ {i} ^ {2} \right\} \right] (P / A, k \%, T) \end{array}\tag{A2}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} P W _ {1} = (1 - \tau) \Big [ C _ {B} \left(1 - e ^ {- \alpha s _ {i}}\right) - \Big \{c P _ {D} ^ {i} \psi + d \left(1 - P _ {D} ^ {i}\right) \psi + c _ {1} P _ {F} ^ {i} (1 - \psi) \Big \} \\ \qquad + \Bigg \{a _ {0 i} + a _ {1 i} s _ {i} + \frac {1}{2} a _ {2 i} s _ {i} ^ {2} \Bigg \} \Bigg ] (1 + P / A, k \%, T - 1). \end{array}\tag{A3}
$$

The usual assumption of option pricing is that an underlying asset follows a geometric Brownian motion (GBM), thus independent random variates have to be generated to calculate $P W _ { 1 }$ and $P W _ { 0 } .$
