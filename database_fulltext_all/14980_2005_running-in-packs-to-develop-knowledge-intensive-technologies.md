# RUNNING IN PACKS TO DEVELOP KNOWLEDGE-INTENSIVE TECHNOLOGIES $^{1}$

## Abstract

Advances in information technologies and the growth of a knowledge-based service economy are transforming the basis of technological innovation and corporate competition. This transformation requires taking a broader, institutional and political view of information technology and knowledge management. To succeed, firms are advised to focus on building their distinctive competencies, outsource the rest, and become nodes in value chain networks. This shifts the level of competition from between individual firms to between networks of firms. In these networks, individual firms or entrepreneurs seldom have the resources, power, or legitimacy to produce change alone. As a result, “running in packs” is often more successful than “going it alone” to develop and commercialize knowledge-intensive technologies. Many different actors in public and private sectors make important contributions. These actors do not play impartial roles; instead, they are active participants who become embroiled in diverse, partisan, and embedded issues of innovation development. In this setting, success requires not only technical and rational competence, but also political savvy to understand and mobilize the interests of other players with stakes in an emerging industry.

Keywords: Knowledge management, competence, infrastructure

## Introduction

Once upon a time, a technological innovation represented a new physical artifact or device that a firm manufactured and distributed to customers for proprietary competitive advantage. This image of innovations as being physical devices that individual firms develop and own has been changing dramatically over the past 20 years. It is true for a dwindling proportion of technological innovations in today's economies. The first part of this paper discusses the growth of a global knowledge-based service economy, where technical innovations often consist of new design knowledge that is developed and owned by many organizations worldwide. The knowledge and information necessary for developing and commercializing an innovation transcends the borders of individual firms, industries, and countries. In the second part of the paper, we discuss two key implications of this transformation for managing knowledge-based innovations. First, because an individual firm seldom commands the resources, power, or legitimacy to go it alone; it must “run in packs” with other cooperating and competing firms. Second, in this context, managing knowledge and innovation not only entails sound technical and rational skills, it also requires political savvy.

## Knowledge-Intensive Innovations and Services

Knowledge-intensive services $^{2}$ have become the dominant form of work in the industrialized world. Quinn et al. (1997) report that three-fourths of all economic activity is based on managing intellectual activities and the interface to their service outputs. The value of most products and services depends primarily on the development of knowledge-based intangibles, like technological know-how, product design, marketing, understanding of customers, personal creativity, and innovation. Generating these intangible technologies depends more on managing intellectual resources and information than on directing the physical actions of people or the deployment of tangible assets.

In these economies, the basis for sustained competitive advantage is not found in the physical products or service procedures, but in advancements in the design knowledge that is used to create these products or services. This competence often develops in several locations simultaneously and cuts across the boundaries of firms, industries, and nations. As a result, knowledge-intensive technologies have no nationality (Murtha et al. 2001). Such global distribution of knowledge and work has been made possible by (1) information technologies that enable division, distribution, and coordination of work across national boundaries, (2) the lowering of institutional trade barriers across countries adopting policies of economic liberalization, and (3) global diffusion of competencies to do the same work.

In this knowledge-intensive society, technology itself is gaining a new meaning. Initially viewed as a physical artifact, the definition of technology is broadening to include the body of knowledge that is embodied in the design or architecture of the artifact (Layton 1986). This knowledge is socially constructed, meaning that it is an evolving product of human interactions and understandings at the time and place the artifact was created (Bijker et al. 1987). In this sense, a technical artifact represents a time capsule of the body of knowledge, institutions, and social constructions instantiated in the artifact at the time of its construction.

Viewing technology as design knowledge focuses attention not only on outcomes, but also on how this design knowledge emerges. Understanding the processes or practices in which design knowledge develops has gained increasing research attention. Technology scholars have begun to focus on the practice knowledge of innovating—what people do and know in the actual work of innovating (Barley and Orr 1997; Dougherty et al. 2002; Garud 1997). Orr's (1990) ethnographic studies of service technicians illustrates how formal views of work embodied in manuals and training programs may conceal what is actually needed to achieve the outcome because they ignore the social interactions and situated context in which people engage while doing their work. Technical work is a collective effort, and the people who do it understand themselves to be a part of a community of practitioners that creates and shares knowledge.

Given these trends, how can a firm gain sustained competitive advantage from intangible knowledge-based products? Williamson (1994) and Teece (1987) point out that many of the products and services from knowledge-intensive innovations have weak appropriability regimes, where development costs are idiosyncratic and often exceed proprietary benefits, and where intellectual property rights are difficult to protect and not durable, especially given the fast pace of knowledge creation and learning intensity. Transactions cost economics recommends that these kinds of knowledge-intensive technologies should be developed within a firm's hierarchy. But since the knowledge and complementary assets that are needed to develop knowledge-intensive technologies are seldom contained within a single organization, vertical integration is not a feasible option.

Other strategy scholars (e.g., Bartlett and Ghoshal 1995; Kogut and Zander 1993; Quinn 1992) have been arguing that as work becomes increasingly knowledge intensive and competitive, firms should focus on building their distinctive competence, outsource the rest to “best in world” suppliers, and establish a leadership niche in the value chain network. As firms follow this advice, they become nodes in the network of a technological innovation system. This shifts the level of competition from between individual firms to between networks of firms.

Quinn et al. (1997, pp. 228-229) point out that this competition takes place not among individual firms, but rather among “spider’s webs” of organizations, which are competing work design networks consisting of firms that have specialized knowledge and are geographically dispersed yet need to interact often and in depth. Similarly, Porter links regional prosperity to the development of economic clusters, which are “geographically proximate groups of interconnected companies and associated institutions in a particular field linked by commonalities and complementarities” (2001, p. 26). These clusters enhance competitiveness by providing firms with ready access to specialized suppliers, facilitating communication so that new needs and new processes come to light, giving firms the support and flexibility they need to take risks, and fostering the creation of startups and spin-offs. Electronic technologies often enable this form of agglomeration economics among networks of firms that are geographically dispersed throughout the world (Sambamurthy et al. 2003; Shaver and Flyer 2000). Researchers such as Gulati et al. (2000) and Subramani and Venkatraman (2003) note that strategic networks provide access to information, resources, markets, and technologies; allow firms to share risks and outsource value-chain stages; and confer advantages from learning and scale and scope economies.

Many studies have found a systemic nature to technological advances, as demonstrated by Hughes (1983) of electrical power; Ruttan and Hayami (1984) of agricultural innovations; Kuhn (1962/1982) and Hull (1988) of scientific advances; Tushman and Anderson (1986) of technological revolutions in cement, minicomputers, and glass; Powell (1998) and Zucker and Darby (1997) of biotechnology, and Van de Ven and Garud (1993) of biomedical devices. Many complementary innovations in technical and institutional arrangements are usually required before a particular technology is suitable for commercial application (Binswanger and Ruttan 1978). Developments in other complementary technologies and institutions often explain bottlenecks and breakthroughs in the development of a given technology. Thus, as Rosenberg (1983, p. 49) says,

What is really involved is a process of cumulative accretion of useful knowledge to which many people from business, education, research, and government make essential contributions, even though the prizes and recognition are usually accorded to an actor who happens to have been on the stage at a critical moment.

The specific characteristics of an industrial infrastructure vary according to the technology on which it is based. Figure 1 illustrates a framework for examining the infrastructure of a technological innovation (Van de Ven and Garud 1989, 1993). The framework takes an augmented view of an industry as consisting not only of the set of firms producing similar or substitute products (Porter 1980), but also many other public and private sector actors who perform critical roles in developing and commercializing a new technology. This industrial infrastructure includes four subsystems or sectors.

\- Institutional arrangements. The governmental agencies, professional trade associations, and scientific/technical communities that legitimate, regulate, and standardize a technology.

• Resource endowments, which include advancements in basic scientific and technological knowledge, financing and insurance arrangements, and training of competent professionals.

\- Consumer demand. For new-to-the-world technologies, informed, competent, and responsible consumers do not preexist; the market must be created.

\- Proprietary activities, which transform the available supply of public resources (scientific knowledge and work force competence) into proprietary products and services to meet customer demand.

This framework for examining an industrial infrastructure in terms of these four subsystems developed initially from studies of the development of the cochlear-implant technology by Van de Ven and Garud (1989, 1993), and was extended in studies of technological communities by Garud and Rappa (1994); new business startups by Aldrich and Fiol (1994); the American film industry by Mezias and Kuperman (2000); flat panel display technologies by Murtha et al. (2001); and Java technology standards by Garud et al. (2002). These studies emphasized the need to take an augmented view of an industry if we are to understand how an industrial infrastructure emerges to support and constrain innovation. Powell, for example, argues that the organizational field of an emerging industry is not only multidisciplinary but also multi-institutional. He writes that all the necessary skills and organizational capabilities needed to compete are not readily found under a single firm's roof and that technological process goes hand-in-hand with the evolution of the industry and its supporting institutions (1998, p. 233).

Different components of the industrial infrastructure in Figure 1 are particularly salient in developing and commercializing different kinds of technologies. For example, Nakagawa (2001) states that the three largest impediments to the development of the software industry in Asia are the lack of resource endowments (such as skilled labor for both programming and technical support), consumer demand (such as the creation of a market for computer software applications), and institutional arrangements (such as compliance with international conventions, treaties, and organizations to reduce intellectual property violations and piracy). With regard to trade policy in East Asian economies, Krumm and Kharas )2003, p. 18) state that

Trade policies do not stand alone. Mutually supportive companion policies are also necessary....Technical standards, logistics, service sectors, intellectual property rights, competition policy, and environmental and labor standards have become as important a part of the private sector environment as trade policy itself.

Thus, notwithstanding common folklore of the entrepreneur as a rugged individualist who works alone to develop his or her innovation, Ruttan (2001) reviews numerous studies demonstrating that innovation and entrepreneurship are collective achievements. New technologies and new businesses are seldom developed by a single firm in the vacuum of an institutionalized environment (see Chandler 1990; Jewkes et al. 1958; Nelson 1982). Collective action is necessary to build and sustain an industrial infrastructure that makes it possible for individual firms to succeed and prosper.

The structure of these networks where innovation takes place does not emerge at random, nor are they ecological niches waiting to be filled (Baum and Singh 1994). Instead, they are collective achievements involving numerous actors from public and private sectors who pursue their different partisan interests and roles in constructing the infrastructure (Van de Ven et al. 1999). These people are not just impartial role actors playing out their scripts as detached outside observers. Instead, they are active participants who become embroiled in diverse, partisan, and increasingly embedded ways (Garud et al. 2002; Van de Ven and Garud 1993).

Actors are diverse and distributed in the sense that many different public and private actors play roles in developing the institutional infrastructure that supports and enables technological development. No single actor controls the technology development process. New technologies and related institutions coevolve and reflect the institutional practices and social norms of the cultures in which they are socially constructed.

Actors become embedded in the sense that the knowledge and roles they acquire in the development of a technology are determined and constrained by the actions they undertook in the past. Thus technology development is partially a path-dependent process in which past actions and contexts accumulate into a trajectory of competence and approach with time. Past behaviors and experiences matter; they both enable and constrain subsequent behaviors and opportunities.

However, the trajectory of actions is not completely path dependent because learning can occur as the process unfolds. Actors invent new technologies and institutions and achieve legitimacy by recombining existing skills and knowledge.

Finally, actors are partisan in the sense that they participate from their own frames of references and often have different and conflicting interests. It is well known that the interests of researchers, producers, regulators, and customers engaged in the development of a technology are not alike. Less well understood is that these partisan interests are embedded in different cognitive, cultural, and regulatory institutions (Scott 2001) that prescribe different procedures for the resolution of conflicts among partisan interest groups (Lindblom 1965). For example, Ahlstrom et al. (2004) describe significant differences between East Asian and Western business communities in the resolution of conflicts and establishment of business practices and governance structures.

Several scholars have noted that this process has striking similarities with social movements (Campbell 2002; Dacin et al. 2002; Dowell et al. 2002; Hargrave and Van de Ven 2005; Schoonhoven and Romanelli 2001). For example, Wade and his collaborators make the link between social movement theory and industry emergence in their account of how proponents and opponents of high density television (HDTV) engaged in political processes of gaining cognitive and social-political legitimacy in order to influence the trajectory of HDTV in the United States (Dowell et al. 2002; Swaminathan and Wade 2001).

These political behaviors of entrepreneurs demonstrate that the innovation process is not merely a technical and rational process; it is also a contested and negotiated political process. Technological innovation entails the creation of institutional infrastructure arrangements (such as property rights, standards, regulations, trade policies, legitimating practices, research and development efforts, financing arrangements, consumption patterns, market structure, etc. as illustrated in Figure 1) that help and hinder the development and commercialization of new products, services, and technologies.

## Implications for Managing Innovation

The view sketched here of technological innovation and corporate competition as collective, embedded, and emergent does not deny the important roles of individual entrepreneurs; instead it celebrates collective entrepreneurship in specific national and institutional contexts. It emphasizes the need for individual managers and entrepreneurs to take a broader, institutional and political view of the innovation process than the traditional focus on activities within their firms. Numerous entrepreneurs in the public and private sectors play crucial roles in constructing the institutions needed for developing and commercializing technological innovations. Two key propositions follow from this collective view of innovation dealing with running in packs and developing political savvy. We now discuss these propositions.

## Running in Packs

Our first proposition is that the actors who run in packs will be more successful than those who go it alone.

The “rugged individualist” model of technology and innovation management emphasizes that entrepreneurs act independently and compete to be the first into the market in order to reap monopoly profits from their new product or service. This strategy may be successful when the institutions of property rights to knowledge and technologies are clear and enforceable (e.g., with strong patents). We discussed above that such situations seldom exist for knowledge-intensive and revolutionary technologies because the institutions that govern property rights may not yet exist or need to be constructed while the technology is being commercialized. In these situations, entrepreneurs place themselves into a risky and costly position if they choose to "go it alone." Running in packs means that entrepreneurs coordinate (i.e., simultaneously cooperate and compete, with others as they develop and commercialize their innovation). Running in packs is analogous to bicycle racers who cue their pace to one another and take turns breaking wind resistance until the ending sprint (Van de Ven et al. 1999).

Actors who go it alone to be the first movers of a technical innovation experience the greatest conflicts of interest because they tend to generate the greatest amount of visibility, which limits their abilities to capture significant proprietary advantages. This is because their dominance serves as a model that is imitated by others and diffused throughout the community. Thus, the single actor who chooses to go it alone must bear significant first mover burdens which permit free-riding by others. In return for these burdens, first movers are generally believed to have the greatest degrees of freedom to shape institutional rules, standards, and legitimacy in the directions that benefit them the most (Porter 1985).

However, studies have found that these first-mover benefits do not appear to materialize for technologies with weak, appropriable regimes (i.e., those that are easy to imitate, reverse engineer, or substitute) (Teece 1987). Anderson and Tushman (1990) found that the original breakthroughs in cement, glass, and minicomputers almost never became the dominant design except where strong patent protection existed. As a result, the technological designs of the first movers often do not become the dominant design that ultimately yields the greatest profits. This is because while striking out to be the first to introduce a new technology, the first mover inevitably makes mistakes. And the followers, who are observing the practice of the first mover, can make adjustments in their own technologies. As a result, after the first mover has introduced the product in the market, then the second, third, and fourth movers, who have been carefully following the leader, can often and rapidly introduce a more significant, advanced, and better product or service.

In short, there are strong economic motives for first movers to run in packs, not alone. Running in packs is necessary during highly ambiguous periods of technological development when knowledge and interests change rapidly, the costs of innovation exceed their proprietary benefits, and when the property rights to an innovation are difficult to protect.

The argument for running in packs emphasizes that the interests of entrepreneurial actors with a stake in a technological innovation or commercial development are both intertwined and divergent (Ben-Ner 1993). Actors seek both to maximize their total surplus and their respective shares in the surplus. The total surplus amounts to creating institutions that make it collectively possible to commercialize a new technology or business. This draws actors together and drives them to cooperate because no one actor has sufficient resources, competence, or legitimacy to do it alone. The goal of maximizing individual shares propels actors to compete with each other to reap monopoly profits or political capital that derive from introducing a dominant technology. However, enlightened entrepreneurs realize that the total expected value from reaping monopoly shares of an orphan technology are much lower than they are from gaining relatively small shares of a larger and growing new industry. This perhaps is why population ecology studies have found that having more competitors in a new organizational niche increases the survival probability of its members until a threshold level is reached where resource scarcity limits the growth of all members of a population (Hannan and Freeman 1989). Gaining legitimacy is a key problem in the emergence of a new technology, and the growth of a critical mass of actors is often a prerequisite for legitimacy.

## Political Savvy

Our second proposition is that actors with political savvy—an ability to recognize the interests of key actors and enroll them to one's viewpoint—will be more successful in effecting institutional change and realizing their goals than actors without political savvy.

We have argued that many new service and information-based technologies do not provide sufficient proprietary benefits for sustainable competitive advantage to individual firms; instead, they provide collective benefits for cooperative advantage. Successfully commercializing these new products and services requires resources that are beyond the capabilities of any one firm; they require collective action to build an infrastructure that reduces the time, costs, and risks for each participating member. The process of industry emergence is far more political than has been recognized in the literature. Successful development and commercialization of a new technology requires political savvy based on an understanding of the interests of other players and how their roles are influenced by the specific national institutional context (Hargrave and Van de Ven 2005; Spencer et al. 2005).

Braithwaite and Drahos (2000) describe processes of international business regulations as “contests of principles” and “contests of actors” in which weak actors can gain power by enrolling more powerful actors to their viewpoints. This view is reminiscent of the Lipsky (1980) model of protest as a political resource for influencing policy makers through the media who cover and report on protests and riots in the streets. Garud et al. describe technology development in terms of mobilization and counter-mobilization and as a “battle fought in political and cognitive realms” (2002, p. 210). Politically savvy entrepreneurs recognize that their interests are both intertwined with and diverge from those of others in the inter-organizational field. Therefore, they simultaneously cooperate and compete with these other organizations.

Information technology scholars and practitioners can gain an enriched sense of the political nature of change by viewing their technical innovations as social movements (Van de Ven and Hargrave 2003). While they have discovered that technology entrepreneurs must not only develop new technologies but also engage in framing contests to mobilize resources in the context of different political environments, they have not paid much attention to contests over the framing of public discourses and understandings of new technologies. Social movement scholars (Campbell 2002; McAdam et al. 1996) and IS researchers studying the improvisational processes surrounding information technologies (Orlikowski 1996, 2000; Sabherwal and Newman 2003) provide insightful descriptions of the dialectical struggles that take place to shape interpretations and understandings of social and technical issues.

Because the fields in which technical change takes place are pluralistic, agents of change must be politically savvy to accomplish their goals (Hargrave and Van de Ven 2005). This proposition applies not only in the technology arena, where it suggests that technical competence is necessary but not sufficient, but also in the normal conduct of business in unique country settings and contexts. Actors who create resonant frames and who are schooled in the tactics of local social and political cultures are more likely to achieve success than those who are not. Indeed, a political view of technological change leads to an extension of the old adage that “the world is run by those who show up—and it usually favors those who are involved and who are politically savvy.”

IT scholars have tended to emphasize the technical and rational competence of entrepreneurs and to ignore their social-political skills. While technical competence is necessary, it may not be sufficient to mobilize technological change; also needed is political savvy. Stryker (2000) defines politics as follows:

Politics involves individual, group, and organizational actors mobilizing material and symbolic resources to influence authoritative decision making in accord with their perceived interests and values, some of which necessarily conflict with the perceived interests and values of other individuals, groups and organizations. Politics also involves rules of the game [i.e., institutions] that establish authority figures and influence processes. The constitutive rules of any system, whether it is a global economy, a national polity, or a single complex organization, create positions allocating authoritative decision making to incumbents of some positions and duties to obey to incumbents of others (see, e.g., Weber, 1978). Constitutive rules, for example, of property ownership, majority vote, legal rule, professional expertise, also define the source of authority and govern the distribution and aggregation of key influence-relevant resources. Thus, constitutive rules shape actor expectations about whose influence attempts and which influence strategies are more or less likely to be effective (pp. 170-180).

Stryker (2002) elaborates the elements of a more political approach to institutional research. She integrates the insights of the new institutionalists with the political sensibilities of the old institutionalism. Stryker describes 10 principles for undertaking a political analysis of organizations and institutions. They include attention to multiple levels of social organization (micro/macro theory); a social construction approach to describing causal relations among levels of social structure and institutional spheres; specification of the lines of conflict and identification of relevant interests, values, and cognitive schema; attention to the cultural framing of problems, solutions, and institutional models; and explicit conceptualization of competing institutional models. Stryker recommends that scholars address questions such as: Where did competing institutional models come from? Who constructed them? Who carried them? Why are some more persuasive than others?

This political approach that Stryker advocates may just be emerging. Scholars have just started to view social structure as “institutionally and politically contested” and technical change as mobilized and driven by politically savvy institutional entrepreneurs (Lounsbury and Ventresca 2002). A fruitful next step for management scholars would be to return to and extend classic works such as those of Lindblom (1965), Alinsky (1971), and Fligstein (1997), who described the political strategies and tactics that purposive actors employ to pursue their interests in the face of opposing forces.

## Conclusion

The argument presented in this paper is that managing technological innovation in an increasingly knowledge-intensive service economy requires taking a broader, institutional and political view of information technology and knowledge management. Technological innovation is fundamentally a collective action process of building an infrastructure that reduces the time, costs, and risks for each participating member. Knowledge-intensive technologies seldom provide sufficient proprietary benefits for sustainable competitive advantage to individual organizations; instead, they provide collective benefits for cooperative advantage. Developing and commercializing these new products and services require resources that are beyond the capabilities of any one firm.

To compete successfully, strategy scholars advise individual firms to build a distinctive “world-class” competence in a particular niche of a value chain network where they can establish a leadership position and outsource the rest of its functions and activities to other firms that are the most competent in these outsourced activities. Building a distinctive competence requires enduring investments in a focused area over an extended period of time. This competence becomes embedded in the specific contexts and situations in which it develops. As a result, it is difficult to imitate and transfer and thereby assumes a proprietary value that may yield sustained competitive advantages for a firm.

As firms focus on building their distinctive competencies and outsourcing the rest, they become nodes in value chain networks. This shifts the level of competition from between individual firms to between networks of firms. No single entrepreneur has the resources, power, or legitimacy to produce change alone. As a result, running in packs is often more successful than going it alone to develop knowledge-intensive technologies. Developments in information technologies such as the convergence of computing and communications technologies have significantly enhanced the ability of firms to collaborate and act in concert in value chain networks that are not limited by the constraints of physical proximity and geography. In such a context, we are increasingly likely to witness firms located across the globe that are united by common interests running in packs and intensifying the pace of development of knowledge-intensive technologies.

Many different actors in public and private sectors make important contributions to developing the industry infrastructure by recombining inherited practices, technologies, and institutions to address their own unique and partisan interests. Because the interorganizational field in which the collective action takes place is pluralistic, success requires not only technical and rational competence but also political savvy to understand and mobilize the interests of other players with stakes in an emerging industry. Technical savvy is necessary, but not sufficient for success; also needed is political savvy.

## About the Author

Andrew H. Van de Ven is Vernon H. Heath Professor of Organizational Innovation and Change in the Carlson School of Management of the University of Minnesota. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Wisconsin at Madison in 1972, and was Professor of Management at the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania before his present appointment. He served as 2000-2001 President of the Academy of Management.
