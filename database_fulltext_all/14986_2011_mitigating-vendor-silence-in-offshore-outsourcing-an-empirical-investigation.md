Abs tr ac t: The tendency to remain silent about project-related issues can contribute to suboptimal project performance or project failure. Prior research in offshore outsourcing suggests that client managers should play a critical role to induce offshore vendors’ employees not only to report project problems in a timely fashion but also to brainstorm and contribute ideas to a project. Also, the extant research on cross-cultural teams has emphasized the importance of cultural adaptation in the smooth functioning of these teams, but the role of cultural adaptation in silence mitigation has been largely underdeveloped in the literature. In this research, we bring these concepts of vendor silence and cultural adaptation in cross-cultural teams together and develop a process framework that illustrates how vendor silence may be mitigated in offshore outsourcing through various silence mitigation mechanisms. We then develop three propositions for organizational action toward mitigating vendor silence, which highlight the mediating role of cultural adaptation.

Or ganizations ar e attr ac ted to offs hor e outs ourc ing because of the large-scale availability of highly talented and skilled workers at reduced wages, along with increased business opportunities and the pressures of globalization [22], and also as a way to enhance their international competitiveness [18], but these organizations often find themselves facing challenges unique to offshore outsourcing. Often, the anticipated cost savings are not achieved due to the hidden costs of unforeseen challenges, such as maturity/capability differences [74]; inadequate understanding of offshore information technology (IT) labor markets [10]; underestimation of geopolitical, cultural, and temporal differences [12]; project management issues [79]; restructuring of development and project management processes to fit with vendor needs; the need for business domain knowledge transfer [39]; and infrastructure and security issues [12]. Lack of understanding of the challenges specific to offshore environments can severely limit the ability of client managers (CMs) to effectively monitor progress in offshored projects [34]. When software projects are outsourced, in addition to project management issues like those faced when projects are managed internally, CMs are likely to face new issues that arise because of geographical, cultural, temporal, and organizational differences [39] that require different approaches. One issue that can lead to project failure includes the tendency to remain silent about project problems [29, 30] as well as about project improvement ideas [39, 50, 77].

Our research differs from the prior research on several dimensions. We focus on offshore client–vendor relationships, specifically between a U.S. client and its Indian vendors, to examine the issue of vendor silence. Prior research [29, 30] has examined the tendency to remain silent and an unwillingness to report bad news in intracultural settings—that is, within a single country [29]. For example, Keil et al. [29] compare this tendency for U.S. and Korean project managers in an intracultural setting. However, most offshore outsourcing projects involve intercultural settings. Prior research on the tendency to remain silent has focused extensively on several Asian countries (e.g., Korea [29]), but not on India. When it comes to delivering offshore IT services, India continues to maintain a strong lead over China and other Asian countries [17]. There has been an increased interest in examining project management issues involving offshore Indian IT vendors (see, e.g., [12, 50, 62, 74]).

We examine vendor silence in the projects that were not runaway projects (i.e., any project that grossly exceeds budget and time targets but has still failed to produce an acceptable deliverable [41]), unlike prior research, which examined this tendency in the context of runaway projects [29, 30]. We argue that it is desirable to uncover project problems as early as possible so that the effect of those problems can be contained easily. Lack of communication about negative news and discovery of project problems in the later stages of projects can result in negative impressions about the quality of offshore IT service delivery. Even though the projects in our case study were not runaway projects, and the severity of bad news about the project status and problems was lower, individuals in the vendor organization were reluctant to report problems. In light of these consequences and the increased reliance on offshore IT vendors, it is crucial to understand how CMs can intervene to mitigage this tendency. Silence mitigation strategies may facilitate the detection of trouble early on and allow managers to make necessary adjustments to the project.

In this research, we take a comprehensive approach not only to identify the critical elements that promote vendor silence but also to examine the process of mitigating the tendency to remain silent in offshore outsourcing. Prior research in information systems (IS) on the tendency to remain silent has focused on identifying the factors that influence individuals to remain silent about project problems. The critical role played by cultural adaptation in mitigating vendor silence has remained largely unexplored. We draw from the literature on cultural adaptation to explain the dynamics behind how different silence mitigation mechanisms alleviate concerns of vendor silence.

To address the challenges discussed above, we focus on the following research question:

RQ: How do CMs mitigate vendor silence in offshore outsourcing and what are the critical elements that shape the process of mitigating vendor silence?

Because the tendency to remain silent is not unique to offshore outsourcing, some of our findings may be applicable to domestic outsourcing and in-house projects as well. This paper is organized as follows. We first review literature on offshore outsourcing and the tendency to remain silent. We then present details of research methodology, followed by our findings and the process framework that emerged from our data analysis. In the Discussion section, we draw from the literature on cultural adaptation to explain how social and structural mechanisms lead to silence mitigation and develop our propositions. We then conclude with a discussion of the implications for research and practice.

## Literature Review

## Tendency to Remain Silent

One of the imp or tant c onc er ns in managing IT projects is getting timely project status reports that convey the correct status of projects and discovering problems that may derail the project schedule. The tendency to remain silent has been examined in the IS literature [8, 29, 30, 54, 64, 67] extensively for in-house projects or domestically outsourced projects (see Table 1 for a summary), although primarily through experimental approaches. Whereas experimental approaches provide greater control for extraneous factors, a case study approach allows one to examine a phenomenon in its natural setting, providing detailed insights [75] that otherwise may never be uncovered. Smith and Keil [64] identify factors such as risk perception, time pressure, behavioral immorality, and information asymmetry that affect an individual’s decision to report bad news using scenario-based experiments. These studies focus primarily on examining the influence of various factors on the tendency to remain silent and not on how this tendency may be mitigated.

## Offshore Outsourcing

The outsourcing literature can be categorized in three streams [16]: (1) an economic perspective, which draws upon transaction cost economics and agency theory [19, 69]; (2) a strategic management perspective, which takes a resource-based view of the firm and employs resource-dependency theory [5, 33]; and (3) a social perspective, which is based on the premise that formal contracts are necessary but not sufficient and that shared meanings and norms that go beyond formal contracts are equally crucial for a successful relationship [51]. In this research, we take a social perspective and focus on the role of various contextual elements and their interactions.

As the practice of offshore outsourcing matures, there has been a growing emphasis on empirically examining issues that arise during postcontractual phases. With offshore outsourcing, cultural, geographical, temporal, and organizational differences accentuate [61] already present communication [74], collaboration [39], and coordination [51] issues in the delivery of IT services. For example, U.S. culture differs from Indian culture on three of Hofstede’s cultural dimensions [24]: the United States scores higher on the individualism dimension, lower on power distance, and lower on long-term orientation (LTO) compared to India. Individuals in high-LTO cultures such as India show a greater tendency to keep information within their own faction [24, p. 359], reinforcing their collectivistic values.

IT outsourcing practices in Western cultures (such as the United States and the United Kingdom) and Asian cultures (such as Korea) differ [63] in how contracts are pursued, whether in-house groups compete for the work, the criticality and familiarity of tasks outsourced, and withholding portions of a contract as incentives. Notably, in the Asian context, there was greater emphasis on maximizing reliability and relationships rather than flexibility and control as in the Western context [63]. Using a field survey of Korean organizations, Lee et al. [36] found that mutual trust was positively related to knowledge sharing. Beugre and Acar [2] explored how cultural distance influences behavioral uncertainty and the boundary spanners’ perceptions of justice. Offshored projects were deemed more successful when CMs selected appropriate communication modes for certain technical discussions [74]. Rottman and Lacity [61] observed that strong social networks between U.S. organizations’ internal IT employees and domestic vendors were not easily replaceable and replicable. Also, incompatible project management processes made offshore project management challenging [61].

## Tendency to Remain Silent in Offshore Outsourcing

Ramingwong and Sajeev [59] suggest that significant potential risks of offshore vendors keeping silent may arise from cultural factors. However, they do not examine this premise empirically. Taylor [68] found that vendor managers were unwilling to leapfrog their client counterparts to report bad news directly to senior client management because such sidestepping would betray the trust of their counterparts. Nicholson and Sahay [49] noted that Indian offshore teams were reluctant to openly question their superior, unlike their UK -based counterparts, who valued questioning behavior irrespective of hierarchy. The UK team felt that such practices were crucial for effective problem solving. When they attempted to engage the Indian team in “learning through conflict,” their attempts were unsuccessful. The UK team was unable to negotiate and adapt to their offshore vendor’s cultural preferences, leading to a failed offshoring relationship. Winkler et al. [77] similarly observed that German clients felt that their Indian counterparts always kept to the specifications and rarely contributed their suggestions to the project.

Straddlers or bridgeheads or bridge roles [7, 26, 49, 72], who develop a comprehensive understanding of the client’s business and IT operations to improve communications of requirements with the offshore team [26], can help alleviate a cultural misfit and enable smooth functioning of offshoring relationships. In such cases, mutual cultural adaptation between client and vendor personnel needs to occur [6, 32, 73]. Lack of such cultural adaptation, intentional or not, may lead vendor employees to remain silent. For example, Metiu [45] found that a high-status U.S. development team monopolized opportunities to methodically reinforce their higher status and silence offshore vendor personnel from contributing design improvements.

Brett et al. [3] recommend four intervention strategies to manage multicultural teams. Cultural adaptation strategy requires team members to openly acknowledge cultural differences and find ways to work around or adapt each other’s practices. This strategy is highly relevant in offshore outsourcing [6, 32, 73], as discussed earlier. Structural interventions require creating homogeneous subgroups to subdue cultural variables that reduce sources of conflict. Such subgrouping is less desirable in offshore outsourcing as it would isolate on-site vendor employees from client personnel, reducing communication. Managerial interventions suggest setting clear norms as early as possible, although care must be taken to avoid misinterpretation of these norms because in the early stages of relationships there exists minimal mutual understanding and common knowledge between the parties. Exit strategy suggests removing a member when all other strategies have failed. This may not be an immediate option in many offshore outsourcing relationships for various reasons, including contractual agreements and relationship-specific investments made by the client.

In sum, to enable effective offshore outsourcing relationships, mutual cultural adaptation is necessary for multicultural teams to understand each other’s cultural preferences to develop “negotiated culture” [73] or “palatable project management practices” [7]. We build on this literature to elaborate how cultural adaptation can help mitigate the tendency to remain silent in offshore outsourcing.

## Vendor Silence and Collaboration Effectiveness in Offshore Outsourcing

The prior literature on tendency to remain silent in offshore outsourcing suggests that in several offshore client–vendor relationships, offshore vendor employees were unwilling to report project problems or to share ideas that could potentially add to the value proposition of client–vendor relationships [39, 50, 59, 68, 77]. The unwillingness of offshore vendor personnel to share improvement ideas in offshore outsourcing literature draws attention to Van Dyne et al.’s [71] discussion on organizational silence to also encompass deliberate decisions of individuals to conceal innovative ideas, information, and opinions to improve the workplace. Similarly, the literature on organizational silence [25, 47, 48, 57, 58] encourages speaking up about (1) workplace problems or (2) alternative approaches to address workplace issues. These two forms of speaking up represent different degrees of adjustments that can inform the concept of vendor silence in offshore outsourcing. In our context, the first form suggests mitigating vendor silence by having vendors speak up about project problems in a timely fashion. The latter form suggests mitigating vendor silence more comprehensively, encouraging vendors to share and jointly implement project improvement ideas and not just the project problems.

This conceptualization of mitigating vendor silence more comprehensively has some parallels to the concept of collaboration effectiveness [37, 39]. Effective collaboration involves expression, acceptance, and systematic integration of ideas in a mutually beneficial manner [39]. It is crucial that client managers keep an open mind and value the opinions expressed by vendors and be willing to embrace and engage in such a discourse with their offshore vendor counterparts. The emphasis on expression of ideas (or sharing of ideas), their acceptance (or brainstorming on the shared ideas, which also involves listening), and systematic integration (or joint implementation) to enable effective collaboration captures the essence of mitigating vendor silence comprehensively. As the extent of collaboration increased, offshore participants felt “more as a part of the bank” [39, p. 320]. Decision makers’ (here, CMs’) unwillingness to listen to the relevant information, also known as the deaf effect [28], can be detrimental to effective collaboration (see, e.g., [45]).

## Research Methodology

Given the lac k of a s ys tematic pr oc es fr amewor k in the literature to investigate the process of silence mitigation, we take an exploratory rather than a confirmatory approach [78]. When there is limited understanding of a given phenomenon—in this case, how the tendency to remain silent can be mitigated—the case study methodology is a suitable approach, in which the research process is “tightly linked to data” [11]. The case study methodology allows one to incorporate participants’ viewpoints and interpretations [75] while providing detailed insight into behaviors that otherwise may not be discovered. Also, when “how” or “why” questions are posed, a case study is more suitable [78] to examine the phenomena in a real-life context. Our investigation of how vendor employees’ tendency to remain silent may be mitigated satisfied these criteria.

## Case Study Site

We selected a Fortune 500 multinational service organization, ServCo (a pseudonym), headquartered in the United States. ServCo is a global organization operating for over 40 years with over 200,000 employees and revenues over \$10 billion. It operates in more than 200 countries and has over 4,000 IT employees with separate IS development and testing divisions. The testing division has 3 managing directors, 14 project managers, and 149 full-time employees. Development teams perform the unit test and turn the application over to the testing division to do the system and integration testing. ServCo has three or four major releases of hundreds of new and modified software versions per year. The testing division engages extensively with several offshore IT vendors for its systems testing needs, which include internal as well as external (i.e., customer-facing) systems. A year before we began our data collection, there was a change in the senior management in ServCo’s testing division with one of the primary goals to improve management of offshore IT vendors.

With offshore outsourcing, ServCo was able to get fixed costs as well as a variable work capacity for its testing activities. Over the years, offshore outsourcing at the testing division had grown from “finger outsourcing,” where vendors simply executed the given test cases, to a more strategic offshore outsourcing, where vendors took a lead role in test management and planning. This strategic nature required that client and on-site vendor employees interact on a daily basis. The on-site vendor employees served as a bridge between the on-site client and offshore teams in India and had intensive daily interactions with both teams.

ServCo has been working with three primary vendors (referred to as V1, V2, and V3) based in India. V1 is an IT and business process outsourcing (BPO) company, and has a history of an effective working relationship with ServCo since 1999. V1 has several offices in countries worldwide. The second vendor, V2, is a global provider of IT and BPO solutions. V2 has been working with ServCo since 2001 and has worked hard to establish strong relational ties to ServCo as it represents a significant percentage of V2’s total revenue. The third vendor, V3, is a consulting and IT services company that has been working with ServCo since 2007. Being the newest vendor, V3 is in the early stages of building a relationship with ServCo.

Because our primary objective was to identify how CMs managed their relationships with the vendors to mitigate vendor silence, we needed a case where the client organization was (1) routinely involved in the offshore outsourcing of a major activity within its IS function, (2) willing to share employees’ perceptions regarding client– vendor relationships, and (3) willing to introduce us to the appropriate vendor staff. ServCo satisfied these criteria.

## Data Collection

Qualitative data were collected over a seven-month period (Phase 1 data collection: December 2006–February 2007, 14 interviews; Phase 2 data collection: April–June 2007, 8 interviews). Our data gathering began with unstructured and semistructured interviews of key informants from the client and vendor sides to better understand the issues related to offshore outsourcing of testing projects. Key informants included senior client management, CMs, and offshore vendor personnel (see Table 2 for a summary). Our interviews initially focused on several dimensions of offshore outsourcing: the characteristics of outsourced activities, factors that influence a decision to outsource, and the project management issues faced by CMs. The interviews lasted from 60 to 150 minutes each. In total, we conducted 22 interviews with 15 informants. Some key informants were interviewed twice. All the interviews were conducted in a face-to-face setting with one interviewee and two researchers present. In one case, we had three CMs and a vendor manager present together. We interviewed this vendor manager alone later. We recorded and transcribed these face-to-face interviews. Copious notes were taken during and after the interviews, which were added to the transcribed notes. We also examined software development and testing process documentation used by ServCo and its vendors. We conducted follow-up phone interviews and e-mails as needed to get clarifications and to validate our interpretations.

## Data Analysis

Our data collection and analysis progressed iteratively. As data were being collected, we analyzed it and identified major themes to guide our further interviews, allowing us to refine/broaden and verify/disconfirm the identified themes and patterns [56]. Phase 1 data collection resulted in a write-up of various facets of client–vendor relationship management, such as the client’s offshore strategy, contract development process, vendor management issues, and what testing work should be outsourced. This writeup was shared at a college-wide local research colloquium. At this time, the theme of vendor silence had begun to emerge as a crucial issue in the management of offshore projects; however, our analysis of vendor silence was preliminary. We had little data on how CMs mitigated this tendency, which triggered Phase 2 data collection to focus on the phenomenon of vendor silence and how CMs mitigated it.

Data collected in Phase 2 provided us with more in-depth insights, which we presented at an international research workshop attended by IS researchers and management executives from ServCo, vendor companies, and other major organizations. The relevance of the vendor silence phenomenon was corroborated through our discussions at this workshop. The development of a second case write-up was more elaborate and focused on the topic of vendor silence.

To develop the second case write-up, we analyzed data collected in both phases. We followed the coding techniques of grounded theory method [66]. The process of open coding involved coding qualitative data line-by-line to break it down into separate units of meaning and assigning each unit a conceptual code. These codes were compared with the codes that emerged later as more data were collected, resulting in identification of 58 open codes that covered 682 interview text segments (see Table 3 for coding examples). These codes, which are the basic building blocks in the construction of grounded theory, reflected concerns of project problem escalation, vendor silence, project improvement ideas, project management practices, cultural dynamics, and communication and collaboration with vendors.

We organized these open codes through a process of sorting and clustering [46] to identify major concepts in the data. The concepts were compared and contrasted with each other to determine commonalities and differences between them to facilitate identification of broader categories (see Table 4). We ended data collection when theoretical saturation was reached [52, 66], that is, when no new or relevant data regarding a category (e.g., variations in the properties/dimensions of a category) or linkages among the categories emerged during the coding. We reexamined the qualitative data to ensure that the concepts and categories identified covered as much of the data as possible. Following axial coding procedure [66], we determined the essential paradigmatic properties of each category in terms of whether it embodied context, condition, interactional strategy, or consequences of our central phenomenon of vendor silence. Assigning paradigmatic features to each category allows researchers “to think systematically about the data, . . . to propose linkages and look to the data for validation” [53]. The first two categories reflect the environmental context in which the phenomenon of vendor silence was observed (axial category: context). The third category reflected how CMs realized the presence of vendor silence (axial category: preconditions). The fourth category reflected social and structural mechanisms that CMs developed to mitigate vendor silence (axial category: interactional strategy). The fifth category reflected the consequences of using these mechanisms on the extent of silence mitigation (axial category: consequence). We reexamined the data to see whether it supported our mapping of categories to these paradigmatic features. We then followed Strauss and Corbin’s [66] suggestion of viewing the process framework as a transactional system, also referred to as conditional matrix, to systematically relate contextual categories with process categories.

To establish that the criterion of credibility (akin to internal validity in rationalistic inquiry [20]) has been met, the procedure of triangulation was followed through the use of multiple sources and multiple investigators to provide additional evidence to support the findings [42]. Iterative cross-examination across multiple informants allowed us to ensure that the categories were well developed and that they represented multiple viewpoints (multiple sources). Two researchers conducted the interviews, with one researcher asking the questions and the other listening and taking notes and asking follow-up questions (multiple investigators). The presence of multiple investigators allowed us to systematically recognize, discuss, and debate different interpretations and improve our understanding of the challenges faced by the informants in recognizing and mitigating vendor silence. To further improve credibility, we also employed member checking [20, 70] and peer debriefing [20] by presenting our findings to the members of ServCo (member checking) and other researchers (peer debriefing) at the research colloquium and international workshop. To enhance the internal validity and improve the generalizability of the research [52, 53, 66], we tie our findings to existing literature on offshore outsourcing, organizational silence, cultural adaptation, and business–IT alignment.

## Salient Relationships Between the Context and the Process of Silence Mitigation

## Client Management Philosophy and Realizing the Presence of Vendor Silence (Arrow 1, Figure 1)

The previous senior management at ServCo had a very competitive and frictional relationship with its offshore vendors. Influenced by this frictional relationship (client management philosophy), vendor employees did not report the problems to the CMs until it was too late. Past research has proposed that organizational policies and managerial practices (e.g., tendency to respond negatively to negative feedback) promote the climate of silence in organizations and deter employees from speaking freely about project problems [47]. In the IS literature, Park and Keil [54] and Tan et al. [67] have made similar observations.

The offshore vendors’ tendency to remain silent was further aggravated by various cultural values of vendor employees that included “fear of penalty,” “face-saving behavior,” and “tendency to please.” Ramingwong and Sajeev [59] propose that cultural differences can heighten the risk of vendor silence. Individual-level factors such as race and ethnicity can also reinforce the tendency to remain silent [47]. At ServCo, vendor silence was becoming apparent in many different forms, including hiding project problems, delaying disclosure, or toning down the severity of the project problems. We build on prior research by identifying and providing empirical evidence on the cultural values that affected vendor silence.

As the CMs began to explore vendor silence as a problem, they identified social mechanisms that emphasized relationship-building efforts between client and vendor managers while structural mechanisms stressed programmatic efforts to induce transparency in vendor operations, prompting vendors to speak up about the project issues in a timely manner.

## Organizational Context

## Client Management Philosophy

At ServCo, the client management philosophy changed in the following three areas: vendor management policy, openness of environment, and level of knowledge shared.

Vendor Management Policy. In the past, ServCo’s relationship with its vendors had been frictional in nature, forcing vendors to conceal issues and appear as positive as possible. This frictional relationship furthered the belief among the vendors that if ServCo were to become aware of project problems, it would damage the vendor’s reputation for quality, reflecting negatively on their contracts. The new senior management put an emphasis on building open and collaborative relationships with offshore vendors. For example, the new testing director commented on the importance of relationship management:

It would be bad if we had to manage the vendor based on the contract only.

The CMs clearly saw this difference in the emphasis on building relationships with vendors with the change in senior management. One CM who had worked with both management teams commented on the nature of the relationship under the old management:

We’ve experienced something different. Used to be that way [i.e., frictional and competitive], and part of the reason it was that way was because the relationship was frictional. We were driving them to low cost, reduce numbers, increase productivity, and it was very antagonistic.

In the past, there was also a significant emphasis on having someone off-site to “keep an eye on” how vendors were doing their work. Even though today ServCo sends someone offshore, it is primarily to develop a closer relationship with the off shore vendor team:

Is there a benefit to having someone there at offshore? Two years ago, yes, because of suspicions of what was going on there. Today, there is no need to send someone over because we are closer to it today. . . . Now with current vendors, one of the reasons [we send someone offshore] is to understand [vendors] a little better, make them feel more a part of what we are doing.

The new senior client management also put greater emphasis on openness and knowledge sharing between client and vendor personnel, as is discussed below.

Openness of Environment. The new senior management believed that vendor employees would feel more comfortable in sharing project problems without worry if there was openness in the relationship. The new client management stressed getting vendors input to improve ServCo’s testing practices. ServCo’s contract manager’s statement underscored this openness:

We have asked all the offshore vendors to come together and give us a seminar which tells us how to do work with them better. It is their competency not ours. They could collectively teach us how to do this.

This underscored a more receptive, approachable, and collaborative environment than what had existed in the past. Another CM commented on the openness in the relationship at all levels:

There are VP [vice president]-level, director-level, managerial-level discussions where the vendors are now engaging, but that has been a recent, in the past year, change for us.

The CMs noted that there were constant dialogues between client and vendor managers to identify best practices to perform large-scale systems testing that would benefit both parties, such as introducing automation earlier in the testing life cycle. One CM commented:

Since there has been a change in our upper management, I have seen it more as a “I need you to help, come sit at the table, help brainstorm on ideas.” They are getting more open communication than existed in the past. More of a collaboration today. In the past, we said do this for us or I’ll take the business away and give it to somebody else.

The changed attitudes toward offshore vendors also called for ways to make offshore vendors part of the team by increasing the level of business and testing knowledge shared with them.

Level of Knowledge Shared. As part of the new collaborative philosophy, some CMs argued that sharing project knowledge should go beyond the basic operational knowledge that was sufficient for “finger outsourcing.” With an increasing emphasis on strategic outsourcing of test planning and management tasks, the CMs argued that critical business knowledge of why a project is important, what is at stake, and what risk factors can change the direction of the project must be shared with the vendors to keep them in the loop and to help them understand ServCo’s business priorities. One CM commented about the extent of vendor involvement he would like to see:

I mean having the vendor go to requirements meetings, think about the scope, and how this is going to impact the other systems and what is this going to mean. Is this a big revenue leak?

However, this CM remained doubtful that such increased participation by the vendors would translate into increased reliance on the vendors. Yet there was growing consensus on the role of such knowledge sharing as the foundation to cultivate a collaborative relationship with the vendors to encourage them to share project issues and improvement ideas. One CM argued that this would also help vendors to identify ways to better plan and develop testing strategies that were aligned with ServCo’s dynamic environment:

Vendors need to understand what to do as well as why the work is being done. The relationship between vendor and client works more smoothly if the client [tells] what is happening and the reason for any changes in direction.

This was echoed by a vendor manager who suggested that if clients want to be good at managing relationships, they should trust vendors and share vital project information. He also insinuated that the higher the level of involvement one expects from a vendor, the greater the need for sharing the right kind of knowledge for the relationship to thrive:

If two arms have to work together, the other arm should not just get instructions of what to do but should understand why it is being done. There should not be conflicting instructions. We have meetings here which talk about directions for change [in a project] and we have to go and convey to offshore [team] the same. They [offshore team] have to understand why it is happening.

The notion of knowledge sharing was based on the premise that vendors would feel free to share project problems.

## Individual Context

Characteristics of vendors and CMs also affected the vendor personnel’s tendency to remain silent. Given the limited number of interviews with vendor employees, we relied on CMs’ accounts of what they thought were the factors that influenced vendor employees to remain silent. These accounts were substantiated with vendor managers accounts wherever possible.

## Vendor Manager Characteristics

CMs remarked that the factors that prevented vendor employees from reporting project problems were the result of their cultural values, specifically fear of penalty, facesaving behavior, and tendency to please, as discussed below.

Fear of Penalty. CMs argued that one major factor that intensified vendor employees’ tendency to remain silent was their fear of being punished by vendor and client management. To report project problems, vendor employees must go through their own management hierarchy before reporting it to the CMs. One CM noted that vendor employees would talk about problems but only in the absence of senior vendor managers as long as the communication remained confidential:

In the evening after managers went home, the actual testers would introduce themselves and tell [me] about the problems and the challenges but they did not want me to share that with their management chain because they had to follow the hierarchy. They were not supposed to bring these issues directly to us.

One of the vendor managers admitted that he finds himself in situations where to provide the level of service that clients need, he brought in additional resources without his senior management’s explicit knowledge:

So [ServCo’s] management understands the need to be flexible, but the flexibility I provide is hidden and not visible to my management. If I make it [flexible resources] visible, I’m in trouble.

Some CMs said that if the problems had been brought to a CM’s attention before it was too late to resolve, CMs would have reacted differently. While the CMs were somewhat tolerant of people making a few errors, vendor management seemed less tolerant of such behavior:

You can tell them over and over “enter a defect, enter a defect” and they just hate to enter a defect because someone [from vendor management] is going to get mad. I mean they just know [that someone from vendor management will be upset].

This fear of penalty, whether from the client or vendor management, led to facesaving behavior.

Face-Saving Behavior. At ServCo, many CMs were aware of the cultural differences between the United States and India. One such difference was face-saving behavior, which CMs observed on various occasions even when admitting that weaknesses or mistakes would not have affected the relationship unfavorably. The social embarrassment associated with such admittance, however, prompted vendor employees to remain reluctant to share any project difficulties and problems they faced:

I have noticed that in one-on-one interviews with folks from India, they never answer the question that asks about their weakness. They think about it but can’t answer it. I asked vendors why this was so. They said it is an insult, they don’t show their weaknesses.

The CM noted that this behavioral pattern was the result of their cultural belief system and highlighted their face-saving behavior. Over time, as on-site vendor employees adapted to the U.S. culture, they learned to share their limitations to build an open relationship with the CMs.

Tendency to Please. Another factor that intensified the tension between the client and the vendor was the vendor employees’ tendency to please, or say “yes” even when what they really wanted to say was “no, I can’t do it.” Vendor employees would say “yes” even when they knew that completing the project was going to be difficult if not impossible by the deadline:

Because culture in India is such that they do not like to displease. So I have to teach everyone that “please tell me, let me know, if you really cannot do that please tell me. It is nothing personal. I am not going to get upset.” So it is very, very hard to get them to tell me that they cannot do anything.

One CM from the development division summarized how these cultural values sometimes left vendor employees in a quandary:

The vendor representative gets pressure not only from the client but also from their management team. So the vendor representative has got double pressure. This leads to a situation where the vendor representative does not say no to the client and may not inform their own management team.

This suggests that on-site vendor employees often find themselves in situations to please everyone on the client and vendor management side and end up not reporting problems.

## Client Manager Characteristics

Our analysis identified two characteristics that affected how CMs dealt with vendor silence: project management style and grasp of cultural dynamics.

Project Management Style. One CM suggested that when projects were outsourced domestically, CMs visited the vendor site to “check up on things.” Site visits to domestic vendors were more feasible than to offshore vendors as domestic visits required shorter travel times, less expense, and no immigration paperwork. The frequent visits to domestic vendors allowed CMs to develop comfort with the vendor’s work and build relationships with them. They noted fewer differences in the work ethics and cultural values when outsourcing domestically.

Several CMs faced difficulties in breaking away from this traditional project management mind-set. These CMs were used to having complete transparency in how their project teams worked, including who is working on what, when, and the skill sets of the team members. Due to the deliverables-based contract, the CMs were unable to get involved at this level. One CM expressed frustration with the lack of visibility in the daily operational details of planning and staffing:

I cannot tell them [offshore teams] what to do. And this is project management I know. It is part of the control thing, I am sure. I am just used to that old fashioned control. . . . But I don’t know anything now. I don’t know how to manage otherwise.

Another CM commented on the nature of project information shared by vendors:

I just know the due dates and quality level targets. As for the infrastructure of the [team], we do not have a lot of input.

Many CMs were used to the visual monitoring of their project personnel collocated at their facility. The physical proximity had allowed the managers to discover project issues and status “on the fly” by talking with the individuals on their team. Without this knowledge, these CMs perceived no control of daily project management chores and felt at a loss, as was expressed by one CM:

[I] need to see the work being done to control the work, but we cannot see the offshore work.

Many CMs at ServCo noted the difficulties in applying traditional approaches to monitor the progress of offshored projects. One vendor manager attributed these difficulties to lack of trust and said that experience and relationship building over time can help:

Managers start by not having trust when they do not know what’s happening 10,000 miles away. They don’t know what is happening and they are used to having visual control. I can see that this person comes into work at night. So it takes a lot of time to break away from this.

Some CMs found ways to manage their offshore projects by specifying their statement of work in as much detail as possible and identifying clear points of contact—that is, what, how, and when vendors should communicate with them. This allowed CMs to gain visibility in their offshore projects without getting involved in daily operations. As we discuss in the section “Identifying Mechanisms to Mitigate Vendor Silence,” such adapted project management approaches to bring transparency into vendors’ operations constitute the foundation for designing effective structural mechanisms.

Grasp of Cultural Dynamics. One CM underscored the influence of cultural diversity on communication practices:

Pay attention to cultural diversity. They say “yes” but they may not mean it. [You] must be very specific with them.

Several CMs suggested that the vendors’ cultural values such as face-saving behavior and tendency to please obscured the process of problem escalation and getting accurate project status updates. Many CMs were unable to identify effective approaches to address the impact of these cultural values. For example, one CM compared how the communication with Canadian vendors was the least of their problems:

Canad[ian] cultur[al] difference is not as great. They are clear because they ask questions and understand what we need. They ask a lot of the right questions. . . . [When outsourced to India] it took two to four times to explain what we needed, they asked no questions, and they didn’t get it.

Another CM elaborated on how the use of remote communication technologies in the presence of such cultural dynamics created communication barriers:

I will be doing a video conference with my remote staff, I’ll be sharing information, they will be nodding their heads, smiling, and I’m saying, “Do you understand me?” After the call, someone will later call me and say they do not have a clue; they do not understand.

We noted that CMs who developed a better grasp of cultural dynamics devised strategies that relied on the social aspects to persuade vendor employees to communicate freely. These strategies form the basis for developing social mechanisms discussed below in the “Identifying Mechanisms to Mitigate Vendor Silence” section.

## Process of Mitigating Vendor Silence

Here, we describe the process of how CMs mitigated the vendor silence. The recognition of vendor silence set off the identification of social and structural mechanisms to induce cultural adaptation, which had varying degrees of success in mitigating vendor silence.

## Realizing the Presence of Vendor Silence

To mitigate vendor employees’ tendency to remain silent, it was crucial for the CMs to be aware of this tendency and to understand why vendors kept silent.

Understanding Vendor Silence. The new senior client management had recognized that vendor silence was resulting in unexpected delays in completing projects. Most CMs initially realized the presence of silence about the problems that led to delay in completions only after or close to the project deadline. Over a period of time, CMs noted three distinct patterns in how vendor employees kept silent about these problems: (1) toning down the severity of project problems, (2) delaying disclosure of project problems, and (3) failing to disclose project problems. One CM noted that when faced with project problems, vendors would tone down the severity of problems and inaccurately recount these problems in their status reports:

Vendors must do status reporting, and tracking must be solid. Yet we oftentimes get dilution of their problems and don’t know what is going on until there are fires to put out.

Another CM gave an example of how offshore vendors toned down the severity of problems by fixing the test automation code to work around problems:

They have an incentive to hide any problems with their automation code this way. We want to know it is running and you aren’t helping it along by fixing the problems in the automation code.

Some CMs also observed this tendency in the form of delays in problem escalation. One CM argued that vendor employees would keep trying to solve the problem on their own, in vain:

And escalation really bothers me. They won’t escalate. They just refuse. And you can tell them over and over, “escalate this, escalate this.” Well, [they will say] we can do this, [but] they won’t escalate. They just will not escalate.

One CM noted such delays only much later, typically very close to the deadline:

If we don’t check, we think everything is okay, then we find out at the last minute that it’s not okay.

The CMs found themselves in serious trouble as a result. Such nondisclosures accounted for CMs’ unsuccessful attempts to recognize and mitigate vendor silence. For example, one CM commented:

I do not have any way to plan. I am dead in the water. It is very, very hard to get them to tell me that they cannot do anything.

The second major concern regarding vendor silence was the lack of value-added contributions from the offshore vendors to improve ServCo’s systems testing practices. For example, senior client management wanted offshore vendors to share industry best practices. For example, a CM suggested that “the vendor does have a little more technical expertise than my employee base.” Senior management wanted offshore vendors to brainstorm their viewpoints on developing approaches to the complex testing scenarios that ServCo faced regularly. One such scenario involved testing of multiple interdependent systems concurrently:

When looking at multiple development teams from troubleshooting perspective adds additional complexity. . . . If you are looking at development effort [where] multiple development teams are trying to hit a parallel date and work is not staggered and staged, [then] it becomes very difficult for the testing team especially when they are working remotely.

To enable brainstorming for handling such complex testing scenarios and for this level of collaboration to materialize, senior management needed to recognize what led offshore vendor employees to remain silent about the problems and their improvement ideas.

Recognizing Vendor Concerns. To overcome this tendency, the new senior management worked progressively to identify the sources of vendor employees’ unwillingness to share project problems and improvement ideas. One CM commented that vendor silence was in part due to the frictional vendor management policy:

It [frictional relationship] caused the vendor to go back and think about it and come forward with as positive a look as possible even if it was artificial.

In the presence of a frictional relationship, work ethics and the work performed by vendor employees was always suspected. The attitude was to simply “throw” the work at the vendors. Realizing this, the new management decided to adopt a more open policy in managing offshore vendors and emphasized that CMs should make vendor employees feel welcome and part of the team:

We also want to educate [management and CMs] to think about taking off their hat as a full-time employee versus a vendor employee, but work as if we are partners.

Another source of concern was the communication gaps due to cultural differences. Realizing that some of the communication issues were the result of the vendor’s cultural values, senior management asked the CMs to foster receptive communication conduits with vendor employees. The client contract manager argued that

we must work on and maintain vendor relations and [we] could be more open and candid about our relationships and the work we do with vendors.

As the CMs became more aware of why vendor employees were keeping silent about the project problems and improvement ideas, the CMs sought to alleviate this tendency.

## Identifying Mechanisms to Mitigate Vendor Silence

Offshoring does not always translate into less work for the client organizations, especially in the early stages of a relationship. CMs argued that the work took on a different form of identifying novel approaches to manage offshore projects and vendors.

Developing Social Mechanisms. Social mechanisms focus on the different ways CMs managed their relationships with vendor employees. Some CMs, anticipating the pivotal role played by cultural dynamics, actively addressed the influence of cultural dynamics on project status reporting. One crucial strategy here was to gain the affectbased trust of vendor employees (personalization strategy) so vendor employees do not feel threatened if they report problems or contribute their ideas.

To gain affect-based trust of vendor employees in the early stages of the relationship required the CMs to “go the extra mile” to get to know the vendor employees on a personal level and develop social-emotional bonds. This “courtship behavior” [9] aimed at relationship development by learning more about the other party not only allows one party to understand what drives another party’s behavior but also helps to create shared values and norms. The lack of shared values and norms [1] can aggravate communications among parties. Without such courting, one CM suggested that any efforts to build a relationship in the light of divergent cultural values would be ineffective. He argued that managers should be more involved with their offshore vendors:

With outsourcing, especially offshoring, you must manage the work even more. . . . They [referring to other CMs] are not close enough to them [vendor employees]. They should know their [vendor employees’] names, phone numbers, and families. I call them, and do know their lives. It is a business partnership. Hands-off doesn’t work.

Another CM similarly talked about how he manages his relationship building by developing a closer personal relationship. He argued that his goal was to promote an atmosphere in which vendor employees can communicate freely:

It is part of the job. We bring experienced folks two to three years on-site before moving [them] offshore. [We] get to know their personalities and processes of communication. They learn not to be afraid to tell me the truth. We establish that relationship.

While bringing vendor employees on-site may be deemed a structural mechanism, CMs must take extra measures to develop trust and build a relationship with the vendor. Without on-site vendor employees, it is even harder to develop a relationship with the vendor. Hence, we combined the “presence of on-site vendor employees” and “developing affect-based trust” together to formulate the social mechanism of “developing affect-based trust with the on-site vendor employees.” The presence of an open communication environment, sharing of relevant information and offering explanations for making decisions, and the presence of receptive senior management contributed significantly to the development of affect-based trust [43, 76] between these client and vendor managers.

The intention to foster affect-based trust with vendor employees draws attention to the contrasting approach followed by some CMs at ServCo. Other CMs argued that it was best to stay away from developing any social bonds or closer personal ties with vendors. For example, one CM said:

The best way to deal with vendors is to depersonalize, by using standards and guidelines, then to manage criteria that is set in the contracts.

Another CM concurred and similarly elaborated on how service-level agreements set in the contract are the best way to design clear communication strategies with the vendors:

SLAs [service-level agreements] are a very good, very positive way to keep communication open.”

In sum, depersonalization mechanisms put a heavier emphasis on deploying contractual agreements to mitigate vendor silence. Next, we elaborate on the structural mechanisms that ServCo’s CMs identified to alleviate concerns of vendor silence.

Developing Structural Mechanisms. Many of ServCo’s CMs, learning from their past experiences, adapted the traditional project management style to encourage vendors to report project problems in a timely fashion. The CMs noted that vendors must understand what communication strategies to follow to reduce the ambiguity around problem escalation. These communication strategies improved transparency in vendors’ testing operations and helped to detect problems as soon as they arose (high task transparency). One strategy was providing and strictly enforcing all-inclusive problem escalation and project status reporting guidelines. These guidelines clearly indicated when, how, and whom vendors should contact for problem escalation. One CM suggested that the CMs should proactively look out for delayed problem escalation and should follow up with vendors to unearth any unclear testing requirements:

They say yes to everything we ask of them but they may not be able to do it. We have to be careful to dig some and see if there are unasked questions. We follow up and check on things.

Despite the contractual requirements for status reporting, divergence in the communication practices due to cultural differences hampered CMs’ ability to monitor the project progress.

Another strategy involved prioritizing of the systems testing work. One CM suggested that prioritization of systems testing work made a substantial difference to set the right expectations and induce vendors to escalate the problems when they encountered challenges. In the absence of such prioritization, vendor employees would spend excessive time trying to solve problems on their own (while keeping silent about the problems) without success, thus delaying problem escalation and timely completion of testing work:

[An] important communication point is to help vendors prioritize the work. The client must prioritize the work for vendors so if they hit a roadblock, they do not stop working altogether. We want them to go around it and keep working.

Prioritization of systems testing tasks had another positive effect: it provided vendors a window into how ServCo’s business environment was changing dynamically and to appreciate the complexities of the dependencies of the testing work within ServCo’s systems environment, as was elaborated by one CM as he talked about the testing work at ServCo:

The work is variable, I must reorder test schedules at times, and I must daily redirect the order of the work being done.

Another strategy of frequent communication with vendors was deemed necessary to bring visibility in vendors’ operations and to ensure that projects were on schedule. Communicating frequently with the vendors, though wearisome, helped CMs to uncover problems in time:

So I have to continuously ask, “Are you finished? Done? Done? Are you done? Tell me, really, what is going on?”

## Another CM summed up these strategies:

When dealing with offshore vendors some [CMs] do it well and some don’t. Things that work well include partner with the vendor, give them clear expectation, ask them to give you regular status reports at detail level, and make sure to communicate constantly.

These communication strategies did less to provide an open, collegial, and receptive environment that would have led vendors to speak more candidly about the project improvement ideas, but they did provide vendors a structured and unambiguous way to share project issues with the CMs. In sum, structural mechanisms refer to the programmatic efforts of CMs to induce vendor employees to speak up about project problems. These include (1) comprehensive problem escalation and project status reporting guidelines, (2) prioritizing work and proactively digging for project issues, and (3) communicating frequently with clear expectations of deliverables.

## Consequences of Using Silence Mitigation Mechanisms

Prior to the new management, silence mitigation was virtually unobserved with neither side making efforts to adapt to the needs of the other. In contrast, vendor silence was predominant under the previous management. To determine the effectiveness of the social and structural mechanisms on mitigating vendor silence, we examined the CMs accounts of whether they saw any change in the vendors’ tendency to remain silent and corroborated these with the vendor employees’ accounts wherever possible. Although we did not run any tests to determine statistical significance, we examined whether there were greater occurrences of problem escalation and collaboration.

Partial Silence Mitigation. The CMs who emphasized managing through contracts along with programmatic efforts to induce higher transparency in vendors’ operations noted higher instances of problem escalation alone—that is, they were able to see only partial results in silence mitigation. These CMs noted that after they began communicating frequently and prioritizing the testing work, the problem disclosures were occurring more frequently and in a timely fashion. These CMs rarely mentioned occurrences of any collaborative initiatives with offshore vendors. In sum, these CMs were partially successful in mitigating vendor silence because they did not succeed in establishing greater collaboration that would have led to joint implementation of mutually beneficial novel approaches to improve testing practices.

Comprehensive Silence Mitigation. The CMs who emphasized building affect-based trust, sharing of vital project information, and open communication along with programmatic efforts to increase transparency of vendor operations mentioned greater occurrences of collaborative partnerships with vendors and greater incidences of timely problem escalations. Offshore vendors brainstormed regularly with these CMs about better problem-solving strategies, strategic test planning, and implementation of innovative testing approaches. These CMs used social mechanisms and structural mechanisms in a complementary fashion in their efforts to mitigate vendor silence in a comprehensive manner.

One CM mentioned that it took him a while to get vendors to escalate project problems:

Before, they would not move on to the next work. Today we are getting them to escalate issues but it has taken three years where now they ask for help.

This CM then talked about his close relationship with the vendor manager and how they socialized on a regular basis. The vendor manager similarly acknowledged that in light of the relationship he had with the CM, he was more focused on managing his relationship and less worried about disclosing problems:

You do a mistake once. And I start to steer clear from that day. I try always to keep that in mind. [It is] the relationship [that] we should have.

One CM pointed out that in the recent past there was increased interaction between client and vendor personnel about the best ways to implement testing. The vendor manager highlighted how this interaction enabled open communication, emphasizing the trust CMs put in him. The following quotation from the vendor manager illustrates the highly effective win-win collaboration:

I tell [ServCo] what is going on in the industry. We do testing not just for [ServCo], we do it for many others. We do it in many different ways and in different forms. I inform them of other ways of doing [testing] to contribute to their success. For example, [ServCo] doesn’t have security testing, [but they] need to do it. Or [ServCo] made us do business process testing. It is a new technology and way to design tests for us. Probably we should have gone to [ServCo] and said let’s try it. . . . I care more that [ServCo] trusts me. Not good to hide [project problems], better to tell them.

Another CM mentioned that he and his vendor counterpart formed a shared understanding of what it meant to do “risk-driven testing” and ways to implement it. Both of them concurred that given the virtually infinite ways of doing software testing and lack of industry guidelines for robust testing, risk-driven testing was the best way to utilize limited resources:

If we have five days, what is the best we can do in five days. This is easier to define than what’s the right number of testing and forget the number of days. Because the real problem is I have X days and X dollars, what is the best you can do with these two constraints? Think that way, then [you] will get to the most practical decision.

The above examples provide better insights into how CMs who used social and structural mechanisms succeeded in comprehensively mitigating vendor silence while underscoring the increasing degree of collaboration between ServCo and its offshore vendors.

## Discussion

While our data s ugges t a r elations hip between silence mitigation mechanisms and vendor silence mitigation, the literature on cultural adaptation [3, 6, 32, 45, 73] sheds light on how and why these mechanisms mitigate vendor silence. Specifically, we explore the mediating role of cultural adaptation in mitigating vendor silence in offshore outsourcing through such mechanisms.

Cultural adaptation occurs when client, vendor, or both adapt each other’s ways of doing things to bridge the cultural differences and meet others’ expectations. This process occurs gradually and involves adapting communication, cooperation, trust-building, attitudes, rules, norms, and knowledge-sharing strategies [32]. At ServCo, it was evident through the adjustments in communication and trust-building strategies that eventually led to a higher degree of knowledge sharing between both parties in many instances. Vendor-side cultural adaptation entailed vendor managers to follow the unequivocal communication practices for project status reporting. Such vendor-side cultural adaptation may take place with or without client intervention.

Client-side cultural adaptation at ServCo enabled CMs to gain “in-group” status so that the vendor employees would feel free to communicate with the CMs. In cultures that exhibit higher collectivistic tendencies, such in-group status is an important factor in determining with whom individuals share what kind of information [24, p. 359]. At ServCo, mutual cultural adaptation required both parties to adapt to each other’s ways of communication styles to build mutual trust, develop a shared context, and meet each other’s expectations.

After the change in client management, ServCo’s CMs were encouraged to address concerns of vendor silence. Many CMs believed that the best way to mitigate vendor silence was by addressing the lack of timely problem escalation. Consequently, these CMs adopted structural mechanisms that included work prioritization, comprehensive problem escalation and project status reporting guidelines, and frequent communication with vendors to improve the timely escalation of project problems. Structural mechanisms enacted by the CMs compelled the vendors to adapt their communication style for reporting project status to be as unequivocal as possible. It is possible that the vendors may themselves establish mechanisms to align their communication practices to their clients’ communication preferences [31, 39], based on past experiences. Given the individualistic culture exhibited in the United States, a low-context, direct communication style emphasizing unequivocal communication of a message is preferred [4]. In effect, ServCo’s CMs brought the vendors’ communication style as close to their preferred style of communication as possible, reducing the cultural gap to some extent:

## Proposition 1: Structural mechanisms are likely to improve cultural adaptation.

After the change in senior management, many CMs argued that to achieve the most from the relationship with the offshore vendors, it is necessary not only to have structural mechanisms that make vendor operations more transparent but also to have social mechanisms that would allow vendors to freely communicate about project problems and share their improvement ideas. These CMs encouraged building affect-based trust to form closer social ties with vendor employees that went beyond the typical professional relationship. Development of these social ties is especially vital in the light of prior research [61] that has noted the difficulties client organizations face in replicating the social ties that exist with their domestic vendors to their offshore vendors.

In collectivistic cultures, such as that in India, affect-based trust has been proposed to be more positively related to collaborative behavior [4]. In cultures with high power distance, structural mechanisms such as formal procedures that require employees to speak up will produce weaker results in breaking silence [25]. However, the joint effect of social mechanisms such as a strong participative climate and structural mechanisms such as formal procedures of employee involvement produced increased voicing of ideas [25]. Studies in outsourcing suggest that formal contracts are necessary but not sufficient and that shared meanings, norms, common interests, and mutual trust [36] that go beyond formal contracts but operate within the “spirit of the contract” [32] are equally crucial for knowledge sharing and successful relationships [51]. Gibson and Birkinshaw argue that relationships that emphasize social mechanisms too heavily may create “a ‘country club’ atmosphere in which no work gets done” [14, p. 213]. Ghoshal and Bartlett [13] similarly argue for the need to balance the hard elements (discipline and stretch) and soft elements (trust and support) to accomplish a mutually beneficial relationship. The above discussion suggests balancing social and structural approaches for a successful relationship:

Proposition 2: Social mechanisms are likely to strengthen the relationship between structural mechanisms and cultural adaptation.

Cultural adaptation that alters communication preferences of only one party is likely to partially reduce cultural gaps between two parties in a relationship. At ServCo, a heavy emphasis on structural mechanisms along with a minimal emphasis on affectbased trust to manage the vendor relationships led to an increase in timely problem escalations; however, it did not result in collaborative partnerships, addressing silence mitigation only partially. On the other hand, when the CMs used the social mechanisms of affect-based trust along with structural mechanisms, not only did the offshore vendors adapt to follow the communication practices preferred by the CMs, but the CMs also aligned themselves as close to the collectivistic values of their Indian offshore vendors as possible through the affect-based trust in their relationship. This suggests that mutual cultural adaptation reduced the cultural distance the most. These CMs saw not only increased instances of problem escalation but also greater collaboration with their vendors, achieving comprehensive silence mitigation. Such a relationship is likely to foster increasing commitment from both partners and may result in a higher degree of satisfaction [35]. This leads to our final proposition:

Proposition 3: Cultural adaptation is likely to be positively related to vendor silence mitigation.

In sum, through different degrees of cultural adaptation, social and structural mechanisms lead to different levels of vendor silence mitigation. Figure 2 summarizes our propositions.

The social and structural mechanisms identified here draw similarities to the strategic literature on business–IT alignment. Client–vendor relationships are comprised of alignment challenges that have parallels to those found in the strategic business–IT alignment literature. Business–IT alignment suggests that the strategic goals of an organization are in unison with the supporting IT infrastructure [44]. This alignment has been investigated in the literature along two perspectives—social or relational and structural or intellectual [60]. The structural perspective focuses on congruence between IT and the business on programmatic aspects such as alignment of infrastructures and strategies [21] and missions and plans [23]. The social perspective focuses on the shared understanding and knowledge among the business and its IT partners [60], demographic and experiential similarity, and the reciprocal value and partnership between units [40]. Prior research on alignment implies that both the structural and social dimensions are important to achieve high levels of alignment [40, 60]. Similarly, here we illustrate that both social and structural mechanisms are critical in aligning client and vendors communication styles to mitigate vendor silence comprehensively.

## Conclusion

Our r es earc h is motivated by the pr imar y r es earc h ques tion: “How do CMs mitigate vendor silence in offshore outsourcing and what are the critical elements that shape the process of mitigating vendor silence?” Our findings are particularly important given the increased prevalence of offshoring of IT services from European and North American continents to offshore destinations such as India that exhibit higher power distance and collectivism.

Before highlighting our contributions, we briefly discuss some limitations of our research. Our findings are based on a single case study, limiting the generalizability of our results. We did, however, examine multiple accounts of client and vendor managers to enhance the generalizability of our findings. Because we had limited data on the vendors’ perspective, our findings do not capture a complete vendor perspective on why they choose to remain silent. Finally, we tried to overcome commonly observed issues of response bias, poor recall, and poor articulation by interviewees [78, p. 108] through interviews with multiple informants. Notwithstanding these limitations, we believe our research has significant implications for research and practice.

## Implications for Research

Prior research in offshore outsourcing has highlighted that client managers should play a critical role to induce offshore vendors’ employees to brainstorm and contribute ideas to a project [39, 77], and has emphasized the importance of cultural adaptation [3, 6, 32, 45, 73] in cross-cultural teams. However, the role of cultural adaptation in silence mitigation has been largely underdeveloped in the extant organizational silence literature [25, 47, 48, 57, 58]. Our primary contribution lies in bringing these concepts of vendor silence, silence mitigation mechanisms, and cultural adaptation together and empirically developing a process framework that illustrates how vendor silence may be mitigated in offshore outsourcing through various silence mitigation mechanisms. We theorize about how social and structural mechanisms are linked to different levels of cultural adaptations through the alignment of communication preferences and relationship building between client and vendor managers. Drawing from this, we put forward three propositions that should be empirically validated in future research.

Extant research on silence mitigation offers strategies primarily at the organizational level (e.g., top management philosophy on participative climate [25]). While our research confirms support for receptive organizational policies, silence mitigation mechanisms identified in our research offer clear guidance on how such organizational policies may be instantiated at lower levels of management. Silence mitigation mechanisms identified in our research are primarily based on adapting communication and trust-building strategies at the managerial level. Kern [32] identifies several ways through which adaptation can occur, thus opening up fertile ground for future research into identifying additional silence mitigation mechanisms at various levels of analysis in an interorganizational relationship. Future studies should explore other constructs that may offer alternate explanations for how structural and social mechanisms facilitate silence mitigation.

The comparison between the process followed by CMs to mitigate vendor silence at ServCo and the process for achieving collaboration effectiveness documented by Levina and Vaast [39] overlaps in many areas. For example, structural and social changes that the CMs instituted at ServCo and in Levina and Vaast’s [39] study emphasize sharing of business knowledge with offshore vendors, building of interpersonal trust, and recognizing what matters most to vendors to facilitate collaborative partnership. While the CMs in Levina and Vaast’s [39] study drew on their organizational position, financial resources, onshore personal networks with business–IT stakeholders, and expertise in financial services and systems development to induce offshore vendors to innovate, the CMs at ServCo relied greatly on their expertise in service domain and systems testing and the social mechanisms to persuade offshore vendor employees to contribute improvement ideas. Our data did not indicate that CMs drew upon their organizational position, financial resources, or personal networks with business/IT stakeholders to persuade vendors to innovate. Combining insights from this discussion, the literature on offshore outsourcing [39, 77], and organizational silence [71], we suggest that IS researchers should encompass deliberate decisions of vendor employees to conceal and withhold not only the project problems but also relevant project improvement ideas in their conceptualization of organizational silence. This has implications for identifying elements that impact collaboration effectiveness in cross-cultural settings at different levels of analysis, including organizational, group, and individual levels.

The specific context of offshore outsourcing relationships between a U.S.-based client and Indian vendors in the current research might have influenced our conclusions [38]. Discovery of suitable silence mitigation mechanisms in diverse contexts remains an open field for further inquiries. To build a more generalizable “formal grounded theory” [15, 27] on organizational silence and silence mitigation in general, diverse contexts must be explored. These include (1) outsourcing (domestic, near-shore, offshore under different cultural dynamics), (2) insourcing within global organizations, and (3) projects that are considered to be runaway projects.

## Implications for Practice

The challenges faced by CMs indicate that offshore outsourcing entails a significant investment of time and effort on a CM’s behalf, and CMs should not assume that outsourcing will make their jobs easier or lighter. CMs’ job responsibilities take on a different role of developing and managing relationships with their offshore vendors. Organizations need to ensure that CMs are aware of such redirected efforts and train them to meet these expectations. Also, our findings indicate that to manage offshore projects effectively, CMs need to be well versed in domain knowledge and systems development practices.

Our findings provide guidance to CMs on how to recognize silence and mitigate it. The existence of silence was indicated by (1) toning down the severity of project problems, (2) a delay in the disclosure of project problems, (3) failure to disclose project problems, and (4) the lack of value-added contributions from the offshore vendors to improve ServCo’s systems testing practices. It is important to correctly attribute the employee motives for remaining silent, as misattribution may result in implementing strategies that are ill-targeted and ineffective in mitigating vendor silence [71]. As our propositions suggest, in order to mitigate vendor silence under given circumstances, managers should strive to identify social and structural mechanisms that would best reduce cultural misfit and thus lead to better cultural adaptation.
