# Mobile music business in Japan and Korea: Copyright management institutions as a reverse salient

Akira Takeishi\*, Kyoung-Joo Lee

Institute of Innovation Research, Hitotsubashi University, 2-1 Naka, Kunitachi, Tokyo 186-8603, Japan Available online 9 August 2005

## Abstract

This paper examines the development process of music businesses on mobile Internet in Japan and Korea, two leading countries in the world that have been enjoying rapid growth of mobile Internet businesses. Based on the ‘Large Technological System’ perspective (Hughes, T.P., 1983. Networks of Power: Electrification in Western Society, 1890–1930. John Hopkins University Press, Baltimore; Hughes, T.P., 1989. The evolution of large technological systems. In: Wiebe, E.B., Hughes, T.P., Pinch, T. (Eds.), The Social Construction of Technological Systems. MIT Press, Cambridge, MA, pp. 51–82), this paper sees music copyright management institutions as a ‘reverse salient’ in the large technological system of mobile music businesses. We argue that the development of mobile music business has been and will continue to be dependent on how to revise copyright management institutions in accordance with changes and advancement of technologies and other sub-components in the large technological system.

Keywords: Mobile Internet; Mobile music; Large technological system; Reverse salient; Copyright institution; Japan; Korea

## 1. Objectives

This paper examines the development process of music businesses on mobile Internet in Japan and Korea. Both Japan and Korea have been at the forefront of mobile Internet usage in the world. From among a variety of applications, music-related services have become one of the most important businesses in the mobile commerce markets of both countries. The world’s first mobile Internet has been launched in Japan, and the ringing melody download service has driven the mobile Internet market growth as the largest mobile Internet business in the country. Similarly in Korea, mobile music services, including those to download ringing melodies and ring-back songs, have been the most popular mobile content services. As their mobile communication systems enter the 3rd Generation (3G), new mobile music services have also been introduced, with an expectation that it would lead to a full-blown mobile music business in the near future.

Mobile music businesses in Japan and Korea would therefore provide researchers with precious research materials to probe into causes and conditions for the development of mobile commerce. Especially, understanding common characteristics as well as specific national differences between the two leading countries could provide important analytical cues to project the future development of mobile Internet businesses around the world.

How have mobile music businesses been developed in Japan and Korea? What are the critical factors behind the growth of the businesses? To answer these questions, this paper takes a hint from the ‘Large Technological System’ perspective (Hughes, 1983, 1989). Mobile music businesses develop through dynamic interactions of technologies, firms, and institutions, all of which constitute the large technological system of mobile Internet. In particular, this paper draws on the concept of a ‘reverse salient’ in a large technological system, which plays a crucial role in the development of the system.

This paper shows that institutions to manage and control music copyrights have played a reverse salient role and influenced the development of mobile music businesses in different ways in the two countries. Well-established institutions to protect copyrights in Japan have contributed to the smooth launch and subsequent development of mobile music businesses in the 2.5G and 3G systems. In comparison, less effective institutions in Korea have caused some confusion and deterred the development of mobile music businesses in the 2.5G systems. However, Korean institutions have been creating new applications more aggressively than in Japan in the 3G system, owing to the country’s ambiguity to manage copyrights.<sup>1</sup>

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present a brief overview of mobile music businesses and their development in the two countries. In Section 3, in order to understand how these businesses have been developed, we introduce the concept of a reverse salient in a large technological system (LTS) as an analytical perspective, and explain why we focus on copyright management institutions in the analysis that follows. In Section 4, we explain the characteristics and development mechanism of the two countries’ mobile music businesses with primary attention to institutions to control and manage music copyright as a reverse salient. Section 5 concludes this paper by providing some implications for practitioners and researchers.

## 2. Overview of the mobile music business in Japan and Korea<sup>2</sup>

Japan and Korea are ahead of other countries in the diffusion of mobile Internet. At the end of 2002, mobile communication subscribers reached 63% of the total population in Japan and 69% in Korea. Among the subscribers, those owning handsets with a mobile Internet function accounted for 93% in Japan and 90% in Korea. The mobile Internet services, embodied in the 2.5G mobile communication system, were first launched in February 1999 in Japan and September 1999 in Korea, and have grown rapidly with E-mail and content services. Both countries then moved into the 3G mobile communication system—in October 2001 in Japan and June 2002 in Korea—with an expectation for further growth and development of mobile Internet businesses.

Among various mobile Internet services, music-related services have been the most important businesses in the two countries, having driven the development of mobile Internet businesses as the largest market. To date, the following services have been available in Japan and Korea.

## Ringing melody (Chaku-mero in Japan; Bellsori in Korea)

Ringing melody is a short melody for a mobile handset’s ringing tone use. A mobile phone subscriber downloads melody files from content providers to use as a ringing tone. A melody file is coded with a file format according to the handset’s music processing chip, the download channel, and the carrier’s data transmission specifications and capacity. The music processing chip’s capacity has rapidly advanced from simple 1–3 chords through 16–40 chords to the current 64 chords, which can replay sound quality close to CD records. The ringing melody service was first launched in Japan on NTT DoCoMo’s i-mode system in December 1999, and in Korea in the summer of 2000.

## Ring-back song (melody call in Japan; coloring in Korea)

Ring-back song service is a service in which a phone call receiver selects and pays for the music to be played for callers. Instead of a traditional mechanical ringing sound, callers can listen to about 15-s of popular music or humorous voices while he/she is waiting for the receiver to answer. It was first developed in 2002 in Korea and has become a killer application within just several months. In Japan, NTT DoCoMo introduced a similar service in September 2003.

## Ringing song (Chaku-Uta in Japan; Live Bell in Korea)

This is a service in which a subscriber downloads a portion of music, rather than a melody, for the ring tone. This service became available on the 3G system with the introduction of mobile music processing chips that achieve high sound quality closer to

CDs. A service for downloading a portion of music was launched in Japan by KDDI in December 2002 and in Korea in November 2002.

## Full-music download (music-on-demand in Korea) and MP3Phone

In Korea, a full-music download service (MOD, music-on-demand) was launched in November 2002 as a part of JUNE service, which is a 3G mobile content service provided by SKT, offering both MOD and video-on-demand (VOD) functions. Then in early 2004, mobile handsets with an MP3 player function, or MP3Phone were introduced in Korea. MP3Phone users can download MP3 files through either mobile or wired (PC) Internet. In the latter case, users transform the files into MP3Phone compatible files and send them to handsets by USB cable. Mobile communication carriers provide subscribers the transforming software and MP3Phones can save at least 16 songs (64 Mb). These kinds of services or handsets are not available in Japan.

These mobile music businesses have grown into a large market. The most notable is Japan’s ringing melody (‘Chaku-Mero’) service. As of December 2002, the total number of official sites for three carriers (NTT DoCoMo, KDDI, and J-Phone) reached 244, and Chaku-Mero commanded the largest sales in each carrier’s mobile Internet services. Chaku-Mero claimed about 40–60% of the entire mobile Internet sales for each carrier. It was estimated that in 2002 the three carriers’ total revenue from paid mobile Internet services (subscribers’ total payments) was approximately 1.5 billion US dollars, and the revenue from Chaku-Mero sites was 800–900 million US dollars, a very rapid growth from zero within 3 years.<sup>4</sup>

In Korea, the ringing melody service has also been the most popular mobile content service, accounting for about a half of 230 million US dollars of total mobile content market in 2002—ringing melody for about 80 million US dollars and ring-back song for about 30 million US dollars, together creating a 110 million US dollar mobile music market.<sup>5</sup>

Furthermore, music download services were launched in both Japan and Korea with the advent of the 3G mobile communication system. In Japan, KDDI’s ringing song service (Chaku-Uta) has proved to the first killer application in its 3G mobile service (CDMA 1X), creating a 50 million US dollar market in 2003. In Korea, the number of subscribers to SKT’s 3G service reached 1.5 million at the end of 2003. JUNE’s MOD and VOD services together brought in revenue of 200 million US dollars in 2003.<sup>6</sup>

## 3. Reverse salient in a large technological system: an analytical perspective

How have these mobile music businesses been developed in the two countries? To deal with this empirical question, this paper takes a hint from the ‘Large Technological System’ (LTS) perspective proposed by Hughes (1983, 1989). A large technological system is a collection of distinct but interrelated sub-systems, each of which is designed to perform independent tasks, but structured and coordinated to achieve a common goal (Hughes, 1983). Large technological systems include such technological infrastructures as air transportation, railroad networks, telecommunication systems, and electronic commerce systems.

In many advanced economies, large technological systems constitute a significant proportion of industrial output and fixed capital formation, and serve as infrastructure upon which goods and services are produced and traded. It is difficult to understand the innovation mechanisms and future directions of various goods and services without proper consideration of large technological systems behind them (Hobday, 1998). Mobile Internet services are no exception.

A distinctive feature of the LTS perspective is that it brings all the complex interconnections and interactions among sub-systems together into a unit of analysis. It broadly encompasses not only technological components such as various technological goods and services but also firms in related industries and social institutions such as government, universities, industrial policies, and regulations.

To understand how a large technological system develops and evolves, Hughes proposed several important analytical concepts. From among them, this paper focuses on his concept of reverse salient (Hughes, 1983, 1989). A reverse salient refers to a component in the system that has fallen behind or is out of phase with the others. The way in which a reserve salient, or a lagging component or part of the system, is handled and coordinated with other components of the system could be a decisive factor for directions and speed of large technological system development.

In a sense, a reverse salient shares conceptual grounds with a ‘focusing device’ presented by Rosenberg (1976), which refers to an imbalance in an interdependent technological system. In this approach, Rosenberg focused on a ‘technological’ imbalance for the explanation of the innovation process, especially delving into timing and direction of new technological innovations. Application of reverse salient, however, is not necessarily limited to technological components of the system. It could be applied to organizational and institutional components of the system. Given the unprecedented high speed of changes in information and communication technologies, continuous restructuring and realigning of social institutions in accordance with technological advances will be critical in order to realize their potentials. A reverse salient caused by social institutions would therefore merit our close attention in this field.

Drawing on this perspective, this paper will analyze the developmental process of mobile music businesses in Japan and Korea. Consumers simply download their favorite music from content providers by way of mobile Internet network and enjoy it. All these electronic transactions, however, are materialized in a complexly interconnected large technological system. The large technological system of the mobile Internet network consists of a variety of sub-systems as follows: mobile communication networks and their carriers; mobile handsets and their manufacturers; mobile communication network hardware and software and their manufacturers; music contents played on handsets and their creators (artists and record companies); mobile music content files and their providers; music copyrights and institutions managing them; and public policies and regulations on mobile communication and the government bodies that design and implement them. All of them are a part or component of the large technological system of mobile Internet. Although there will be endless lists if we detail them down into the sub-levels, the above list may encompass major technological, organizational, and institutional components (artifacts) of the large mobile Internet technological system.

Mobile music services could be materialized only when these heterogeneous subcomponents are effectively structured and coordinated as a large system. The growth, differentiation, and advancement of mobile music businesses depend on changes in relevant sub-components or sometimes the addition of new sub-components to the system.

Even though Japan and Korea share a certain resemblance in the basic structure of the whole mobile music system, characteristics of sub-components and relations among them differ. Differences stem from various causes such as the context and path of development of each sub-component, and the timing of the formation of the system. We focus on one important sub-component that could be considered as a critical determinant to create national differences of mobile music businesses between Japan and Korea, and as a reverse salient to affect their development processes. This sub-component is made up of ‘institutions to manage and control music copyrights’.

While technologies change rather rapidly and frequently in the field of mobile Internet, social institutions are relatively slow to change. Social institutions involve a variety of relevant parties each with vested interests. To overcome the conflict of interests for changing these institutions, intensive time-consuming political negotiations and creative solutions are required. How social institutions are changed, thus influences the directions and speed of the development of mobile Internet businesses as a whole.

The large technological system perspective should examine all important subcomponents and their relationships within a whole system. However, since our research is still in progress and the space of this paper is limited, we will limit our focus to copyright management institutions as a reverse salient. The picture this paper offers is therefore rather incomplete. Nevertheless, we believe that it is worthwhile to examine copyright management institutions to understand how mobile music businesses have been developed. This view is shared by Kwok et al. (2002), who emphasized the importance of copyright management in understanding business for information and content files.

Section 4 analyzes characteristics of the institutions to manage music copyrights and how they have influenced the development of mobile music businesses in the two countries, first on the 2.5G mobile system and then on the 3G mobile system.

## 4. The evolution of mobile music businesses in Japan and Korea

## 4.1. Development of ringing melody service on the 2.5G mobile system

The basic structure of the ringing melody business in Japan and Korea could be depicted as Figs. 1 and 2, respectively. We will examine Japan’s case first. In Japan, official content provider sites, which are approved by each carrier and listed on the carrier’s official menu pages, provide melody download services. Users subscribe to their favorite sites, and download music melodies on a monthly fee base or by discrete payment for each download. On each monthly bill, carriers charge and collect the service fees from subscribers on behalf of the service providers. Carriers gain their own business revenue by charging fees as follows: (1) data communication time to subscribers for their site access (the packet communication fee, for instance, costs around 6–7 US cents for a ringing melody download); (2) a fixed rate of handling fee to content providers (DoCoMo and KDDI charge 9% and J-Phone 12% as the handling fee of the collected revenue for the provider). The service providers pay copyright fees to the Japanese Society for Rights of Authors, Composers, and Publishers (JASRAC). For melodies shorter than 45 seconds 5 US cents per melody is charged, and 7.7 US cents for melodies longer than 45 seconds.

It is important to point out that most of the music copyrights for ringing melody are trusted to and managed by JASRAC in Japan.<sup>7</sup> As JASRAC has taken charge of copyright procedures for most ringing tone melodies, ringing melody service providers could use most popular musical tunes rather freely as long as they pay fees to JASRAC. A historical background of such smooth copyright coordination for the melody service could be traced back to institutional arrangements made for the Karaoke business and J-Phone’s Sky Melody service, which started to provide ringing melody service based on the ARS system on the SMS network in 1998. For the ringing melody service, copyright negotiations with JASRAC were conducted by the Association of the Musical Electronics Industry (AMEI) under the leadership of Yamaha, Faith, and major service providers. They agreed on the current copyright rule in late 2000, about one year after the first ringing melody download service was introduced (Table 1).

In the beginning, as ringing melody gained wide popularity, a large number of general sites (or unofficial sites not approved by the carrier) for ringing melody sprung into existence, offering a wide variety of free melody download services. To counter this undesirable trend, JASRAC vigorously monitored and charged copyright violations, and eventually cooled down the movement. JASRAC thus made an important contribution to the ringing melody business by preventing the spread of improper free services, in addition to the provision of smooth copyright coordination. This pattern contrasts with the US situation in which the widespread free music download services like Napster on the fixed Internet in earlier days subsequently disturbed the creation of paid online music download services, creating a false impression that music was free on the web.

When we look at other parts of the world, we also find situations that are not as favorable as the Japanese case. As shown in Table 2, for example, copyrights are generally managed in a more complicated manner in other countries with different rules. In some countries, there is no such an institution that could manage all necessary copyrights for one-stop shopping, thus raising the cost of copyright coordination or allowing the spread of illegal, or free, download sites. Indeed, Japanese service providers often complain about the absence of desirable copyright procedures as a major obstacle for their business overseas.

Korea is one such country. As you see in Figs. 1 and 2, Korea and Japan look similar in the basic structures of their ringing melody business. However, there are some seemingly subtle but critical differences, which do matter very much to the development of mobile music businesses. In Korea, general recognition for copyright protection in general has been comparably weak not only among users but also among music content service providers and copyright claimers. Record companies have even failed to pay due attention to copyright issues.

In principle, all copyright fees are to be collected by Korea Music Copyright Association (KOMCA) and be redistributed to the copyright owners. As a rule, any mobile music content providers could initiate service by paying required copyright fees. While no specific copyright rule for ringing melody has been formulated, content providers are usually supposed to pay 6.5–8% of download service revenue on monthly basis unlike their Japanese counterparts who pay for individual melodies downloaded. This rule gives mobile music content providers in Korea more flexibility in designing their music business models and pricing than their counterparts in Japan. As a result, both content providers and carriers have often provided free music services for promotional purposes. Also, only carriers have accurate data of ringing melody download transactions, which they do not make public, causing problems of limited transparency in copyright control and transactions. To make things worse, there exist some copyrights that are not trusted to KOMCA. This sometimes provokes legal conflicts between content providers and copyright holders who demand additional copyright charges. Furthermore, all these ambiguous principles and improper institutional operations have given birth to ‘illegitimate’ or ‘inadequate’ free services, which could cause substantial and unjust competitive damage to legitimate service providers and would not properly compensate the creative efforts of artists.

Overall, due to weak copyright recognition regarding musical products in general and limited capacity and ambiguous rules of copyright management institutions, many companies have provided music service without paying due attention to copyright issues. In a highly competitive environment, most content providers have maintained the policy of ‘music service first and copyright clearance later’. In effect, music copyright institutions in Korea have been unable to justly protect and properly manage musical copyrights, and have thus somewhat restrained further market growth potential and caused problems, confusion, and less incentive to music creation activities.

In Japan, owing to efficient and effective copyright operation mechanisms, any mobile music content provider could legitimately build up music services just by paying dues to JASRAC based on accurate records of download transactions. Without competing against illegitimate free sites, the legal, official service providers could reap proper returns for its initial investments. It also works in such a way that easy market entry strongly propels market competition among multiple content providers, pressing each provider to differentiate their service and create new values for survival. Particularly, those content providers which formerly did business in the wired-Karaoke business gained market share with their competitive advantage of having already accumulated a huge stock of music data files and creating new files quickly and efficiently. In this manner, smooth copyright coordination has substantially contributed to the success of Japan’s ringing melody business, which is protected against illegal services and yet very competitive.<sup>8</sup>

However, the story does not stop here. Though Japanese copyright institutions have worked more effectively than Korea for ringing melody, a different picture has come out for the music download service market on the 3G system.

## 4.2. Development of music download services on the 3G mobile system

As we move from the 2.5G system to 3G, people expect that full-music download services will be available before long. However, the transition is not so simple, not because of technological changes but because of institutional changes. Since ringing melody services only use melody, composers are the only ones who are compensated for their ‘copyright in musical compositions’. However, ringing song and ring-back song use original music, usually with singing and instrumental performances, for which a full set of copyright fees should be paid to multiple parties such as composers, lyricists, singers, musicians, recording companies, and artist management offices that hold related copyrights, including ‘copyright in sound recordings’. Copyright issues for mobile services to download musical song are therefore much more complicated than for those to download simple musical melody (Table 1).

NTT DoCoMo, which has successfully developed i-mode service and nurtured the ringing melody market, has provided a full-music distribution service, the M-stage, in its PHS mobile platform without using Internet channels. However, the demand for this service has been rather limited partly because of complex copyright issues. Due to concerns for illegal usages and demand for strong copyright protection, the service is rather expensive and the amount of music available is limited.

By contrast, KDDI’s ringing song (‘Chaku-Uta’) service, introduced on their 3G platform, uses only parts of musical compositions and has managed to clear copyright protection concerns by limiting the scope of music file usages only to the handset. So far, the service has been smoothly launched and proved to be a new killer application. The market reached 50 million US dollars for the first year, 2003.

One notable fact for the ringing song business is that record companies, which could not gain much benefit from the lucrative ringing melody business, are now reaping the fruits of their investment into creating and promoting popular music. A joint venture of 21 major Japanese record companies, ‘Label Mobile’, has launched ringing song download business with their copyrights on original music. Since, leading content providers for ringing melody do not possess copyrights for original songs, they cannot enter the ringing song business. Record companies thus provide the ringing song service of the music under their exclusive control. It is reported that Label Mobile has captured 70–80% of the ringing song market. Yet, there are many popular songs that are not offered for ringing song services, because their copyrights are not owned by record companies but by individual artists and other artist management firms, some of which are not interested in the mobile music business. Therefore, whereas in the ringing melody service most melodies are provided by many service providers with each melody provided by multiple providers, in the ringing song service a limited number of popular songs are provided by a limited number of service providers with each song provided by only one provider.

Furthermore, if we want to move to more advanced mobile music services, where fullmusic download service and data exchanges across mobile handsets, PC and other audio devices are available, copyright protection and proper coordination would become much more complex and problematic. Particularly, Japanese record companies might try hard to maintain their copyrights and prevent other parties from exploring new music business models.<sup>9</sup> So there is no guarantee that Japan’s success in mobile music businesses in the earlier stages will be maintained in following stages.

In Korea, in contrast, where copyright protection is less strict and the power of record companies is weaker, new mobile music services on the 3G system have been created rather rapidly and aggressively, although confusion and problems still continue. Korea’s 3G music services have quickly made the leap into full-music download services. Both ringing song (Live Bell) and full-music download (MOD) services were introduced in November 2002. The latter was launched with a video downloading service (VOD) as a part of JUNE by SKT.

In the basic business structure of MOD, subscribers pay 80 US cents for a full-music download. Of this revenue, 30% goes to the mobile carrier, 25% to the publisher, which is entitled to manage the copyright in sound recordings usually owned by record companies, 10% to the composers (through KOMCA) and music performers, and the remaining to the content provider and others. Basically, each song is provided by only one content provider. Successfully launched, the JUNE service captured 1.5 million subscribers by the end of 2003 with JUNE’s MOD and VOD services realizing total revenue of 200 million US dollars in the same year. The majority of MOD users are in their 20 s and 30 s due to expensive handset and packet fees to download music files.

Interestingly, unlike Japan, major content providers in this new 3G music business are not record companies. Rather, technology-based content providers have been leading players in the business. For instance, the first JUNE’s MOD service provider was Cowon System. This company specializes in PC-based MP3 player software and SKT’s server technology for mobile-Internet, and captured approximately 20% of JUNE’s MOD market in 2003.

There are several reasons why major record companies could not establish a direct presence in the new mobile MOD market. First, with strong industry influence and as the owner of a mobile network, SKT did not allow record companies to directly provide music download services. The market of traditional record music in Korea was 373 million US dollars in 2001 and decreased to 270 million US dollars in 2003 (compared to four billion US dollars in Japan in 2003). The Korean record music industry itself has been small in size and power, compared with ‘giant’ mobile carriers. Second, from the outset, traditional record companies did not properly understand the market potential of mobile music services and could not take collective actions to proactively safeguard their copyright claims as Japanese record companies did. Finally, the record industry has been so strongly battered by illegal free MP3 file exchange on the wired Internet that it welcomes any profit sources even if the businesses are operated by outside parties.

However, damage by illegal MP3 files in the wired Internet and failure to build direct market presence in mobile music businesses have prompted record companies to seek collective actions in order to restructure copyright institutions. Some efforts have been made to establish new copyright management institutions to administer all content services for digitalized music, be it fixed or mobile. Yet, there still remain some conflicts of interest among different parties, with three institutions now established and competing for leadership. The Korean Association of Phonogram Producers (KAPP) is a public organization officially recognized by the Korean government. The Manine Media is a private copyright publisher joined by major record companies and music distributors. Einsdigital is sponsored by mobile music service providers and three mobile carriers. How this competition for leadership in digital copyright institutions will be resolved is yet to be seen.

In the meantime, the introduction of MP3Phone has caused a critical problem in the Korean mobile music business. The key copyright issue regarding MP3Phone is whether the phones should be compatible with the existing MP3 files available on users’ PCs.

While MP3-handset manufacturers are inclined to produce a fully compatible handset to stimulate rapid diffusion, record companies reasonably oppose such a plan and demand incompatibility with existing illegal files. Carriers stay closer to record companies, calculating the potential of the full-music download market. Yet, LG Telecom has taken a different position. LG Telecom, the smallest carrier in the Korean mobile market, wants to sell fully compatible MP3Phone to gain market share. A possible problem is that once MP3Phones become fully compatible with existing MP3 files in PC, the market boundary between mobile and wired Internet will disappear and the mobile MOD service market would no longer develop. The outcome of ongoing negotiations will be an important turning point for the future development of mobile music businesses in Korea.

## 5. Discussions and conclusion

Our observation has indicated that music copyright institutions play an important role as a reverse salient in the large technological system of mobile music businesses. Technologies to process, transmit, and replay music have ceaselessly advanced with development of information and communication technologies. Such technological progress has created and will continue to create enormous potentials for cheaper, faster, and better mobile music services. However, in order to materialize new technological potentials into real business chances and enable new service providers to be smoothly wheeled into the evolving mobile technological system, the corresponding music copyright institutions should be rearranged in a timely manner in accordance with other sub-components and their technological changes in the large system. Yet because social institutions are slow to change, they act as a reverse salient on the directions and pace of the development of mobile Internet systems, and different countries with different institutions thus take different paths of development.

A comparative analysis between Japan and Korea makes clear that social alignments are indeed complex and proceed in a distinct manner in each country. Well-established and strict copyright institutions in Japan have realized expanding market and successful businesses based on new mobile technologies, without hurting due compensation for the copyright owners. In this environment, content providers from wired Karaoke business have led the ringing melody market, given easy access to composers’ copyrights through JASRAC. Then, a joint venture by major record companies (Label Mobile) has dominated the ringing song market because they own copyrights over original music. However, it seems that copyright institutions in Japan are not free from limitation. Strict copyright institution has protected the interests of record companies, and enabled major record companies to extend their market power to new online distribution channels on mobile Internet. In this situation, an emerging concern is that the mobile music business could develop in such way that it may not realize potential consumer benefits made possible by new technologies. Large record companies may not have incentive to actively promote development of mobile music technologies if it is expected to hurt their offline music market.

Looser copyright institutions in Korea have somehow restrained full market potential in newly emerging mobile music markets. Weak copyright protection did some harm to market growth of mobile music services and caused continuous legal haggling and social unrest between the copyright holders and copyright users. In the long term, such weak copyright protection could hurt the viability of the music industry as a whole, depressing creative music works. In this environment, unlike Japan, where record companies or content providers with expertise in music-related businesses have been leading the market, technology-based companies with close relations with carriers have been the winners in mobile music businesses.

However, it is also true that flexibility (or ambiguity) in music copyright management and control in Korea has stimulated innovations not only in new mobile music businesses but also in mobile handset and communication technologies. With the introduction of MP3Phone, boundaries between mobile and fixed Internets may become blurred in the Korean music business and may spur innovations in new business models. Such technological infrastructure may also lead to further innovations in music distribution channels as a whole, realizing benefits from distribution cost saving both for the music industry and consumers in the long-term period. Moreover, new mobile music businesses would provide stimulus and pressures to handset manufacturers and carriers to advance their technological basis. For the realization of MP3Phone, handset manufacturers should increase memory capacity, and design new music playing process chips, and prolong battery time. And mobile carriers should configure new data transmitting systems so that they are fast enough to download large MP3 music files. Such challenges in the domestic market seem to have helped Korean handset manufacturers like Samsung and LG grow rapidly to claim 10.8% and 5.3%, respectively, in the world mobile handset market in 2003.

Yet, at the same time, we should be aware that in Korea conflicts and confusion in copyright management and control still remain. Although one could expect that new applications and models will be explored more aggressively and rapidly in Korea than in Japan, we do not yet know whether new businesses and markets will evolve smoothly or not in Korea. Korea needs more stable and legitimate institutions for music copyrights to achieve further development of mobile music businesses in the long run.

Different from technological problems residing in specific hardware and software or organizational problems arising within individual firms, music copyright problems necessitate a complex social re-engineering across various entities with diverging interests. By adopting the large technological system perspective, we have observed that copyright institutions could be seen as a reverse salient against rapid advances of mobile music businesses to provide better contents faster and more cheaply to make the most of technological progress. Realignment of social institutions, which usually embroils a variety of conflicting entities, often causes more complex problems than that of particular technologies, products, or services, consuming considerable social efforts and energies, and demanding creative institutional solutions and political coordination.

It is therefore important to pay more attention to social and political factors in understanding and managing the mobile Internet market and business. This argument echoes with Tushman and Rosenkopf (1992), who emphasized the importance of social and political elements in technological changes. They pointed out that such elements are particularly influential for open systems during eras of ferment. Indeed, the mobile music business is a part of an ever-changing open network system, where eras of ferment arrive cyclically at frequent intervals. As shown in this case of mobile music business development, a distinctive nature and impact of information and communication technologies is that technological potentials frequently necessitate corresponding realignments of social institutions. Our position of emphasizing the importance of social institutions is not new in the field of information system research. As already pointed out, for example, our view echoes with Kwok et al. (2002) study of peer-to-peer technology business and service. They argued that without explicitly acknowledging and protecting ownership rights and controlling the distribution of digital content, the business community would not embrace P2P technologies.

This paper suggests that public policy and regulations are important for developing mobile Internet commerce and that a laissez faire economic policy may not always be the best solution. It also indicates that managers are advised to pay more attention to social institutions and public policies. Competitive strategy tends to focus on economic factors. For example, Porter (1980) asserted, “although the relevant environment is very broad, encompassing social as well as economic forces, the key aspect of the firm’s environment is the industry or industries in which it competes”. However, to be successful in the field of mobile Internet, companies should learn more about how to bring about timely and effective changes in social and political realms.

We investigated the characteristic nature of the emerging music business in the context of emerging mobile Internet technology. The large technological system perspective could help managers formulate business strategies by providing a systematic picture of the complex environment of the mobile Internet business. For those who study the mobile Internet market and businesses, a critical question would be not only how new mobile technology could change existing business and market structures, but also how we could change social institutions in order to materialize the technological potentials.
