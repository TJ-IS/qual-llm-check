# An app a day keeps a customer connected: Explicating loyalty to brands and branded applications through the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance and service-dominant logic

Yu-Hui Fang

Department of Accounting, Tamkang University, No. 151, Ying-Chuan Rd., Tamsui, New Taipei City, 25137, Taiwan, ROC

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Service-dominant logic Brand competence Brand warmth Branded applications A<sup>f</sup>ordance Brand loyalty

## A B S T R A C T

The rise of mobile apps is compelling ambitious companies to rush to market their own branded apps in hopes of building customers’ brand loyalty. By applying service-dominant logic, the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance, and two brand perceptions (brand competence and brand warmth), this study explores critical determinants of app continuance intention and brand loyalty among app users. Five a<sup>f</sup>ordances of branded apps are linked to value-in-use, which contributes both directly and indirectly to the two loyalty-related outcomes, through either brand competence or brand warmth. Data collected from 634 app users support all proposed hypotheses. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

In 2016, high smartphone penetration (81%) increased the growth rate of mobile commerce (m-commerce) to outpace that of other retail channels (e.g., desktop e-commerce and brick-and-mortar stores).<sup>1</sup> According to a 2017 report by comScore,<sup>1</sup> as of December 2016, mcommerce accounted for nearly one in <sup>fi</sup>ve US dollars spent in digital commerce, while mobile communication contributed approximately 7 in every 10 min spent in digital media. Indeed, mobile applications (apps) alone drive a majority (60%) of digital media consumption in the US.<sup>1</sup> These observations, combined with increasingly heavy depen dence on mobile devices in diverse areas of daily life, suggest that we are currently in the “app age”.<sup>2</sup> As a result, ambitious companies pay substantial attention to the promise of mobile apps and the <sup>fi</sup>nancial potential of marketing their own branded apps in hopes of seizing onthe-go customers [1] and their brand loyalty, both for today’s survival and tomorrow’s existence. However, a branded app cannot exert its in<sup>fl</sup>uence unless customers continue to use the app. This precondition thus elicits a crucial question: How can loyalty toward a branded app and a brand be promoted among app users as they use a branded app? Put di<sup>f</sup>erently, what are the essential determinants of continuance intention and brand loyalty among app users? This study aims to incorporate interdisciplinary concepts into a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed model to shed further light on the research questions.

A review of recent work on mobile continuance and loyalty indicates that the technology acceptance model (TAM) and expectation con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory are two main theories in this <sup>fi</sup>eld, and related factors, such as usefulness, ease of use, perceived value, and satisfaction, are possible antecedents (e.g., [2–4]). A common feature of these studies is their use of goods-dominant logic (GDL) to explore m-commerce and value. Through this logic, mobile technology and mechan isms (e.g., mobile apps) are considered products (operand resources); therefore, focus is thus placed on the control of tangible outcomes of economic processes and the embedded value-in-exchange [2,5], instead of value-in-use (VIU), intangibility, and relationships (i.e., the keys of service-dominant logic [SDL] [6]). GDL may be insu<sup>fi</sup>cient to provide a comprehensive understanding of marketing and interactive services because GDL regards goods as the core of economic exchange and services as a special category of goods, thus providing a relatively restricted interpretation in the case of branded apps [7,5]. Because branded apps can be seen as value-added services that enable users to execute m-commerce activities (e.g., seeking and purchasing products, searching driving directions, and sur<sup>fi</sup>ng menus and reviews of local restaurants [1]), the emerging concept of SDL may be superior to GDL in shedding light on branded app matters. The justi<sup>fi</sup>cation is threefold. First, SDL emphasizes the intangible processes of serving (VIU), instead of the narrow outcomes of economic exchange [7]. It can reveal opportunities for market expansion by supporting customers in the process of specialization and value creation [7]. Second, by strengthening the value creation process, expanding the scope of resources, and helping collaboration within and between service systems, SDL can clarify dynamic activities in the new service-based economy [8]. Third, SDL has recently been applied to various service platforms, such as those for innovative ride sharing [9], mobile apps [10], mobile valueadded services [11], company websites [12], and social media-based brand communities [13]. These studies support not only the applic ability of SDL to the digitally enabled services but also the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role of VIU in enhancing customer loyalty. Accordingly, taking VIU as its starting point, this study extends its sphere of in<sup>fl</sup>uence to predict loyalty to both branded apps and brands.

According to SDL, value arises when customers and companies in teract on platforms (e.g., branded apps) the company provides. It is created by customers’ integration of o<sup>f</sup>erings into their personal processes [7], e.g., using a branded app to <sup>fi</sup>nd a store nearby. Although it has been acknowledged that value is derived from the user’s use context and processes [14], there have been few attempts to propose speci<sup>fi</sup>c constructs for these sources of value (e.g., interactivity, localization, visibility, and accessibility [10,15]) and test them. To <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap, this study suggests integrating the SDL perspective with another framework that can elaborate further on the relationships between users, platforms, and use processes in presenting these sources of value. Indeed, the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance may be such a framework to complement current SDL literature to gain an explicit understanding of this new mobilemarketing phenomenon. According to the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance, a<sup>f</sup>ordance represents the “multifaceted relational structure” ([16], p. 254) between an object/technology (a branded app) and the user that facil itates or restricts behavioral outcomes in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context [17]. Through this lens, <sup>fi</sup>ve a<sup>f</sup>ordances—visibility, persistence, interactivity, association, and selectivity—explicitly illustrate the mutuality between customers, the features of a branded app, and the situated nature of use [17]. Such a<sup>f</sup>ordances characterize possibilities for action [16] in the user’s use context and processes [14] and can thus be used to appropriately interpret the preconditions of value (VIU) in the case of branded apps. Hence, this study extends the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance from communication research to empirically investigate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of <sup>fi</sup>ve a<sup>f</sup>ordances on VIU in the context of branded apps.

Beyond direct e<sup>f</sup>ects on loyalty to branded apps and brands, VIU may exert indirect in<sup>fl</sup>uence through cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective perceptions toward brands—i.e., brand competence and brand warmth. Recently, an increasing attention has been paid to both brand competence and brand warmth in shaping customer intention and attitudes toward brands [18–20]. Although previous work has con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of both perceptions on customer responses and atti tudes toward brands in traditional advertising contexts [18,20], whether this relationship holds true in the case of branded apps remains unexplored. In a sense, branded apps di<sup>f</sup>er from other service platforms by enabling customers to (a) customize them with personal information they voluntarily disclose, (b) pull advertising with opt-in permission to advertise on mobile devices, and (c) enjoy receiving favorable information for location-sensitive and time-critical events [21,22]. Indeed, branded apps can help customers build up their preferable use experiences and values. Provided that cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective inferences coexist in everyday experiences [23], it would be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to extend cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective perceptions of brand to the context of branded apps and examine their roles in directing customer loyalty. Altogether, the links between VIU, brand perceptions, and loyalty, in the context of branded apps, deserve our unequivocal attention and investigation.

Overall, in casting new light on the abovementioned gaps and customer behaviors in the context of branded apps, this study integrates SDL (from the marketing <sup>fi</sup>eld), the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance (from the com munications <sup>fi</sup>eld), as well as brand warmth and competence (from brand literature) into information system (IS) research to arrive at a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed model. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this study empirically investigates the effects of a<sup>f</sup>ordances of branded apps on VIU, which in turn a<sup>f</sup>ects brand competence, brand warmth, and the two loyalty outcomes (app continuance intention and brand loyalty), directly and indirectly. This study’s contributions to the existing literature are fourfold. First, by proposing and testing an integrated model with superior ability to explain consumer behavior [24], it provides new evidence and a broader view for understanding the antecedents of customer loyalty than the explanation given in prior IS research. Second, and di<sup>f</sup>erent from most previous conceptual studies on SDL, it advances marketing theory by empirically demonstrating the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of VIU in the new phenomenon of mobile marketing. Third, by empirically examining the <sup>fi</sup>ve aforementioned a<sup>f</sup>ordances in the context of branded apps, it not only sheds light on the determinants of values (VIU) but also extends the boundary of the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance to m-commerce. Such extension o<sup>f</sup>ers practical implications for app designers and theoretical implications for SDL research. Fourth, by providing evidence and intelligently illuminating the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant roles of brand competence and brand warmth in the use of branded apps, the boundary of brand humanization research is extended to now encompass the emerging context of branded apps. These results therefore lend themselves to actionable managerial recommendations for app designers and marketers (e.g., incorporating new technologies to present brand humanization when designing a branded app).<sup>3</sup>

This paper is organized as follows. We review the theoretical background about this research in Section 2 and justify the hypothesis development in Section 3. Section 4 describes the methodology and research design, while Section 5 presents the analysis results. Finally, we discuss our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings, implications, and limitations of the study, as well as recommendations for future research in Section 6.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Research on branded applications

Technically speaking, branded apps are software downloadable to a mobile device which prominently displays a brand identity, often via the name of the app and the appearance of a brand logo or icon, throughout the user experience” ([21], p. 191). According to Bruggeman [25], branded apps boast new possibilities beyond that of websites because of smartphone capabilities (e.g., camera, GPS, touchscreen, and accelerometer), thereby producing distinctive value and a better experience for customers. The fundamental di<sup>f</sup>erence between a branded app and a website is that to access information and use the app, customers have to <sup>fi</sup>rst download and install the app and then periodically update it, giving their voluntary permission to do so. These observations highlight the active role of customers when they use a branded app [26] and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c values required for directing their continuance app usage over a long period of time [25]. Previous research on technology continuance has paid little attention to certain values customers pursue, conceivably because the conventional model (e.g., TAM) holds an assumption that technology users are passive and only respond to technology—as opposed to active users who determine what the technology is [27]. The restricted view of passive use is inadequate to clarify customer continuance in the context of branded apps, where customers are active users served by freedom of app choice [26]. Such a view provides insu<sup>fi</sup>cient justi<sup>fi</sup>cation regarding what branded apps mean to their users/customers (i.e., it ignores the diverse values derived from branded apps). As branded apps become more prevalent and versatile, the need for identifying diverse user values emerges because values are commonly acknowledged as a challenge for marketers, who devote their e<sup>f</sup>orts toward gaining customers’ loyalty [25,26].

Alternatively, branded apps, as digitally enabled services, are distinguished from mobile technologies in the delivery of intangible services, for instance, dispersing interactive advertising, valuable information, and unique brand experiences that aid customers in determining consumption selections [1]. Although recent IS scholars have incorporated the concept of value into their models (e.g. [2],), they approach it from the standpoint of GDL, regarding technology as a product to be distributed to users, thus merely focusing on value-inexchange and tangible output, rather than VIU, intangibility, and relationships (i.e., the core of SDL [6]). Hence, a strictly GDL approach o<sup>f</sup>ers a relatively constricted explanation for app continuance in the case of branded apps because it focuses on tangibility for numerous o<sup>f</sup>erings, which may be a limiting factor that raises cost and obstructs marketability [7]. Given the extensive embeddedness of mobile devices and apps in a customer’s daily life, this study applies a recent SDL marketing trend to explore continuance app usage and brand loyalty because it deviates from this constraint and points to the potential for expanding the market by supporting customers in the process of value creation [7].

## 2.2. Service-dominant logic and value-in-use

SDL is characterized by shifting attention “from goods to service, from operand resources to operant resources, from being to doing, and somewhat less precisely, from what is exchanged to the process of exchange and from tangible to the intangible ([5], p. 51). SDL is aimed at customizing o<sup>f</sup>erings, because customers are always regarded as coproducers and SDL endeavors to amplify customer participation in customization to better meet their needs [14]. Indeed, SDL posits that customers are active participants in value creation rather than merely passive recipients of value [28]. This notion is especially relevant in today’s digitally enabled services because branded apps are “lean for ward” media where users actively take part in and interact with apps [29]. This study thus applies SDL to the context of branded apps because of three considerations. First, SDL o<sup>f</sup>ers a framework for observing interactive, complex, and resource-integrating systems in a more comprehensive way [5]. Second, according to Huang and Choi [8], SDL embraces wider issues around service marketing and provides a more generalizable framework than GDL for an enhanced understanding of dynamic activities in the new service-based economy. It enables a brand or company to re-evaluate customers’ contributions to value creation processes, which is ignored in GDL. Third, SDL departs from other logics in service science by heightening the value creation process, broadening the scope of resources, and supporting collaboration within and between service systems. It is thus bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to apply SDL to explore the phenomenon of branded apps, which also broadens the depth and breadth of existing SDL research.

SDL views are the key reason for exchange, facilitated mainly by operant resources (skills and knowledge) and realized through value co creation processes [14]. Value emerges through customers’ use context and processes involving individual experience, thought, time, location, or uncertain conditions, and relational a<sup>f</sup>ect [12,14]. Value is co-cre ated in use (namely, VIU) because customers judge and decide the value of a proposition based on the speci<sup>fi</sup>city of their usage [12,14,30]. Concisely, VIU re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree to which a customer feels better o<sup>f</sup> given their consumption-related experiences (e.g., using a branded app and embedded GPS to e<sup>fi</sup>ciently locate the nearest store). According to Ranjan and Read [12], VIU is a multidimensional construct that incorporates three subconstructs: experience, personalization, and re lationship. Experience refers to a memorable, cognitive, and emotional interaction that generates intrinsic value. Personalization is the uniqueness of the usage process, the value being determined by individual needs. Relationship refers to a reciprocal, enduring exchange and col laboration between a customer and object in a setting of active com munication (e.g., a branded app). Relationship and collaboration help to empower customers by settling problems, thus engendering value [31]. In SDL, customers act as network partners, co-creating and de termining their own experiences and value, whereas market actors (companies), having the necessary resources, serve as passive value facilitators, thus supporting the value generation process by recognizing, developing, and o<sup>f</sup>ering value propositions to customers [14]. While the idea of VIU is central to SDL and becomes increasingly imperative to understand customers’ needs [32], research has not yet empirically tested VIU in the context of branded apps, conceivably because literature on branded apps is in the early stage of development [33]. To our knowledge, this study constitutes a <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt in this regard.

## 2.3. The lens of afordance

While SDL provides a broader view in elucidating the service ex change, it has not yet speci<sup>fi</sup>ed precise constructs for the determinants of values, especially in the case of branded apps use. In a sense, a branded app can o<sup>f</sup>er various service possibilities for its customers to co-create their VIU with the app, such as the possibility to stay abreast of the latest information (promotion) according to a customers’ setting. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, customers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from customized o<sup>f</sup>erings that better meet their needs [14] and, thereafter, generate VIU only when they use the app service while providing individual preferences and location, i.e., the value co-creation process.<sup>4</sup> Indeed, the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance might make the sources of VIU (the abovementioned service possibilities) more comprehensive, thus <sup>fi</sup>lling the research void, because it provides a relational structure of possibilities for action [16], and incorporates more complete views of visibility, persistence, interactivity, association, and selectivity.<sup>5</sup> A<sup>f</sup>ordances denote relational actions that occur among users, technologies (branded apps), and outcomes [17]. They have also been extended to the observation of diverse interactions between users and technologies (social media, Wikis; [113, 17, 115]). Consequently, this study suggests applying the scheme of a<sup>f</sup>ordances to represent sources of VIU to build a holistic picture of the service exchange.

A<sup>f</sup>ordances, generally illustrated as possibilities for action, refer to the “multifaceted relational structure” ([16], p. 254) between an object (a branded app, in our case) and the user who facilitates possible outcomes in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context (i.e., what occurs from the user’s interac tions with the branded app; [17]). As Hutchby [34] notes, a<sup>f</sup>ordances provide functional and relational features that construct, and do not decide, possibilities for users’ actions with respect to an object. Given that the attributes of users, the materiality of branded apps, and the contexts of apps use (e.g., a user’ location) are not invariable, the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordances o<sup>f</sup>ers a valuable framework for exploring these relationships in diverse ways, while maintaining a relational ontology (not favoring any one side as deterministic of the others [17]). Because the relational facet of a<sup>f</sup>ordances requires justifying the potential for mutual in<sup>fl</sup>uence, it is crucial to identify the multidimensional nature of a<sup>f</sup>ordances [35]. Accordingly, and established on the existing structure of a<sup>f</sup>ordances [17,36], we put forward <sup>fi</sup>ve a<sup>f</sup>ordance constructs, particularly for branded apps—visibility, persistence, interactivity, association, and selectivity. To date, recent studies have extended an affordance lens to the context of social media [36,37]. Scholars [17,36] further call for empirical studies on the investigation of the role of affordances in diverse contexts and re<sup>fi</sup>nement of particular constructs for each application. Our extension of a<sup>f</sup>ordances to the milieu of branded apps, and the empirical investigation of a<sup>f</sup>ordance, contributes to affordance literature in this direction.

## 3. Hypotheses development

This study links the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance and SDL to build a research model (Fig. 1) for understanding loyalty to branded apps. Because VIU becomes the focal construct of interest in this study, further exploration of its antecedents (a<sup>f</sup>ordances) and consequences (two loyalty constructs) advances branded apps research and the boundary of SDL ap plication. The lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance paves a promising and attractive way to co-create VIU because it presents a comprehensive view of the relationship between customers and branded apps. In addition to its direct e<sup>f</sup>ects, VIU may in<sup>fl</sup>uence two loyalties through cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective considerations. Because brand competence and brand warmth can serve as predictors of positive brand attitudes and mediate the in-<sup>fl</sup>uence of diverse values on brand intentions [18–20], they are included in our model to shed new light on loyalty related to branded apps and brands.

## 3.1. Antecedents of value-in-use: afordances of branded apps

In this study, a<sup>f</sup>ordances of branded apps manifest in the form of visibility, persistence, interactivity, association, and selectivity, with their meanings re<sup>fi</sup>ned to adapt to the setting of branded apps. Visibility indicates the potential for customer access to relevant, needed, updated, and/or useful information a branded app e<sup>f</sup>ectively provides. Persistence is analogous to recordability [37] and communality [38] in that it denotes the potential for users to retrieve past records and content over time (e.g., records associated with a shopping cart). Interactivity denotes the degree to which awareness of interaction with a branded app can be synchronized and controlled through communication with it [39]. As a multidimensional construct, interactivity incorporates three components: active control (over the communication process), synchronicity (synchronized information during communication), and two-way communication (reciprocity during communication; [39]). Association represents the potential for connecting users to branded apps [36,40] and is exhibited in the context of branded apps through connectivity and a sense of safety. Connectivity denotes a physical association between users and branded apps that allows users access to services anytime and anywhere [41], whereas sense of safety represents a psychological aspect of association wherein users feel safe, easiness, and calmness when using the apps in an unfamiliar place [42]. Selectivity denotes the possibility of selecting a speci<sup>fi</sup>c branded app and the information users see as relevant for them [43]. Selectivity, in the context of branded apps, stems from the availability of customization and localization. Customization is the degree to which information obtained from a branded app can be customized by user preferences [44], whereas localization indicates the extent to which services and information re<sup>fl</sup>ect user context information (e.g., location and user activities [45]). Table 1 presents a list of supporting literature and examples of these <sup>fi</sup>ve a<sup>f</sup>ordances, along with their subcomponents. Notably, as Evans et al. [17] pointed out, “there are gradations of affordance” (p. 6). For instance, interactivity can be evaluated in consideration of greater or lesser interactivity. The variability of a<sup>f</sup>or dances has been empirically and conceptually con<sup>fi</sup>rmed (e.g., [17,43, 113]). Hence, these a<sup>f</sup>ordances of branded apps may not be applicable every time a customer uses an app because there is a range of engagement experiences. That is, he/she may perceive that a branded app provides partial a<sup>f</sup>ordances for action.

Indeed, these <sup>fi</sup>ve a<sup>f</sup>ordances represent multidimensional con siderations around branded apps, users/customers, and their use outcomes, suggesting a holistic perspective and approach to explore the antecedents of VIU. Although the literature is scarce on links between a<sup>f</sup>ordances and VIU because attention has been given to them only recently, the integration of a<sup>f</sup>ordances and VIU does have its reasons. With regard to the a<sup>f</sup>ordance of selectivity, recent research [10,53] suggests that branded apps equipped with location-based services have a greater potential to deliver optimal information and services (e.g., push noti<sup>fi</sup>cations) to customers in terms of their preferences and locations (i.e., customization and localization). Similarly, Junglas et al. [54] concluded that a branded app a<sup>f</sup>ording customization and localization is believed to be a killer application. Thus, the selectivity af fordance can better match with customers’ needs (personalization), create a better user experience and relationship, and co-create positive values (VIU) for customers [10].

The a<sup>f</sup>ordance of interactivity has positive impacts on user experience and values (VIU in our case) because users/customers are essentially active participants in a branded app system [29]. They can explicitly select personal preferences about what information to collect (active control), obtain an instantaneous response when clicking on an option (synchronicity), and engage in two-way communication with the branded app [55]. Indeed, beyond traditional marketing communications, branded apps support more interactive communications between companies and users [56]. As Fang [22] stated, a high interactivity suggests that customers can determine their level of interaction with a branded app when the app provides individually relevant information on a timely basis, thus cultivating the customer-brand relationship, impressive experience, and positive perceptions of the app (VIU).

The a<sup>f</sup>ordance of visibility has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a precondition a<sup>f</sup>ordance in digitally enabled services [17]. It mainly focuses on the information component for denoting accessibility to needed information [57] obtainable through branded apps. Branded apps that a<sup>f</sup>ord visibility may seize superior opportunities to create distinctive use experiences with customers (personalization and experience) and establish a close relationship with them [1,33]; this implies the link between visibility and VIU in the context of branded apps.

The a<sup>f</sup>ordance of persistence enables customers to keep personal shopping records regarding favorite products and services for future shopping considerations (e.g., a comparison pertaining to price or al ternatives). By allowing customers to easily review and recombine thei previous purchase history and selections in their shopping cart [113], persistence supports their comprehensive evaluation regarding future purchase decisions, which may contribute to a more distinctive, and therefore better, usage experience, thereby strengthening an enduring relationship with customers (VIU). Consequently, this observation suggests the persistence-VIU linkage.

The a<sup>f</sup>ordance of association manifests in the form of connectivity and sense of safety in the case of branded apps. Because branded apps allow users/customers to place an order while on the move, with always-on mobile functionality (connectivity; [58]), ubiquitous connectivity is one crucial determinant in advancing users’ experiential value in the context of m-commerce [59]. In a sense, a branded app a<sup>f</sup>ords customers on-demand access to services and products when they are in a new location. Such an a<sup>f</sup>ordance may ease nervousness and result in a feeling of calmness (sense of safety), thus denoting a psychological association with the app. Both physical and psychological associations with the app (connectivity and sense of safety, respec tively) can promote customers’ sense of value (VIU in our case) by permitting them to perform m-commerce activities regardless of spatial and temporal restrictions [52]. As such, the association a<sup>f</sup>ordance is at the core of value creation for customers because it nurtures a seamless relationship with them [59] and a positive experience [52], hence suggesting the link between association and VIU.

Altogether, each a<sup>f</sup>ordance of branded apps can, to a certain degree, contribute to higher VIU by facilitating the user-to-app relation ship, enriching memorable, cognitive, and emotional experience, and enhancing the level of personalization. Concisely, the preceding arguments establish the link between a<sup>f</sup>ordances and VIU in the context of branded apps. Hence, we put forth <sup>fi</sup>ve hypotheses as follows:

H1a-1e. A<sup>f</sup>ordances of branded apps—(a) visibility, (b) persistence, (c) interactivity, (d) association, and (e) selectivity—are positively related to value-in-use.

## 3.2. Consequences of value-in-use: loyalty (continuance intention and brand loyalty)

Another key link in our model is between VIU and loyalty. Previous literature has widely acknowledged that value is presumed to be a vital prerequisite for loyalty and postconsumption intention (e.g., continuance intention; [60]). In general, the link between value and loyalty is not only well established in the literature (e.g. [60,61],) but also consistent with the nomological structure of the conventional valueloyalty model [62]. In line with this reasoning, SDL literature indicates that a customer and branded app act as resource integrators exchanging information to co-create higher value (i.e., VIU) in online explorations and shopping requests [63], which results in customer continuance with the branded app. Notably, because a branded app is a proxy of its corresponding brand, three building blocks of VIU—personalization, relationship, and experience with the branded app—nurture longstanding loyalty to the brand as well as the value co-creation process.

In essence, given that a branded app can exert its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on customers only if customers indeed continue to use it and generate loyalty toward the brand [22], this study thus focuses on two outcomes—app continuance intention and brand loyalty—to represent loyalty toward both a branded app and a brand, respectively. By extending the value-loyalty link to our model, we conjecture a positive relationship between VIU and app continuance intention and brand loyalty. With regard to continuance intention, it refers to the intention to continue using related services through a speci<sup>fi</sup>c branded app in the future [64]. Beyond support of the link between multifaceted value perceptions and continuance (e.g. [2]), recent work has variously suggested support for the link between experience, personalization, relationship, and continuance intention. Brie<sup>fl</sup>y, experience denotes customers’ emotional and memorable interactions with a branded app. Personalization indicates uniqueness of their actual or perceived use, while relationship denotes reciprocal and iterative processes between them and the app [12]. Indeed, SDL gives priority attention to the matter of understanding and delivering positive and unique customer experiences (i.e., experience and personalization) because it shapes customers’ continuance intention [11]. As Ranjan and Read [12] suggest, personalized proposition and unforgettable experience indeed facilitate recon<sup>fi</sup>guration/production of future app use (i.e., continuance). In support of this view, Fang [22] <sup>fi</sup>nds that a favorable and rewarding customer relationship with a branded app (relationship) cultivates positive customer attitudes toward both the brand and the branded app, thus resulting in repurchase intention and continuance usage intention toward the branded app. Concisely, customers who enjoy personalized, unforgettable experiences and a preferred re lationship with a branded app will perceive global VIU. This should raise their propensity to stick with the app and be continual bene <sup>fi</sup>ciaries of its services [30]. Thus, we propose the following:

## H2a. Value-in-use is positively related to app continuance intention.

Brand loyalty refers to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a brand on customers while they use its branded app, something manifested by recommendations of the brand to others and brand repurchases [65]. Given that brand loyalty signals the longstanding relationship between customers and a brand, it should stand for a desired outcome for both a branded app and marketer in the value co-creation process. Generally, the literature on brand loyalty consistently identi<sup>fi</sup>es value perceptions as the most important determinant [60,61]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when customers assess their interactions with a branded app, they may sense value with respect to their memorable and unique usage experiences (i.e., experience and personalization) and relationship with the app; this eventually helps to build loyalty toward the brand [65]. For example, while using a branded app, customers who received push noti<sup>fi</sup>cations depending on their preferences might sense the uniqueness of the process (personalization), generate impressive experience, and strengthen their relationship with the app as well as the brand. All three values perceived in the use process (VIU) might promote loyalty toward the brand be cause higher customer-brand relationship quality leads to higher levels of brand loyalty [65,66]. Similarly, customers tend to engage in ap proach behaviors (e.g., brand loyalty and brand repurchase) to main tain their relationship with a brand when they derive bene<sup>fi</sup>t from unique and impressive experiences with its branded app [22]. From the preceding reasoning, a positive relationship between VIU and brand loyalty is put forth as follows:

H2b. Value-in-use is positively related to brand loyalty.

## 3.3. Brand perceptions

Another key concept to be explored in this study is brand percep tion, i.e., brand competence and brand warmth. Brand competence refers to the extent to which a customer believes that a brand possesses the necessary ability to deliver on its promise [67]. Brand warmth refers to the belief that a brand has good intentions and positive qualities and that it is perceived as good-natured, friendly, trustworthy, and truthful [68]. According to Cuddy et al. [69], people evaluate others based on perceived competence and warmth. The same dynamics associated with interpersonal relationships now appear in customerbrand relationships. That is, people relate to brands in a similar way they do to people; therefore, they establish relationships with brands [70]. In fact, this application has recently drawn the attention of marketing researchers [18,20]. Scholars also consistently call for an investigation of predictors and outcomes of competence and warmth, as well as an extension to diverse contexts [20]. Following this direction, we integrate brand perceptions with SDL and apply them to the context of branded mobile apps.

In a sense, brand competence, exhibited through credibility and reliability, is analogous to cognitive consideration, whereas brand warmth, manifested through sincerity and friendliness, corresponds to a<sup>f</sup>ective consideration [20]. According to previous research on branded apps, people interacting with new media may lead to cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective brand responses [71]. Similarly, in SDL, customers take both functional and emotional considerations into account when shaping the value co-creation process [72]. This observation justi<sup>fi</sup>es the rationale of incorporating both cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective views while studying the value co-creation process in the context of branded apps. However, no study we are aware of has established and tested the underlying brand perceptions. Given that competence and warmth constitute key building blocks of social perceptions, we postulate that customers infer the warmth and competence of a brand based on value generated from usage of its app (VIU), which ultimately in<sup>fl</sup>uences focal outcomes. The rationale for this proposition is that, beyond direct effects, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of VIU derived from a branded app may spill over to perceptions of the corresponding brand. Overall, this study regards brand competence and brand warmth as the focal interest of cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective perceptions, respectively, which complements the de<sup>fi</sup>- ciency of existing work in this <sup>fi</sup>eld. The next section describes proposed alternative paths, starting from VIU, to either continuance intention or brand loyalty in terms of cognitive or a<sup>f</sup>ective considerations.

## 3.3.1. The cognitive path through brand competence to app continuance intention

The <sup>fi</sup>rst path pertains to cognitive consideration, which represents a more rational means of determining competence perception of a brand based on VIU derived from the use of a branded app, albeit no explicit cues are obtainable for such impression [73]. Although custo mers possessing di<sup>f</sup>erent app knowledge and skills may use the app di<sup>f</sup>erently (user competence; Koo et al., [114]), subjective perceptions of the brand dominate their subsequent responses and decisions [47]. In general, competence is closely related to psychological concepts such as individualism and agency, where the primary focus is on achieving tasks, solving problems, and reaching one’s goal [74,75]. Competence perception is likely to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the self (self-interest [68]) and engender positive brand attitudes [18]. Along the same logic, in the context of branded apps, app continuance intention can denote such self-interest and positive outcomes alongside the cognitive path. Customers are willing to be continual bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries of a branded app if they associate the brand with capable, intelligent, and con<sup>fi</sup>dent traits. This observation thus builds the competence-continuance link, which constructs the cognitive path. This link is also consistent with previous work where cognitive perceptions shape customer continuance app usage (e.g. [2,22]).

In addition, a spillover e<sup>f</sup>ect may occur from VIU to the cognitive path. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, customers generating a higher VIU using a branded app may experience a higher level of cognitive perception of the brand (brand competence), thus inferring that the brand has a higher level of reliability, competitiveness, and farsightedness. We know of no speci<sup>fi</sup>c work in which VIU is tested in direct relationship to brand competence. However, because VIU manifests in the three values of experience (memorable interaction), personalization (uniqueness of the use), and relationship (reciprocal processes with customers; [12]), scholars suggest that a positive experience and relationship with a branded app (VIU, in our case) contributes to a positive inference that the brand is capable of being e<sup>fi</sup>cient, reliable, and dependable [76]. For instance, when using a branded app with embedded GPS, customers who found a local store based on proximity to their current location might notice the advanced competence of the corresponding brand in e<sup>fi</sup>ciently delivering personalized service and superior experience, therefore building a more intense customer-brand relationship. The perceptions of personalization, experience, and relationship during app use process might thereby activate customers’ cognitive inferences about the competence of the brand. Altogether, inasmuch as the preceding arguments can be applied to VIU, brand competence, and app continuance intention, the following hypotheses regarding the cognitive path are drawn:

H3a. Value-in-use is positively related to brand competence.

H3b. Brand competence is positively related to continuance intention.

## 3.3.2. Afective perception: brand warmth

The a<sup>f</sup>ective path focuses on customers’ inferences about the level of warmth they associate with a brand, derived from prior experience and relationship with the branded app as well as their level of mental activity [18]. It is acknowledged that warmth is associated with collectivism and communion, in that it focuses on practices linking relationships, interpersonal behaviors, and interactions [20,74]. Warmth thus links to positive and collaborative intentions [77]. In applying this logic to the context of branded apps, brand loyalty represents the positive brand attitude and collaborative outcome alongside the a<sup>f</sup>ective path. According to Fournier and Alvarez [70], a brand characterized by friendliness and warmth paves the way for customerbrand enhancement and positive brand attitudes. This line of reasoning therefore justi<sup>fi</sup>es the link between brand warmth and brand loyalty. The same logic is supported by Kim [18].

Furthermore, as abovementioned, and alternatively, VIU may spill over into the a<sup>f</sup>ective path as well. Because VIU incorporates the three components experience, personalization, and relationship, customers who obtained a novel and interesting message according to their requests and branded app settings, for example, might also make a<sup>f</sup>ective inferences about the good intentions of the brand (i.e., brand warmth). This is because such attractive and personalized information might stimulate customers’ interest, allow them to have fun with it, and enjoy their use experience, which ultimately leads to their inferences about the friendliness of the corresponding brand. In a sense, customers with a personalized, positive experience and pleasant relationship with a branded app may attribute a<sup>f</sup>ective humanlike features to the corresponding brand [20], bestowing on it good-naturedness, trustworthiness, friendliness, and sincerity [69], which may directly tie their loy alty to the brand. Overall then, the preceding arguments construct the a<sup>f</sup>ective path by linking together VIU, brand warmth, and brand loy alty. Thus,

H4a. Value-in-use is positively related to brand warmth.

H4b. Brand warmth is positively related to brand loyalty.

## 3.4. Control variables

This study involves four descriptive statistical variables as control variables to evaluate the research model. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two variables—gender and age—are speci<sup>fi</sup>ed for controlling potentially spurious a<sup>f</sup>ects in our model because they have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on customers’ intentions to use services or apps [10,51] and, ultimately, brand loyalty [72]. The last two variables are app usage frequency and usage period (the length of usage time with a branded app), which have positive e<sup>f</sup>ects on service continuance [78,79]. Previous studies have acknowledged that a longer service usage period leads to customers accumulation of experiences and knowledge particular to their re lationship with the service (e.g., a branded app; [80]), which implies greater customer competence. Similarly, higher usage frequency—a proxy of past use experience [81] and usage behavior [82]—results in customers’ favorable and positive perceptions when they evaluate service quality [81]. This observation therefore justi<sup>fi</sup>es utilization of usage period and usage frequency as suitable control variables in the case of branded apps. Note that we do not control for income in our model because the two dependent variables are app continuance and brand loyalty, rather than purchase behavior.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Measurement development

A survey method was employed to collect data and empirically test our research hypotheses. Measures of the survey were initially drawn from relevant literature and adapted to <sup>fi</sup>t the context of branded apps (see Appendix A for measures and their sources). Items were measured with a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Usage period was measured by the length of a usage interval, ranging from 1 (less than six months) to 7 (more than four years). Usage frequency was measured by the frequency with which a customer uses the branded app, ranging from 1 (less than once a month) to 7 (several times a day). Notably, interactivity, association, selectivity, and VIU were operationalized as re<sup>fl</sup>ective-formative secondorder constructs [83], which is formed by certain re<sup>fl</sup>ectively speci<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>rst-order dimensions. This operationalization is rational because it is compatible with existing work (e.g. [12,22],) and explicates complex phenomena more parsimoniously [84]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, interactivity is formed by three <sup>fi</sup>rst-order dimensions measured on several re<sup>fl</sup>ective scales, i.e., active control (three items), two-way communication (three items), and synchronicity (three items [22]). In line with the conceptual de<sup>fi</sup>nition of VIU, personalization, experience, and relationship (<sup>fi</sup>ve items for each, adapted from [12]) should be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as forming VIU, rather than the other way around. This operationalization is in line with Ranjan and Read’s [12] work where they “treated. . . ViU as second order formative constructs and estimated the model using re<sup>fl</sup>ective measures on the other side” (p. 304). Association is formed by connectivity (four items) and sense of safety (four items) [42], while selectivity is formed by customization (four items [22]) and localization (four items [44,52]), with all indicators clearly distinguishable, unique, and not interchangeable. Drafted measuring instruments were initially evaluated by a panel of experts including four professors, three mobile users, and six graduate students knowledgeable about the issue of branded apps. On the basis of the comments from the panel, some wording of existing items was modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to more clearly express the intended meaning of the construct.

## 4.2. Survey administration

The survey questionnaire was designed in English, but English-to-Chinese and Chinese-to-English translations were available to ensure all respondents understood its content, despite language-speci<sup>fi</sup>c di<sup>f</sup>erences (e.g., interpretation and nuance). To enhance readability (e.g., ease of understanding, sequence of items, logical consistency, and contextual relevance of the measures), a pretest of the questionnaire was performed using another 28 graduate students familiar with the topic under investigation. After rephrasing ambiguous or inappropriate wording, the formal survey was conducted online using Google docs. We surveyed smartphone users of several chosen branded apps, consisting of those related to Starbucks, Eva Airlines, 7-Eleven, Lion Travel, and MUJI, among others. According to the suggestions of the panel of experts, these branded apps were selected because (1) these brands are well recognized by customers given their innovative services<sup>6</sup> (2) these brands mainly sell experience goods<sup>7</sup> (e.g., food, clothing, and traveling services), which require more search e<sup>f</sup>orts than search goods to be thoroughly evaluated before purchase; and (3) these apps are armed with assorted services such as ubiquitous connections, location-based o<sup>f</sup>erings, interactive and instant responses (to users’ queries), personalized information (e.g., personal favor) storage and retrieval, friendly and customized brand advertising (e.g., timely and relevant push noti<sup>fi</sup>cations), and psychological connections to customers with seamless support. These services provide the building blocks for constructing a<sup>f</sup>ordances of branded apps. Although the brands may be di<sup>f</sup>erent in terms of revenue, their experience goods nature is expected to keep customers engaged with their apps longer, which may involve more intense involvement [85]. By such means, customers of these branded apps may have better opportunities to assess their use experience and related perceptions (e.g., brand competence and brand warmth). To target users of these branded apps, we published our online survey on diverse websites, consisting of various chat rooms, Web forums, and fan pages of these chosen brands.

The cover page of the questionnaire stated the purpose of the study, the identity of the researchers, the length of the questionnaire, the importance of respondent participation, and the incentive and assured the participants of con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality. Because prerequisites for responden eligibility are that respondents must use one of the chosen apps per taining to any experience goods or service within the past month, we applied purposive sampling technique using two screening questions to ensure speci<sup>fi</sup>c criteria. Because the prerequisite for participant elig ibility was that respondents must have used one of the chosen apps pertaining to any experience goods or service within the past month, we applied the purposive sampling technique using two screening ques tions to ensure that speci<sup>fi</sup>c criteria were met. This sampling plan serves the purpose of our study because eligible participants are believed to be representatives of the population of interest [86]. Then, the <sup>fi</sup>rst question asked respondents to indicate whether they had used any of the chosen apps in the past month, and the second asked for their used app and their evaluated products and/or services. Next, eligible respondents were required to recall their most impressive use experience over the last month while answering the survey. On completion, a prize draw for US\$180 worth of gift card was o<sup>f</sup>ered to eligible respondents to improve the quality of survey responses. Finally, 634 completed and valid questionnaires were obtained and used for data analysis. The 634 usable questionnaires consisted of 58.4% male respondents, with an average age of 24.9 years and a standard deviation of 5.9. Of the re spondents, 92.2% had an education level of college or above, and 68.1% were between 20 and 29 years of age. All the respondents were present users of the selected apps, and 56.9% of them had joined the branded app above one year. The demographic information associated with this study is comparable to the sample pro<sup>fi</sup>le of users of similar branded apps published in a recent journal article [22]. Fang’s [22] survey reported that the majority of users were male (64.2%), around the age of 24 years, with a high level of education (83.4% with college or above education). Our demographics are also consistent with the pro<sup>fi</sup>le of app users of a 2017 survey reported by InsightXplorer, a market research <sup>fi</sup>rm, in that mainstream app users were between 20 and 29 years of age<sup>8</sup> and the majority of e-commerce users were male (53.6%).<sup>9</sup> Consequently, our study sample can be regarded as re presentative of users of these branded apps.

## 5. Results

Data analysis was conducted using SmartPLS 3.0 [87] with a twostep approach. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step analyzed the measurement model to establish reliability and validity of the measures, while the second step validated structural relationships among the latent constructs. SmartPLS was chosen because (a) it places minimal restrictions on residual distribution, measurement scales, and sample size [88] and (b) it is preferable for analyses involving complex models, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally higher order or hierarchical component models [89]. Given that our research model includes four re<sup>fl</sup>ective-formative second-order constructs (in teractivity, association, selectivity, and VIU), SmartPLS permits the estimation of further information concerning their dimensions.

## 5.1. Measurement model

Before testing hypotheses, we evaluated the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the measurement model. Findings suggest adequate reliability and convergent validity because Table 2 reveals that all constructs ful<sup>fi</sup>lled the recommended threshold of 0.7 for composite reliability (CR), 0.5 for average variance extracted (AVE), and 0.7 for Cronbach’s alpha (α), whereas all indicator loadings exceeded 0.7 (Appendix A; [90]). Discriminant validity is su<sup>fi</sup>cient given the two analyses we used. First, according to Fornell and Larcker’s [90] procedure, Table 2 shows that the square root of any construct’s AVE exceeded its correlations with other constructs. Second, according to the test of heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT; [91]), Table 3 indicates that the HTMT matrix values were below the threshold of HTMT 0.85, and the upper con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals (CIS) derived from the HTMT bootstrapping procedure were below the criterion of 1.0.

For the formative second-order constructs, taking VIU for example, we treated personalization, experience, and relationship as <sup>fi</sup>rst-order indicators of VIU to tackle the concern that the VIU variables are highly correlated (0.69 in Table 2) and possibly nondiscriminant. This operationalization is justi<sup>fi</sup>ed because formative constructs facilitate the determination of whether any of the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs need to be disregarded owing to high correlations [92]. Hence, we conclude that there is su<sup>fi</sup>cient construct validity concerning the scales. In addition, according to Petter et al.’s [84] formative measurement guidelines, the validity of association, interactivity, selectivity, and VIU as formative second-order constructs were assessed by (1) the magnitude of path weights and correlations among the second-order constructs and their corresponding <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs and (2) the determination of multicollinearity among the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs. First, all the path weights between these four second-order constructs and their associated <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs (ranging from 0.34 to 0.61) were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (Fig. 2). As Chin [93] states, the magnitude and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the weights are indicative of the relative importance of the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs toward the formation of their respective second-order constructs, which validates the reliability of the constructs. In addition, the bivariate correlations between these second-order constructs and their associated <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs range from 0.83 to 0.93, indicating signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly absolute relationships between them. Second, a collinearity test was conducted using the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF) generated by SPSS when regressing the means of the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs on the mean of the repeated indicators of their corresponding second-order constructs. The VIFs, ranging from 1.52 to 2.53, are well below the 3.3 threshold [94]. Finally, we examined whether these four second-order constructs fully mediated the impact of their respective <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructors on the dependent variable. Regression analysis showed these four second-order constructs to be the only signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors when including the associated <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs in the model. This observation suggests that the second-order construct model is a more parsimonious representation of the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs and optimizes their predictive power on the dependent variable [93].

To minimize common method bias in survey design, as well as avoid ambiguity and acquiescence problems, several reverse-scored measurement items were added to the questionnaire [95], and an expert review was conducted before administration of the formal survey. After the data were collected, the correlation matrix (Table 2) did not reveal any highly correlated constructs, (e.g., r > 0.90), thereby implying the nonexistence of common method bias [96]. Harman’s one-factor test was then employed to detect the risk of common method bias by entering all the principal constructs into a principal components factor analysis [97]. The result indicated that the <sup>fi</sup>rst (largest) factor explained 9.2% of the variance, which is below the suggested threshold of 50%. When all 16 factors were considered, the variance explained in creased to 91.2%. Because the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor did not explain the majority of the variance, we conclude that common method bias is not a threat to the validity of our study. Furthermore, VIF and condition number analyses were used to detect multicollinearity. In this study, VIF (ran ging from 1.47 to 3.08) is less than the criterion of 3.3 [94], while the condition number (5.83) is less than 10. Therefore, multicollinearity is not a serious concern in this study either.

## 5.2. Structural model

In PLS analysis, the explanatory power of a structural model is predominantly assessed by the $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ values and structural paths of endogenous variables. Fig. 2 shows that all paths were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , providing support for all hypotheses in our model. The $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ values for VIU (0.69), brand competence (0.45), brand warmth (0.44), continuance intention (0.53), and brand loyalty (0.48) explain a sub stantial proportion of the variance, as well as verify the validity and explanatory power of the proposed model. In addition, the goodness-of-<sup>fi</sup>t (GoF) index (0.60), computed from the geometric mean of the average communality and average $\scriptstyle \mathrm { \mathrm { R } } ^ { 2 }$ (for endogenous constructs), is above the 0.36 criterion for a large e<sup>f</sup>ect size [98], thus supporting global validity and <sup>fi</sup>t of the PLS-based complex model. Ultimately, Stone-Geisser's $Q ^ { 2 }$ value [99] was calculated by a blindfolding procedure to judge the predictive power of the model. $Q ^ { 2 }$ values for VIU (0.32), brand competence (0.30), brand warmth (0.33), brand loyalty (0.33), and continuance intention (0.37) are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly above both 0.15 and 0.35, indicating medium-to-large or large predictive relevance, respectively, for the model [89].<sup>10</sup> Concisely then, the values of $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } ,$ , GoF, and $Q ^ { 2 }$ substantiate the reasonably high quality of the proposed model. An extended analysis using AMOS was conducted to generate the model <sup>fi</sup>t indices for the structural model. Overall, the indices were within the acceptable thresholds recommended by Chau [112]: $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { D F } = 2 . 6 0 ~ ( \leq 3 )$ , NNFI = 0.90 (≧0.9), CFI = 0.91 (≧0.9), AGFI = 0.83 (≧0.8), and RMSEA = 0.05 (≦0.1), indicating su<sup>fi</sup>cient model <sup>fi</sup>t for the structural model.<sup>11</sup>

Mediation analyses for brand competence and brand warmth were performed by Preacher and Hayes’ [100] bootstrapping approach with 5000 bootstrap resamples and bias-corrected con<sup>fi</sup>dence estimates [95% CI]. The indirect e<sup>f</sup>ect is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant if the CI for the mediating e<sup>f</sup>ect does not include zero. Our results provide support for signi<sup>fi</sup>cant mediation on both the cognitive path (i.e., the path emphasizing brand competence; e<sup>f</sup>ect value = 0.08; 95% CI of 0.03 and 0.14) and a<sup>f</sup>ective path $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , the path emphasizing brand warmth; e<sup>f</sup>ect value = 0.11; 95% CI of 0.05 and 0.17). Both direct e<sup>f</sup>ects of VIU—on app continuance intention (e<sup>f</sup>ect value decreasing from 0.67 to 0.59) and brand loyalty (e<sup>f</sup>ect value decreasing from 0.67 to 0.56)—decrease but remain signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (t-values = 26.86 and 24.2, respectively) when controlling for the corresponding mediator. Results indicate partial mediation for both brand competence and brand loyalty and con<sup>fi</sup>rm that VIU indeed in<sup>fl</sup>uences app continuance and brand loyalty along both the cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective paths.

## 6. Discussion and implications

Despite branded apps being a notable emerging service for brand marketing, our literature review indicated a lack of theoretical understanding on how a branded app facilitates customer loyalty toward a branded app and its brand from the perspective of SDL. Against this background, we devised an integrated model by incorporating SDL, the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance, along with brand warmth and competence to shed theoretical and managerial light on this issue. Study <sup>fi</sup>ndings are noteworthy for further discussions, as are the theoretical implications and certain guidelines for further research.

The results yield a rich set of insights. First, our results suggest that customers of a branded app indeed engage in app continuance and brand loyalty, with a mean of 5.44 and 5.35, respectively. Such a high mean implies that customers’ continuance with a branded app and corresponding brand can pave a way for them to achieve value cocreation via the app platform.

Second, considering antecedents of these two outcomes, the extent of variance in continuance intention $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 5 3 \% )$ and brand loyalty $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 4 8 \% )$ suggests that the proposed VIU, brand competence, and brand warmth are potentially among the most crucial antecedents in the case of branded apps. In particular, our expectation, based on SDL, that VIU can contribute to predict users’ loyalty toward branded apps and respective brands is con<sup>fi</sup>rmed. Indeed, VIU, formed by personalization, experience, and relationship, holds the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential role in increasing customers’ app continuance $( \mathrm { \beta } = 0 . 5 9 ;$ H2a) and their loyalty toward a brand $( \beta = 0 . 5 6 ; \mathrm { H } 2 \mathrm { b } )$ . On the other hand, brand competence $( \beta = 0 . 1 2 ;$ H3b) and warmth $( \beta = 0 . 1 6 ;$ H4b) have relatively less in<sup>fl</sup>uence on each corresponding outcome (Fig. 2). With these path coe<sup>fi</sup>cient comparison results, it can be concluded that VIU plays a more promising role than other factors in explaining customer loyalty. This observation also suggests that the applicability of SDL and related constructs (e.g., VIU) advances the current work in branded apps because such an extension is needed for a deeper understanding of antecedents of app continuance [10] and brand loyalty.<sup>12</sup>

Third, alongside the full model evaluated, which further included both cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective paths, was a model without the two brand perceptions (competence and warmth) to continuance intention $( R ^ { 2 } = 5 1 . 9 \% )$ and brand loyalty $( R ^ { 2 } = 4 6 . 5 \% )$ . We compared the $R ^ { 2 }$ of both models to evaluate the incremental validity of adding the brand perceptions. The e<sup>f</sup>ect sizes $[ f ^ { \ 2 }$ (0.02 and 0.03)] of the di<sup>f</sup>erences between $R ^ { 2 }$ values (1.1% and 1.5%) lie between the small and medium e<sup>f</sup>ect size. As Chin et al. [101] note, a small $f ^ { 2 }$ does not necessarily suggest an unimportant e<sup>f</sup>ect. We provide evidence for the contribution of $R ^ { 2 }$ values pertaining to both paths to continuance intention and brand loyalty. Concisely, the full model indeed possesses relatively higher explanatory power than the model without both cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective paths. Further mediation tests<sup>13</sup> also conclude that brand competence and warmth can act as partial mediators of the relationships between VIU and the two loyalty outcomes. This observation thus adds extra value to the previous research on branded apps, beyond the direct link between VIU and loyalty.

Fourth, following the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the <sup>fi</sup>ve antecedents of VIU—visibility, persistence, interactivity, association, and selectivity—in the context of branded apps. Our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that all the proposed app a<sup>f</sup>ordances are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors of VIU (H1a-1e) and their high explanatory power on VIU $( R ^ { 2 } = 6 9 \% )$ Comparing the importance of the <sup>fi</sup>ve app a<sup>f</sup>ordances to VIU, interactivity plays a leading role (H1c), followed by visibility, selectivity, persistence, and then association. On the one hand, this observation is consistent with Fang’s [22] <sup>fi</sup>ndings concerning the dominant role of interactivity in supporting brand-customer communication through branded apps. Our results regarding the link between selectivity and VIU (H1e) are also compatible with those of previous work (e.g., [44,52]) that found customization and localization to be positive dri vers facilitating diverse customer values in the context of mobile systems. On the other hand, our results reveal that the other a<sup>f</sup>ordances (e.g., visibility, persistence, and association), more or less, enhance customers’ VIU when they use the branded apps, which have been largely unexplored in existing work. The new <sup>fi</sup>ndings not only empirically support our proposed hypotheses (H1a, H1b, and H1d) but also add value to the academic literature.

## 6.1. Implications for theory

This study is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst scholarly e<sup>f</sup>orts in the direction of exploring branded apps from the perspective of SDL, rather than the conventional view of GDL. In a sense, branded apps are characterized by digitally enabled services of a multidisciplinary nature [102] playing a transformative role in developing, designing, and delivering services [11]. It seems to be insu<sup>fi</sup>cient to scrutinize issues related to branded apps without involving SDL and relevant factors. Hence, we followed Lusch and Vargo’s [5] suggestions for moving beyond GDL by adopting a more expansive viewpoint of branded apps that transcends the tangible-intangible divide and shifts to the service foci of experience, customer, and network orientations [6]. Accordingly, the theoretical model we tested integrates the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance, SDL, and two particular brand perceptions to advance understanding of antecedents of branded app continuance and brand loyalty. As Taylor and Strutton [24] suggest, such a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed model incorporating interdisciplinary notions is warranted because of its superior capability of enlightening our understanding of customer behaviors. Indeed, our results are of great value because, to date, the majority of work in branded apps has applied the technology-as-goods view (GDL), emphasizing tangible output, discrete transactions, and value-in-exchange [5]. This study thus takes a step forward in this direction by focusing on VIU and ap plying the perspective of SDL.

Connecting back to the literature and previous conceptual research on SDL, a key contribution of this study lies in its extension of the boundary of SDL. Our study empirically illustrates the e<sup>f</sup>ects of VIU on its consequent variables in the new context of branded apps. In line with Vargo and Lusch’s [30] proposition, our results empirically con <sup>fi</sup>rm that users’ VIU—derived from personalized, unforgettable experiences and preferred relationship with a branded app—not only enhances their tendency to continue using an app for the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial services it o<sup>f</sup>ers them but also nurtures an unwavering loyalty toward the brand. In addition, as Achrol and Kotler [103] remind us, only certain parts of SDL can be realized; more theory and methodologies are warranted to help enhance our knowledge of the relational aspect of SDL. To heed this call and strengthen the claim of SDL, we applied the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance to propose the <sup>fi</sup>ve antecedents of VIU because affordances can better characterize the multifaceted relational structure between a branded app and the user/customer that in<sup>fl</sup>uences possible outcomes in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context $[ 1 6 , 1 7 ]$ ]. We thus found that the proposed a<sup>f</sup>ordances of branded apps collectively explain substantial variance in VIU $( R ^ { 2 } = 6 9 \% )$ , thus supporting the applicability of the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance to elucidate VIU. Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings broaden the original context of existing a<sup>f</sup>ordance research to the context of branded apps.

Another valuable contribution is that this study signi<sup>fi</sup>es an extension of the realm of brand anthropomorphism into the context of branded apps by taking brand competence and brand warmth into consideration while studying the value co-creation process. Beyond showing the direct e<sup>f</sup>ects of VIU on two loyalty outcomes, we provide empirical evidence that a user’s VIU also translates well into app continuance and brand loyalty through corresponding brand competence and brand warmth, i.e., the cognitive path and a<sup>f</sup>ective path. Moreover, this investigation not only advances prior conceptual work of Grönroos and Voima $\left[ 1 0 4 \right] ^ { 1 4 }$ by empirical evidence but also agrees with van Noort’s [71] claim that the cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective responses indeed occur in customers’ interaction with new media. By being among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to map brand competence and warmth to cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective considerations, our empirical study directs a promising avenue for academics to incorporate the humanization of brands to increase our knowledge of how new media can facilitate positive brand perceptions and resultant loyalty. Furthermore, this study adds value to the sphere of brand anthropomorphism and brand humanization to address the call for further exploration of antecedents and outcomes of competence and warmth in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts [20]. By showing that VIU alone explains substantial amounts of variance in brand competence $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 5 )$ and brand warmth $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 4 )$ , our results suggest that VIU may be the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential predictor of competence and warmth in the context of branded apps. Our results can serve as a springboard for future research to examine the relationships in other environments.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

As illustrated by our study, for app designers and marketers, a priority to strive for is to seek access to users’/customers’ value sphere (VIU). According to Grönroos and Voima [104], beyond existing direct interactions with customers, companies should endeavor to create extra interactions. Our study shows that branded apps could be such an avenue to establish additional connections with customers and o<sup>f</sup>ers design directions for practitioners. In a sense, although companies and app designers cannot exclusively control the construction of VIU because it mainly rests in the mind of the customer, they can in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ perceptions of their apps, products, and services, along with their brand, by actively managing the value co-creation process made possible by their apps. Given that our results reveal that the <sup>fi</sup>ve app a<sup>f</sup>ordances remarkably contribute to customer VIU, app designers and marketers can take advantage of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by understanding that how they should shape branded app design. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, while the role of interactivity is prominent, and the other four a<sup>f</sup>ordances (visibility, persistence, association, and selection) have relatively less in<sup>fl</sup>uence on VIU, it is inappropriate for app designers to conclude that less attention can be paid to them. Instead, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be attributed to the currently insu<sup>fi</sup>cient development of branded apps in supporting the a<sup>f</sup>ordances of visibility, persistence, association, and selection to advance customers’ VIU. This observation o<sup>f</sup>ers a new creative avenue for app designers because it is advisable to employ novel technologies (e.g., arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence [AI] system) and other advanced mechanisms (e.g., Siri, requesting systems [105], and location-dependent services) in support of app-user interaction, such as easing the mind of users with precise location-sensitive and customized information, as well as enhanced e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of information accessibility and recordability. Hence, continuous attention must be paid to these a<sup>f</sup>ordances for practical considerations.

Our key <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding the applicability of SDL and the sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cance of VIU in branded apps can be of special interest to marketers and branded app designers. Our results suggest that VIU is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to enhance customer loyalty toward a branded app and its associated brand. In SDL, customers must actively engage in the value co-creation process, which is phenomenologically dependent on themselves [7]. This observation presents a challenge for marketers and designers because for a customer to be involved in the co-creation process, she/he must not only download and use the app but log in to the app for marketers and designers to acquire her/his feedback and usage information. For instance, Starbucks’s app allows a user/customer to keep personalized and favorite menu items in a database and download the latest Starbucks news through his/her smartphone. Starbucks, therefore, has more opportunities to co-create value with the customer because the company can o<sup>f</sup>er suitable customized services and customer care that generate better experiences, thereby retaining a close relationship with the customer through the app.

Concerning the extension of brand humanization, our study takes a preliminary step by empirically supporting not only the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of brand competence on app continuance but also that of brand warmth on brand loyalty. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide promising design guidance for app designers in that, beyond functional features alone, what is demanded are new mechanisms that make branded apps more humanlike. For example, app designers can improve the way services and com munication are presented to customers by incorporating AI into interactions (e.g., an AI chat system) to create an experience that has the quality of feeling more humanlike [105]. In a sense, a branded app delivering brand warmth to customers generates better opportunities to cultivate loyalty toward a brand, a desired goal among marketers due to its crucial role in relationship marketing and expanding existing markets [106]. Hence, we encourage app designers and marketers to pay continuous attention to the emerging humanization trend in designing and developing branded apps.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research directions

Several study limitations warrant future research. First, this study predominantly integrates the lens of a<sup>f</sup>ordance, SDL, and brand per ceptions to study app continuance intention and brand loyalty of current users of branded apps. Former users who ceased using the apps might have di<sup>f</sup>erent thoughts regarding our proposed relationships. Notably, the results should be interpreted as merely illuminating app continuance intention and brand loyalty of current users. Promising avenues for future research lie in (a) studying in<sup>fl</sup>uential factors of app usage and brand loyalty from the standpoint of former users, and (b) using other applicable theories to extend our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Second, with regard to the humanization of brands, this study considers merely brand competence and brand warmth in terms of cognitive concern and af fective concern in this model. In the context of branded apps, future research is encouraged to explore other humanlike brand concepts (e.g., brand personality) along the cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective paths in the hope of contributing to research on both brand anthropomorphism and branded apps. Third, the <sup>fi</sup>ve a<sup>f</sup>ordances proposed in this study are particularly developed for the context of branded apps. Whether these a<sup>f</sup>ordance can be generalized to apply to other types of platforms (e.g., fans pages, online stores) requires additional research because inherent distinction (e.g., characteristics exclusive to other online stores) occur between

them.

Fourth, this study mainly focuses on examining app continuance intention because even though the <sup>fi</sup>rst-time usage of a branded app is a crucial <sup>fi</sup>rst step toward achieving its success, its long-term viability and eventual success rely on customers’ continuance usage [107]. For a branded app, <sup>fi</sup>rst-time usage represents user acquirement, something that can convert potential users to actual users, whereas continuance usage denotes customer retention and the conversion of users to loyal customers [108]. First-time users might have di<sup>f</sup>erent concerns about the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the <sup>fi</sup>ve a<sup>f</sup>ordances of a branded app. Future research could compare our proposed relationships between <sup>fi</sup>rst-time users and loyal users. Fifth, this study currently incorporates only four control variables (gender, age, usage frequency, and usage period) in the research model. An interesting area for future research is to extend the research model by including other factors (e.g., prior attitude toward the branded app, income, and technology orientation) and investigating their possible e<sup>f</sup>ects on app continuance and brand loyalty. In addition, IS literature has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the importance of user competence—in cluding knowledge breadth, depth, and <sup>fi</sup>nesse of a user in a particular domain [114]—in IS and technology usage. To date, the extant literature on brand anthropomorphism and brand humanization has not taken user competence into consideration while investigating brand competence. Future research is needed to verify whether user competence in<sup>fl</sup>uences brand competence and service continuance in the context of branded apps and other digitally enabled services as well.
