# Cognitive engagement with a multimedia ERP training tool: Assessing computer self-efficacy and technology acceptance

Judy E. Scott \*, Steven Walczak

The Business School, University of Colorado Denver, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 16 November 2005 Received in revised form 27 June 2007 Accepted 9 October 2008 Available online 14 March 2009

Keywords: Computer self-efficacy Cognitive engagement Technology acceptance ERP Training tools Neural network

## A B S T R A C T

Computer self-efficacy (CSE) is a person’s judgment of his or her ability to use a computer system. We investigated cognitive engagement, prior experience, computer anxiety, and organizational support as determinants of CSE in the use of a multimedia ERP system’s training tool. We also examined the impact of CSE on its acceptance. We determined the benefits of a sequential multi-method approach using structural equation modeling and neural network analysis. High reliability predictions of individual CSE were achieved with a sequential multi-method approach. Specifically, we obtained almost 68% perfect CSE group prediction overall, with almost 85% perfect CSE group prediction using fuzzy sets and over 94% accuracy within one group classification. The resulting CSE assessment and classification enables management interventions, such as allocating users to appropriate instruction for more effective training.

## 1. Introduction

ERP systems are deployed in over 70% of all U.S. medium to large corporations; they spent about \$51 billion on formal training in 2004 and 38.4% of that was for end-users. End-user training normally accounts for 30% of ERP project costs, but organizations that spend less than 15% are likely to have inadequately trained users resulting in implementation delays and escalating costs. However, very little research has focused on the use of multimedia technology for training. Given this gap, we decided to investigate user engagement and acceptance of a multimedia ERP training tool.

We built on past research by incorporating user engagement in TAM. In fact, perceived ease of use (PEOU) is strongly anchored to general beliefs about computers, such as CSE, an individual selfassessment of ability to use a computer. Low CSE may hinder computer learning. Consequently, assessing CSE and its determinants could help an organization understand the role of PEOU on acceptance of a multimedia ERP training tool.

The training tool we tested was professionally produced for an ERP vendor; it included movie clips, audio enhanced presentations and screen cams. Prior research had suggested that experience, computer anxiety (CA), and organizational support were important antecedents to CSE, though empirical results had shown inconsistency. We analyzed this and proposed cognitive engagement as a critical determinant of CSE in the use of a multimedia-training tool. Therefore, our goal was to improve understanding of the role of multimedia technology in user engagement during ERP training and to assess CSE as potential inhibitors or enablers of the use and acceptance of a training tool.

Our research questions were: (1) ‘‘Does engagement influence CSE?,’’ (2) ‘‘What other factors affect CSE?,’’ (3) ‘‘Does CSE in a multimedia ERP training context affect system acceptance?’’ and (4) ‘‘How can CSE classification be derived from engagement and support measures?’’.

To examine these questions, we employed two analytic methods, with the results from the first, SEM, feeding into a neural network analysis, NN. This was therefore a sequential multimethod research design. SEMs strength in path analysis was used to help answer the first three research questions while NNs strength in classification was needed to answer the last question. Since different analytic methods focus on different aspects of reality, a richer understanding of the topic could be gained by combining methods. Mingers advocated that research situations were inherently complex and multidimensional and would benefit from a range of methods [18]. By combining methodologies we can develop a method where ‘‘the advantages of one analysis technique offset the disadvantages of another’’ [4].

## 2. Conceptual development

CSE is personal judgment of ability to use a computer. Obviously it is affected by the users’ perception of the task and the training received [17]. CSE is thus influenced by expectations of the outcomes of using a computer, and emotional reaction to the activity [8]. CSE predicts user acceptance of the Internet and perception of multimedia-training effectiveness. Judgments of self-efficacy serve as key antecedents of PEOU. Similarly, initial general CSE belief will strongly predict subsequent specific CSE belief [3] and an individual’s PEOU of a particular system affects his/her general CSE. Empirical results show CSE is one of the strongest factors of use.

In our study we focused on CSE as a malleable state that was an outcome of using the training tool.

## 2.1. Antecedents to CSE

Prior research has identified prior experience, CA, organizational support and constructs related to engagement as antecedents to CSE. See Tables 1–5 for references. Individuals vary in these factors, while organizations vary in the amount of support they provide to users.

CA and cognitive engagement (sustained attention to a task requiring mental effort, intrinsic interest and curiosity) are associated with emotions. On the other hand, absorption, enjoyment and playfulness may satisfy basic human needs for competence and autonomy. Intrinsic motivation makes the activity interesting and likely to be performed for its own sake. In contrast, social influence, such as organizational support is a form of extrinsic motivation, because it is perceived to be instrumental in achieving valued outcomes that are distinct from the activity.

The model for our study and its hypotheses are shown in Fig. 1. We hypothesized that one can predict the CSE outcome from four antecedents: prior experience, CA, engagement with the system, and organizational support. We controlled for demographic (age and gender) and data collection variables.

## 2.1.2. Computer anxiety

CA manifests itself as a reluctance to use computers because of fear and apprehension of making mistakes. However, some studies suggest uncertainty as to its relevance today. The 4- to 8-item subsets of the 19-item CA Rating Scale (CARS) has provided higher reliability scores than the original CARS, which had multidimensional issues.

Table 2 shows that empirically the results are mixed. Studies differed on whether they treat CA as an individual trait or a malleable state specific to the training [3,29]. Some found support for an inverse relationship between CA and CSE; a significant correlation between CA and CSE (about -0.30) [26], (-0.71) [10];

CA as an antecedent of CSE [6,24]; or CA as a consequence of CSE. The complex reciprocal relationship created confusion on whether CA was an antecedent to or a consequence of CSE. Although CA could arise from low CSE [8,15], theoretically CSE determines behavior, so we hypothesized CA as an antecedent to CSE as having more theoretical support and we considered both CA and CSE as malleable states specific to the ERP training tool setting:

## H2. CA negatively affects CSE.

Researchers have measured OS as a subset of control and include ‘‘knowledge’’ as a measure of an important resource along with availability of time in its formative measures of perceived resources [16,26]. However, ‘‘knowledge’’ in OS may overlap with CSE because proficiency is an often-used measure of CSE. Assistance, help, influence and encouragement are other measures of OS [8,12,21,22]. The type of technology may influence the results. For example, a study in a professional association bulletin board context found ‘‘help’’ was not significant.

The sources of support have been systems staff (not significant [21]), support staff and co-workers (negative), management [12] or superiors [23]. Since management and superiors are significant sources of support while systems staff are not, the source of support should be explicit. Although it is not clear why some results were significant but negative, most of the studies in Table 3 find that OS does influence CSE. Consequently we hypothesize:

H3. Organizational support has a positive effect on CSE.

## 2.1.4. Cognitive engagement

The richness of multimedia technology stimulates the senses thereby encouraging cognitive engagement [5]. Intrinsic interest, curiosity, focused attention; concentration and absorption in a task indicate engagement. Table 4 shows that cognitive engagement is differentiated from involvement, flow and cognitive absorption. Involvement does not rely on intrinsic interest although focused attention is a characteristic. Cognitive absorption is associated with positive attitudes and greater exploratory use of technology as well as perceived usefulness (PU) and PEOU of technology. It is theoretically derived from research on (1) the propensity to experience the personality trait of absorption, (2) the state of flow whereby time disappears and (3) cognitive engagement. Unlike cognitive absorption, engagement does not include measurement items for control or temporal dissociation, which describe unawareness of the passage of time. Table 5 shows intrinsic motivators (e.g. engagement, cognitive absorption, playfulness) and the relationships with CSE.

The following argument suggests that cognitive engagement would increase CSE. Multimedia tools are engaging and fun to use [5,28]. Learning theory explains that you can motivate learning with tools that are fun to use [1]. The resulting positive attitudes associated with intrinsic motivation, intrinsic interest and focused attention will improve competence, which is a basic need and essential for CSE. Game-based training has been related to heightened enjoyment and perceived enjoyment having a positive effect on application-specific self-efficacy has empirical support [30]. Further, individuals with characteristics of playfulness and Personal Innovativeness in Information Technology (PIIT) show greater cognitive absorption and have higher CSE [24,29]. Given that a multimedia tool can trigger playfulness and cognitive engagement [5,28], which is a subset of cognitive absorption, we propose that there is theoretical support for cognitive engagement enhancing CSE.

H4. Cognitive engagement has a positive effect on CSE.

## 2.2. PEOU and PU

High CSE promotes PEOU because users who rate their computer ability as competent perceive that less effort is required in using computers. Multimedia ERP training tools differ from other technologies in terms of their availability, purpose, and sensory aspects. Therefore, it is necessary to confirm whether its PEOU is affected by the CSE. Thus:

H5. CSE has a positive effect on PEOU of the multimedia ERP training tool.

Drawing on social cognitive theory, high CSE users have high outcome expectancies when they execute behaviors using technology. Consequently, we hypothesized:

H6. CSE has a positive effect on PU of the multimedia ERP training tool.

PEOU also affects PU because users believe that the technology is more useful when it requires less effort. Consequently we hypothesized:

H7. PEOU has a positive effect on PU of the multimedia ERP training tool.

People are more likely to have an intention to use a technology that facilitates their work and does not require excessive effort. Thus:

H8. PEOU has a positive effect on intention to use the multimedia ERP training tool.

H9. PU has a positive effect on intention to use the multimedia ERP training tool.

## 2.3. Control and data collection variables

Prior results are mixed on gender or age as determinants of CSE. For example, although some studies found gender and age moderate technology acceptance [27], gender was not always significant in predicting CSE [13,25]. In environments where males have had more access to technology and more encouragement to use it, females have lower CSE on average. Similarly, younger individuals having grown up with more exposure to computers are supposedly more comfortable with technology. On the other hand, education and the increasing pervasiveness of computers tend to overcome gender and age effects and since the participants in this study are relatively young high achieving college students we do not expect any significant differences due to age or gender. Graduate status and the year and semester of data collection are included in the model to see whether we can justify pooling of the data.

This study tests the hypotheses that prior experience, CA, OS, and engagement are antecedents and predictors to assess CSE. In addition, we use a NN to verify that CSE can be predicted from these antecedents. The NN analysis is also used to estimate CSE scores, which assign individuals to training groups. In the next sections we explain the methodology used in this study, including demographics of the sample, description of the measures, and the results of partial least squares structural equation modeling (SEM) and NN analysis.

## 3. Methodology

The data were collected as part of a larger study on technology acceptance of an ERP multimedia-training tool. The tool was professionally produced by the ERP vendor to include movie clips, audio enhanced presentations and screen cams.

Similar to many published TAM and training studies that use student samples, we sampled undergraduate and graduate students in ERP classes, who had access to the multimedia ERP training tool. Use of the tool was not mandatory, since students could choose to complete their assignments without the tool. The study consisted of a survey which students completed in class at the end of the semester. They were not given incentives to participate.

## 3.1. Sample

Students taking a hands-on ERP course are reasonably representative of organizational ERP users undergoing training. The sample consisted of 239 students taking an ERP course elective in the information systems undergraduate and graduate programs of a major state university in the southwest of the United States. Data were collected from all (four undergraduate and four graduate) ERP classes over four semesters. Some students were absent and some chose to not respond to the survey questions resulting in a response rate of 98%. Three responses were dropped because of incomplete information on prior experience, and two responses with outliers for CSE were dropped (determined using SPSS) resulting in 234 usable surveys. The sample consisted of about 50% each of graduates and undergraduates and approximately two-thirds males. Almost 50% were age 23 years or older and 99% had more than 1 year of PC experience.

## 3.2. Measures

The constructs of CSE and its antecedents, prior experience, CA, OS, and engagement, depicted in the model in Fig. 1, were operationalized in a survey. As much as possible, validated measures from prior research were used. Items were chosen that were applicable and meaningful for the context. See Appendix 2 for details and sources of the measures.

We considered prior experience with CBT, PC, application software and work experience as relevant for the multimedia ERP training tool. Four survey questions measured prior experience along a 4-point scale: from ‘‘1 = none’’ to ‘‘4 = More than 3 years.’’ We reasoned that since ERP training usually takes place in an organizational context, work experience would increase the likelihood that the student would understand the business processes embedded in the ERP software and be more amenable to training and enactive mastery of the training tool. However, the business process in the training session was customer order management, which is not demanding in terms of work experience. Multimedia ERP training tools are not widely available so prior experience specific to the technology tested is unlikely. We do not measure experience at two different points in time because of restrictions on the availability of the tool. A 5-point scale was considered adequate for capturing the age distribution of the sample.

All other survey questions used a 7-point Likert scale for responses, as do the prior studies that used those measures. The 10-item CSE instrument was not applicable for the ERP multimedia-training tool context, since it refers to completing the job using the software package if certain conditions were available such as ‘‘someone else had helped me get started.’’ Instead five survey questions for CSE about judgments of ‘‘proficiency’’ and ‘‘confidence’’ from the training tool were adapted from prior research. For measuring CA, we adapted two survey questions that focused on the most important concepts ‘‘hesitation’’ and ‘‘apprehensiveness’’ [28]. Three survey questions about ‘‘providing resources,’’ ‘‘realizing benefits’’ and ‘‘encouragement’’ used in prior research measured organizational support. Six survey questions about ‘‘absorption,’’ ‘‘attention,’’ ‘‘fun’’ and ‘‘interesting’’ measured engagement. These questions were applicable since the multimedia and playfulness contexts from those studies were similar to the context in this study.

## 4. Analytic methods and results

This study used a multi-analytic approach, partial least squares (PLS), a type of SEM, and neural network (NN) analysis. SEM tests the hypotheses and the reliability of the measures, while NN analysis verifies the antecedents as predictors of CSE, estimates CSE scores, and assigns individuals to groups based on their CSE scores.

SEM is a methodology for analyzing latent variables, such as CSE and CA, which cannot be measured directly. PLS is appropriate because unlike SEM using covariance-based LISREL, AMOS and other similar packages, component-based PLS makes no assumptions of a normal distribution of the data. Furthermore, PLS using an iterative algorithm of a series of ordinary least square analyses avoids problems of inadmissible solutions and factor indeterminacy. In this study, we used PLS Graph Version 3.00 to estimate the reliability of the indicators and the statistical significance of the proposed antecedents of CSE.

We then turned to NN analysis to assess CSE scores and use the scores to classify users into groups. NN analysis is capable of learning complex linear and nonlinear associations between input and output variables. This approach is non-parametric and hence population and error distributions are not required in advance of building the model unlike regression and discriminant analysis, which require normally distributed data or heterogeneous normally distributed error terms. Further, NN analysis helps to mitigate CSE measurement issues, as described next.

The critical factor in developing a high quality NN model is determining the required input variables to accurately model the problem in the NN. Thus, the decision on the input variables to the NN is based on the results from the PLS analysis determination of the statistically reliable and significant hypothesized antecedents. However, rather than inputting CSE values to the NN, the NN uses inputs that are easier to measure objectively. For instance, prior experience and organizational support are easier to measure than CSE because they are more objective and are less dependent on perceptions and do not threaten the ego. In contrast, people have a tendency to avoid situations that expose low CSE feelings to save themselves embarrassment. As a result, self-assessment of CSE introduces possible biases, since people are likely to overestimate their proficiency and confidence. An NN analysis helps to overcome these CSE measurement issues.

## 4.1. Structural equation modeling

SEM was used to analyze a measurement model and a structural model. The measurement model determines whether the measures used were reliable and whether the discriminant validity was adequate. The loading on its construct assesses the reliability of an indicator. Loading values should be at least 0.60 and ideally at 0.70 or above indicating that each measure was accounting for 50% or more of the variance of the underlying latent variable. Two indicators for CSE were dropped because loadings on the construct were lower than the threshold. Similarly, CBT experience and business applications experience were dropped since their loading on prior experience was lower than the acceptable value.

In Table 6, composite reliability scores show high reliability for the final constructs. All values for composite reliability are greater than 0.8 (except prior experience which is measured with unrelated formative indicators that are not expected to highly correlate with each other). The diagonal of the correlation matrix shows the square root of the AVE (average variance extracted). The ‘‘square root of the AVE’’ values are much greater than the correlations among the constructs supporting convergent and discriminant validity. ## 4.2. Neural networks analysis

As mentioned previously, the selection of input (independent) variables that are related to the prediction or classification goal (dependent variable), but not highly correlated with each other is critical for optimizing the performance of any NN model. We leverage PLS as an antecedent statistical method for determining the independent variables for the NN analysis. The fully specified NN model is included in the analysis, even though it includes antecedents to CSE that PLS did not find statistically significant, because some prior research claims that NNs are capable of selfdetermining the importance of independent variables and should therefore be given any information that could possibly be related to the dependent variable(s).

The network architectures for the 5 input variable NN model representing the engagement construct and the 7 input variable model representing the combination of the engagement and CA constructs are displayed in Fig. 3. The development of all 36 combinations of input variable models follows the same methodology used for development of the single construct models and provides an evaluation of the utility provided by the PLS analysis of the constructs and their corresponding variables.

Further details on the NN analysis and results are reported in Appendix 1.

## 5. Discussion

Our results show the importance of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation to ERP training. Using SEM, we found that cognitive engagement, a form of intrinsic motivation, and organizational support, a form of extrinsic motivation, were statistically significant computer self-efficacy determinants. Using these results as inputs to a NN analysis, we classified CSE scores into five groups. This type of classification could help management to improve the effectiveness of ERP training. Further, we found that statistically insignificant CSE determinants from SEM were less effective as inputs to the NN.

Statistical significance was found for organizational support and engagement as antecedents of CSE, but not for prior experience and CA. Table 6 shows that the mean prior experience of the sample is 3.36 with a standard deviation of 0.86. In general, the individuals had little relevant experience. This is not surprising given that ERP and associated training tools are not widely available to the public compared to spreadsheets for example. The explanation for the lack of support for CA as an antecedent of CSE is likely to be the ubiquity of computers. As shown in the results in Table 6, CA has a mean of 3.66 and standard deviation of 1.58. On the 7-point scale used CA is approximately neutral. Since the students in the sample grew up with computers in both their home and school, and 99% had more than 1 year of PC experience, CA was not likely to be an issue. CA is becoming less common since technology has become pervasive in businesses, schools and homes. The lack of effects from age and gender on CSE in the sample is also not surprising given increasing ubiquity of computers.

The NN results displayed in Table 9 provide empirical evidence to support the antecedent model of CSE (Fig. 1) and the efficacy of a NN model to predict an individual’s CSE. We find partial validation for the effectiveness of utilizing the engagement and organizational support constructs identified by PLS to determine necessary input variables in the NN models. The analysis also showed how NN produces a CSE classification using engagement and organizational support inputs. Furthermore, fuzzy sets improve classification accuracy by 10 to 15% for most NN models. CSE classification provides the potential to allocate users to customized training that varies in the level of support.

Our study contributes in several ways. First, although several studies demonstrate a relationship to related variables, this is the first to show empirical support for engagement as an antecedent of CSE. Second, it confirms the importance of organizational support

This study is the first to confirm prior research that CSE impacts PEOU, PU and technology use in a multimedia ERP training tool context. In addition, our study advances methodology by using a multi-analytic approach as recommended by IS researchers. SEM tests the hypotheses and the reliability of the measures, while NNs verify the antecedents as predictors of CSE, estimate CSE scores and assign individuals to groups based on their CSE scores.

We used the results from the SEM as input to the neural network analysis. Recent research demonstrates that NNs are a very strong classification methodology and that their utilization may be improved through the use of antecedent statistical methods. Combining analytic methods is rare in information systems research despite such documented benefits as a richer understanding of the topic, triangulation of empirical results, provision of a set of checks and balances, verification of conclusions, and creativity and widening the scope of the study. Further, although NNs have been applied to many business problems, including assessing technology adoption [19], they have not been previously used to estimate CSE.

Our sequential multi-analytic approach to assessing CSE has implications for both academics and practitioners. This approach demonstrates how combining two data analysis techniques can offset any weakness in either methodology, using an alternative analysis to increase the validity and confidence in the empirical results. For example, the strong statistical significance of engagement predicting CSE is reinforced by the NN analysis. Although the multi-analytic approach is rarely used, we hope our study will encourage other researchers to also take this approach. Thus an important contribution of this study is a new perspective on assessing CSE using this multi-method approach.

Self-report issues concerning measurement of CSE can be avoided by utilizing antecedents to CSE, such as perceived OS and engagement, to provide reliable inputs to a CSE NN prediction model. This study is the first to use an ERP multimedia-training tool context to verify prior studies that CSE affects PEOU, PU and intention to use technology.

This study developed NN models to predict individual user CSE, using variables that measure engagement, prior experience, CA, and OS. The NN CSE prediction models are capable of predicting both the specific CSE value (within one standard deviation) and more accurately the CSE level of the user, with over 94 % of the 234 user population in the research study accurately predicted to within one group of their corresponding CSE level out of five possible groups.

Student samples are used in many published articles and are especially appropriate for training-related research. ERP users in organizations with diverse ages and educational backgrounds often find ERP training challenging, despite their work experience. Although our participants were younger and more educated than average workers, they are reasonably representative of people undergoing ERP training in organizations; indeed 93% had some work experience. Another limitation was our inability to measure relevant experience over time. A final limitation relates to the cross-sectional nature of our data collection.

Managers will find it useful to predict CSE based on OS and engagement since both are relatively easy to measure. OS is already measured in many organizations and engagement is observable as well as obtainable from self-reporting surveys. This study provides justification for increasing OS to improve CSE for low scoring employees. Management with knowledge of users’ CSE levels could use this information to plan interventions, distribute resources and provide differentiated training classes based on CSE scores to exert better control over the effectiveness of training.

Our study provides valuable implications to research in three directions. First, our study informs ERP research, which recognizes the criticality of effective training for organizations implementing ERP. Because ERP training is a major expense, identifying users’ CSE level has the potential to impact the return on investment for the implementation of complex enterprise systems. Currently, ERP systems are of much more concern to practitioners and researchers than spreadsheets and desktop applications, the context of most prior TAM and CSE studies. The impact on the bottom line is much more significant, the potential disruption to the business is greater and the training challenges are much more pronounced.

Second, our study adds value to software application training research and in particular multimedia training. We found that engagement does influence CSE, which in a multimedia-training context affects technology acceptance. The theoretical importance of engagement affecting CSE is the support for providing multimedia in training tools. Despite the appeal of multimedia, there has not been much evidence of its effectiveness for training. This study makes progress on a theoretical explanation of why multimedia can be helpful for business software training through its positive impact on CSE. Finally, our study demonstrates the value of a relatively rarely used multi-analytic approach to assessing CSE with the objective of improving ERP training.

## Appendix 1. Neural networks analysis, results and discussion

After determining input variables, the next step in developing a NN model is to evaluate training algorithms and architectures. The backpropagation (BP) training algorithm is selected for its popularity and ease of comparison to other research, its superior performance for a wide variety of domain problems and its documented robustness when modeling complex nonlinear solution spaces. On the other hand, the radial basis function (RBF) training algorithm overcomes some reported problems of the BP alternative when extrapolation from the input vector values is required.

Multiple architectures are tested for each set of independent variables, including single and two hidden layer BP architectures. Other factors including learning coefficient, momentum term, intra-layer learning rule, and inter-layer transfer function are kept constant across all of the NN models to limit extraneous effects on the CSE prediction model’s performance.

Over-fitting the problem space, a recognized problem in NNs is overcome by starting with a small number of hidden nodes and then incrementing the number of hidden nodes by a consistent step-size until the performance of the NN begins to decrease. We start the BP and RBF trained NN models with 3 hidden nodes, based on the heuristic of using 50% of the quantity of input parameters. Subsequent single layer BP and RBF NN models increment the first hidden layer by one node for each model. Two hidden layer BP models are also implemented with the second hidden layer equal to one-half of the quantity of nodes in the first hidden layer (rounded up).

Well over 500 different NN models are evaluated (12–20 different combinations of hidden node architectures and training algorithm for each of the 32 different construct combinations), with only the best performing models for each of the attempted construct combinations reported. The single hidden layer BP trained NN models consistently outperformed both the two hidden layer BP trained NN models and the RBF trained NN models. The best performing architecture was almost uniformly the one that contained either the same quantity or one fewer hidden nodes as input nodes.

Evaluation of the different NN models uses a bootstrap approach of 12-fold cross-validation to maximize the number of validation cases available. This approach randomly selects out approximately one tenth of the total population to be used as a validation set, with the remaining members serving as the training set to train the neural network model. This process is repeated until all population samples appear in at least one validation set, resulting in 12 independent NN models. Whenever a validation sample is repeated across more than 1-fold, it is only evaluated on its first occurrence. The aggregation of the independent validation trial results approximates the obtainable results from utilizing a model that would have been trained on the full population set, but maintains the integrity of the data as all validation samples are withheld from the training data set for the 12 individual crossvalidation NN models.

From the PLS analysis above, the three high reliability indicators of CSE are aggregated to produce the CSE output value for the NN models. Aggregated CSE is on a 21-point scale since each of the three indicators is on a 7-point scale. The two hidden layer BP-trained and RBF-trained NN models consistently underperformed the corresponding single hidden layer architecture and as such are not reported.

The results for the best performing single hidden layer BPtrained models are displayed in Table 9, with the minimum mean absolute error (MAE) and maximum prediction percentages within a column highlighted. The 234 evaluation cases enabled through the 12-fold cross-validation had a CSE range from 3 to 21 with a mean of 13.4 and a standard deviation of 3.35. As displayed in Table 9, all of the NN CSE prediction models have a MAE well below one standard deviation. Also the table lists the percentage of predictions that exactly matched the subject’s reported CSE and also the percentage of total predictions, including the exact matches, that were within 1 point of the reported CSE on the 21 point aggregated scale. The BP CSE prediction model that uses the PLS identified construct of engagement produces the lowest MAE and the engagement construct is present in all of the top NN models with a MAE below 2.1. Furthermore, the engagement construct is present in both of the top performing NN models.

The engagement construct variables alone produce a NN model that has a MAE that is less than six tenths of a standard deviation and also perfectly identifies over 20% of individual CSE ratings and over 50% of individual CSE ratings within one point (on the 21 point aggregate scale). Slightly weaker empirical evidence indicates that OS is valid in the CSE component model of Fig. 1. The OS and engagement constructs combined produce the second lowest CSE prediction MAE and third highest within one point prediction accuracy out of the 32 different construct combinations implemented in the partial factorial research design. Additionally, the NN model that utilizes only the OS construct variables achieves the third highest perfect prediction accuracy.

Although the performance for the engagement, engagement/ OS, and the engagement/CA NN models are all statistically similar (p values well above 0.10), when CA is combined with the engagement construct, it produces the highest accuracy for predicting an individual’s CSE to within one point on the 21-point scale. Nevertheless, the CA model produces the largest MAE value and the second worst accuracy for predicting within 1 the specific CSE for an individual, producing a difference or error of 19 more individuals out of 234 not being predicted within 1 when compared to the engagement model’s performance. The NN models verify the PLS findings that demographics and prior experience were not significant contributors to predicting CSE. For almost all the NN models, addition of demographic variables or prior experience to the input vector increased the MAE significantly and decreased the CSE prediction accuracy, supporting the research opinion that selection of independent input variables is critical to NN model performance, and contrary to the alternative view that NN learning algorithms can select out non-contributing variables. Simplifying NN models reduces cost and improves the interpretation and usability of the resulting model. The NN model with input from engagement, which is the most significant construct from the PLS analysis (see Table 8), has the best performance for MAE and perfect CSE predictions and the second best performance for predictions within 1 of actual CSE. This empirical evidence suggests that the engagement construct, at least within a computer literate population, is a significant predictor of CSE with or without organizational support and CA measures.

## A.1. Group classification

A further analysis of the BP-trained NN model’s output determines group classification. We distinguish five different CSE levels for the population from very-low to very-high. For example, an individual with an aggregate CSE score up to 7 is placed in the very low CSE group. The predicted CSE output values are also converted into a group classification using the same cutoffs that are applied to the user responses. The new classification results are displayed in Table 10 for each of the NN construct models reported previously in Table 9, with the highest prediction accuracy values for each column highlighted.

The NN classification places the user in the correct CSE group 68% and within one group 94% of the time for the engagement construct only NN model. The remaining models are within two groups of the correct classification (meaning that a very-low or low CSE user is never categorized as a very-high CSE user and very-high and high CSE users are never classified as very-low CSE users). Hence, 94% of all users may have their respective CSE group classification predicted by the proposed NN models to within a one-group difference. It should be noted that OS and CA inputs, result in the same CSE classification.

CSE level classifications of individuals may also be viewed as fuzzy sets, with the specific cutoffs for placement within a group (i.e. the boundary conditions) being indeterminate, but contained. For example, although an aggregated CSE score of 17 may belong to the very high CSE group in a typical setting, it might only be placed in the high CSE group at an organization with mostly technology workers. We transform the results into a more compatible fuzzy set notation using an overlap of 1–2 points between groups. Boundary members may be viewed as belonging to two possible groups, but still maintaining high homogeneity of members within a group. The fuzzy group classification results, displayed in Table 10 with the highest classification accuracy highlighted, lend further empirical support for inclusion of the combination of the organizational support and engagement constructs in the CSE prediction model. The fuzzy classification improves classification accuracy by 10–15% for most of the NN models. This may ultimately be a more realistic approach for organizations in trying to determine the CSE level of employees.
