# Research Note

# An Investigation of the Appropriation of Technology-Mediated Training Methods Incorporating Enactive and Collaborative Learning

Saurabh Gupta

Department of Management, Coggin School of Business, University of North Florida, Jacksonville, Florida 32224, s.gupta@unf.edu

Robert Bostrom

MIS Department, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, Georgia 30602, bostrom@uga.edu

he growth in the application of information technology to student and employee learning underscores the need to understand the impact of technology-mediated learning (TML) methods. Using previous developed TML models, based on social cognitive theory and adaptive structuration meta-theory, the effectiveness of three training methods were examined in this study: technology mediated (using both vicarious and enactive learning), and collaborative and combined (collaborative plus technology mediated). The study also focused on the learning process. The experimental study results showed a significant positive influence of enactive learning enabled TML and collaborative training on specific training outcomes, and the combined training method shows positive results on all training outcomes. The study results also showed that faithful appropriation of the training methods during the learning process has a moderator effect on training outcomes. The study provides important research implications for theory and practice

## 1. Introduction

Training within organizations is one of the most pervasive methods for enhancing productivity of individuals and communicating organizations’ goals to new personnel. A recent meta-analysis reported a weighted effect size of training’s positive impact on behavior/skill change in business organizations as 0.60 to 0.63, a medium to large effect (Arthur et al. 2003). In 2010, U.S. organizations with 100 or more employees spent \$171.5 billion on formal training, with the highest amount being spent on information technology (IT) training (ASTD 2011). The importance and growth of IT training, makes it an ideal context to study the impact of new training methods such as technology-mediated learning (TML).

TML, also referred to as e-learning (a term that is used interchangeably with TML in this manuscript), is “an environment in which the learner’s interactions with learning materials, peers, and/or instructor are mediated through advanced information technology” (Alavi and Leidner 2001, p. 2). TML has steadily grown over the last 10 years. For example, 40.1% of training of users on computer tools in 2010 was done through TML (ASTD 2011). There are similar trends in universities and other educational institutions. For example, over 5.6 million students took a TML class in the Fall of 2009 (Allen and Seaman 2010). In spite of this growth in TML, it has only recently attracted the attention of information systems (IS) scholars (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Sasidharan and Santhanam 2006).

The emergent research does not address three important issues. First, new technologies allow for a more immersive training experience in a simulated environment, which includes practicing the skills. Limited research exists on these simulated environments to provide a clear picture regarding their impact. Secondly, a review of literature points out that learning strategies are shifting toward a more active and team-oriented learning approach referred to as collaborative learning (Alavi et al. 1995). The few collaborative TML studies in IT training (summarized in Gupta et al. 2010) and Education (summarized in Lehtinen et al. 2003) have found mixed results. More collaborative learning research is needed in the IT training context and these mixed results need to be explained. Finally, recent researchers have called for an examination of the learning process (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Gupta et al. 2010).

This paper summarizes a theoretically grounded empirical research study that investigates these issues. Using previously developed TML models, the effectiveness of three training methods were examined using an experimental research design: (1) a simulated software-based course or classroom environment using both vicarious and enactive learning; (2) collaborative; and (3) a combined collaborative technology-mediated method. The next section presents the research framework used in this study. Section 3 outlines the experimental research design used to test hypotheses derived from the research model. We conclude by summarizing and interpreting the findings as well as outlining future research areas.

## 2. Research Framework

## 2.1. Prior Research

The most prevalent theory used to understand participant learning in IT training is social cognitive theory (SCT) (Bandura 1986). According to this theory, learning interventions affect learning outcomes through reflection on observations. SCT outlines two kinds of observational training methods: (1) observation of others’ actions, referred to as vicarious learning or behavioral modeling; and (2) observation of self-actions or enactive learning (Schunk 2004).

Most research studies have focused on vicarious/ behavioral modeling as a method of learning (Gupta et al. 2010). Vicarious modeling in previous research has usually been done by using an instructor to demonstrate actions in a video. A consistent finding is that vicarious modeling leads to better training outcomes compared to other methods, such as lecturebased instruction or studying from a manual (Bolt et al. 2001, Davis and Davis 1990, Gupta et al. 2010, Yi and Davis 2003).

The other training method, enactive learning, has received very limited attention in IS, especially in IT training research. Recently, a research study focusing on the cognitive impact of practice found no significant impact (Yi and Davis 2003). Other studies, although not examining the effect of enactive learning, have focused on the impact of feedback, a key component of enactive learning (Martocchio 1994, Sein and Santhanam 1999). These studies have found a positive effect of feedback on learning outcomes.

The use of collaborative learning (referred to as cooperative learning as well) has also not received much attention in a TML-based IT training environment. In fact, even some definitions of e-learning (e.g., Zhang et al. 2004, Jonassen and Reeves 2001) describe e-learning as a platform for individual learning. Although considerable evidence exists regarding the importance of collaborative learning outside IT training research (Rohrbeck et al. 2003), its role in IT training has been inconclusive. For example whereas Lim et al. (1997) found a positive impact of codiscovery in a calendar and scheduling task, Davis and Yi (2004) found a nonsignificant impact when using Excel instruction as the learning task. The use of collaboration in a IT training environment has only been addressed in these two studies, and general studies in computer-supported collaborative learning have also yielded inconsistent results (Lehtinen et al. 2003).

Finally, the research on using TML tools to train people on software systems, though limited, has also resulted in inconsistent outcomes. Gist et al. (1989) used computer technology to provide examples and programmed instructions without showing a demonstration and found a positive influence of computerbased training on self-efficacy and task performance. In a similar experiment, Bowman et al. (1995) found no significant difference when comparing classroom instruction to TML training. Bohlen and Ferratt (1997) and Desai (2000) found that TML training was either more effective or had no positive effect depending on individual differences or the target system used. These studies, however, gave limited descriptions of the TML tools used, making them difficult to compare (Gupta et al. 2010).

A number of possible reasons can be identified to explain the discrepancies in these research results. A primary reason is the fact that most studies have used input–output research designs, ignoring critical aspects of the learning process (Gupta et al. 2010). Another reason is the cross-sectional nature of most studies. Researchers have suggested, especially in collaborative learning, that a positive effect is more likely to be observed in longitudinal rather than cross-sectional studies, implying the need to focus on team development (Davis and Yi 2004, Lou et al. 2001). In addition, training activities in organizations are continuous operations, and thus, there is a need to study the learning process longitudinally. Content-wise, most studies have focused on just the introductory aspects of the target system, with onetime training sessions lasting 60–90 minutes. This provides an incomplete picture of IT training because the impacts might be different for learning complex target system components and multiple training sessions.

This study extends and builds on research on TML from Alavi and Leidner (2001), Piccoli et al. (2001), and Santhanam et al. (2008) as well as the collaborative learning ideas presented in Lim et al. (1997) to address the limitations described earlier. It uses a research model developed in Gupta and Bostrom (2009) as a theoretical basis. This model used adaptive structuration theory (AST) meta-theoretical approach and integrated a more predictive social cognitive theory (SCT) into it to develop a theoretical framework for investigating TML contexts (Gupta 2008, 2006). Herein, we present the results of an empirical study that tests the Gupta and Bostrom (2009) model in an IT training context.

## 2.2. Training Methods

The study compares different implementations of four training methods based on social cognitive theory across two axes: type of technology mediation and extent of collaboration. Technology mediation consisted of computer-based training conducted via an instructor led video recording or a Web-based training (WBT), and extent of collaboration consisted of either an individual learning or collaborative learning condition. The instructor led video with no collaboration training method was used as a baseline because most previous research had focus on this method and it is one of the most prevalent training methods used in business. The main interest of current study was the other three methods and how they compared to the baseline method.

Gupta and Bostrom (2009) outlined four distinct model constructs to understand any TML system: learning context, training method structures, learning process, and learning outcomes. The learning context is primarily comprised of the training goals and spirit of the training design. In this study, the learning goals deal with training participants in a specific information technology application software. The design spirit for the training methods came from the social cognitive perspective. The training method structures operationalized were enactive and vicarious training methods. The specific training method structural dimensions are discussed in subsequent paragraphs. The learning process is captured in terms of participant’s appropriation of the learning technology. The learning outcomes measure the extent to which participants have been trained in the software system in terms of cognitive knowledge, self-efficacy, and satisfaction with the learning process.

In this study, two similar TML training technologies were used to deliver video-based demonstrations: an instructor-led video recording and video demonstrations delivered through a WBT. The Gupta and

Bostrom (2009) model postulates that the implementation similarities or differences between training methods would be evident in their levels of structural dimensions. Although SCT does not identify structural dimensions explicitly, a review of the broader training literature and existing tools and manuals (a process suggested by DeSanctis and Poole 1994) identified eight SCT structural dimensions that are relevant to a TML environment. These dimensions are described in Table 1.

Four of these dimensions are primarily relevant to the implementation of vicarious learning: restrictiveness of demonstration, richness of demonstration, authenticity of demonstration, and model prestige and competence (Bandura 1986, 2001; Sasidharan and Santhanam 2006; Schunk 2004). In the current study, both video-based demonstrations used the same software system (Microsoft Excel) for demonstrations, as well as identical scripts and the exact same demonstrations. This approach leads to similar levels of realism, authenticity, and restrictiveness across the training methods. To maintain similar model credibility, the video-based training was presented by an experienced instructor and the same instructor was used to introduce the WBT. Thus, all four training methods had a similar vicarious learning component based on the levels of the four structural dimensions.

The other four dimensions in Table 1 (production pattern, structuredness of practice, feedback, and guidance) were used primarily to implement or differentiate the level of enactive learning in the TML training methods (Gupta and Bostrom 2009). Although all training methods had an enactive learning component, the WBT training methods used technology features to implement a short production pattern, structured practice, guided simulation, and tracking student actions to provide specific feedback and contextual information that significantly enhanced these four structural dimensions in WBT training methods.

We argue that higher levels of the enactive structural dimensions in addition to vicarious demonstrations more accurately reflect the underlying SCT cognitive “spirit” and should have a significant positive impact on the training outcomes (Bandura 1986). Some preliminary evidence of the positive impact of the enactive structural dimension already exists in the literature. For example, a post hoc analysis presented in Zhang et al. (2004) focused on two structural dimensions manipulated using TML technology (feedback and guidance) and found a positive impact. Given this, we hypothesize:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> Participants in WBT training methods, who had access to higher levels of enactive learning, will exhibit higher outcomes when compared to participants that did not use WBT.

The arguments regarding the importance of structural dimensions can also be extended to teams in a collaborative TML environment. Although SCT highlights the importance of peer modeling (Bandura 1986), most SCT-based research has focused primarily on individual learning. However, an extensive set of research in the areas of collaborative learning and teams identifies six structural dimensions: interdependence, individual accountability, support, development, goal emphasis, and team feedback (Franklin et al. 1976; Johnson and Johnson 2003, 1975, 1999; Johnson et al. 1992) (see Table 2 for definitions).

In the current study, the collaboration training method used dyads and the reciprocal questioning technique to establish interaction ground rules as well as other guidelines, such as dyad rewards, to operationalize the collaboration dimensions. Reciprocal questioning technique is a popular and well-used research technique (King 1992). It enables students to learn by summarizing and asking each other questions in a guided format. The reciprocal questioning technique is also based on enhancing cognitive understanding.

A meta-analysis examining various collaboration techniques did suggest that methods incorporating more of the above six dimensions had a greater positive effect on learning outcomes (Johnson et al. 2000). Thus, we argue that rather than focusing on teams in general (as in Davis and Yi 2004 or Lim et al. 1997), the effectiveness of a collaboration-based training method will depend on the extent to which these dimensions are operationalized in a method. Given this argument, we hypothesize:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> Participants learning in collaboration training method, who participated in a team that had higher levels of the collaboration structural dimensions, will exhibit higher outcomes when compared to participants learning alone.

When combining two training methods (TML + collaboration), an incoherent combination of structures could result. Such a combination may send contradictory signals, making the learning process more difficult (DeSanctis and Poole 1994). An example of this is the virtual collaborative learning environment in Hiltz et al. (2000). Although the pedagogy in that study was designed around team-based learning, encouraging the development of team structures, the use of technology was restricted to simply receiving posted material and sending back individual work, thus encouraging individual work not teamwork. This combination of structures provided contradictory signals, negatively affecting learning outcomes.

In the current study, the collaboration guidelines were distinct and separate from the training technology and did not interfere with the functionality or procedure of WBT usage. No advanced technology features were used to enhance the collaborative dimensions to maintain the focus on learning from computers not learning by computers. To further verify that no contradiction existed between the two techniques, two pilot studies were done to refine and test the procedure. Overall, a coherent set of structures reinforcing the social cognitive spirit were used in the combined training method. Thus, we state

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> Participants in the combined WBT and collaboration training method, who had access to higher levels of enactive learning and participated in a team that had higher levels of the collaboration structural dimension, will exhibit higher outcomes than either the WBT or collaboration methods separately.

## 2.3. Learning Process

Besides the impact of training methods on learning outcomes, it is critical to focus on learning process effects. SCT highlights, along with the other learning theories, the two important components of the learning process: appropriation of the training method and assimilation of the learning content. These are distinct (though interrelated) concepts and processes. Content assimilation refers to the psychological process of building cognitive models and thought processes in a learning/training environment. This is different from process appropriation, which refers to how a trainee uses and adapts the training method structures while going through the learning process.

Although both these components are interrelated, most learning theories have tended to focus on content assimilation. For example, Ausubel’s theory of subsumption has focused primarily on how the use of advance organizers helps an individual create better cognitive models (Sein et al. 1985). Content assimilation is reflected and usually measured in higher performance on learning outcomes and is related to successful appropriation of a training method. Content assimilation theories argue that the psychological processes mediate the relationship between content presented and content learned, resulting in a model similar to the one in Alavi and Leidner (2001). In the current study, the focus was on process appropriation while content assimilation was controlled for through the randomization of the novice learners across training method groups.

The Gupta and Bostrom (2009) research model uses the constructs of appropriation and moves to capture and investigate the reciprocal relationship involved in the training process. Appropriation is the view taken of the overall process, while moves focuses on the specific actions learners use to produce and reproduce structures (Poole and DeSanctis 1992). We studied the impact of specific training method structures on overall appropriation, not on an in-depth view of the “moves.” As a first step in understanding this phenomenon, we focus our attention on appropriation.

The degree to which learners interact in a manner consistent with the spirit of the training method can be measured on a continuum from faithful to ironic appropriation (Bostrom et al. 2009, DeSanctis and Poole 1994, Gupta and Bostrom 2009). When well-designed and relevant structures are faithfully appropriated, it should lead to higher learning outcomes. An unfaithful or ironic appropriation of welldesigned and relevant structures, on the other hand, might result in lower learning outcomes.

Though no TML research has been found that investigates this area, support can be found in reference disciplines. In terms of technology effect, early research in group support system (GSS) literature shows a positive moderating effect of the level of faithfulness on decision outcomes (Fjermestad and Hiltz 1998). In addition, faithfulness of use of technology has been found to have a strong positive correlation with process satisfaction (Chin et al. 1997). Metaanalyses comparing collaboration methods point to the greater effectiveness in terms of student achievement where the underlying team structural dimensions are better developed (Johnson et al. 2000). Thus, we argue that the levels of learning outcomes are moderated by the level of appropriation of training method structures:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4A</sup> <sup>(H4A).</sup> The appropriation of WBT methods structures (measured by faithfulness of use and attitude toward technology) will moderate the relationship between training methods and learning outcomes such that more faithful appropriation will lead to a stronger positive impact of WBT methods.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4B</sup> <sup>(H4B).</sup> The appropriation of collaboration training methods structures will moderate the relationship between training methods and learning outcomes such that more faithful appropriation will lead to a stronger positive impact of collaboration training methods.

## 3. Research Design

This study conducted a rigorous exploration of training methods using an interrupted multisession experiment based on the research model shown in Figure 1. The learning goal was to train participants in Microsoft Excel 2003, a contemporary application package. Microsoft Excel has also been used in previous studies in IT training, thus, making the results of this study more comparable to the existing literature. The experimental setting emulates introduction of an application package in an organization and subsequent IT training.

## 3.1. Tools, Tasks, and Procedures

The 2 × 2 experiment had four treatments/training methods: instructor recorded video + practice (TM1), WBT (TM2), instructor recorded video + practice + collaboration (TM3), and WBT + collaboration (TM4) (see Figure 1). Prior studies on collaborative learning indicate that a group size of two has led to the best learning outcomes (Lou et al. 2001, p. 473). Therefore, this study used dyads for the collaboration training method (TM3 and TM4). For these training methods, each participant was randomly paired with another participant for the rest of the experiment. Appendix B outlines the collaboration techniques and guidelines used.

The WBT delivery system selected was a commercially available system called elementK. The tool contained a video demonstration (vicarious training) followed by simulated sessions for enactive learning. It was selected because it included features that implemented vicarious and enhanced enactive learning. elementK is designed based on industry best practices and accepted instructional design principles. The product has gained large acceptance in universities and organizations alike to train trainees. Participants in TM2 and TM4 used elementK to go through the training process.

Given the complexity of concepts covered, the training was designed to be done in three logically separate sessions, each building on the previous one. The first phase (week 1) trained participants on working with existing workbooks. The second phase (week 2), building on the first one, trained participants on analyzing and managing data in Excel. The third phase (week 3), building on the second one, trained participants on writing formulas in Excel.

Participants for the experiment were drawn from an introductory management information system (MIS) course for undergraduates from a large southern university. Student incentives included course credit for participation and monetary incentive for performance. All prospective participants were asked to take a pretest that contained a subset of questions from the three content knowledge quizzes. These questions are shown in Appendix C. Though all participants went through the training process, only participants with scores less than 35% were included in data analysis to ensure that participants were Excel novices. Students were randomly assigned to various training methods.

Overall, 701 students participated in the experiment. After all the restrictions were implemented and subjects with missing data were removed, a usable sample of 435 (∼ 61%) remained. The final numbers of participants considered for analysis in each treatment are 113 individual students in TM1 (individual video), 117 in TM2 (individual WBT), 85 in TM3 (video + dyad), and 120 in TM4 (WBT + dyad). A one-way ANOVA and chi-square confirmed that the randomization procedure for grade point average, college major, motivation, and pretest knowledge provided an even distribution of the subjects (p < 0005) except for gender differences. Thus, gender was included as a covariate.

Multiple controls were included to check for treatment manipulation in the design of the experiment. These are outlined in Figure 1. Two facilitators (both from a MIS doctoral program) were used to oversee training sessions. The facilitators used a detailed script to prevent variation in implementation. Additionally, as suggested in existing literature, covariates were used including pretest scores, motivation to learn and initial computer self-efficacy.

![](/api/attachments/983RE6MU/fulltext/images/ed7798395757b9e27aa82df15665456694b4e58ba1dbf5651b6e414d83282531.jpg)

Overall, all participants had an initial introduction session followed by three training sessions. Each training session was 75 minutes. Figure 2 outlines the time spent in each component of each training session as well as the activities done in each component. There was a week between sessions. The entire experiment lasted for one month. As an additional control, students were not allowed to repeat any component of the training. The experimental design also restricted students use of elementK, which enhanced the internal validity of the design (Cook and Campbell 1979).

Two variable sets were measured during the experimental process: process/appropriation and learning outcome variables. Extent of appropriation of technology-mediation methods was measured at an individual level using two constructs: faithfulness of use (measured using items from Chin et al. 1997) and attitude toward the technology (items from Gopal et al. 1992/93). Consensus in use, the other appropriation construct is measured by the agreement on how to use the technology when multiple options and multiple people are present. In the current study, the use of TML software as well as the lab exercise was constrained though the use of guidelines and supervision. Thus, individuals did not have any alternative forms of technology to use. Consequently, consensus was not measured.

No measures for the underlying team structure appropriation existed, thus, a new six-item scale (referred to as collaboration appropriation), reflecting the six dimensions in Table 2, was developed using items from prior literature (Franklin et al. 1976, Johnson et al. 2000). Items were piloted with a sample of 65 executive MBA students and were found to have good internal consistency (inter-item correlation > 0080). These items are presented in Appendix C.

Three different types of learning outcome measures were used: cognitive knowledge acquisition, metacognitive, and affective (Gupta and Bostrom 2010). Cognitive knowledge focused on the ability to use the target system and a 15-question multiple choice quiz score for each session was used to measure cognitive knowledge. Each quiz score was modified based on the average perceived difficulty score measured using a four-item scale (Huber 1985). A meta-cognitive or self-regulated learning goal refers to an individual’s knowledge of one’s own learning processes. Excel self-efficacy, a learner’s judgments of their capability to use Excel, was used to measure this outcome (measured on a five-point scale from Hollenbeck and Brief 1987). Finally, affective goals focus on the emotional aspects of the user’s behavior and was measured using level of satisfaction with the learning process scale from Green and Taber (1980). All items used for the constructs are listed in Appendix C.

## 3.2. Data Analysis

All constructs showed good convergent and discriminant validity as well as good reliability across all periods (see Appendix A). The data analysis involved a two-pronged approach depending on the nature of the hypothesis.

The first three hypotheses (H1–H3) focused on testing of mean differences across training methods. Traditional techniques, such as MANOVA, are ideally suited for such analysis because they use regression for differentiation (Maruyama 1997). Repeated measures MANOVA was used because repeated observations were taken of the same subject. Repeated measures MANOVA allows for interval independent variables, such as gender, initial motivation, etc. to be used as covariates (Hair 1998).

The second set of hypotheses (H4A–H4B) focused on the learning process and effect of training method appropriation on learning outcomes. WBT groups (TM2, TM4) were analyzed to see if the level of faithfulness of WBT structures had a moderation effect on learning outcomes, and collaboration groups (TM3, TM4) were analyzed to see if the level of collaboration had a moderation effect on learning outcomes. Here again, MANOVA was used to analyze the data. Median scores (after adding variables across time) for faithfulness of WBT structures appropriation (21.00), attitude toward WBT (52.00), and collaboration structures appropriation (101.00) were used to split the groups into high levels of appropriation and low levels of appropriation. Effect size was calculated using partial $\dot { \eta } ^ { 2 }$ where 0.009 constitutes a small effect, 0.058 a medium effect and 0.138 a large effect (Cohen 1988). In cases where degree of freedom of the numerator is one guidelines for eta-squared would be the square of these, i.e., 0.01 is a small effect, 0.09 a medium effect, and 0.25 a large effect (Levine and Hullett 2002).

## 4. Discussion

One of the key contributions of the study is a practical illustration of distinguishing two similar looking training methods through the concept of structural dimensions. More importantly, the study answered the following question using the structural dimensions comparison approach: Do Web-based training methods with higher levels of enactive learning dimensions have a greater positive effect than other technology-mediated training methods? The answer is “yes.” The WBT method featured higher levels of the enactive learning structural dimensions and had a positive impact on all learning outcomes except process satisfaction when compared to non-WBT methods.

There was a great variance in student satisfaction from the learning process $( \sigma = 3 . 8 9 , \ \bar { x } = 3 . 7 ,$ range = 2–7 on seven point scale). The variance is also captured in qualitative data gathered. One student commented “I would prefer to be taught by a human being than by a computer 0 0 0” and another commented $\bar { \cdot } \ldots . \mathrm { i t }$ really helped me develop into a real go getter. Thanks Element $\mathrm { K ! ^ { \prime \prime } }$ This indicates that some participants were very satisfied with the use of TML, and some were not. This indicates that individual differences and interpersonal dynamics might play an important role in training satisfaction. The discussion on individual attitudes toward technology discussed later in this section provides support for this assertion. Overall, the results suggest that trainers and researchers should focus on structured enactive learning (beyond practice) as an addition to behavioral training to enhance learning outcomes.

Paired learning is a relatively new method of training in IT training literature. This study used a much broader conceptualization of learning outcomes than the previous study, which looked at paired learning, in IT training (Yi and Davis 2001). When conceptualized with multiple learning outcomes, interpretation of the findings from this study shows that participants do indeed benefit from paired learning. Self-efficacy perceptions are improved, which may be due to the implicit comparison of skills between participants. In addition, participants were encouraged to teach each other. Pairs were also able to discuss and explore the software together as exemplified by this quote from a participant: “We were able to find new and cool charts through teamwork.” Such teamwork can alleviate the concerns that some students have regarding the impersonal nature of technology-based learning, enhancing overall satisfaction with training.

There was, however, no direct benefit from collaboration on cognitive outcomes. A possible reason for this could be the lack of initial development of collaboration dimensions as a part of the experimental design. Team development activities have been shown to enhance collaboration levels in GSS settings and could prove to be useful in training as well. We obtained some evidence to support this conjecture in the study. Levels of collaboration showed a positive change over time (mean $\Delta = 1 . 7 , ~ p < 0 . 1 0 )$ . As pairs spent more time with each other, they seemed to develop a working relationship around the guidelines presented to them. This result showed that not only is it necessary to have team development up front, but continued support is needed to ensure enhanced learning outcomes. More research is needed on how to develop these collaboration dimensions especially in technology-mediated environments. Additionally, the study also developed an instrument to measure the appropriation of collaboration dimensions; however, more development and validation of the instrument is needed.

This study also illustrates how two different training methods can be combined to achieve greater benefit compared to using each individually. Such a combination should be done at a structural dimension level, ensuring that no contradictory dimensions exist in the combined training method. In this study, the WBT and collaboration-based training methods were combined, making sure that there were no contradictory structural dimensions in the combined method. This resulted in the most successful training method, which had significant effects on all learning outcomes. This result has important implications in the current discussions regarding blended training methods. The study results indicated that it is worth exploring collaboration blended with TML techniques and provides an approach for combining such techniques.

This research also opens up the black box of learning process not studied by earlier researchers and provides a method to account for the learning process. The study showed that the learning process can be conceptualized and measured using the AST construct of faithful appropriation. Two of three measures of faithful appropriation were used in study: faithfulness of use and attitude toward technology. The study found that the more WBT is used as designed (faithfully), the more the positive effect on all learning outcomes. Thus, not only do training methods have to be well designed, they also need to be appropriated faithfully to have a significant impact. This finding suggests that any TML training study, not investigating appropriation, needs to account for appropriation as a control variable or manipulation check.

Additionally, the results also showed that a negative attitude toward technology acts as a barrier in enhancing satisfaction of participants. Satisfaction was also, though to a lesser degree, influenced by faithful use. The more diligently students used the system, the more satisfied they became. Thus, it is important that the participants come in with an open mind for technology-based training to be effective. Positive attitude and faithful use have a strong influence on learning outcomes and organizations should invest in developing and ensuring these before and during the training sessions. Technology-based scaffolds, such as online wizards, can also provide assistance in enhancing appropriation levels. Facilitating technology appropriation via technology-based scaffolds is rich area for future research.

The study also showed a significant change in technology appropriation over time. Although the level of faithfulness of use did not show signification change (attributed to the ease of use of technology, experiment guidelines and supervision, as well as the initial high levels of appropriation), the attitude toward technology showed a significant positive change over time (mean $\Delta = 2 . 0 , p < \mathrm { \bar { 0 } } . 0 1 )$ . This implies that as students get positive experiences in their learning with technology, their attitude toward technology use in learning/training situations improves.

## 4.1. Limitations

The focus of the study was to conduct a robust experiment investigating the TML environments. Experiments provide high levels of internal validity, as well as the ability to focus on specific manipulations. The general concerns about the external validity of a laboratory study have been well documented (Campbell and Stanley 1973). There were five potential limitations in the research design. First, a quiz was used instead of hand-on exercises to measure the cognitive knowledge outcome. Because studies have shown a high correlation between quiz tests and hands-on tests as a measurement of skill (Santhanam et al. 2008), we decided to go with a quiz. Secondly, based on empirical studies, a group size of two was chosen. Thus, the results may not be generalizable to larger group sizes. Thirdly, the analysis might suffer from common method variance issues because all the data collection in the experiment utilized a single method. Finally, the study was also carried out longitudinally, but one of the limitations of the study is that the periods selected were short (75 minutes) and substantially close to each other. Typical training sessions are often longer and may spread out over more than three weeks.

## 4.2. Contributions to Theory: A Substantiation in a New Area

Bostrom et al. (2009) pointed out that AST provides a template and a set of guidelines for creating context or system specific theoretical models. Bostrom et al. (2009) found that AST has been applied in at least five theory development domains within IS. The Gupta and Bostrom (2009) model used to guide this study was the first to apply AST to learning environments. This study not only suggests that the Gupta and Bostrom model is a useful theoretical model for future TML research, but it also provides additional support for the usefulness of using AST as a meta-theory for developing research models. More specifically, the following two major theoretical contributions were demonstrated.

First, although SCT has been used in training research, AST has not been applied to this area. However, because of the use of newer training delivery methods such as TML, AST principles can now be applied. This study used AST principles to provide a richer view of SCT methods by developing structural learning dimensions and then used these dimensions to describe SCT-based training methods. This approach helped distill the differences between two similar TML technologies. This view can be used to identify the structural dimensions of other TML learning theory-based training methods.

As shown in this study, via the use of collaboration dimensions, the structural perspective can be extended to other structural elements as well. Internal structures, such as individual differences, though not tested in this study, present a promising area for future research. This is supported by the significant effects of covariates in this study, emphasizing the impact of learner differences on learning outcomes.

Second, the study also challenged the deterministic perspective of SCT by explicitly looking at the learning process as an appropriation of training method structures. In this study, appropriation occurred as participants learned and adapted the structures of the training method based on their interpretation of the training method design spirit. It is important to remember that although the SCT-based training method guided the directionality of the mediation, the level was determined by the faithfulness of appropriation.

## 5. Conclusion

This research extends the existing TML research literature (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Piccoli et al. 2001, Santhanam et al. 2008) and other training literature (Compeau et al. 1995, Lim and Benbasat 2002) in four critical ways. First, it provides a method to obtain a deeper understanding of training methods by outlining and comparing structural dimensions. This comparison approach was used in the study to demonstrate the importance of enactive learning structures as an addition to vicarious learning. Second, it provides initial validation for the use of a relatively new collaboration-based training method. Third, the study outlines how, using the concepts of structural dimensions, two different training methods can be combined or blended to achieve greater effectiveness. Finally, the study validated the need to focus on the learning process and confirms the use of AST concept of appropriation as a method for exploring the learning process as outlined in Gupta and Bostrom (2009).

## Appendix B. Collaboration Guidelines

Both partners in the team are supposed to work together. Even though the learning software, elementK, discussed later was initially designed to be used by individuals, more and more organizations are now using collaborative or team-based learning. This is because each partner can bring in their own personal perspective and help the other partner understand the content better. The following are guidelines that you need to follow to benefit from your mutual learning experience.

1. You will both be working on separate computers. Once you reach the lab portion of the lab, you will stop and wait for your partner to complete the same.

2. As you go through the sessions, each of you have the following responsibilities:

a. Listen and see attentively and learn.

b. If your partner has any trouble understanding, help and support him or her. If your partner does not understand a topic, he or she should ask you about it.

c. Take notes in your workbook in the space provided.
