## Abstract

This paper focuses on the process of implementing strategic information systems (SIS) by studying the radical changes it may bring to an organization's deep structure. It argues that a full understanding of the process of implementation of such systems should include not only technical aspects but also the social dynamics of an organization; specifically core values, distribution of power and mechanisms of control. A theoretical framework is formulated based on punctuated equilibrium and previous SIS literature, and is applied to an exploratory case study conducted in a Latin American public organization. The case study depicts how the initiative to implement SIS was the result of external and internal disturbances. The case analysis highlights relationships between an organization's deep structure and SIS implementation. The paper concludes by discussing the theoretical and practical implications of the study. These include (1) the role of the formal organizational structure in influencing the outcome of SIS implementations, (2) the impact of exogenous contingencies such as elections and external funding that may create a sense of crisis and (3) the influence of newcomers who may be brought in to solve the crisis.

Keywords: Developing countries, strategic information systems, IS strategy, power, politics, implementation, punctuated equilibrium, deep structure, case study, organizational change, public organization, government, Latin America, Guatemala, corruption, health informatics, health information systems, hospital information systems

## Introduction

Organizations have been using information systems and information technology as strategic tools for at least the last 20 years (Hartono et al. 2003; Ives and Learmonth 1984). Indeed, the deployment of information systems for achieving strategic purposes has been a central theme in the IS literature. It has been examined from a variety of angles such as strategic planning of information systems (Gottschalk 1999b; Newkirk et al. 2002; Segars et al. 1998; Teo and Ang 1999), strategic alignment (Hirschheim and Sabherwal 2001; Reich and Benbasat 2000; Sabherwal and Chan 2001; Sabherwal et al. 2001), strategic information systems (SIS) (Bergeron and Raymond 1991; Ciborra 1991; Eardly et al. 1997; Enns et al. 2003; Gottschalk 1999a; Hamilton 1999; Hartono et al. 2003; Lee and Pai 2003), and recently from the point of view of agility (Sambamurthy et al. 2003) and the resource-based view of the firm (Bharadwaj 2000). These are largely prescriptive approaches that concentrate on proposing theoretical arrangements that organizations should or could embrace to achieve strategic objectives. Yet few concentrate specifically on the issue of implementation (notable exceptions are the works of Sharma and Yetton [2003] and Lederer and Sethi [1988]). This anomaly has been recognized by others as well. Wade and Hulland (2004), for example, suggest that implementation is an important aspect in which future research is needed. They state,

Considerations such as how resources are developed, how they are integrated within the firm, and how they are released have been under-explored in the literature. The mechanisms underlying how exactly key resources benefit the firm are also poorly specified (p. 131).

One of the motives for undertaking the research reported in this paper is to understand the mechanisms and processes that allow an organization to institutionalize those valuable and strategic resources (i.e., information systems).

Such an understanding appears to be of importance because research continues to show that organizations typically fail to achieve the expected strategic benefits (King and Teo 1996). Hartono et al. (2003), for example, recently observed how few SIS are actually implemented, and those that are take longer and cost more than expected. In studying SIS planning, Lederer and Sethi (1988) found that only 24 percent of the IS strategic plans were executed to some degree and 38 percent of systems implemented had not been anticipated during the planning process. Some argue that the low rate of success in implementing SIS is the result of either inadequate planning (Ciborra 1991; Lee and Pai 2003), the lack of leadership (Earl 1993) or organizations not having the necessary skills to implement the plan (Newkirk et al. 2002). $^{2}$ These studies do shed light on the factors that may hinder successful SIS implementation but fall short in theorizing about the processes by which these factors are related to SIS.

Moreover, the vast majority of this research centers on for-profit organizations and how IT can impact the financial performance of firms (see Earl 1996; Piccolli et al. 2003; Premkumar et al. 2004; Wiseman 1985). But how relevant are these ideas in the not-for-profit arena, in particular, government organizations? And what if these organizations are in developing countries? Clearly there are key differences. In the context of developing countries, the application of strategic information systems is central not only for individual organizations but also at the national level where governments spend significant proportions of their national budgets on IT (Bagchi et al. 2004). International agencies have also recognized the criticality of deploying information systems with strategic purposes. Hence they have integrated IT as a fundamental component of their development programs (see King 2002; McLellan 2001; Roman and Colle 2003; Wilks 2002). This is particularly the case in the health sector in which information systems have been found to be fundamental in strategies for enhancing and extending the delivery of health services (González-Block et al. 2001; Rosero-Bixby 2004; Sahay 2001; Schieber 1995). Unfortunately, benefits of SIS cannot be expected to occur automatically as the implementation of these systems in developing countries often presents different types of challenges such as structural obstacles in organizations (Avgerou 2000), lack of infrastructures (Heeks 2002), and in particular the problem of sustainability, that is, how these systems become embedded in the organizations' structures (Braa et al. 2004).

We believe that the theoretical assumptions underlying the traditional SIS literature lack explanatory power in government organizations in developing countries where the main strategic capital is of a political nature; that is, an interest in public interpretation of actions because of their influence on election outcomes (Avgerou 2000; Walsham 2001). Moreover, government organizations, particularly large ones such as the one studied in this research, are characterized by strong bureaucratic structures and limited resources that almost by definition eschew notions such as agility and dynamic capabilities. Even the proponents of agility and dynamic capabilities recognize that their theoretical boundary refers to private organizations immersed in rapid and dynamic competitive environments, which is clearly not the case for government organizations.

Given the differences in goals between the public and private sectors (Rainey et al. 1976), SIS are likely to exhibit different attributes in these types of organizations. While market forces determine the goals in private organizations, those in the public sector are determined by factors such as political timing (i.e., elections, regulations, and a mandate for delivery of services) (Self 1977). In addition, organizational structures are more rigid in government than in private organizations; the former are characterized predominantly by red tape while the latter are more flexible. Thus, SIS in government organizations will tend to support the attainment of political and public service goods and will have to be embedded in relatively rigid organizational structures (Caudle et al. 1991; Dufner et al. 2003). Understanding this type of organizational context requires a different underlying lens. Thus, we have selected the theoretical lens of punctuated equilibrium given its view of organizational change as characterized by long periods of stability punctuated by short periods of upheaval. As will be discussed later in the paper, this theoretical lens was appropriate given the political situation of the developing nation in which we conducted our fieldwork.

The objectives of this paper are to (1) provide an empirical description of the process of SIS implementation in the context of developing countries, and to (2) formulate a theoretical explanation for such a phenomenon. To achieve our objectives, we conducted an exploratory case study in a large public organization in Latin America. By concentrating on such a type of organization and context, this study will allow us to advance our knowledge of systems implementation beyond the domain of private corporations in developed English-speaking economies. The need to extend our scope of research beyond these boundaries has been pointed out in the mainstream literature as well (Avgerou 2000; Barrett et al. 2003; Braa et al. 2004; Jarvenpaa and Ives 1993; Sabherwal and Chan 2001; Walsham 2001, 2002). The case study presented in this paper aims to address this issue since it was conducted in the Ministry of Health of a Latin American country.

The paper is structured as follows. The next section concentrates on our theoretical assumptions; specifically we put forward our conception of SIS and implementation. In the same section we also discuss the scope and main concepts of the theory of punctuated equilibrium and how it is applied in our particular research. The third section introduces our research approach, data collection, and analysis techniques. The fourth section is a narrative of the case while the fifth focuses on our main findings. The last section presents the implications of our findings as well as some final thoughts and reflections.

## Theoretical Background

## SIS and Their Implementation

Rackoff et al. (1985) define SIS as those systems that shape and support overall strategic objectives. They bolster organizational efforts to obtain a competitive edge (Benjamin et al. 1984; Giese 1984; King and Teo 1996). SIS are different from other systems in their scope as they integrate processes with customers and suppliers (Miller 1986). Bergeron and

Raymond (1991) identify a typology of possible information systems based on Porter and Millar's (1985) value chain and from Wiseman's theory of strategic thrust as discussed by Rackoff et al. Bergeron and Raymond propose that SIS have a radical impact on firms' value chains or dramatically change the relationship between the organization and its environment; that is, suppliers, customers, and competitors (by focusing on differentiation, cost, innovation, growth, or alliances). Additionally, SIS display a high level of uncertainty in their requirements and in their technological platforms, resulting in an uncertain cost–benefit structure (Charalambos 1999; McFarlan 1981). Given their complex scope and uncertain cost–benefit structure, SIS are usually developed with unstructured and complex systems development methodologies (Barnes and Targett 1999; Eardly et al. 1997).

As hinted above, SIS and other systems differ in their goals: SIS are intended to enable organizational strategic objectives. Hence the implementation of SIS may entail some sort of radical organizational change (Benjamin and Scott Morton 1986; Galliers et al. 1995; Henderson and Venkatraman 1992; Venkatraman 1994). For example, organizations implementing systems aimed at transforming customer or supplier relations may have to undergo radical changes not only by redistributing responsibilities but also by acquiring new skills (Benjamin et al. 1984; Benjamin and Scott Morton 1986). Even if the system is designed to have an impact on the operations of the organization, an operational SIS may have a radical impact on work tasks as it entails changes in procedures, resource allocations, and job descriptions (Segars and Grover 1999; Walsham and Waema 1994).

Cooper and Zmud (1990, p. 124) define information systems implementation “as an organizational effort toward diffusing appropriate information technology within a user community.” They consider that the implementation of an IS is complete when the organization obtains the benefits of the system operating at its full potential. Accordingly, the system will be integrated into the routines of the organization and will be utilized by its intended users in a comprehensive and integral way (Cooper and Zmud 1990). In other words, the system will be institutionalized (Silva and Backhouse 2003).

Thus, a procedure for establishing whether a SIS has been institutionalized or not would be to ask its users how the system fulfills their expectations and to observe whether the system has been inculcated into the underlying routines of the organization. Table 1 summarizes our main assumptions regarding SIS and their implementation. From the point of view of empirical research, one way to establish whether an IS is strategic or not would be to ask senior managers whether the intended system is designed to fulfill the organization's long term goals. This is because in organizations strategic plans and actions are determined by those holding positions of power (Child 1972).

In sum, we argue that the main attributes of SISs are (1) their focus on attaining organizational strategic (long term) objectives; (2) their radical impacts on an organization's value chains or on an organization's relationship with its environment; and (3) the acknowledgment by senior management of these long term objectives and radical impacts. As will be discussed further in the next sections, the SIS in our fieldwork complies with the above criteria. Furthermore, we would consider a system to be institutionalized when the intended benefits of the application have been fulfilled and when the system has been routinized within the organization. The institutionalization of a SIS will entail some degree of organizational change; hence the next step in completing our argument will be to discuss a theory of organizational change; that is the purpose of the following subsection.

## Punctuated Equilibrium

The theory of punctuated equilibrium explains variations in organizations as occurring mainly in periods of gradual evolution that are “punctuated” by sudden, revolutionary periods of rapid change (Elderidge and Gould 1972; Van de Ven and Poole 1995). Although punctuated equilibrium was initially developed in the field of biology, it has since been applied to study organizational change; specifically, to study how firms respond to sudden changes in their internal composition as well as in their environment (Chang et al. 2003; Fox-Wolftramm and Boal 1998; Gersick 1991; Haveman et al. 2001; Tushman and O'Reilly 1996; Tushman and Romanelli 1985). We selected punctuated equilibrium over other theories of organizational change because of its emphasis on radical change occurring in extended periods of time which suits the context of our case study. Another theory applied to study organizational change and the introduction of IT is structuration theory (Giddens 1984). However, unlike punctuated equilibrium, which concerns long periods of time and the overall deep structure of organizations, structuration theory has been applied to study change over much shorter periods (Orlikowski 1992) and at the individual level (Barret and Walsham 1998; DeSanctis and Poole 1994).

Punctuated equilibrium has also been applied to study organizational change and its relation to information systems. Sabherwal et al. (2001) theorized about the dynamics of organizational change and IT strategic alignment by drawing on the concepts of punctuated equilibrium theory. Similarly, other researchers in IS have adopted a theoretical approach that can be characterized as punctuated equilibrium (Green 1993; Newman and Robey 1992; Porra 1996). For example, Newman and Robey (1992) modeled the IS development process in terms of episodes and encounters, which we argue are similar to evolutionary and revolutionary periods respectively. Green (1993) examined the adoption and use of electronic data interchange in terms of incremental and radical change. Dewar and Dutton (1986) and Ettile et al. (1984) consider incremental and radical changes similar to evolutionary and revolutionary changes, respectively.

Figure 1 depicts the essential features of punctuated equilibrium. One key concept of the theory is that of deep structure. The deep structure consists of “the set of fundamental ‘choices’ a system has made of the basic parts into which the units will be organized and the basic activity patterns that will maintain its existence” (Gersick 1991, p. 14).

For this study we will adopt Gersick's conception of deep structure, that is, the organization's initial choices and factors by virtue of which other options were ruled out. In terms of punctuated equilibrium theory, what concerns us is the deep structure of organizations and how these fundamental properties may change radically during SIS implementation.

## Deep Structure, SIS Implementation and Organizational Change

## Attributes of Deep Structure

To demarcate our conception of deep structure we will draw on the original work of Tushman and Romanelli (1985) grounded on the ideas of Zald (1970) who conceived of organizations as sociopolitical arenas. Tushman and Romanelli (1985, p. 176) consider the deep structure of an organization as composed of five attributes: (1) core beliefs and values regarding the organization, its employees and its environment, (2) services, technology and political time, $^{3}$ (3) the distribution of power, (4) the horizontal and vertical integration arrangements (organizational arrangements), $^{4}$ and (5) the nature, type and pervasiveness of control systems. Tushman and Romanelli propose that transforming the current configuration of these attributes is critical for organizations that seek strategic reorientation.

We suggest that the aforementioned attributes of deep structure are intimately interrelated. To illustrate these relationships, we draw on a metaphor: the structure of a simple classic building (see Figure 2); this helps to highlight the organic and structural properties of deep structure. Accordingly, Figure 2 depicts how core beliefs and values are the foundation of both formal organizational arrangements and distribution of power. Likewise, as shown in Figure 2, the control systems in an organization are supported by both the distribution of power and the formal organizational arrangements. As illustrated in Figure 2, those components of the deep structure are the platform for operations and delivery of services. Hence we suggest that any radical changes in an organization's services, technology, and political time will require modifications in the underlying components of its deep structure.

We believe this conception of deep structure is appropriate for our study given our assumption that the implementation of strategic information systems will entail revolutionary change. Hence we will analyze our case study in terms of these five attributes to see how they manifest throughout the SIS implementation process.

## Factors Affecting Deep Structure During SIS Implementation

Previous IS research (as noted below) supports our theoretical assumption that links the implementation of SIS with the organization's deep structure. It is expected that SIS implementation will bring about high task interdependence as it will have to run across different organizational functions. This implies a profound transformation of the targeted work tasks as well as redistribution in the allocation of resources (Sharma and Yetton 2003). High task interdependence may also bring about resistance from organizational members who perceive their roles and scope of influence threatened by the implementation of the new system (Sharma and Yetton 2003; Silva and Backhouse 2003). In these situations, strong and careful leadership is required. Hence it is not surprising that considerable research on SIS implementation and SIS planning have identified top management support as a key factor for attaining successful implementation (Chan et al. 1997; Davenport and De Long 1998; Hartono et al. 2003; Lederer and Sethi 1988; Premkumar and Ramamurthy 1995; Teo and Ang 1999; Teo et al. 1997; Thong et al. 1996). We argue that this evidence associating top management support to successful implementations suggests a close interplay between an organization's deep structures—specifically, services and technology, power distribution, and control systems—and SIS.

Employees' general attitude toward technology and the history of the firm have also been deemed as fundamental for successful SIS implementation. For example, Mata et al. (1995) suggest that we need to consider an organization's history of technological infrastructure and technical skills developed over time for assessing the capability of an organization to apply IT strategically, which in turn will require a capability to learn from and imitate competitors; such a capability will depend on the set of values and beliefs of the organization. Specifically, Benjamin et al. (1984) found that in order to deploy IT strategically, managers needed an entrepreneurial attitude that considered technology as central in the processes of production, delivery of services, planning, decision-making, and cross functional processes.

Control systems that allow organizations to hire, retain, or train staff are also fundamental for implementing SIS (Hartono et al. 2003; Lederer and Mendelow 1988; Sharma and Yetton 2003). Lack of knowledge in the organization (i.e., the analytical capability of organizational members to realize strategic opportunities and to apply IT to business processes) also impairs IS implementation (see Applegate and Elam 1992; King and Teo 1996; Newkirk et al. 2002). Project management skills to manage contradicting views and rising tensions are also fundamental for the successful implementation of information systems (Charalambos 1999).

## Evolutionary and Revolutionary Change

In the punctuated equilibrium model, evolutionary periods (incremental change) are times when the deep structures undergo little change, and revolutionary periods (radical change) are times when the deep structures are completely transformed.

The difference between the incremental changes of equilibrium periods and revolutionary changes is like the difference between changing the game of basketball by moving the hoops higher and changing it by taking the hoops away. The first kind of change leaves the game's deep structure intact. The second dismantles it. The definitive assertion in this paradigm is that systems do not shift from one kind of game to another through incremental steps. Such transformations occur through wholesale upheaval (Gersick 1991, p. 19).

The differentiation between evolutionary and revolutionary periods is fundamental for our study since, as discussed above, one of our main theoretical assumptions is that the implementation of strategic information systems will entail some degree of revolutionary change. Table 2 summarizes our theoretical assumptions on how SIS affect the deep structure of the organization. In summary, the main research issue concerning this paper consists of studying how the process of implementing a strategic information system relates to an organization's deep structure. We will examine this issue from the perspective of punctuated equilibrium.

## Research Approach

The preferred research approach was an in-depth exploratory case study (Yin 1994). We adopted this approach given that there is still no established theory for studying the relationship between SIS implementation and the deep structure of organizations. According to Yin (1993) and Benbasat et al. (1987), an exploratory case study is an appropriate research strategy to define the early stages of theory development. However, we do not approach our research without any prior theory; rather, we have drawn on punctuated equilibrium and previous IS work to define the topics and areas that we concentrate on in our fieldwork. In this sense, theory and data are linked iteratively (Eisenhardt 1989). We have established initial theoretical assumptions and research issues to guide our fieldwork, as summarized in Tables 1 and 2. In the analysis and implications sections, we will return to refine, modify, and/or abandon these initial theoretical formulations.

## Site, Unit of Analysis, and Timeline

Our case study concerns the attempts of the Ministry of Health of Guatemala to implement a strategic information system from 1998 to 2000. This type of case study may also be labeled as revelatory (Yin 1994) since the researchers had the opportunity to investigate situations and phenomena of which little was known. With this case, we had a favorable opportunity to study the implementation of SIS in an organizational context different from that of a private organization in an English-speaking developed economy. Furthermore, we were offered access to the highest level authorities, assuring necessary depth for studying the deep structure of the organization. Thus, this case study presented us the rare opportunity of exploring the relationship between the deep structure of an organization and SIS implementation as well as expanding our knowledge of that subject matter in a developing countries context.

The main unit of analysis of our case study was the process of implementation of a SIS in Guatemala from 1998 to 2000. Specifically, we studied the implementation of SIS in the two largest hospitals of Guatemala. One of the researchers stayed in Guatemala from January to November of 1998. During this period, he was granted access to senior officials, middle managers and clerical personnel. At the same time, the researcher was given permission to attend meetings and to examine internal documents. Throughout 1999, the researcher made four visits to Guatemala City. Each of the visits lasted for approximately a week and gave the researcher the opportunity to follow up the implementation process by meeting key informants. Subsequent visits over the past years have also been made to better understand the long term implications of the SIS implementation project.

## Data Collection and Analysis

The data collection involved 65 interviews throughout the timeline of the research project. The interviews were semi-structured and had an average duration of 1 hour and 30 minutes. The main questions of the interviews were oriented around the concepts of punctuated equilibrium and its relationship with SIS implementation (see Tables 1 and 2). Interview notes were taken for record keeping purposes. While tape recording would have been the preferred route, this was not possible due to the highly political nature of the case; taping was initially attempted but it was observed that interviewees were less willing to talk freely. The notes of each interview were transcribed immediately. This procedure—note taking and immediate transcription—has been a widely adopted qualitative research technique used in management and information systems (see Eisenhardt and Bourgeois 1988; Walsham and Sahay 1999). Hence the quotations cited in this paper have been paraphrased from our notes. The interviewees were identified with the help of one informant who directed the researcher to the people he considered should be interviewed. Among those interviewed were the Minister, Vice Minister in charge of the hospitals, the directors of the hospitals, IT managers and analysts, as well as middle managers in charge of operations in the hospitals.

In addition to interview data, one of the researchers had the chance to attend 14 meetings in which matters regarding the implementation of the SIS were discussed. The researcher made notes of each of the meetings, registering what was said and who said it, as well as the agreements after each meeting. In addition, the researcher made specific notes regarding his interpretation of the values and social norms that he observed. For example, the researcher would make notes of who seemed uninterested, who talked more and who would tend to dominate the sessions. The researcher had the chance to read official internal documents regarding the SIS; among these documents were plans and progress reports. As with the interviews, the meeting notes and annotations to the documents were in Spanish. The translations and interpretations of the transcripts were facilitated by the fact that the researcher who conducted the fieldwork spoke fluent Spanish. The analysis of the data was conducted in three steps. The first one consisted of organizing and classifying the different pieces of data (i.e., transcripts of the interviews, notes of the observations, and summaries of the examined documents). This step was fundamental in producing an initial overall impression of the data. We used NVIVO $^{5}$ to organize those files. The transcripts were classified according to the organizational position of the person. This was fundamental for later identifying whether the opinions and interpretations of the implementation process varied according to the position held by the interviewees. In this sense, we also defined a field in our database to denote the hierarchical level that the interviewees had in the organization.

The second step consisted in the coding of the document files. The codes corresponded to the concepts and attributes contained in our theoretical formulations. We proceeded initially by reading all of the transcripts and while doing so we decided to which of the attributes the text was to be coded. Figure 3 shows an instance of our coding obtained from NVIVO. It displays on the top left corner the code “poder” (Spanish for power), the name of the files containing instances that we interpreted as being related to power (they are in bold), and the corresponding quotations (text in Spanish). Once we finished with the coding of all the documents, we produced a table for each of the attributes of the organizational deep structure. Each table had three columns, one with reference to the attribute of the deep structure, a column for the associated data to the attribute, and one containing our interpretation.

Those tables were fundamental for creating the syntheses that are presented in the interpretation and discussion section (see Table 3). They were produced through an iterative process as we went back and forth from the attributes to the data to ensure that our interpretations corresponded to our initial theoretical definitions (Eisenhardt 1989). Whenever it was available, we confirmed our interpretations by drawing on the documents that we were able to collect (Denzin and Lincoln 1998; Yin 1994). In order to validate our interpretations, one researcher, the one whose mother tongue is Spanish, would produce the initial entries of the tables mentioned in the paragraph above, then those would be translated to English and handed out to the second researcher with the purpose of clarifying and validating the relationship between data and theory.

The third step consisted of writing the narrative of the case. In writing the narrative, we emphasized the sequence of critical events related to our unit of analysis—implementation of SIS—from its origins to its conclusion (Yin 1994). The selection of events and other components of the narrative were guided by our initial theoretical formulations. In this sense, the latter served as a scaffolding to build the narrative (Walsham 1993). To complete the interpretation we sent a draft of the case narrative to two of our informants in the Ministry. They mostly agreed with our interpretations, but suggested minor changes that were incorporated within the narrative. Once the narrative was completed, we proceeded to collate our findings with our initial theoretical formulations (Eisenhardt 1989; Yin 1994). The reflections resulting from this iterative process are reported in the implications section (see Table 4). We conclude our analysis by considering the theoretical and practical implications that can be learned from our research.

## The Case Study

## The National Context

The case concerns the initiative of the Ministry of Health of Guatemala to implement SIS in its two largest hospitals. Senior officials in the Ministry perceived IS as a vehicle to achieve overall organizational objectives such as reducing costs through a more efficient administration. We can trace the origins of this process to a broad consequence of the peace agreements that ended the Guatemalan civil war in December 1996. With a population of eleven million inhabitants, Guatemala is the most populated country of Central America. Guatemala City, where the two hospitals under study are situated, is the largest city in Central America with three million inhabitants. Guatemala has been officially a democratic republic since its independence from Spain in 1821; however, from 1944 to the end of the 20 $^{th}$ century, there were only six free elections, two in the middle of the century and the latest four since 1986. During the cold war, Guatemala was ruled by a military dictatorship supported in part by the United States (Gleijeses 1991). A guerilla war lasted from 1962 to 1996. Throughout the civil war the Ministry of Defense took the largest amount of money from the national budget, relegating the ministries of health and education to a secondary role (Aguilera et al. 1991).

The signing of the subsequent peace accords was followed by offers of financial support made by international funding and cooperation agencies. One of those offers came from an international bank that offered a soft loan to finance the reform of the health sector in Guatemala. $^{6}$ This initiative not only involved the Bank but also other national organizations, such as the Ministry of Health and NGOs (nongovernmental organizations). Since the majority of people in Guatemala (80 percent) receive health care from organizations administrated by the state, the Bank agreed that the main institution leading this project would be the government through the Ministry of Health. Among the objectives of the Reform were the extension of the Guatemalan public health services and an improvement in the quality of those services. Both the Bank and government expected that eventually the achievement of these objectives would lead to an enhanced overall health situation for the population.

## Organizational Context

In this subsection we will first describe the formal structure (i.e., the way the Ministry arranges its vertical and horizontal integration along with its IS administrative layout). Then we will describe how the Reform project was a catalyst for change.

## The Formal Structure (Horizontal and Vertical Integration)

The Ministry of Health of Guatemala is a classic example of a hierarchical form of vertical integration. The supreme authority is the Minister and the main functions of the Ministry of Health, including those of primary health care, hospitals, and administration, are managed by respective Vice Ministers. Each Vice Minister has absolute autonomy and authority within his area and they report directly to the Minister. The Ministry of Health has more than 20,000 employees. Nevertheless, by 1998 it did not have a formal structure for the IT function. Instead, there were ad hoc groups formed around the different components of the Ministry. There was a group that collected and processed the information produced by all of the primary health care posts in the country. It was this group's responsibility to give an account of the planning and coverage of primary health care. This unit provided vital information to the Vice Minister responsible for primary health care and it had in place a centralized information system. A regional health worker described it:

The primary health information system is too centralized and has a total top down approach. For example, we have to fill in more than 40 forms each month. In some cases our personnel have to close the clinics to be able to fill in the forms. Moreover, the IS unit never comes back to us with information.

This unit did not support other areas of the Ministry either. Since this unit was dedicated completely to primary health care and was at the service of one of the Vice Ministers, other areas of the Ministry, like hospitals for instance, did not get any information.

Horizontal integration in the Ministry was weak so each unit had its own information services. The Ministry did not have an integrated approach to the use of IS and IT. Although it was not explicit through any sort of regulation, each Vice Minister had the authority and resources to hire the IS personnel they wanted to develop their own IS. Each ostensibly had an IS but there was neither coordination nor collaboration among groups. The norm was that, if someone like the Vice Ministers were to have the resources and authority, they would be allowed to create their own IS groups. It is fair to say that by 1998, IS was seen only as a support function to the main activities of the Ministry. The new administration, however, wanted this to change with the advent of the Reform initiative.

## The Reform and the Program of Improvement of Health Services

The Reform was composed of several projects, including the Program for the Improvement of Health Services (PIOHS). The objectives of this program were to extend health services to areas and population groups that were formerly neglected. This program envisaged the transformation of the administrative structures and procedures of the Ministry as well as a complete renewal of the hospital systems. The Reform of the health sector proposed a new delivery model for the health services based on efficient administration, decentralization, and outsourcing. It was clearly stated in the “terms of reference” of the PIOHS that IT would be an important component to bring about these changes. The PIOHS was organized into three projects, one of which was responsible for the hospitals’ transformations—the subject of this case study. It will be referred to as the Hospitals project, a significant component of which is the SIS implementation in the two largest hospitals in Guatemala.

As will be shown in the next section, the IS under study was indeed strategic as it complies with the criteria expounded in the theory section. Accordingly, (1) the hospital system was aimed at attaining the overall long term objectives of the government to curb corruption, (2) it was designed to radically change the way the hospitals were managed, and (3) it was explicitly recognized by the Bank and Ministry as key to fulfilling the overall health strategy.

The starting of the PIOHS coincided with the inauguration of the new democratically elected government of Guatemala in 1995. At the time before the election, the hospitals were under a severe financial and administrative crisis—there was a shortage of supplies and people had to wait a very long time to receive treatment. Consequently, throughout their campaigns, presidential candidates had public health as a top priority. When elected, the new authorities regarded the PIOHS as fundamental for delivering their campaign promises of improving health services by making them more efficient, wider in scope, and less costly. One of the members of the Hospitals project team expressed the relevance of the PIOHS for the government in the following manner: “This government really wanted to do well in the administration of health. For them, this was important as they wanted to accumulate political capital to be reelected in the next election of 1999.”

Senior officials of the Ministry believed that they required new people to bring about the changes they wanted to introduce. The PIOHS became the right arm of the Minister of Health as it allowed him to hire technicians and professionals of very high caliber. It was a way around the rigid hiring procedures of the Ministry. The salaries offered by the Ministry were limited by an old salary scale mandated by the civil servants law; it paid significantly below market level. In addition the bureaucratic procedures for hiring could take as long as a year. The funds provided by the Bank for the PIOHS were not only enough to pay for highly skilled technicians but were not attached to the Guatemalan civil servants legislation.

Although the PIOHS was controlled by the Guatemalan government, one of the conditions of the Bank was that the administration and control of the funds had to be separate from the Ministry. Thus, the PIOHS constituted a parallel structure to the Ministry of Health and had its own manager and personnel (see Figure 4). To house their offices, they rented a property five minutes from the Ministry headquarters. The manager of the PIOHS was appointed by the Minister of Health and reported directly to him. Furthermore, the Minister was entitled to appoint the managers of each of the components of the PIOHS. For the Hospitals project, the Minister appointed a very influential doctor (K.C.).

The situation of the hospitals was critical; the Minister required someone he could trust and that had enough energy to lead such a crucial project. He appointed K.C. because this person was a very close friend of the Minister and a close relative of an influential person in the President's cabinet. As a professional, K.C. was a doctor who specialized in intensive care, and was, before being appointed to lead the Hospitals project, a manager of a company that distributes medical equipment for hospitals. Despite the fact that K.C. did not have any experience in the public sector or administrative experience in projects such as PIOHS, he was respected by his subordinates.

K.C. has a very interesting personality. Being a doctor accustomed to running intensive care units in hospitals, he in a way thought that the project could be managed in a similar manner. So it was common to see him walking into our offices and give commands. He expected things to be done quickly. However, after a while he understood that things in public organizations take longer than they take in intensive care units. I regard him as a very well educated and intelligent man with great respect for other human beings.

## The Hospitals and the SIS

The Bank did not lend the Reform money unconditionally. For the PIOHS, the Bank specifically requested that the

Guatemalan government improve and extend health services. It was a fundamental principle of the Reform that the health services provided by the government could not possibly be improved without changing the administration and structures of the Ministry and its dependencies. In this sense, both the Bank and Guatemalan authorities agreed. That the whole administration of health services in the Ministry required reform was particularly evident for the two largest hospitals: San Juan de Dios and Roosevelt, the main targets of the resources and efforts of the PIOHS.

Hospital San Juan de Dios $^{7}$ is the largest hospital in the country and is situated in Guatemala City. In 2003, it attended to 236,754 patients and was running at full capacity. Of those, 90,000 were emergencies and 36,940 stayed one or more nights for treatment. It covers most medical specialties, such as pediatrics, maternity, and internal medicine. It had an annual budget in 2003 of about 80 million Quetzales (approximately \$10 million), funded completely by the government. The relatively low budget in comparison with its huge demand put pressure on the administration of the hospital.

The other hospital, Hospital Roosevelt, $^{8}$ was founded in 1955 and is the second largest hospital in the country; it covers the same specialties as Hospital San Juan de Dios. It had 842 beds and 13 operating rooms. In the year 2000, the hospital conducted 13,000 surgical operations. Its budget was about 60 million Quetzales (about \$7.5 million). Hospital Roosevelt was also fully funded by the government and, like the other hospital, was constantly operating at full capacity.

In addition to these challenges, the administrative situation in the hospitals was precarious as corruption was pervasive at most levels. K.C. notes,

The major problem with our hospitals is corruption, which is present at every level. Just to give you an example: suppliers of medicines bribe managers to ensure the selling of their products; medicine disappears from the shelves of the warehouses and personnel supposedly hired to work in the hospitals do not show up at work.

IT was regarded as a critical component of the PIOHS to make the administration of the hospitals more efficient and accountable. Accordingly, for the Hospitals project, K.C. earmarked a substantial amount of financial resources, one million US dollars, for the implementation of SIS in the hospitals. K.C.'s plan was to invest those funds initially in San Juan de Dios and Roosevelt and then extend the project to the other 17 national hospitals.

In carrying out his plans, K.C. faced a great challenge as he did not have any IS professionals in his team and there was no IT infrastructure in any of the hospitals. Hence his initial reaction was to rely on the extant IS personnel in the Ministry. Yet, as was explained above, there was no formal IS function in the Ministry, so he had to count on other departments sharing their personnel. With nobody to depend on for in-house software development, K.C. was advised to use the IS funds to buy a customized software package. One of the systems analysts who worked in the area of primary health care was called in to cooperate initially with the PIOHS. He recalled these initiatives:

I remember that K.C. asked me to help him. I did not have too much time since I was working with some projects in my area. However, here in the Ministry we organized a trip to Mexico to see a couple of systems for hospitals. None of those systems were appropriate for Guatemalan hospitals, since those were running in mainframes and the hospitals in Guatemala only had very old PCs at that time. After the trip, K.C. did not pursue this issue further; he kind of lost interest in IT.

Activities related to SIS in hospitals emerged from their lethargic state when in January 1998, K.C. hired a person to be in charge of the information systems component of the Hospitals project. This person was hired under the PIOHS; that meant he did not undergo the lengthy bureaucratic process required by government and was offered a competitive salary. He was a young systems engineer who returned to Guatemala after having done a M.Sc. in information systems at a reputable North American university. People in the Ministry would refer to him as the Engineer. K.C. asked the Engineer to examine the budget assigned for IT and then to write a plan on what to do in the two hospitals so the SIS could be implemented. The Engineer was enthusiastic and very keen to undertake this work. One of the early members of the PIOHS remembered the arrival and attitudes of the Engineer:

I recall him very well. In our meetings the Engineer would talk about information systems with authority, although I may say he did it with some tone of arrogance. That was not explicit but you could tell it was there. He was on very good terms with K.C. He identified with the Engineer in the sense that they belonged to the same social stratum and were educated abroad. K.C. gave him a very good salary. So although the ideas and presence of the Engineer were welcomed for the majority of the group, that tone of arrogance and the big differences in salaries with us did not make him very popular. I would say that some people even resented that.

After 2 months the Engineer presented a document containing an analysis of the current situation in the hospitals and his ideas for using IT strategically. There was a starting section describing the lack of IT infrastructure in the two hospitals. There were several PCs but most were old and stand-alone. The second problem was that there was not a single person in the hospitals who specialized in information systems. The document stipulated that, before doing anything, the Hospitals project should work to create a minimum required IT infrastructure. The Engineer proposed a client server architecture supported by an Ethernet network. The operating system suggested was Windows NT and the basic software was also from Microsoft. The Engineer recommended this because of the relatively low cost of these products, their ease-of-use, and the availability of technicians in the Guatemalan market.

The Engineer also proposed the hiring of systems administrators for each of the hospitals.

The document presented by the Engineer also included a proposal for the analysis, design, and implementation of the SIS. The core idea of his proposal was that the SIS should focus on the administration of the hospitals rather than managing patient interactions. The purpose was to curb corruption, which was deemed as the most serious problem, thereby bringing approval and political capital to the party in office. The plan also specified that the requirements of the system would be elicited by consulting the users of the system (i.e., senior management and all other administrative levels including those in charge of the administrative operations of the hospitals). Once the requirements had been elicited, the development and implementation stages of the SIS would be outsourced. The Engineer had this rationale for his strategy:

During my Master course I learned many things about developing systems. One option is to outsource the development. This seemed to me as a good idea since there were no information systems specialists in the Ministry and the creation of the unit with those professionals would have taken too long. Another thing about outsourcing is that you have to be careful which tasks you outsource, so you do not become dependent on the providers. I decided that in the hospitals we were pretty good in knowing our own procedures and that an outsourcer would provide expertise in developing the systems.

K.C and his team presented the Minister and the Bank with the plan prepared by the Engineer emphasizing how the SIS could both dramatically improve the administration of the hospitals and curb corruption. The Minister, the Bank, and personnel of the hospitals received the plan with great enthusiasm. For the Minister, the plan was ideal for delivering the government's promises of improving the hospitals' services and tackling corruption. The Bank welcomed this plan because, from the Bank's point of view, its implementation would have a significant impact on the administration of hospitals and would strengthen the hospitals' infrastructure.

After the approval of the plan, the Engineer began working in two areas: the infrastructure and the design of the SIS. For the infrastructure, the Engineer and his team prepared the contractual terms of the tendering process that would select the supplier of the technological infrastructure. The infrastructure was finished 8 months after the tender was first offered. Meanwhile the Engineer managed to convince K.C. to finance one system administrator for each hospital. According to the Engineer, the most difficult part in the hiring was in convincing K.C. to offer the prospective system administrators competitive salaries, since those salaries in the market were higher than those of young physicians. Finally, by February 1999, the two systems administrators were hired under the parallel structure of the PIOHS.

For the development of the SIS, a participative methodology based on focus groups was proposed. The focus groups were made up of representatives of the different departments involved in the administration of the hospitals. K.C. was very keen on having the focus groups for eliciting user requirements. The participative approach seemed appropriate because it allowed previously neglected stakeholders, namely middle managers and others, to voice their concerns and share their know how. Middle managers of the hospitals were initially skeptical of the PIOHS as they had experienced many years of neglect from the central authorities. They believed this to be just another attempt that would fail because of lack of funds or the absence of capable human resources. In fact, the culture of the administration of public health in Guatemala showed a pattern of disappointment with unfulfilled promises. The deputy director of one hospital in downtown Guatemala City said,

Many people come here to the hospital offering support for information systems. For example, international agencies come here with software developed by them. But these programs are not very useful since they do not respond to our needs and most importantly we do not have the technology to make them run. People from the Ministry also come to promise that they are going to do something regarding the lack of computers; they never come back with what they have promised, so when K.C. and the Engineer talked about information systems again, the generalized feeling among us here was of disbelief. Yet, we gradually changed our minds as we saw how serious and committed they were.

Initially, the focus groups began analyzing two different sets of processes. One hospital concentrated on the medicine supply process while the other focused on the administration of human resources. Each focus group was composed of the head of the leading department (i.e., the head of human resources for one hospital and the heads of the purchasing, warehouse, and pharmacy sections in the other). Other members of the focus groups included operational personnel and representatives of the senior management. Their first task was that of specifying the different actions and those responsible for each procedure related to their particular area of concern. The Engineer recalls the work and outcomes of the focus groups:

At the outset the mood in the focus groups was a bit skeptical. People believed that it was an initiative that soon would wind down. However, after a few sessions people got enthusiastic as they had the chance to discuss the matters related to their particular tasks and were able to compare the prescribed courses of action with what they were actually doing. The result of these meetings was sound documents that we eventually used as the system requirements for outsourcing the development and implementation of the systems.

We had the chance to examine the documents and they indeed looked professional. They had a detailed data flow for each task, as well as who were the personnel responsible for executing the tasks. The focus groups also distinguished between the tasks that had to be automated from those that would remain manual. The documents included a section specifying the data required for each transaction and another comparing the as-is system with the to-be system. The documents were clear and detailed and were a good guide for developing the necessary information systems. Everything was going according to plan until the end of 1998.

## Controversies and Sustainability

One event that weakened the implementation of the SIS was the resignation of the Engineer at the end of 1998 (see chronology of the case in Figure 5). The Engineer had two major motivations for his leaving. First, the relationship between the Engineer and the permanent staff in the Ministry deteriorated over time. Some members of staff deemed the Engineer to be arrogant and did not feel comfortable with the prominent position and salary he achieved in a relatively short period of time. This situation created a tense environment for the Engineer who needed the full cooperation of the permanent staff in the Ministry for the SIS. The Engineer expressed his views in this regard:

People in the Ministry did not like me at all. They used to block most of my initiatives. In general all my requests and petitions were delayed if not ignored. For example, when I hired my assistant, the person in charge of the building would not get me an office for him. He had to sit in the corner of a crowded office. It was different in the hospitals where people greeted me enthusiastically.

In addition, the Engineer received an offer from a consultant firm in Guatemala City, which according to the Engineer “was too good to be rejected.” He saw the offer as an opportunity to earn more and to work in a more homogeneous, amicable, stable and cooperative environment.

Nevertheless, the implementation of the SIS continued its course. The position of the Engineer was taken by another young systems analyst so the plan for implementing the SIS remained unchanged, but its execution lost much of its momentum. One of the closest aids of K.C. commented:

The resignation of the Engineer was a big loss for the project. He was really the one who conceived of the whole strategy. He was knowledgeable and had great leadership skills, and most importantly K.C. trusted him. It was a pity we were not able to retain him. His departure was a blow to the motivation and enthusiasm of the members of the project. People in the hospitals began saying again that the systems initiative would end up being a disappointment as many others launched by the authorities in the Ministry.

Actions for outsourcing the development of the systems began in early September 1999, immediately after the focus groups finished their tasks and the infrastructure was in place. The Minister and K.C. wanted the SIS to be in place before the presidential election so that the then current administration could show the achievements of their work in terms of curbing corruption in the national hospitals. According to the political constitution of Guatemala, elections are held every 4 years, so the government knew their administration was to be tested in the polls. The Minister and his team ran out of time for setting up the SIS because of the lengthy processes for the approval of funds from the Bank and the difficulties in hiring new staff for the PIOHS project. Another reason for the delay was the initial decision by K.C. to obtain the systems for the hospitals as packaged software; hence the Engineer was hired early in 1998 when the current party had been in office for 4 years.

The plan was to show the two hospitals as models of what the government could do, if they were granted another presidential term. This put great pressure on the SIS team since general elections would be held in November of that year. This would leave the outsourcers only 3 months for developing and implementing the systems.

The proposed timeline was challenged by the Bank. The person in charge of managing the outsourcing tendering process was very clear about what he thought:

I told K.C. and his group that they were crazy. That it was impossible to develop information systems of such complexity in only 2 months and then leave 1 month for training and testing. Nevertheless, K.C. is so stubborn that he managed to convince the Minister and other authorities and got their approval.

In addition, he convinced the Minister about the positive impact that bringing efficiency and transparency to the hospitals would have on voters. For K.C., the support of the Minister was crucial given that the proposal for the SIS was then presented by the Minister himself to the Bank as a matter of great importance for the country. No doubt the close relationship of K.C. with the Minister was key for obtaining his support. The approval from the Bank was not difficult to ensure given that the funds for the systems were already committed 2 years earlier when the budget of the PIOHS was first approved. In addition, at the time that K.C. submitted the tendering proposal for approval to the Bank, the Bank saw the endorsement of the funds as a necessary risk for the hospitals to take advantage of the infrastructure already in place. Once the approval of the Bank was filed in the Ministry, the tendering process began.

Advertisements were placed in national and international newspapers inviting IT firms to bid for the development of the hospital SIS. However, when they heard that the time was only 3 months and that there was not much money available, they quickly demurred. One was from a firm who said it was impossible to develop a strategic information system in such a short period of time and that participating in the project would jeopardize the reputation of their firm. The last envelope was a bid from a European company accompanied by a box of documents explaining their approach. Their bid fell within the specified timeline—3 three months—and also within the budgetary ceiling. That firm was selected without further discussions.

## After the Elections

After the elections, all of the activities related to the implementation of the SIS were halted. The political party in power when the PIOHS began was ousted from office; they lost the elections by a landslide. Although the European company managed to have the majority of the software of the SIS ready by December 1999, it was far from complete. Thus, by January 2000, it was clear that the development and implementation of the SIS would have to be carried over to the new government. This posed a serious problem for the SIS team given that the relationship between the newly elected and former authorities was far from cordial; after a bitter campaign, both camps accused each other of corruption. In fact, in February 2000, after 1 month of being in office, the new Minister fired the whole PIOHS team including the SIS team. This was possible because the personnel of the PIOHS were on a 1 year contract as part of the parallel structure created by the Reform program.

The new authorities questioned the validity and relevance of the hospital SIS. They argued that aid agencies already had software packages for hospital administration and that the proposed infrastructure was too expensive. They also questioned the usefulness and transparency of the SIS and the tendering processes that resulted in selecting the European firm. Government officials balked at the amount of money that was supposed to be paid to the Europeans. The person who replaced K.C. as the head of the PIOHS described the situation:

Those systems are far too expensive. A poor country like ours cannot afford to pay such amounts of money for information systems. I have made contacts with an international agency of cooperation and they are going to give us a system for free.

One consultant who had worked for the former government, and was close to the new one, expressed his disappointment with the new situation:

That was a wasted opportunity. The infrastructure and systems were appropriate. The administrative information systems that they outsourced were unique, and cannot be replaced by a packaged program. What aid agencies offer regarding software only covers hospital statistics and does nothing regarding finances, human resources, and administration. For me, the obliteration of the Hospitals project leaves the hospitals at the same stage they were 4 years ago.

Furthermore, the new authorities also considered that having information systems administrators in the hospitals was a waste of resources and fired them almost immediately. When one of the hospitals was subsequently visited in September 2000, it was observed that the infrastructure was being neglected. There were no personnel responsible for the equipment so no maintenance was being done and the main use of the networked PCs was word processing. The new government had a different rationale regarding the use of IT. One of the new senior officials said that, as far as the government was concerned, IS was an expense as it does not directly have an impact on health care. IS went from being a strategic partner to a line on the expense report.

## Analysis

In this section we present the results of our interpretation of the case. It is structured around the concepts of punctuated equilibrium theory. Accordingly, we will initially discuss the deep structure of the organization before the implementation of the SIS. This is followed by the description of the triggers and mechanisms of change related to the implementation of the SIS. This process is depicted in Figure 6. Finally, we reflect on the reasons for the noninstitutionalization of the SIS.

## The Deep Structure Before the Implementation of the System

Before the implementation of the SIS, the core values and beliefs of the organization regarding the possibility of any sort of change can be characterized as predominantly skeptical. This overall skeptical attitude toward change can largely be understood as the result of a long history of dictatorship and military control in Guatemala (Martínez 1998). From the beginning of the 20 $^{th}$ century and throughout the civil war, Guatemala had been ruled by military regimes (Gleijeses 1991; Schlesinger and Kinzer 1982). The long dictatorship made Guatemalans skeptical about economic and social change, even after the signing of the peace accords (see Godoy 2002; Miller 1996; Stepputat 1999). As was described in the narrative of the case, this disbelief toward change was a common theme in our data.

Before the Reform project and the implementation of the SIS, the overall strategic orientation of the Ministry of Health, in terms of its services, technology, and political time, could be characterized as centralized. The model for delivering health services was greatly centralized and designed to be delivered exclusively through personnel from the Ministry. It was centralized because major decisions and resources were made and kept by the main authorities in the Ministry headquarters in Guatemala City. This explains in part the size of the Ministry, more than 20,000 employees, and the problems it experienced in managing its operations. Likewise, the use of IT reflected its hierarchical structure so its IS were intended solely for serving the purposes of individual units and not for supporting horizontal processes. Furthermore, IT was not integrated into the delivery of services; indeed, planning and execution were conducted based on manual systems. In relation to political time, we found that the strategic orientation of the Ministry, before and throughout the implementation of the SIS, was completely influenced by the scheduling of the presidential elections, which according to the Guatemalan constitution are held every 4 years. Projects and plans were formulated so they could be finished before the elections.

Before the implementation of the SIS, the Ministry of Health was organized hierarchically with the Minister as the highest authority, and two vice ministers, one in charge of primary health care and the other responsible for hospitals (i.e., the formal organizational structure of the Ministry clearly had strong vertical integration). Moreover, the organizational chart did not include an IS function and IT services were provided in each department by medical doctors, statisticians, and other personnel who learned the use of computers by “ear.” The organizational structure of the Ministry was established in the public health law that the Guatemalan congress approved shortly after the signing of the peace accords. Likewise, the hiring of personnel and acquisition of services and goods was regulated by national laws approved during the dictatorship. While such laws would stipulate salaries and roles for regular employees of the Ministry (doctors, nurses, clerks, etc.), they did not consider positions for IS professionals. Within such a legal frame, any type of fundamental change would require a substantial effort in political negotiation.

The distribution of power in the Ministry was concentrated in senior management, specifically Ministers and Vice Ministers, who having their own budgets could hire their own staff and were able to formulate and execute their own programs. In the hospitals, the distribution of power was similar. Formally, the hospital director was the highest authority over operations involving purchases, hiring, and planning of activities. Informally, though, we found that employees executing operations and delivering services to patients had some degree of discretion on their activities which in some cases led to abuse and corruption. Hence, implementing more controls over operations and the delivery of services was one of the main objectives of the SIS.

The Ministry of Health utilized both informal and formal control systems. The formal control systems only reported outcome-related epidemiological and statistical data. This type of system had two main disadvantages. First, output was not linked to input, so the Ministry could not associate results with operations, resources, and costs. Second, the reporting forms were filled in manually and sent by mail to the Ministry's headquarters to be inputted into a spreadsheet. These procedures had been in place in Guatemala for a long time—since the dictatorship—and had been regarded as slow and inaccurate (Burkhalter et al. 1994). Hence the Minister and other senior managers ended up relying on their close aids for monitoring the quality of service and performance. Therefore, Ministers and Vice Ministers would appoint as their aids only people who they could trust. Nonetheless, the informality of this type of system allowed for arbitrary and loose controls, which were a major obstacle for curbing corruption and achieving administrative efficiency. Through the deployment of the SIS, the Bank and government hoped to have more effective and accurate control systems.

Thus, in our case, the deep structure before the implementation of the SIS was characterized by (1) skepticism, (2) centralization, (3) concentration of power, (4) tight hierarchies, and (5) loose control systems. Senior management of the Reform along with those of the new government envisioned a transformation of the Ministry that involved radical changes in its deep structure. These changes are depicted in Figure 6 as the target deep structure that—in opposition to the then current deep structure—would be characterized by (1) enthusiasm, (2) decentralization, (3) empowerment of middle managers, (4) more flexible organizational arrangements, and, at the same time (5) tighter control systems. In sum, the introduction of the SIS would transform the current control systems. Hence the Ministry expected that, as a result of the SIS, the administration and delivery of services of the hospitals would be improved and eventually that would enhance the image of the government before the elections.

## The Implementation of SIS and Punctuated Equilibrium

One of the main propositions of punctuated equilibrium theory is that an organization will remain static for long periods of time. Such long periods of stability are punctuated by revolutionary periods. According to Gersick (1991), there are two sources of disruption that may provoke revolutionary changes: (1) internal disturbances and (2) environmental variations. Any of these types of disturbance can threaten the ability of an organization to obtain resources from its environment. In such circumstances, an organization will need to rearrange its deep structure so it fits with the new environment. In our case study, we found that in the 1990s there were two events that presented a threat to the stability of the Ministry of Health: (1) The signing of the peace accords and (2) the advent of democracy (see Figure 6). After the latter, to ensure their jobs, authorities in the Ministry needed to obtain the support of the population. This was a radical change in comparison with the conditions prevailing during the dictatorship.

During the dictatorship, the Minister of Health would only respond to the demands of the President and accordingly would order vice ministers and hospital directors regarding what had to be done. As long as the Minister was considered to be loyal to the President, his job would be ensured. Once democracy was instituted, the Guatemalan government, of which the Ministry of Health was part, was made accountable not only to the President but also to the population. It thus had to ensure the provision of a proper level of services. To reach such a level of services, the Ministry would need more financial resources. One source of funding was the Bank, which offered money under the condition that the Ministry would reform its model for delivering its services. Hence, with these changes in the environment, the then current deep structure no longer could ensure the permanence of the Minister and that of his close aids. Thus, after the peace accords, and with the institutionalization of elections, the deep structure of the Ministry needed to be reoriented.

However, the theory of punctuated equilibrium suggests that environmental changes on their own are not enough to engender revolutionary changes. These can only be achieved by the convergence of several triggering factors which, according to Gersick, are the

conditions that break a system's inertia and thereby allow revolutions to begin....When internal and/or external events make a system's deep structure obsolete, it usually takes a crisis, and the subsequent attraction of newcomers, to intervene and end equilibrium periods (p. 26).

Gersick recognizes three types of triggers: external or internal events, newcomers, and milestones. We have already discussed the external events that led to the need for radical changes in the Ministry; in the rest of this subsection, we will reflect on the other two triggers: milestones and newcomers (see Figure 6).

Milestones as triggers of organizational change refer to how a group interprets time. Milestones are not associated with an event per se but with the timing of the event. It is the timing of the event that would make it meaningful to a group. This is expressed by Gersick in the following way:

When individuals and groups are reminded, by temporal milestones, that their time is finite, they feel a sense of urgency to reevaluate past choices, pursue aspirations they have put off, and take new steps (p. 24).

In our case, we found that the date of the forthcoming elections was a crucial milestone for the authorities of the Ministry of Health. It instilled a sense of urgency in the authorities to achieve their strategic reorientation before such a deadline. Thus, the forthcoming elections plus the external events caused a sense of crisis in the Ministry.

We found that such a crisis led to the arrival of two newcomers: K.C. and the Engineer. Newcomers are instrumental in triggering radical change in organizations (Gersick 1991; Tushman and Romanelli 1985). Externally recruited executives are three times more likely to initiate change than other triggers (Tushman and Romanelli 1985). According to Gersick, new executives would break old structures and would create new ones:

The newcomer has the opportunity to see the system in an entirely different context than incumbent members, and he or she may begin problem solving on a new path. It is then the newcomer's explicit task to break the old deep structure and establish a new one (p. 23).

In this sense, newcomers are key to leading organizations into breaking the inertia that is characteristic of stability periods. This process of breaking with the inertia is called symmetry breaking (see Figure 6).

Symmetry breaking consists of the recognition of new patterns. It is a metaphor to make sense of how a particular way of thinking is transformed. For Gersick,

The construct of symmetry breaking helps researchers to understand the dawn of insight during transitions. Given a piece of information that provides a new way to look at it, a confusing puzzle can resolve into a coherent picture seemingly instantly. During the very swift transitions that can occur in project groups, this metaphor fits people's experience of things “falling into place” (p. 29).

In our case, the symmetry breaking came from the Engineer and K.C. when they proposed to curb corruption in hospitals and improve the image of the Ministry by implementing the SIS. It was a symmetry breaking situation given that, before the arrival of the Engineer, management thought of IS as an instrument for collecting epidemiological data and for serving departmental goals only.

In summary, the lens of punctuated equilibrium has allowed us to relate the implementation of the SIS with external events such as the end of the civil war, the advent of democracy, and elections (see Figure 6). The combination of those factors engendered a misalignment in the deep structure of the Ministry that we argued created a crisis which brought about two newcomers, K.C. and the Engineer. These newcomers, particularly the Engineer, brought new ideas that initiated a process of symmetry breaking. Accordingly, the Engineer proposed the implementation of the SIS with the purpose of supporting the overall strategy of the Ministry of curbing corruption, improving the administration of services and enhancing their political capital in view of the forthcoming election. Likewise, the proposal of the SIS was aligned with the directives of the Bank in the sense of providing resources to buy IT to support the overall reform of the Ministry of Health. However, the implementation of the SIS would find resistance in the deep structure of the organization. That is the subject of the next subsection.

## What Went Wrong

The theory of punctuated equilibrium suggests that revolutionary periods will be resisted by the deep structure. In this sense, Gersick indicates that the concept of deep structure helps in understanding resistance: “The construct of a deep structure that keeps systems basically stable during equilibrium periods offers a new way to understand systems’ resistance to change” (p. 32). With its emphasis on deep structure, the theory of punctuated equilibrium offers an interesting alternative lens to examine resistance to change associated with SIS implementation. It differs from the traditional IS literature on this topic which has concentrated mainly on the relationships between users and developers (Cavaye and Christiansen 1996; Markus 1983; Markus and Bjørn-Andersen 1987) or between consultants and managers (Bloomfield and Vurdubakis 1994; Yakura 1992). Punctuated equilibrium allows us to see beyond relationships among agents. In our case, we did not find any resistance between users and designers or between consultants and managers. Instead we found that the Engineer was resisted and blocked by other line managers. The source of the resistance lay in the core beliefs and values of old-timers who interpreted the salary and treatment given to the Engineer by senior management as unfair. The tactics employed by old-timers to show this discontent were blocking initiatives and other manifestations of passive aggression that eventually led to the resignation of the Engineer.

However, the toughest resistance did not come from agents but from the structures. It was the statutes that regulate the organizational arrangements of the Ministry, and not the IS function or the role of IS professionals, that proved the real obstacle for the institutionalization of the SIS. This gap in the formal organization of the Ministry engendered a parallel structure that was the locus of the required IS professionals. The parallel structure, by virtue of its temporary status, was not only a factor that contributed to the noninstitutionalization of the SIS but also was a source of distrust and resistance shown to the Engineer. Thus, by drawing on punctuated equilibrium as our theoretical lens, we were able to identify how organizational arrangements can be a significant obstacle for the institutionalization of SIS. Indeed, it was punctuated equilibrium that drew our attention to the deep structure and to how changes are introduced as the result of internal disturbances and/or environmental variations. We claim, as will be discussed in the next section, that this is a key contribution to the body of knowledge of SIS.

As was described in the narrative of the case, the implementation of the SIS was not completed because the political party in office lost the elections and, as a result of it, the parallel structure of the Hospitals project disappeared. In other words, the revolutionary process did not succeed in altering the deep structures of the organization. This outcome is consistent with the theory of punctuated equilibrium, as Gersick observes: “a system may go through a clear, timelimited transition period, experiencing many of the unsettling processes described earlier, yet it can emerge at the end without having accomplished revisions in its deep structure" (p. 31). When this occurs, Gersick points out that an organization will be weakened: "systems that have undergone unsuccessful or abortive transitions are, indeed, different after revolutionary periods because they are weakened" (p. 31). In our case, the deep structure not only persisted without fundamental changes but also was weakened, since the failure in implementing the SIS reinforced skepticism and distrust toward the central administration.

Nonetheless, by focusing on the processes of revolutionary change and not on their outcomes, we have learned about the interplay between SIS implementation and deep structures. ## Implications and Limitations

In this research, we studied the design and implementation of an SIS in a governmental agency of a developing country using the theoretical lens of punctuated equilibrium. This allowed us to identify the links between changes in the environment of the organization and the need to redirect its strategic orientation by altering its deep structure. We found that the SIS was proposed by a newcomer to the organization as a mechanism of symmetry breaking to bring about such a reorientation. The SIS was designed to implement new control mechanisms and to enhance the reputation of the government in the view of the forthcoming elections, and also to ensure and maintain the funding from the Bank. Our study showed that the institutionalization of an SIS can require profound modifications in the organization's regulations and statutes, specifically on those that concern the IS function and the role of IS professionals. Without these modifications in the organization's structure, the institutionalization of an SIS can be compromised. In this section, we discuss the implications of our main findings in relation to the current body of literature of SIS, and also their implications for managers and researchers interested in studying the deployment of IT with strategic purposes in developing countries. A summary of our findings and their relation to the current literature on SIS is presented in Table 4.

We found in the literature that there are three main reasons for the failure of SIS: (1) inadequate planning (Ciborra 1991;

Lee and Pai 2003), (2) lack of leadership (Earl 1993), and (3) organizations not having the necessary skills to implement those types of systems (Newkirk et al. 2002). As we indicated in the introduction, those propositions are the results of studies conducted in private organizations in developed countries. Yet, the results of our study suggest that an SIS can fail to be institutionalized despite having adequate planning, strong leadership, and very knowledgeable IS professionals. Our data shows that the most relevant factors influencing the outcome of the system were the organizational structure and the political timing of the organization. Hence our contribution to the literature of SIS is having identified two specific factors that can influence the institutionalization of an SIS (see Table 4, entry (a)). Thus, our initial assumption regarding the theoretical propositions of the IS literature as having inadequate explanatory power for SIS in developing countries was supported by the results of our study.

The aforementioned factors are interesting since they are contextual. They suggest that international agencies interested in deploying IT for strategic purposes in government organizations in developing countries should look at structural and contextual factors. International agencies should examine carefully the situation of an organization they want to work with in terms of its structure and determine whether those arrangements allow for the presence of IS professionals. If the IT function and the role of IS professionals are not defined, it is very likely that such an obstacle would be only temporarily solved by hiring consultants or by the creation of parallel structures. As was shown in our study, this solution is transitory and can be very risky in terms of guaranteeing the stability of the system (see Table 4, entries (c) and (f)). This is especially critical if the political time of the organization is short. Without attention to those structural and contextual aspects, the efforts and resources spent on deploying SIS can be wasted.

The SIS that we studied was designed to introduce new mechanisms of control with the purpose of improving the administration of the hospitals and enhancing the reputation of the government. The potential of SIS in transforming similar attributes in organizations has already been observed in the literature (see Table 4, entry (b)). Our contribution, though, is having shown empirically that SIS can affect the core values and beliefs of an organization and can potentially contribute—in the case of government organizations—to increase the reputation of the political party in office. In our case, the participatory and rapid approach adopted for the analysis and design of the SIS engendered enthusiasm in the organization and helped create a less skeptical attitude toward the initiatives from the main authorities in the Ministry concerning organizational changes. Enthusiasm and motivation are crucial for implementing radical changes (Gersick 1991). Furthermore, it can be conjectured that had the SIS been institutionalized, the government could have increased its political capital by curbing corruption. Our findings, then, suggest that SIS can affect not only processes and mechanisms of production and control but also can affect core values and beliefs (see Table 4, entry (d)). This is relevant since most of the SIS literature (see Table 4, entries (a), (b), (c), and (f)) concentrates on processes and competition with little emphasis on values, beliefs, and emotions.

Our study also contributes to our understanding of resistance to the implementation of SIS. As already mentioned in the previous section, the IS literature on resistance traditionally focuses on the relationships between users and designers (Markus 1983, Markus and Bjørn-Anderson 1987) and the role of top management (see Table 4, entry (e)). In our study, we found that the professional roles of the participants were not the source of resistance to the SIS but rather whether they were newcomers or old-timers. We found that a newcomer can be resisted by an old-timer if the former is perceived to receive preferential treatment (e.g., having a salary superior to that of the old-timers) and if that person is considered to be an outsider. This finding is of particular relevance for international agencies working in developing countries as most of their IS consultants would not only be earning higher salaries than their national counterparts but also could be considered illegitimate participants because of having a temporary status.

Our research can help practitioners pinpoint the profound organizational transformations likely to occur by implementing SIS. This is relevant given the possibility that SIS implementation may well be in direct opposition to the prevailing deep structure. We claim this is another contribution of our research since the literature tends to take the organizational deep structure as a black box (see, for example, Applegate and Elam 1992; Earl and Feeney 1997; Macmillan 1997; Rockart et al. 1996). Our research opened the black box by indicating precisely what are the components of the organization's deep structure that are affected by the implementation of SIS (see Table 4, entry (d)). Furthermore, for the context of public organizations, our case highlighted that SIS planning should consider the extant political time, since lengthy bureaucratic procedures and elections are crucial factors that constrain strategic plans and the governance of those organizations (see Table 4, entry (a)).

The paper has a number of limitations regarding its scope and generalization. We should not attempt to over-generalize our conclusions beyond the context of a government organization in a country that recently introduced a process of democratization. Nevertheless, the present research allowed us to establish an analytical relationship among our concepts. Another limitation of our research is that the organization under study did not have a fundamental IT infrastructure. So the SIS implementation we followed constituted, in some sense, a revolution. We still need to explore how the deep structure is related to SIS implementation in cases of evolution.

## Conclusion

The implementation of strategic systems challenges an organization's deep structure. In the case of government organizations in developing countries, the implementing of SIS means dealing with bureaucracies that have been institutionalized for many years, even centuries, often during long dictatorships punctuated by periods of political instability. Thus, implementation efforts should be accompanied by a careful consideration of how to deal with these deep structures. Facing the implementation of SIS, believing that they could be institutionalized by decree, is like enacting the spirit of Don Quixote, who thought that by virtue of his solo efforts he could defeat giants, when in fact he was facing solid, massive, and unmovable windmills. The trick is not defeating them, but judiciously moving between and around them without getting one's head cut off. What we learn from this case is that for SIS to be successful (i.e., institutionalized) requires more than simple decree by senior administrators. It requires a change in the organization's deep structure. Our deep structure lens, involving attributes of (1) core values and beliefs, (2) services, technology and political time, (3) distribution of power, (4) organizational structure, and (5) nature, type and pervasiveness of control systems, allows us to understand both why it is so difficult to get innovations such as SIS to be successfully implemented and, by the same token, what has to change for an innovation to be institutionalized. Everyone knows it isn't easy—especially in government organizations—but at least now we know what has to change.

## About the Authors

Leiser Silva is an assistant professor in the Decision and Information Sciences Department at the C. T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston. He holds a Ph.D. in information systems from the London School of Economics and Political Science. His current research examines issues of power and politics in the adoption and implementation of information systems. In addition, he is looking at managerial facets of information systems, specifically contextual and institutional aspects. His research has been published in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Communications of the Association for Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, The Information Society, and Information Technology and People.

Rudy Hirschheim is the Ourso Family Distinguished Professor of Information Systems in the Information Systems and Decision Sciences Department of Louisiana State University. He previously was the Tenneco/Chase International Professor of Information Systems and Director of the Information Systems Research Center at the University of Houston. He has also been on the faculties of Templeton College, Oxford, and the London School of Economics. His Ph.D. is in Information Systems from the University of London and he holds an honorary doctorate from the University of Oulu, Finland. He and Richard Boland are the consulting editors of the John Wiley Series in Information Systems. He is a senior editor for the Journal of the Association for Information Systems and on the editorial boards of the journals Information and Organization, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and Journal of Management Information Systems. He is past vice president of Publications for the Association for Information Systems.
