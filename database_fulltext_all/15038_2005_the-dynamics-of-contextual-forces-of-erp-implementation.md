# The dynamics of contextual forces of ERP implementation

Joe Nandhakumar<sup>a,</sup>\*, Matti Rossi<sup>b</sup>, Jari Talvinen<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Management, University of Bath, Bath BA2 7AY, UK <sup>b</sup>Helsinki School of Economics, P.O. Box 1210, FIN-00101 Helsinki, Finland Available online 17 May 2005

## Abstract

This paper reports the findings of an in-depth case study of implementing Enterprise Resource Planning Systems (ERP) in a long-established multinational company within the telecommunication sector. The company streamlined its operations through an ambitious business process redesign initiative and introduced an ERP system. The study examines the emergence of the ERP system and the enactment of simultaneous changes to the system and to the company context, along with the intentions and actions of the individuals involved. The study seeks to understand the ERP implementation process, in particular, the link between the ERP implementation process and the underlying and often subtle influences within the context. By drawing on the case study the paper offers a theoretical conceptualization of triggers and consequences of the cycles of control and drift of ERP implementation. We argue that by taking account of managers’ intentions, the affordance of technology and the power and cultural context, we can provide richer insights into the ERP implementation process and the dynamics of the underlying, subtle influences within the context. q 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

This paper explores the ERP implementation process in a large multinational company by examining the emergence of its ERP system and enactment of changes to the system along with the intentions and actions of the individuals involved in their company context. The main purpose of this paper is to understand the large-scale ERP implementation in its organizational context; in particular, how we can relate the ERP implementation process to the underlying and often subtle influences within the context and to what extend ERP implementation can be managed.

Many early research studies concentrated on offering guidelines for the successful implementation of ERP systems. They therefore emphasised on the success factors of an implementation process (Aladwani, 2001; Dong, 2000; Holland and Light, 1999; Nah et al., 2001; Shanks et al., 2000) assuming a planned deployment into an environment, where the environmental conditions and their influences are known in advance. This rational view of the context, however, neglects the influences of unfolding events and the subtle contextual forces that are beyond the control of management and implementers (Allen and Kern, 2001; Nandhakumar et al., 2003; Skok and Legge, 2001). In many cases, these influences become the critical issues that decide the fate of the ERP implementation project (Hanseth et al., 2001). Studies suggest that potentially ERP implementation could change the infrastructure and operating practices of an organization, and therefore, the implications of ERP implementation could be fundamentally wider than of any traditional transaction, or functional, system (Bingi et al., 1999; Davenport, 2000). Furthermore, the prevailing research assumes a linear model of technology adoption process based on (Cooper and Zmud, 1986). This view of the implementation process assumes that operations of large entities can be rationalized by the use of best practices and standardized by the use of similar processes throughout the enterprise (e.g. Aladwani, 2001; Deloitte Consulting LLC, 2000; Huber et al., 2000; Taudes et al., 2000). Several authors (Hanseth and Braa, 1998; Hanseth et al., 2001) have identified the complex and mangled nature of the change occurring in both the organization and the ERP system during the implementation. They compared ERP adoption with ‘unleashing’ a beast into the organization that assumes its own authority over things (Koch, 2000).

In this paper, we therefore, explore the process of a large-scale ERP implementation project and the contextual issues that shaped the process, and propose a conceptualization of the emergence and consequences of control and drift of ERP implementation. We argue that the ERP implementation process is triggered by managers’ intentions in response to internal and external contextual conditions. Their actions in terms of introducing new ERP modules and their use are shaped by the nature of technology (affordance), and the power and cultural settings (social structure).

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 provides a discussion of the theoretical basis for our investigation and improvisational model of change. This is then followed by a case description of technology-related change process in a large multinational organization. The organization has streamlined its operations through a major business process redesign initiative and introduced an ERP system. Our findings on the interplay of control and drift are discussed in the fourth section. We conclude the paper with some implications for theory and practice.

While emphasising that a causal connection exists between critical contextual factors, such as the presence of the executive sponsor, and ERP success, these studies provide no explanation of how the assumed causal connection operates in practice. We therefore have limited understanding of how these contextual factors shape the emergence of ERP components and help to realize the planned outcomes. Furthermore, the literature assumes each factor as an independent variable and overlooks the interaction between them and the interaction with other elements in the social and organizational context. Finally, these studies provide a static view of the factors assuming that their influences are frozen in time. Such studies usually neglect the dynamics of the process of organizational implementation.

In order to explain the relationship between the contextual influences and ERP implementation outcomes we draw on social theories. IS researchers (e.g.Walsham, 1993) have been drawing on social theories such as Giddens’ Structuration (Giddens, 1984) and related theoretical concepts to understand IT adoption and use in our organizational life. Walsham (1993) argues that such theoretical concepts could serve as a sensitizing and orienting device for the analysis of interpretive case studies. In this paper, we draw on three, inter-related theoretical concepts: human intentionality (Giddens, 1984), affordance of systems (Norman, 1988), and social structures (Giddens, 1984).

## 2.2. Intentionality, affordance and social structure

As intentional beings, designers and users plan, develop and implement IT systems and monitor to see whether they perform as intended. Their activities are often organized ‘plans and goals’. This does not mean, as Jones (1998) argues, that human plans and goals are always explicitly formulated or that human actors are fully aware of their motivations or capable of realizing them. Intentional human actions also often result in unintentional consequences (Giddens, 1984). Thus the designers and users are involved in an active strategy of response to the inability of technology to perform as intended, which involves accommodation of revised goals and intention, as well as revision of technology and social structure. Pickering (1995) refers to this process as ‘goal oriented and goal revising dialectic of resistance and accommodation’ or the ‘mangle of practice’.

Pickering (1995) claims that technologies have material properties by which they may also exert agency, i.e. ‘doing things’ similar to human agency. However, Pickering (1995) points out that material agency differs from human agency because it lacks intentionality (c.f. Giddens, 1984) and is not organized around plans and goals. Material agency is more like affordance (Gibson, 1979; Norman, 1988) in that it represents users’ and designers perception of whether some action is possible or not with the technology. Thus, a computer system, with its keyboard, LCD screen, and mouse with selection buttons affords pointing, touching, looking, and clicking on every point of the screen. If the screen is not touch-sensitive, it still affords touching, but this has no result on the computer system.

In everyday actions, humans struggle to capture material agency in technology, while the technology may help to shape human practices. Pickering (1995) illustrates this with an example of technology such as a lathe or a milling machine. A human can accomplish things with the lathe that human agency alone cannot accomplish. However, these machines need skilled operators to channel their agency in desired directions. He argues that in metal cutting the lathe and the operator come together as a human–machine couple, a single collective of machine capture or a composite human–non-human agent (c.f. Latour, 1987). In an organizational context arrays of machines are operated by skilled staff that also draw on social structures, such as the norms and rules in their practices.

In the IS context, the users and developers often add and modify the technological properties (add mail filters, group mailing lists, etc.) and are therefore, actively shaping and crafting the technology to fit our particular requirements. The malleability of the system may be based on the nature of the systems (e.g. local PC-based mailing systems or central enterprise wide systems) as well as our perception and understanding of the technology (Nandhakumar and Vidgen, 2001).

The emerging IT system, however, may not always perform as intended by human agents due to material characteristics of the technology. Hence, in the above instance of the email system, we may have to change our behaviour to accommodate the ways of working of the systems or try to find ways to alter or work around the technological properties (Latour, 1987). For example, it might be necessary to keep lists of email addresses in a word-processor to facilitate group mailing if the central email system has no facility for user-defined groups. Ciborra (2002) notes such processes of matching between situated human interventions of use and open technology as ‘drifting’. Technology drifting is often the result of processes such as sabotage, passive resistance, learning-by-doing, radical shifts in conditions or plain serendipity (Ciborra, 2002). Ciborra (2002) claims that ‘an insight into the drifting of technology needs to recombine, on a different basis, the theory and practice of systems development and use’.

This process of accommodation and resistance is not only limited to technology, but social structures such as resources of authority, norms governing organizational conduct and hierarchical status and the knowledge about the methods and procedures also shape the actions of designers and users of technology. For example, imbalance in power relations between two divisional heads might restrict the progress of IT projects. A strong hierarchical culture may prevent executive users from interacting directly with the designers of technology. By drawing on the social structures such as power relations and cultural norms in their action, individuals also enact the structures that shape their action and interaction with technology (Giddens, 1984). Thus, individuals’ action in response to hierarchical norm helps to sustain the hierarchical structure.

Giddens (1984) views human agency and social structures as interacting duality, by adopting a subjective concept of structures. He offers a notion of structure as ‘rules and resources’ organized as properties of systems’ that mediate human action and interaction through three modalities: interpretive schemes, resources and norms. Giddens (1984) describes structure as ‘traces in the mind’ orienting the conduct of human agents and suggests that they cannot exist apart from the human actors, who enact and interpret their dimensions. In this view, structure, therefore exists as time–space presence only in its instantiation in (reproduced social) practices. While drawing on the rules and resources (structures) in their action, individuals also recursively instantiate and hence reconstitute the structures that shape their practices (Jones and Nandhakumar, 1993).

In this study, we use these three concepts: intentionality, affordance and social structure as theoretical ‘lenses’ to study the ERP implementation process and to provide different perspectives relating to social and technical aspects of the process. Each of these concepts represents the properties of people (intentionality), technology (affordance) and social systems (social structures). The reciprocal links between human action and social structure has been broadly drawn on by IS researchers using structuration theory, e.g. (Barley, 1986, 1990; Orlikowski and Robey, 1991; Walsham and Han, 1993; Walsham and Han, 1993; DeSanctis and Poole, 1994). Only a few studies (Orlikowski, 1992) have explicitly provided greater emphasis on technology.

Orlikowski’s model on the ‘duality of technology’ (Orlikowski, 1992) seeks to introduce material technology into the structure/agency duality. She suggests that social rules and resources may be ‘embedded’ in IS during their design. Rules and resources ‘embedded’ in technologies, however, contradict the structuration perspective which suggests that these only exist in the instance of human action. Orlikowski later develops a perspective on technology structure (Orlikowski, 2000), which is seen as ‘virtual, emerging from people’s repeated and situated interaction with particular technologies’, in order to reconcile with the structuration perspective. Monteiro and Hanseth (1995) argue that Actor-Network theory should be used as a means to understand IS phenomena with its explicit commitment to the equal treatment of technological and human actors. While actor-Network theory assumes a symmetrical treatment of human and machine agency (Latour, 1991), (Jones, 1998); argues that human and machines are not quite as symmetrical as Actor-Network theory suggests because intentionality is a human characteristic.

Rather than forcing the technology into the structuration model (c.f. Orlikowski, 1992) we have used the concept of affordance as representing the material agency of the technology. As Jones (1998) argues technology with its objective character is capable of exerting agency in its own right, rather than simply being ‘traces in the mind’ of social actors. Our position in treatment of technology is therefore more akin to the work of Jones (1998).

## 3. Research method

The research study was carried out in a large multinational company (EURMOBIL—a pseudonym), which went through a major re-organization of its operations in the late nineties. The company changed its order fulfillment and logistics operations with the aid of a standardized enterprise wide information system. The selection of EURMOBIL case was mainly determined by an opportunity to study the ERP process in its context from its initiation to implementation. The study mainly focused on an order-fulfillment project as part of a total change program, specifically on business-to-business logistics and sales processes. The research approach adopted in this study is interpretive case study (Walsham, 1993) involving a collection of detailed, qualitative data on the implementation process of ERP in a specific organizational context.

The main phase of data collection involved intensive participation in the project from its initiation in late 1997 to mid 1999. During this period (15 months), one of the researchers worked in EURMOBIL as a project manager for ERP implementation, being given charge of the order-fulfillment part of the ERP project (see Fig. 1). The researcher reported directly to the logistics manager of the company and to the manager responsible for the entire change program. The researcher had access to first-hand empirical data: meeting minutes; project status reports; workshop presentations; documentation on benchmarking, training manuals, project handbook; business blueprints; and detailed work documents.

As the researcher was immersed in the research context, he was able to develop an appreciation of the immediate and broader context of ERP. The researcher maintained detailed notes of events and observations along with his own reflective accounts of the day. By mid 1999, 15 months after the initiation of the project, the researcher left the company following major changes at the management level. The researcher, however, kept close contact with the team leaders and members. This enabled us to carry out several follow-up visits (about 20 visits) until spring 2003 to interview project team members. These interviews were semi-structured and many of them were tape recorded and subsequently transcribed.

From an interpretivist’s perspective such personal involvement of the researcher in the research site enabled adequate access to the actors themselves and to elicit their interpretations directly (Walsham, 1993). This was desirable as the validity of interpretive research depends on gaining sufficient access to the knowledge and meanings of actors to generate a plausible, credible, and relevant representation of their interpretations (Altheide and Johnson, 1994).

On the other hand, closed engagement in the research site, as Nandhakumar and Jones (1997) argue, could give rise to many difficulties. For example, the researcher wanted to experience the taken-for-granted world of the managers at EURMOBIL while adopting the role of the continuously questioning researcher exposing its hidden assumptions. Overcoming the problems of conflicting roles demanded mental agility on behalf of the researcher in regular changes between the roles. Further, the researcher’s hierarchical status as middle manager in this large organization limited his access to the views of others who were at lower hierarchical levels. The researcher’s involvement in managing such a large project meant that achievement of sufficient time and distance for reflection was not always easy. As a manager at EURMOBIL emotional and psychological commitments to his colleagues and friends in the organizations were difficult to recognize and to take account of their effects in the analysis. In order to overcome these problems specific mechanisms were set up to encourage critical reflections of the research process, including maintaining daily reflective accounts and regular discussions of the process and findings with non-participant researchers involved in the study from the academic institution.

The analysis involved a critical examination of large amounts of qualitative data (field notes of events and observations, researcher’s daily reflective accounts, interview transcribes, project reports and workshop presentations, business blueprints; and detailed work documents). These were written up into a detailed case description that was used to develop an understanding of the processes observed at EURMOBIL. All documents were carefully examined to identify statements and actions that reflected similar views and meanings with respect to the ERP implementation process.

Data analysis and interpretation focused on the relationship between the activities of designers and users and the reproduced patterns and forms at the institutional level. The three theoretical concepts described above were identified during the data analysis as describing the important aspect of the ERP process. These were further developed during the analysis of the empirical data. The analysis, therefore, featured a cyclical process, where we moved between theoretical concepts and our empirical data on the ERP implementation. For example, during the analysis we noticed that a project starting as a ‘temporary fix’ suddenly became popular and was rapidly adopted by national companies throughout the world. We systematically traced the contextual forces that contributed to this phenomenon and codified and organized these into lists that we used in meetings with the organization to clarify and confirm the emerging theoretical model. Table 2 in Discussion (Section 5) is an example of the type of tables we used for organizing the data.

## 4. Case description

## 4.1. Company background

EURMOBIL is a large, well-established multinational company within the telecommunication sector which provides high technology products. The company has more than 50,000 employees in over 100 countries and has its headquarters in Europe. EURMOBIL has four major product segments: (i) mobile operations (e.g. mobile base stations), (ii) fixed line telecom operations, (iii) enterprise communication solutions, and (iv) consumer products (e.g. mobile phones). The operations were divided strictly into business-to-business and consumer solutions. Traditionally, the company had a very profitable fixed line telecom business, which had been the cornerstone of the company. However, during 1990s the mobile operations (base station division) had grown at a very fast pace and was at the time of this project the most profitable part of EURMOBIL.

Telecommunication switches, both mobile and fixed line, developed for the telecom network operators were engineered-to-order type products, which typically involved extensive customization for the clients. Their sales and implementation cycles ranged typically from 6 months to 3 years. Each switch comprised several components which were manufactured in several plants and by up to 100 sub-contractors.

In mobile operations, mobile base stations represented the high volume turnover. Mobile base stations were ‘high-tech’ products; however, they were highly standardized and were deployed as ‘plug-in’ enhancements into networks, where the basic infrastructure (e.g. more standardized mobile network based on GSM technology) was in place. The delivery of base stations grew very rapidly throughout the nineties and the logistics involved constituted a major problem for the company. For example, demand from Chinese cellular operators was so high that there were daily cargo planes from production facilities in Europe to China in order to fulfil the orders.

Mobile phones represented consumer products with a very short life span, which were sold to end users through retail channels. The key to profitability in the mobile phone business was the speed of the logistics chain, and therefore, any delay or warehousing of the goods had adverse effects on profitability. Traditionally EURMOBIL handled the sourcing and storage of raw materials and semi-finished goods and individual suppliers (there were several hundreds of them) delivered the goods directly to individual plants. Each national organization was largely independent and had autonomy to procure goods in response to customer orders. This worked well locally, but there was no balancing of the load across borders and divisions. With the boom in the telecom industry and tightening competition, however, the management of EURMOBIL saw that there was a need for a new global vision and standardized operations across the globe. To this end EURMOBIL had started a large-scale process development initiative, which aimed at defining a new global business model for the company. The goal of this initiative was to standardize the customer handling and fulfillment processes across the globe. However, in the largely independent and consensus driven culture of EURMOBIL the progress of this project was slow, which had an adverse effect on the project we were studying at that time.

## 4.2. Logistics processes

During 1997, EURMOBIL began streamlining the logistics and customer response processes and standardized the processes across divisional and national borders, largely in response to convergence of the telecom and IT industries and the rapidly growing volume of EURMOBIL’s product shipments. The idea for a totally new way of handling the logistics process came from the newly appointed CIO, who joined from Microsoft and was used to a culture, where the operations and software were standardized across the company. He saw that EURMOBIL was using a range of applications and there were good opportunities for simultaneously streamlining the processes and saving money on software procurement, training and maintenance.

Several organizations located within different countries (North America and Western Europe) already had working ERP installations in place, and the US plant had a very advanced logistics system based on SAP R/3, the market leader in ERP from SAP A/G. It was decided that this would be used as a template for the implementation of the first version of the companywide logistics fulfillment process. Senior managers believed that this would give a head start for the project, as their ready-made process definitions could be used as blueprints for global implementations. The CEO and CIO paid a visit to the US plant and decided to go ahead using their well working SAP implementation as a blueprint for the global ERP project. Furthermore, the head of IT of the US plant became the project manager for the global process development initiative.

The ERP solution was designated to replace a number of legacy systems that were used in various locations and tied together with custom built ‘bridges’. It was hoped that this would give the company a much better view of its inventory and delivery levels on a global basis. Another reason for this was to prevent fragmented ERP implementations by each national organization that was seen as very expensive for the company (there were numerous ERP projects going on in different countries and divisions). It was expected that all of these separate installations would be replaced by a common SAP ERP platform.

This initiative represented a tremendous change for the company that had traditionally operated so that each national organization and even individual factories had greater freedom to decide how to run the local operation. The new model underlying ERP was in contrast to this, because it was based on a common platform, which would implement standardized global processes. The whole implementation was seen as a major change program affecting the whole organization.

The project sponsors were aware of the task that was ahead of them and thus tried to avoid the commonly known pitfalls of ERP implementations (Akkermans and Helden, 2002). The rest of the case study focuses on the critical issues of the ERP implementation at EURMOBIL and how these were handled.

## 4.3. Critical issues of ERP Implementation

Top-level sponsorship for ERP initiatives was provided by senior company executives who had sought to promote the project as a major initiative for the company’s success. For example, the CEO claimed, ‘This is one of the most critical projects for our future success and I expect you to get personally involved to ensure that we, through co-operation, reach a common company approach’.

The project team responsible for ERP implementation was organized into several functional groups and there were executives and management teams (see Fig. 1) who were responsible for the overall coordination of the project. The functional groups addressed the business processes of Human Relations (HR), Finance, Consumer Supply Chain and Business-to-Business Supply Chain. The technical support group was formed from EURMOBIL’s SAP Competence Centre. The competence centre was in charge of technicalities and rolling out new installations, as well as training managers to use SAP. It was decided to position the project offices between the EURMOBIL business unit headquarters: fixed line networks and mobile networks.

The study mainly focused on the organization of the business-to-business logistics project. The ERP project team was assembled from more than hundred EURMOBIL employees hand picked from different countries. Almost a hundred external consultants were assigned to the project at its peak. Three separate consulting firms worked with fixed line telecom, mobile and consumer products’ divisions.

The original idea of the project was to change all of the order fulfillment processes into one standardized model. However, for this to happen, a standard account list and product catalogues needed to be in place. Implementing these changes was the responsibility of a separate sub-project. There were also several pressing issues: many country organizations, which had their legacy ERP installations in place, were reluctant to change their models, and there were pressures to find a quick fix for the looming year 2000 problem.

Fig. 1 illustrates that the project was functionally divided according to SAP module division. The process side of the matrix was defined by three separate sub-projects: the Y2000ERP project handled year 2000 country specific solutions; LogisticsERP (Logistics Control solution); and CorporateERP (corporate solution) development projects to provide short-term, mid-term and long-term solutions for the global business model and its implementation.

## 4.3.1. Inception

EURMOBIL employed an outside consulting group who carefully investigated various alternative supply chain organization principles. They spent 9 months looking at implemented solutions in other high-tech industries, such as computer manufacturers, especially at the build to order model of Dell and their supply chain. It was expected that there would be a single logistics model for the entire company as the final outcome of the CorporateERP project. Eventually it was decided to reuse US Local ERP as a template for the global ERP project. In essence this led to the initiation of the LogisticsERP project.

## 4.3.2. Planning

The CorporateERP project was launched in January 1998 as ‘a number of initiatives aimed at a more efficient company, which joined forces to reach company’s vision and strategic objectives’ by developing new, improved business and administrative processes and system tools, using SAP R/3, and providing the mechanism to support implementation globally. The choice of ERP package was previously decided, and therefore, work started immediately with definitions of new fulfillment processes. The process designs were developed using Intellicorp’s Live Model process design tool, and were based on structures and planning approaches of SAP’s ASAP (Brand, 1999) implementation methodology. These tools were common and not proprietary to any specific SAP consulting partner.

The logistics fulfillment model chosen was vendor-managed inventory, with a logistics provider responsible for managing the operation. The initial plan was to build a central logistics distribution centre and route everything through this hub. Also all of the outgoing shipments would go through this central logistics hub. This model was very well suited for the complex building and fulfillment process of the switching stations. Fig. 2 depicts this change process of streamlining the supply chain. It shows the planned move from a set of uncoordinated fulfillment processes with variations for each product to a common process with a split of responsibilities. It was planned to subsequently enhance this model to support also HR, maintenance and global accounting practices.

## 4.3.3. Planned implementation of LogisticsERP

Once the blueprints were finalized they were used as a plan for the implementation of the model in SAP R/3. This plan formed the basis for all implementations across 120 countries. Before the LogisticsERP could be rolled out they had to develop a technical infrastructure and support for running the system. Three service centres were planned in three regions: Nordic; North America; and Asia, to give a 24 h support capability and balance the load. There was also a need to develop a comprehensive training and deployment toolkit. Detailed process maps for each business process were developed by the project teams and the consulting firms using the process modelling tools. They also started configuring the SAP R/3 using these process maps.

## 4.3.4. Y2000ERP project

The Y2000ERP project had to be developed in parallel with LogisticsERP, as it was needed by many country organizations, which were unable to continue their operation past the turn of the millennium. This meant that some of the resources from the main project were diverted to help this project. On the other hand, this gave a possibility to test the roll-out of ERP on a country by country basis. The problem with this project was that it represented an initial ‘drift’ from the global model into very country specific local solutions.

The project was handled by EURMOBIL’s internal SAP competence centre and it was executed on time and budget. This was basically an out of the box SAP R/3 implementation but the national organizations that used Y2000ERP found that this was ideal for their local purposes. This would later turn out to be the basis for resistance of the global model. The countries that had Y2000ERP implemented saw no reason to go for the CorporateERP anymore. Although this was designed as a quick fix for the year 2000 problem, it continued to be in operation in one third of the country organizations of EURMOBIL.

## 4.3.5. Changed course

According to the project handbook: “During the project, any task resulting in the addition of new deliverables, the deletion of planned deliverables, the expansion or contraction of planned tasks, or the changing of the project schedule due to availability of user personnel, will be recorded and managed through the formal scope management procedure”. This means in practice that one would need the permission of the project steering committee to make any kind of change. Due to the wide span of the steering group, the consensus on these issues was hard to achieve.

During the course of the workshops the scope of the business-to-business fulfillment project underwent major changes. The project deliverables, whilst trying to reflect the final scope, suffered as a result of the changes. The specification of certain key concepts within EURMOBIL that were still in the process of being defined also affected the depth and extent of some of the deliverables. One example was the debate concerning the nature of the logistics hub and how it should be represented within SAP. Throughout the program lack of clarity existed regarding the likely users of LogisticsERP, and therefore, business requirements.

Six months into the project several changes took place. First, the company went through turmoil, its revenue collapsed and the CEO was replaced. The old CEO had been the sponsor of the program and his replacement caused problems for the process. At the same time, the CIO was demoted and assumed a role in the line management reporting to infrastructure managers. This meant also a decrease of status and importance of the whole project. Furthermore, most of the new management team members (including the new CEO and the new CIO) came from the very profitable mobile division. They saw the central logistics hub based LogisticsERP (what became known as Logistics-hubERP) as mostly helping the somewhat less successful fixed line telecom division. The new management team saw the Logistics-hubERP as an impractical and too complex process. As the mobile division had been very profitable they managed to negotiate that they did not want an overly complex logistics model (i.e. Logistics-hubERP) in addition to covering the losses of the fixed network division. In essence they wanted the LogisticshubERP program to be shelved. This caused a change in focus from the LogisticsERP model to CorporateERP model with Logistic-directERP (a new logistics model).

The idea of a single logistics hub (Logistics-hubERP) was finally abandoned and it was replaced with the new model for Logistics-directERP. Logistics-directERP would not need any logistics hubs, but rather the external logistics provider would position the base sites directly to their final locations, where products were assembled. Logistics-directERP was seen as a better fit for the large-scale deliveries of ‘pluggable’ base stations by the mobile division than the traditional switching station deliveries. This was quite natural, as the solutions in other industries that EURMOBIL had studied were from make-to-order production (e.g. Dell) instead of engineer-to-order. The change in focus meant that most of the process descriptions and blueprints developed previously had to be abandoned and modelling had to be re-started from scratch. Furthermore all of the SAP R/3 configuration work done so far had to be abandoned.

To make matters worse, the new management team started to divert labour from the Logistics-directERP project into other efforts. It was decided that the Logistics-directERP could re-use existing specifications, although they were totally incompatible. This led to the further departure of key project members, who were disappointed by the lack of commitment from the top management. Overall this resulted in a downward spiral of diminishing funding for the project and unrealistic expectations.

## 4.3.6. Epilogue

Fifteen months after the project was launched, the project manager (the researcher) left EURMOBIL and returned to the academic institution. The lack of resource commitments for continuing the project was one of the main reasons for not staying longer at EURMOBIL. The change program was later revitalized with a renewed focus and change of name for the program and sub-projects. At the end of the study period in 2003, about 100 out of 140 national companies were using ERP or were in the process of rolling it out. Interestingly, out of these one third used the Y2000ERP solution which had later become known as BaseERP, one fifth had rolled out the Logistics-directERP solution and the rest of them were pursuing the CorporateERP that would provide the originally planned unified global fulfillment process. The HR module had been implemented and was in use in many countries. Fig. 3 provides a summary of the main components of the realized systems and planned components (dashed-lined boxes), which were abandoned during the project. In general the idea of a global ‘unifying model’ of ERP solution continued to survive in EURMOBIL and they were still trying to get away from the country specific ERP solutions and move towards the global model. However, several strong internal groups were happy with their running systems and did not see the benefits of the global CorporateERP.

## 5. Analysis and discussion

The above case study provides a rich description of the emergence of EURMOBIL’s ERP system and the influences of dynamic contextual forces. In this section we use our three core theoretical concepts: management’s intentions; technology affordances; and social structures, to organize our analysis of the case.

## 5.1. Managers’ intentions

The case study illustrates that at the managerial level, there were clear intentions to replace several legacy systems, and an already fragmented suite of SAP installations in national organizations and to transform all the fulfillment processes into a single, standardized global process. The broader contextual conditions at the time seemed to have given rise to such intentions. For example, the telecom industry was booming and the volume of EURMOBIL’s product shipments was rapidly growing. There were different ERP applications in national companies that were seen as very costly for the company. The newly appointed CIO from Microsoft intended to standardize the software across the company as a way of saving money on software procurement, training and maintenance. Furthermore, he felt that the standardization of global logistics operations was only achievable through a centralized system (i.e. CorporateERP).

The project team intended to proceed with the global ERP (CorporateERP) and wanted to show a working system without further delays. The original intention was to deploy the global model by 1999, before they faced Y2K problem. The project, however was delayed past the 1999 deadline due to several reasons identified below. Many managers insisted that they could not continue operation past the turn of the millennium without an immediate fix to their systems. This forced the project management team to commence the Y2000ERP project, which was intended to be a quick and basic implementation, which would be abandoned as soon as CorporateERP became operational. Unintended by the managers the Y2000ERP, however, became established and could not be abandoned. The team therefore had to revise their intentions to work around the technology.

Similarly, the managers intended to continue with unified logistics model for the entire company. However, progress with Logistics-hubERP project was constrained by unforeseen events and contextual conditions as illustrated below. The project team, was therefore forced to implement Logistics-directERP in place of Logistics-hubERP.

## 5.2. Technology affordances

The case study illustrates that the affordance of the system may either accommodate managers’ intentions and enable them to realize their goals or resist the progress and force them to revise plans and goals. For example, the original intention was to create a unified process for all divisions in different countries (Corporate ERP). The project team, therefore needed standardization of organizational procedures such as product coding, company accounts and product catalogues. The affordance of the legacy system did not accommodate the standardized organizational procedures, and therefore forced them to implement partial solutions to show progress. Whereas adopting the US Local ERP system enabled project team to expand logistics centre based implementation of the system in each national company by providing a working module and thereby creating momentum. The affordance of the system in this case had a positive effect enabling the project team to potentially realize their intentions.

As the case illustrates the SAP R/3 system had to be modified and patched heavily to accommodate the scale of the global model for EURMOBIL. This extensive customization helped to accommodate managers’ intentions as well as slowed down the implementation considerably. This slow progress along with the delay due to the absence of standardized organizational procedures forced them to create a separate project to handle the Y2K problem as the global model was not implemented on time for solving the Y2K problem. As a means of overcoming affordance of the legacy systems’ Y2K incompliancy, they therefore had to improvise temporary solutions (Y2000ERP using SAP) to enable many national organizations to continue operations over the turn of the millennium.

While the Y2000ERP implementation offered a pilot run of ERP implementation on a country by country basis for the project team, it later became a major obstacle for further development of Logistics-direct ERP as the system offered independent country organizations a modern ERP system and they were generally quite satisfied with the implementation and the outcome. This illustrates the variability of the affordance over time as the users’ perception of what actions were possible or not possible with the technology.

## 5.3. Social structures

The emergence of ERP system and its components was shaped by certain contextual forces relating to the social structure that kicked in after the project was launched. For example, at the organizational level, the national companies and subdivisions always had freedom in running the local operations and purchasing software. Such strong cultural norms had a profound influence in constraining managers’ intention of creating a unified model of processes for all local operations in different countries (Corporate ERP). By drawing on such norms and their relative power position many national divisions resisted to give up legacy systems. Senior company executives drew on their recourse of authority to promote the project as a major initiative for the company’s success. The established cultural norms and power relations helped the project team to plan their strategies and to establish themselves in a neutral territory (away from the sphere of influence of both mobile and fixed line divisions) and to proceed with the implementation work. This also illustrates the enabling properties of social structure.

Initially Y2000ERP and its deployment was seen by the team as just a tiny sub-project on a different floor that was not so important. However, as the country organizations were satisfied with the running system, the sub-project became more powerful and influential. The global team actually felt that there was a pressure to go ahead with this working implementation by the national companies, as they did not see any need for the global solution.

The newly appointed CEO from mobile division used his power position to undermine the Logistics-hubERP as this benefited only the fixed line business; and the concept of global systems and processes was seen as conflicting with the culture of national companies which always had greater freedom to run their operations.

In summary, the whole implementation process may therefore be seen as the interaction of intentionality, affordance and social structure. When the system did not perform as originally intended the project team had to get actively involved in accommodating revised goals/plans, and amending technological components (SAP modules or legacy systems) and organizational features (product catalogues). ## 5.4. Cycles of control and drift

Both affordance of the system and social structure allowed or restricted the managers’ intentions with respect to ERP implementation. When there were restrictions they often resorted to revising development plans or rescheduled events; changing existing or new technological components; changing organizational processes; or abandoning their plans. At EURMOBIL managers, therefore tried hard to configure the ERP system to gain control over its functioning. The CorporateERP however never emerged; instead a fragmented, new Corporate ERP emerged out of these new components. When the Y2000ERP came out the management sought to ensure that this was a temporary implementation for a specific purpose. The project team soon lost control of the way the Y2000ERP established itself at EURMOBIL. Subsequently there was no support for implementing LogisticsERP in national companies. While Y2000ERP was initially seen as a major drain of resources from the ERP initiative, it also gave an opportunity for testing the ERP system on a country-by-country basis, which was not intended originally. Similarly despite of planned LogisticsERP, the system evolved into Logistics-hubERP and then Logistics-directERP. So, while managers sought to capture the material agency in ERP system the technology drifted as an unintended consequence of the implementation. The planned global ‘unifying model’ of the ERP solution never materialised along with several planned components but many of the realized ERP components were unintended and emergent. A consequence of these emerging projects is the technological drift. The main components of the realized systems and planned components that were abandoned during the project were depicted in Fig. 3.

The interplay of drift and control was a continuous phenomenon in which the consequences became the triggers for subsequent cycle of drift and control. One of the consequences of initiating the ERP project was that it brought together a large number of organizational members, who worked many years at EURMOBIL in similar positions, and provided a spatio-temporal framework of interactions in which various viewpoints conflicted and co-operated in a ‘power game’ to produce control and drift of ERP implementation. The model of triggers and consequences of the drift and control is depicted in Fig. 4. This illustrates that the ERP implementation process was triggered by designers’ and users’ intentions (intentionality) in response to internal and external contextual conditions. The perception of what action is possible with the technology (affordance) and the power and cultural settings (social structure) in turn shaped the implementation process through cycles of interaction of intentionality, affordance and social structure. On one hand managers attempted to configure the system in ways to achieve control over material phenomena. On the other hand the affordance of the system and the social structure allowed or restricted managers’ actions and intentions with respect to ERP implementation. The ERP system, therefore drifted as a result of the processes of matching between situated human interventions and the ERP system. The outcomes of interactions of intentionality, affordance and social structure were rarely the result of one of the elements and were not always predictable. The outcomes of their interactions were therefore emergent from the process of their interaction and at the same time became the condition for the next set of interactions.

While CSF studies suggest that the presence and absence of contextual factors determines planned outcomes, our findings suggest that the ERP implementation process was shaped by a wide range of contextual factors relating to the social structure and affordance of technological components while the influences of these factors varied over time (rather than ‘fixed’, specific influences). For example, at the beginning of the project senior managers’ commitments and resulting resources of power were the dominant force, however soon the affordance of legacy systems and routines of the national companies became dominant. Similarly the changes in the global telecom industry and market conditions changed the fortunes and power structures within EURMOBIL, and these changes were reflected in the ERP implementation and the chosen process models.

These contextual forces were not independent but interrelated and contingent upon other contextual conditions. Their influences were not fixed but changed, causing forces to shift, as the new CEO was appointed. For example, the authority relationship of the new CEO and his influence was contingent upon the favourable market condition for the mobile network division. Within EURMOBIL, individuals’ action in response to this authority relation helped to sustain a form of power structure that became embedded in the context over time. This form of structure helped the ERP project team to draw on the resources of authority to legitimize the change of plan with respect to LogisticsERP without the direct involvement of the CEO. By investigating the underlying forces, the study offered insights into sources of the influences of the contextual factors and the way they shaped the emergence of ERP components.

Studies identifying the social dimensions of ERP (Boudreau and Robey, 1999; Brown and Vessey, 1999; Markus et al., 2000) often lump ERP system together as a ‘black box’ of technology. Other researchers based on Giddens’ Structurational perspective draw on social structure—agency (intentionality) relationship in their analysis of information systems phenomenon but emphasise little on technology. We however took into account the affordance of the technology and thereby opened the technology for social analysis. Our findings indicate that understanding of the ERP system, especially its possibilities and limitations, by the project team, consultants and the organizational members, and their perception of the system influenced the malleability of the system and its technological components.

While literature suggests a linear model of ERP adoption we have sought to provide further insights into the emergence and consequences of the drift and control of the ERP implementation process (see Fig. 4). The concept of drift indicates that the change process involves purposeful action involving serendipity and chance. The drift emerged out of organizational members’ planned and unplanned actions in response to both previous technology and organizational properties they have enacted in the past. The ERP implementation process is not strictly a process from one stage to another as the traditional literature on change suggests. The emergent nature of the system means that the trajectory of the ERP system was only visible on retrospective sensemaking (Weick, 1979) and the planning and implementation were intertwined.

## 6. Conclusions

This paper has presented the findings of an in-depth study into the implementation of ERP system in a long-established multinational company, and developed a theoretical conceptualization of triggers and consequences of the drift and control of ERP implementation (see Fig. 4). This conceptualization provides one way of examining the ERP implementation process and the underlying and often subtle influences within the context. The paper argues that by taking account of managers’ intentions, the technology affordance and the power/cultural context (social structure), we can explain the triggers of technology drift associated with ERP system, as well as the consequences of these drifts. By drawing on the concept of affordance, we have provided a way of conceptualizing technology in social analysis.

We claim that the EURMOBIL case study is a rather typical large-scale ERP implementation and it exhibits the uncontrollable nature and emergent properties of largescale information systems. Our conceptualization of control and drift of the ERP implementation process seems to indicate that the planning stage may not be able to predefine all changes to be installed and foresees their organizational implications. This is in contrast with the previous literature on ERP that assumes that CSFs are a good representation of contextual forces and outcomes are predictable.

We believe that our conceptualization may be of value to researchers investigating ERP implementation in a variety of situations, by providing concepts for understanding the adoption process. The way we relate the contextual forces through the concepts of intentionality, affordance and social structure could be elaborated theoretically by further empirical grounding in other situations.

The emergent nature of the ERP project and the drift at EURMOBIL gives rise to several questions pertaining to implementation practices. How do we or should we avoid the appearance of side-projects, such as the Y2000ERP project, which diverts effort from the main project? Many in EURMOBIL considered Y2000ERP as the best solution resulting from learning-by-doing or just serendipity. If we were to take advantage of the evolving capabilities and opportunities we should also be prepared to face the numerous possibilities that the project could evolve into. What policy guidance can be given for handling external events that totally change the course of the project? Should we strongly insist that the team effort goes into the main project rather than on internal selling and small side projects? Learning-by-doing or adapting both technology and organization requires dedicated resources. How much slack should there be in the project to handle possible emergent issues and opportunities? Furthermore, if there was such slack, would it become a source of drift by itself?

Our theoretical conceptualization and insights into ERP implementation process have important implications for practicing managers. For example, by understanding the contextual forces and the triggers and consequences of ERP drift, the nature of changes required to the systems can be anticipated and evaluated. Hence responses to some of the unexpected events and chances could be anticipated in advance by evaluating different scenarios and paths that the project could follow. This kind of analysis of alternative outcomes is vital for the projects, because, as shown in our case, some seemingly insignificant decisions in response to the environment in the beginning had huge effects on the entire project trajectory. Thus, projects should have mechanisms and procedures in place that allow for responding to these contextual events, instead of the normal response of either refusing to change the course, or letting the project drift uncontrollably. As Mintzberg and Westley (2001) argue when the situation is novel and confusing, as is often the case with ERP implementation, the preferred course of action should include trial-anderror, venturing, learning by doing, intuitive action and improvising. CSFs and rational planning would be preferred only when the issue is clear and there are well-established organizational processes to be implemented into the system.

The global unification of processes is a complex project even for a company like EURMOBIL that has more than a hundred year’s tradition of multinational operations. We argue that any approach to managing such a project should take into account: the need for customization and improvisation to accommodate revised goals in response to affordance of the system; and the need to take advantage of emerging capabilities and opportunities.
