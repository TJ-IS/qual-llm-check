# Witty invention or dubious fad? Using argument mapping to examine the contours of management fashion

Rudy Hirschheim ⁎, David M. Murungi, Santiago Peña

Department of Information Systems and Decision Sciences, E. J. Ourso College of Business, Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, LA 70803, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 30 October 2011 Received in revised form 14 November 201 Accepted 14 November 2011 Available online 16 December 2011

## a b s t r a c t

This paper examines management fashion discourse based on the premise that management fashions are not neutral, but problematic. It grounds this premise on Abrahamson and Fairchild's (1999) observation that attributes the upswings of management fashion discourse to “emotionally charged, enthusiastic and unreasoned discourse”. Adopting this critical perspective, the paper conducts a careful analysis of faddish discourse in an attempt to understand the discursive ailments that would justify ascribing a diagnosis of “unreasoned” to this discourse. To achieve this goal, the paper employs the technique of argument mapping to examine and compare the structures of early discourse surrounding: (1) Business Process Reengineering (BPR) – typically now considered a fad; (2) Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) – an enduring, non-faddish IS discourse; and (3) Service Oriented Architecture (SOA) – a more recent discourse that is evaluated based on insights derived from comparisons of BPR and ERP. Findings from the resultant argument maps show conspicuous differences between BPR and ERP argumentation, which suggests an association between early argument structure and the faddish trajectory of discourse. Similarly, insights derived from ERP and BPR argument comparisons suggest that SOA is more likely to follow the faddish course of its BPR predecessor rather than the enduring track of ERP. © 2012 Elsevier Ltd. ## 1. Introduction

In today's world of technological innovation, organizations are faced with the daunting task of achieving a balance between novelty or innovation and longevity/stability. Achieving this balance is an extremely complicated endeavor as is suggested by Baskerville and Mvers' (2oo9) observation that both management and information systems research and practice is characterized by fashions or, “the relatively transitory belief that a certain management technique leads rational management progress”. Baskerville and Myers, however, adopt a neutral stance to management fashion and deliberately avoid evaluating, “the wisdom, impact or merit of the various fashions”. Instead, they focus solely on describing the fashion waves and illuminating the degree of correspondence between research and practitioner literature as it relates to these fashion trends. They then, based on this description and on the <sup>fi</sup>nding that IS research closely parallels practice, recommend a tighter coupling between the two: “that IS academics should be more proactively engaged in the fashion setting process and in the evaluation of IS fashions”. While we agree with the basic tenets of this recommendation, we think it is missing a critical element because it fails to address the central ailment of fashionable research or practice – transience.

The transience of management fashion is problematic because when crests of enthusiasm for yesterday's panacea are replaced with troughs of disillusionment for today's mundane application, the changing tides of fashion can leave both managers and workers jaded, if not embittered, by the experience (David & Strang, 2006). Not only, therefore, does the adoption of these <sup>fl</sup>eeting practices have important consequences for an organization's scarce time and monetary resources, but also for leadership opportunities and organizational commitment. The problem of management fashions is also indicated by the observation that the majority of managerial interventions introduced into organizations resemble transient fads more than true programs of social change (Zucker, 1988). The increasing signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of this problem was demonstrated by Carson, Lanier, Carson, and Guidry (2000), who showed that the cycles of management fashions have increased in am plitude and frequency over the last century. The importance of this problem is further demonstrated by the <sup>fl</sup>ood of popular press authors lamenting management's inability to resist such fads and fashions (e.g., Economist, 2000; Forbes, 2004; Wall Street, 1998) and increasing interest among management scholars (e.g., Barley & Kunda, 1992; Carson et al., 2000; Staw & Epstein, 2000; Zbaracki, 1998).

This paper consequently seeks to contribute to the IS literature on management fashion by focusing on the ephemeral nature of management fashions and adopting a more critical perspective than Baskerville and Myers (2009) in its investigation of how these fads are formed. It accomplishes this by adopting Abrahamson and Fairchild's (1999) <sup>fi</sup>nding, which demonstrated that, “emotionally charged, enthusiastic and unreasoned discourse characterizes the upswings of management fashion waves”, as a central premise for this study. In particular, the paper focuses on the role played by “unreasoned” discourse in the creation of management fashions and seeks to explicate the relationship between this discursive trait and the fundamental problem of transience. The paper consequently seeks to conduct a careful examination of management fashion discourse in order to determine what aspects or attributes of this discourse could be justi<sup>fi</sup>ably depicted as unreasoned, and whether or not there is an association between these traits and the problem of transience. To achieve this goal, this research proposes using an argument mapping technique (Fletcher & Huff, 1990a, 1990b; Toulmin, 1958) to analyze the discursive landscape of innovative practice and to serve as a diagnostic tool to help identify the structural <sup>fl</sup>aws that characterize faddish argumentation. The argument maps will focus on early discourse because Abrahamson and Fairchild (1999) suggest that unreasoned discourse is prevalent in the upswings of management fashion.<sup>1</sup> Literature that uses the concept of “organizing vision" developed by Swanson and Ramiller (1997) also highlights the importance that the early stages of an innovation play in its diffusion and adoption. For example, <sup>fi</sup>ndings from Wendy Currie's (2004) processes-oriented analysis of how organizing visions are interpreted, legitimized and mobilized suggested that underdevelopment of an organizing vision at an early stage may inhibit its later adoption and institutionalization. Similarly, Wang and Swanson (2007), when offering a theory for the institutionalization process of an organizing vision, argued that if interested actors fail to make favorable institutional arrangements for an innovation at an early stage, the innovation will never progress insofar that institutional factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence the later phases of its diffusion. Argument mapping is particularly useful in this regard because at this early stage of discourse, there is a dearth of case studies and/or “real results,” leaving the strength of the argumentation surrounding the innovation as the primary means of evaluating the merits or shortcomings of its implementation in practice. Consequently, the research question that this study proposes to answer is: Could argument maps of early stages of innovative discourse provide useful insights into whether or not an innovation will likely develop into an enduring practice or if it will disintegrate into a transient fad?

To answer this research question, this paper applies the argument mapping technique to examine and compare the structure of the early discourse surrounding three prominent, IS-related innovations – Business Process Reengineering (BPR), Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) and Service Oriented Architecture (SOA). BPR was selected for this analysis because although it was a prominent buzzword that was popularized in the early 1990's by proponents such as Hammer (1990) and Thomas Davenport (1993), it is now typically regarded to be a classic example of a <sup>fl</sup>eeting management fashion (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999; Baskerville & Myers, 2009; Benders, Van den Berg, & Bijsterveld, 1998; Carson et al., 2000; Davenport & Stoddard; 1994, and Jackson, 2001).<sup>2</sup> Conversely, ERP was selected as a contrast to BPR because while the term ERP originated at around the same time as the BPR phenomenon (Klaus, Rosemann, & Gable, 2000; Wylie, 1990), ERP has largely eluded classi<sup>fi</sup>cation as a passing fad, although not everyone would agree with this. Scholars such as Westrup and Knight (2000), for example, predicted a swift demise of the ERP discourse and corroborated this with early evidence which showed that the term “ERP” appeared in 1996, reached a peak in popularity in 1999 and fell back almost as swiftly to a relative low rate in 2002 (Westrup, 2005). However, as a search of Google trends performed on May 18, 2011 using the key terms ‘ERP’ and ‘BPR’ demonstrates (see Graph 1), it is apparent that even after almost a decade of its reported demise, ERP discourse is still ongoing, while BPR discourse has practically disappeared. Evidence of the enduring nature of this discourse may also be seen in: (1) the continuing stream of publications on ERP in both practitioner and scholarly outlets (Chellappa & Saraf, 2010; Morris & Venkatesh, 2010; Stasiukonis, 2010; Wailgum, 2009); (2) the success of SAP, Oracle's Enterprise System, and other ERP systems; (3) the various IS academic programs that teach SAP (or some other form of ERP); (4) consulting companies (e.g., Accenture) who devote entire business practice sections to ERP; and (5) industry trend reports (e.g., Gartner) showing the prevalence of ERP in corporations.

In addition to the difference in terms of discourse trajectories, BPR and ERP were thought to be suitable for comparison because the two discourses show striking convergence in terms of their expressed core goals and procedures. The key claim of BPR discourse was the notion that organizations could operate more effectively if they moved from a function-oriented approach to problem-solving to a process-oriented approach to addressing business concerns. Proponents of BPR argued that IS had created new synergies between functional units that minimized the import of functional structures, and emphasized the need to view the organization as sets of interlinked processes that delivered value to the business. Synergy or integration between functional entities is also the hallmark of ERP discourse and an imperative goal for organizations that acquire and implement ERP systems (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005; Markus, 2001; Markus & Tanis, 2000). Like BPR, the key contribution of ERP discourse is its advocacy for an integration of the complete range of a business's processes in order to provide a holistic approach to meeting overall business objectives. Because the two discourses have ostensibly similar origins and objectives, a comparative examination of their early argument structures was conducted to determine if these structures had any distinctive characteristics that could help explain differences in their subsequent discourse trajectories.

SOA was added to this analysis because it is a more recent IS innovation whose discourse has remarkable similarities with both BPR and ERP phenomena. The distinctive feature of SOA discourse is that it recommends shedding the old resource or function-based view of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, and recon<sup>fi</sup>guring the organization into a more nimble IS-enabled collection of “services” that provide value for the business enterprise (Cox & Kreger, 2005; Keel, Orr, Hernandez, Patrocinio, & Bouchard, 2007). Apart from the focus on “services” in SOA and the emphasis on “processes" in BPR and ERP. the parallels between the three discourses (at a cursory level of comparison) remain striking. This paper consequently attempts to validate the insights it derives from comparisons of BPR and ERP discourses by applying these insights to the relatively newer SOA discourse. Because SOA has begun to show early signs of following BPR's faddish trajectory, as is evidenced by increasing practitioner disenchantment (Hirschheim, Welke, & Schwarz, 2010; Keene, 2009; Krill, 2007; Manes, 2009; Taft, 2009) and the considerable drop in Internet searches that SOA has experienced as the interest in cloud computing has gained momentum (See Graph 2 in Results Section), the case for a relationship between early argument structure and transience could be strengthened by demonstrating its similarities to BPR as well as its differences from ERP argument forms. The comparison of the three discourses may also help discover what, if any, parts of the argumentation were “unreasoned” as suggested by Abrahamson and Fairchild (1999) and if these reasoning or structural argumentation <sup>fl</sup>aws are unique to faddish discourse. This comparison will also be useful in determining whether or not argument mapping is an effective technique for diagnosing early faddish inclinations

This paper also, by mapping the discourse in academic literature, examines the role played by academics in the propagation of management fashions and, like Baskerville and Myers (2009), calls for more active participation of academia in the discussion. Unlike Baskerville and Myers, however, this paper advocates a more critical role for academia by suggesting that academics could play a crucial role in identifying the reasoning failures that produce ephemeral fads. Academics, we argue, are by training and occupational disposition, ideally situated to serve as arbiters of reason and could serve as an aid to practice by charting out the scope of reason represented by the various claims related to novel IS practice. Additionally, the paper suggests that academics, by using scholarly techniques such as argument mapping to portray the boundaries of reason for a particular discourse, could not only help identify potential fads, but could also add value to the discourse by offering suggestions to improve or correct this discourse.

To achieve these goals the paper proceeds as follows: First, we present an overview of BPR, ERP and SOA discourses; next, we offer an introduction to the theoretical framework used in the paper (Argument Mapping based on Toulmin's application of the theory of informal logic and Management Fashion theory); the following section discusses our methodology, detailing how the argument maps were constructed; we then offer our results and discussion; and the last section contains our conclusions in which we use the literature on management fashions and Toulmin's theory of informal logic to determine whether or not the ERP and BPR argument maps reveal disparate discursive traits and if insights derived from this comparison can be applied to SOA as well as future IS innovations. It also identi<sup>fi</sup>es insights that could be used to enlighten practitioner – academic engagement in the innovation process.

## 2. Overview of SOA, BPR and ERP discourses

SOA discourse is a relatively recent addition to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (circa 2005). The central claim of this discourse is that organizations could attain the <sup>fl</sup>exibility needed to succeed in highly dynamic markets by focusing more on “services” rather than on products (Bitner & Brown, 2006; Cherbakov, Galambos, Harishankar, Kalyana, & Rackham, 2005; Hirschheim et al., 2010). It is based on the premise that, although manufacturing remains an important part of the economy, today we are living in a “post-manufacturing” world (Bitner & Brown, 2006; Chesbrough & Spohrer, 2006). Pal and Zimmerie (2005), for example, reported that services accounted for 75% of the U.S. gross domestic product (GDP) while Karmakar (2004) noted that services accounted for 80% of private sector employment in the United States. This new business landscape requires organizations to be nimble enough to adroitly recon<sup>fi</sup>gure their activities to manage their portfolio of resources – including IS – as services supplied to the business function (Rai & Sambamurthy, 2006; Sawhney, Balasubramanian, & Krishnan, 2004). A crucial aspect of this adaptive recon-<sup>fi</sup>guration is a move away from the historic resource-oriented or functional organization structure to a more dy namic, cross-functional, and service-oriented arrangement (Cox & Kreger, 2005; Keel et al., 2007). SOA is a “new” paradigm that posits that using services as the fundamental elements for developing applications may mean that many of the traditional problems of integrating disparate business processes and applications disappear. Consequently, SOA is deemed to facilitate alignment of existing IT infrastructure and systems to achieve end-to-end enterprise connectivity by removing redundancies, generating uni<sup>fi</sup>ed collaboration tools, and streamlining IT processes (Bieberstein, Bose, Walker, & Lynch, 2005; Hirschheim et al., 2010; Keel et al., 2007).

The notion of recon<sup>fi</sup>guring an organization in order to take advantage of new synergies produced by the development of new technologies, however, is not new. Indeed, “recon<sup>fi</sup>guration” is terminology evocative of the language used by BPR proponents. In his widely cited 1990 article entitled “Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate”, Michael Hammer claimed that <sup>fi</sup>rms must use the power of modern information technology to radically redesign their business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in their performance and remain competitive. The key feature of this discourse was that it recommended analyzing organizations not by function, but by sets of interlinked processes (Kawalek, 1994). According to authors such as Davenport and Short (1990), Hammer (1990), Hammer and Champy (1993), Harrington (1991), and Davenport (1993), developments in information technology had produced a new business environment that required managers to manage by process, not function. To further explain how this new world of process came into being, Teng, Grover, and Fiedler (1994), observed that advancements in information technology offered new opportunities for cooperation between functional units and that this new capability made the old function-oriented way of thinking inappropriate or out of sync with the new technology. Processes and not functional areas, it was also argued, were the mechanism of delivering value to customers (Davenport, 1993; Hammer, 1990; Kawalek, 1994). Detaching processes from the underlying bureaucratic or functional assumptions that impeded them and focusing on outcomes rather than tasks was deemed as the means to providing innovation, speed, service, and quality to these customers. Additionally, authors of that time period argued that a process-oriented view of IT would improve its alignment with the business strategies and reduce integration problems (Earl, Sampler, & Short, 1995; Knorr, 1991; Short & Venkatraman, 1992). BPR was to accomplish this alignment by providing the kind of <sup>fl</sup>exibility that would be required to keep up with the changing business landscape (Teng et al., 1994).

Like SOA and BPR, ERP's main innovative contribution to management discourse was the idea of integrating business processes across the entire business enterprise by using a single information and IT architecture (Davenport, 1998). Although the origins of ERP can be traced to back to the 1970's with business integration concepts such as material requirements planning (MRP) and manufacturing resource planning (MRPII), the term ERP was probably <sup>fi</sup>rst used in the press in the early 1990's (Klaus et al., 2000; Umble et al., 2003). The <sup>fi</sup>rst IS articles on ERP emerged in 1997 after Thomas Davenport introduced the application to the IS community at AMCIS '96 (Davenport, 1996 in Klaus et al., 2000). ERP's key distinction from its MRP and MRPII predecessors was the extension of coordination activities beyond the manufacturing function of organizations to incorporate all resource planning across the entire enterprise and with applications in non-manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rms (Umble et al., 2003). While the component functions of ERP were already available to organizations as individual software packages, ERP's standardized and integrated software environment provided a degree of interoperability that would have been dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to attain using legacy stand-alone systems. ERP discourse consequently emerged on to the marketplace of ideas with lofty promises of cutting cycle times, reduced error rates, multi-business software solutions and more intimate employee-to-customer interactions (Clemons, 1998; Somers & Nelson, 2001; Stevens, 1996). ERP, because of its holistic approach to business processes, was also envisioned as an ideal solution to the alignment conundrum, as it helped facilitate the compatibility of task characteristics and technology characteristic (Chung & Snyder, 1999; Kelly et al., 1999). Indeed, ERP was envisioned as a facilitator of the contemporary BPR discourse by providing companies that implemented the systems with “best practice” templates embedded in the software that helped <sup>fi</sup>t business redesign programs with the new systems (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005; Somers & Nelson, 2001).

While SOA, BPR, and ERP are distinctly different organizational discourses it is apparent that all three discourses have strikingly similar objectives and share a common, broad-based approach to achieving organizational goals. Indeed, they share one common theme: All three discourses mandate that drastic organizational change must occur to ensure successful implementation. This non-negotiable requirement for an extreme overhaul of the organization has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the main cause of BPR failures (Davenport, 1996; Hammer & Champy, 1993, Teng et al., 1994). This paper, therefore, focuses its analysis on the discourse surrounding the required organizational change in order to determine what aspects of the discourse could explain why one innovation (BPR) ended up being classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a fad while the other (ERP) has maintained enduring traction in organizational research and practice. In addition, by including SOA in the analysis, this paper will examine if the discursive traits associated with management fashion persist over time. The tendency for management fad characteristics to repeat themselves can be seen in the observation that one of the early criticisms of BPR was that it was not really new, but simply another (albeit impoverished) version of outdated management fashions, such as socio-technical systems design, which was developed in the 1950s (Mumford, 1994). Similarly, Egan (1995) (in Newell, Swan, & Galliers, 2000) argued that BPR offered “nothing new, nothing tangible, and nothing sustainable in terms of sustainable advantage.” Now with SOA manifesting similarly faddish traits, it would seem prudent for practitioners considering a new IS implementation and academics considering a dive into a new stream of research to examine whether or not the new discourse offers anything that will endure, or if the new approach will be – at least in terms of transience – BPR in sheep's clothing.

## 3. Theoretical framework

In order to examine the structure of discourse surrounding the early stages of BPR, ERP and SOA, and to discover the particular discursive failings that produce management fashion, this paper employs argument mapping, a novel argument analysis technique based on Toulmin's (1958) theory of informal logic, to establish the reasonability of the arguments, and Management Fashion Theory.

## 3.1. Argument mapping

BPR, SOA and ERP are all IS-related management innovations that have strikingly similar objectives and approaches to achieving organizational goals, but BPR and ERP have remarkably different trajectories in terms of practitioner and scholarly-based discourse. Abrahamson and Fairchild's (1999) research lends itself well to our supposition that this divergence might be explained by differences in the reasoning structures that characterize early stages of the respective discourses. To evaluate our inference, this paper chose the technique of argument mapping because it is well suited for examining innovative discourses at their nascent stage, in which there is a dearth of empirical results or cases for examination, leaving the power of the argument as the main criterion for decision-making (Fletcher & Huff, 1990a, 1990b).

Argument mapping is a technique based on the work of British philosopher Steven Toulmin (1958). It is based on the theory of informal logic which treats arguments as rhetorical acts intended to persuade others (Pawlowski, Watson, Davis, & Kaganer, 2008). Argument mapping was also chosen because it <sup>fi</sup>ts with the type of informal logic and practical reasoning typically involved in the group discourse and decision-making surrounding the implementation of a new innovation in an organization. It also lends itself well to the stream of research in the management fashion literature that focuses on the role that discourse plays in giving rise to management fashions.

In his book Uses of Argument (1958, p. 95), Toulmin proposed that a valid argument has a proper form, analogous to a legal argument that can be laid out for inspection. Toulmin further proposed that the question “How does our cognitive equipment function?” is philosophically equivalent to the question, “What sorts of arguments could be produced for the things we claim to know?” (1958, p. 254).

As Perelman and Olbrechts-Tyteca (1969) observed, “The domain of argumentation is that of the credible, the plausible, the probable, to the degree that the latter eludes the certainty of calculations”. Toulmin provides a formal method to analyze arguments and thus, determine their credibility, plausibility and possibility.

Toulmin identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the different components of arguments used by people who assert something they want others to believe, while Fletcher and Huff (1990a, 1990b) built on this to develop a diagrammatic form that illustrates the components of an argument.

## 3.1.1. Components of an argument

• Key claims: According to Toulmin, the claim is “the explicit appeal produced by the argument, and is always of a potential controversial nature.” In other words, a claim is a statement put forward for the audience to believe (Fletcher & Huff, 1990a, 1990b).

• Grounds: Grounds are evidence produced in support of a claim and are given in answer to the question: What do you have to go on? The general format is: Given these GROUNDS I assert that this CLAIM is true. Grounds are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed on the basis of their primary function within the context of the argument (Fletcher & Huff, 1990a, 1990b).

• Warrants: These show the logical connection between claim and grounds. Warrants answer the question: How did you get from these grounds to that claim? (Toulmin, 1958). Warrants present the problems that are often implicit in the argument, in which case the reader must infer the warrant that connects grounds with a particular claim (Fletcher & Huff, 1990a, 1990b).

• Quali<sup>fi</sup>ers: Quali<sup>fi</sup>ers are used to communicate to us the degree to which we are to accept the claim as true. Quali<sup>fi</sup>ers may re<sup>fl</sup>ect genuine doubts on the part of the speaker regarding a particular claim (Fletcher & Huff, 1990a, 1990b).

• Rebuttals: These statements manage potential objections by stating conditions under which the claim might hold or not hold (Fletcher & Huff, 1990a, 1990b).

Argument mapping thus provides a formal method to deconstruct an argument for further analysis and inspection. This article employs argument mapping as the foundation for evaluating whether or not there are discernable differences between BPR, ERP, and SOA argument structures and whether or not these differences could be used to explain their divergent discourse trajectories.

## 3.2. Management fashions

Abrahamson (1996) de<sup>fi</sup>ned management fashions as a relatively transitory collective belief, dissembled by management fashion setters (e.g., consulting <sup>fi</sup>rms, business schools, business mass-media publications), that a management technique leads to rational management progress. To qualify as a management fashion, such beliefs have to be produced by a community of knowledge organizations and idea entrepreneurs (i.e., management-fashion setters) and marketed through discourse to various knowledge consumer organizations (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999). The importance of management-fashion setters to the trajectory of an innovation is highlighted by <sup>fi</sup>ndings that demonstrate the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of industry analysts in shaping product markets. For example, Pollock and Williams' (2011) research demonstrates how industry analysts exercise control over innovative terminology by making regular "naming interventions" and attempts to regulate the boundaries of discourse with episodes of "categorisation work". Similarly, Pollock and Williams (2009) depicted how an IT research organization established its market analysis tool as an "impartial" and "legitimate" arbiter of vendor performance despite the contested nature of these assessments. In both instances, discourse plays a prominent role serving as the primary vehicle through which industry analysts exert their in<sup>fl</sup>uence.

Discourse consequently is an integral part of management fashion as is manifested in the scholarly treatment of the subject in which a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant stream of research focuses on the role that discourse plays in the origin and development of management fashions. Kieser (1997), for example, argued that rhetoric is the main fabric of management fashion and explained the role played by various rhetorical devices in promoting its diffusion. Similarly, Benders and van Veen (2001) proposed that management fashions are best conceptualized as “the production and consumption of temporarily intensive management discourse, and the organizational changes induced by and associated with this discourse.” Newell, Robertson, and Swan (2001) also highlighted the importance of discourse to management fashions when they observed that theorizations of management fashion, “have tended to focus on the rhetorical strategies of fashion suppliers (consultants, gurus, core texts) in broadcasting “new” (or repackaged ideas) to fashion adopters”, and when they acknowledged the role of management fashions as “powerful rhetorics”. An illustration of these “powerful rhetorics” can be found in the notion of "best practices" that have helped shape the trajectory of innovations. Wagner, Scott, and Galliers (2006), for example, showed how a small group of powerful actors came to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the "best practices" for an industry and how later such practices were locally refuted and amended, despite the original product continuing to be sold by the software vendor. Similarly, Yeow and Kien Sia (2008) show how the implementation of a "best practice" package involves a political process of persuasion to build a coalition of major stakeholders and requires a coherent set of rhetoric (e.g., arguments and counter arguments) to articulate rational evaluation. Abrahamson and Fairchild (1999) gave further credence to the import of discourse when they published <sup>fi</sup>ndings that empirically demonstrated that, “emotionally charged, enthusiasti and unreasoned discourse characterizes the upswings of management fashion waves”.

According to Abrahamson, the notion of management fashions is centered on the discourse supplied by knowledge entrepreneurs to create (or devise) and disseminate new fashions. The demand for this discourse is rooted in two pillars of Western society: norms of rationality, and norms of progress. The former are predi cated on the expectation held by organizational stakeholders that managers will employ the most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective means to achieve desired ends. The latter are driven by the assumption that current management techniques will be replaced by new, improved ones and that this evolution is perpetual. Managers, seeking to effectively deal with such normative pressures, solicit rational and progressive techniques to help them run their organizations (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999). There are three major features of this discourse: lifecycles, triggers, and learning processes. With respect to lifecycles, Abrahamson proposes that there exists a close correlation between the lifecycle of the discourse promoting a management fashion and the lifecycle of that fashion's diffusion across an organizational community. Triggers are forces both exogenous and endogenous to the knowledge management market that initiate and mold the development of new management fashions. Endogenous triggers primarily relate to either the collapse and/or elimination of an old management fashion, signaling to the management knowledge entrepreneurs a need to <sup>fi</sup>ll the void (the empty niche). Exogenous triggers generally re<sup>fl</sup>ect a severe performance gap (or perceived performance gap) experienced by a large number of organizations that is recognized and thought to be of critical importance in public discourse. In this context, Abrahamson suggests that for a new management fashion to emerge and be widely accepted, both endogenous and exogenous triggers must be simultaneously present, and a replacement fashion that purportedly addresses the problem at hand must exist in its latency. Finally, Abrahamson and Fairchild (1999) differentiate between two types of discourse that affect learning and hence, the diffusion process. The <sup>fi</sup>rst type is characterized by rhetoric that facilitates what is termed “real learning”, which is the careful speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the relationship between adopting a new practice and closing the perceived performance gap. In contrast, the sec ond type of discourse tends to generate “superstitious learning”, as it consists of emotionally-charged and largely uncritical accounts of the “quasi-magical potency of a management technique” (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999, pg. 735). According to Cleverly (1971, pg. 9), such “beliefs cannot be destroyed by the presentation of contrary evidence”. These beliefs become, in a sense, “management ideology” which encourages managers: “to accept, to believe, to commit, to expound accepted doctrine, and even to glorify, and never question” the basis for their beliefs (Astley, 1984, pg. 270). Juxtaposing discourse dynamics and diffusion lifecycle curves, Abrahamson and Fairchild contend that the superstitious learning discourse will usually be observed during the up-swing (beginning stages) of a fashion wave, while the real learning rhetoric accompany its downswing (ending stage) phase.

Not only, therefore, is it apparent that discourse plays an important role in the genesis of management fashion, but it is also evident that it is particularly associated with what Abrahamson and Fairchild describe as “unreasoned discourse” or “superstitious learning”. The notion of “reason” (or the lack thereof) consequently is central to the concept of management fashion. Indeed, as Newell et al. (2001) observe, theorization of fads and fashions emerged at least partly in response to the limitations of normative-rational approaches to innovation diffusion. In Zbaracki's (1998) conceptualization of management fashions, for example, management fashions are deemed to occur when “reasonably” well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned technical interventions devolve into “ambiguous or sometimes dubious interventions”. Indeed, in his paper on the Total Quality Management (TQM) fad, Zbaracki (1998) observed two versions of TQM. One TQM, a technical TQM, incorporates wellde<sup>fi</sup>ned organizational interventions with clear rules for the use and analysis of information. The second TQM, a rhetorical TQM, is characterized by rhetorical excess, ambiguity and unclear organizational implications. It was this second amorphous form of TQM that was associated with transient management fashions.

In his seminal piece on management fashion, Abrahamson (1996) suggested that this breakdown in reasoning was produced by cultural pressures that pushed for the extension of rational techniques to ambiguous social purposes that are not necessarily amenable to these well-structured procedures. Quoting Meyer and Rowan (1977), Abrahamson wrote that, “organizational stakeholders expect managers to manage their organizations and employees rationally – that is by the most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient means to important ends.” Meeting these expectations, he went on to observe, was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for managers because in many contexts not only are ends ambiguous, but so, too, is the understanding of what constitutes the most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient means of pursuing these ends. Managers consequently are forced to create the appearance that they are conforming to norms of rationality. Zbaracki (1998) similarly observed that managers bringing wellde<sup>fi</sup>ned, structured techniques into an organization face the uncomfortable task of bridging multiple realities in a more problematic sphere where everyday realities are subject to ongoing negotiation.

In sum, the theory of management fashion highlights the role of discourse produced by change agents in shaping diffusion patterns of novel management practices. It contends that for a new management practice to spread, a demand for this type of solution must <sup>fi</sup>rst exist (the void) or be developed within a business community, and the dissemination of the fashion must be supported by simplistic, uncritical rhetoric hailing its superior qualities. The down-swing in a fashion lifecycle is explained by the overall reduction in the discourse regarding the practice and the relative increase in the number of more thoughtful and critical accounts after the fashion has been in place for some period of time.

Based on the above discussion, therefore, it is apparent that a de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristic of management fashion discourse is <sup>fl</sup>awed reasoning that occurs in the upswing of an innovation or, as Zbaracki describes this phenomenon, a breakdown of reason that occurs when moving from well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned technical interventions to unstructured social applications. This paper consequently attempts to determine if early signs of this breakdown in reason can be observed from argument maps that provide a comprehensive view of nascent argument structures set forth by the BPR, ERP, and SOA innovations.

## 4. Methodology

The technique of argument mapping was employed to determine whether or not the differences between the discourse trajectories of BPR and ERP could be linked to differences in their early argumentation and whether or not these differences could be useful in analyzing emergent phenomena such as SOA. Argument mapping is particularly useful for emergent discourses such as SOA because SOA's relative novelty has resulted in a dearth of case studies and/or “real results”, leaving only the strength of the argument as a means of evaluating the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the SOA implementation in practice. Argument maps allow us to evaluate the argument further by providing a structure that succinctly summarizes unsupported claims, potential pitfalls, and counterclaims that may be overlooked in a literature review or may be hidden in the rhetoric of a paper.

The strength of this technique directly depends on the data to which it is applied. In this context, we developed a comprehensive approach to capturing the positions of the trend setters (business schools, consultants, etc.) as related to SOA, BPR and ERP. The method used to <sup>fi</sup>nd relevant publications on ERP, BPR and SOA was the following:

• To ensure that the literature review was as exhaustive as possible, a broad search was conducted using the two to three key words per innovation as the only delimiters of the search. This allowed for a search of all publications (academic, trade publications, books, etc.) that contained the terms ERP, Enterprise Resource Planning, BPR, Business Process Reengineering, SOA, or Service Oriented Architecture. Articles were then selected on the basis of their availability and relevance (i.e., business setting application). It is worth mentioning that while we included the reports provided by vendors (e.g., HP, Oracle, IBM, and Accenture) in our analysis, we primarily focused our attention on scholarly trade publications (e.g., IBM Systems Journal, Communications of the ACM) and IS conference proceedings and used these academic articles to serve as proxies for the vendor reports. Vendor reports were only reviewed to determine if they contributed any new claims or grounds to the discourse. We adopted this approach because scholarly articles have clear argument structures that make them particularly suitable to the argument mapping technique employed by our paper.

Since the vendor reports contributed no new claims to the arguments found in the conference proceedings and scholarly trade publications to the discourse, we collapsed these reports into a single category labeled “Trade Publications/Conferences” in Tables 1, 2, and 3 (below).

• Perform a review of the top <sup>fi</sup>ve journals in the Management <sup>fi</sup>eld (based on the 2007 ISI Web of Knowledge Impact Factors), <sup>fi</sup>ve of the top journals in the Information Systems <sup>fi</sup>eld (based on recent AIS rankings) and three of the top Applied Management journals. The journals are listed in Tables 1, 2 and 3. We also included the journal Information and Organization (I&O) because of the journal's emphasis on the analysis of narratives, which is highly relevant for this article.

• The Business Source Complete database was used in the search process. For each journal, two different keywords were entered in the search function for BPR: “BPR,” and “Business Process Reengineering,” two keywords for ERP: “ERP,” and “Enterprise Resource Planning,” and three keywords were used for SOA: “SOA,” “Service Oriented Architecture,” and “SSME” (Service Science, Management and Engineering). These keywords were used to search the full text of the articles in order to <sup>fi</sup>nd papers that did not contain author-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed keywords.

Discourse analysis, in any form, is inherently subjective, and argument mapping is no exception. However, argument mapping improves on traditional discourse analysis by employing a relatively transparent process in its identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of knowledge claims. This methodology is especially suited to academic articles because most of these articles are structured in the forms of arguments that are comprised of clearly articulated claims and supports for these claims. While the validity of these claims is typically contentious, the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of these claims is a relatively straightforward process, as the claims are normally expressly stated in a scholarly paper's argument structure. In the case of this paper, we focused our attention on claims that speci<sup>fi</sup>cally addressed the reasons why an organization should or should not adopt the novel IS innovations that are the subject of its study. Consequently, all papers that presented arguments (claims) that supported or challenged the adoption of one of these innovations were considered relevant and were included in our analysis

The particular style of scholarly articles helps to mitigate the muddy interpretation problem, but this is never completely eliminated. Therefore, in order to account for differences in interpretation, the authors of this paper independently read each article, formed claims, and performed a comparison of the claims. The authors then resolved any con<sup>fl</sup>icting claims. The result of this process was a set of claims that the authors agreed most accurately described the early discourse surrounding the subject of interest – the motivation to adopt a novel IS innovation.

The development of the argument maps closely followed the methodology proposed by Fletcher and Huff (1990a, 1990b), which involved the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and marking of topic blocks and argument components.

In order to complete the argument mapping process, Fletcher and Huff (1990a, 1990b) suggest multiple passes through the material to be coded, moving from the general to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c with each pass.

These stages are as follows:

• First Pass: Read through the whole document, identifying topics, arguments, and the most obvious key claims.

• Second Pass: Mark all claims, and identify grounds for each claim.

• Third Pass: Within each argument, identify sub claims, elaborations and reiterations.

• Fourth Pass: Provide implicit warrants wherever they are not obvious.

Each pass was performed individually by two of the authors and results at the end of each stage were compared and differences reconciled. The process was repeated for all selected papers. We then consolidated these results into one overall argument map for each innovation (ERP, SOA and BPR), as shown in Figs. 1, 2 and 3. In order to further illustrate the map construction process, we provide below a brief example depicting the aggregation process for SOA's main claim. The articles chosen for this example, although a small subset of the total number of articles analyzed, are representative enough to demonstrate the variety of arguments set forth to promote the adoption of SOA.

During the <sup>fi</sup>rst pass, the researchers identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the main claims in each paper. An excerpt of those claims is shown below.

## 5. Results and discussion

We developed argument maps to better compare and evaluate the level of discourse that is associated with the ERP, BPR and SOA innovations. The argument maps (shown below in Figs. 1, 2, and 3) represent consolidations of the different arguments found in the papers analyzed. Argument mapping proved to be a very effective technique for deconstructing and analyzing arguments. The graphical representation of the different components of an argument allows for a comprehensive understanding of the structure of an argument while reducing complexity and effort to interpret the results.

The use of argument mapping allowed us to visually display the key claims for the three discourses as well as the counter claims, quali<sup>fi</sup>ers, grounds and warrants for these claims. A key <sup>fi</sup>nding that was observed from a comparison of the three maps is the presence of counter claims in the ERP discourse and the absence of these opposing views in the BPR and SOA argument maps. While ERP, BPR and SOA all share the key claim that promises <sup>fl</sup>exibility and IT alignment capabilities to organizations that adopt the innovations, it is only ERP's key claim that faced rebuttal in the literature. Early ERP skeptics argued that the technology was too immature to make its grand claims (Chung & Charles, 1999) and that the process of ful<sup>fi</sup>lling ERP promises would be complex, organizationally disruptive, and resource intensive (Mahrer, 1999; Sumner, 1999). While there were similar traces of such skepticism in the early BPR and SOA discourses (e.g. Teng et al.'s, 1994 observation that local loyalties may lessen the appeal of networked forms), these were tangential disclaimers that did not contradict or temper the central or key claim of these discourses.

Apart from the presence of counterclaims in the ERP discourse, the argument structures of all three discourses have remarkable similarities in terms of their key claims and the grounds for these claims. The key claim is the same for all innovations, as noted above. Additionally, the key grounds provided for support of this claim could be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three common categories (e.g. Operational Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, Flexibility, and Organizational Impact) and one unique category (View of the Organization) for all three cases:

Fig. 2. BPR argument map. (Data from: Earl et al., 1995; Hammer, 1990; Hammer and Champy, 1993; Hung, 2006; Kawalek, 1994; Knorr, 1991; Short and Venkatraman, 1992; Teng, Grover, and Fiedler, 1994).

• Operational Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency: adopting SOA, BPR or ERP reduces costs and improves operations.

• Flexibility: processes, services, or an enterprise wide system are keys to guaranteeing <sup>fl</sup>exibility and business/IT integration.

• Organizational Impact: BPR, SOA or ERP allow cross-functional/cross-organization collaboration.

• View of the Organization: BPR and SOA argumentation shared a similarity in that they both based their claims on grounds that suggested a distinct view of the organization (processes and services). ERP discourse did not directly draw its support from a particular view of the organization, but nevertheless implied a holistic view of the organization when it recommended an enterprise wide technological approach to the alignment problem.

In spite of the similarities we found between the three discourses in terms of the key grounds, it is apparent from the ERP map (see Fig. 3) that the discourse surrounding the Organizational Impact of this innovation was much more extensive than the BPR and SOA discussions of this area. Indeed. while the domains of Operational Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and Flexibility (although Flexibility is slightly less pronounced in the ERP discourse) seem to have had a similar extent of argumentation across the three discourses, it is evident from the maps that ERPs discussion of Organizational Impact was more far-reaching than the other two discourses. Compared to ERP, both BPR and SOA had very “light” arguments concerning the social and organizational impact of their respective innovations and had scarce discussion on the topic. As shown in the maps (Figs. 1 and 2), the social impact dimension of BPR and SOA discourse is limited to quali<sup>fi</sup>ers and grounds for these quali<sup>fi</sup>ers, without any extensive, in-depth discussion of the implications of the adoption of BPR/SOA for the organization. This is contrasted with ERP discourse which distinguishes itself from the BPR and SOA discourses by its extensive litany of claims, grounds, quali<sup>fi</sup>ers and warrants related to the organizational and social impacts and implications for the innovation. This distinction, coupled with the apparent lack of counterarguments for both BPR and SOA, is striking when we consider that both discourses positioned themselves as radical departures from the prevailing practices of their time. The lack of discourse surrounding the potential pitfalls of these innovations could give a false sense of security to practitioners who would be swayed by well-structured (in terms of appropriately-supported grounds for the claims), but preponderantly positive claims that downplay or omit both rebuttal and organizational context.

Insights derived from a comparison of the distinct and prior ERP and BPR discourses could be applied to the more recent discourse surrounding SOA implementations in order to determine if SOA discourse is in danger of becoming a management fad, particularly because we observed that the BPR and SOA argument structures are remarkably similar. To accomplish this, we use Toulmin's breakdown of the argument structures to evaluate the persuasiveness or logical consistency of the three discourses in an attempt to link argumentation to faddish trajectory. The two key insights derived from the map comparisons were the lack of rebuttals and the diminished discussion of organizational context in both BPR and SOA discourses. While the lack of counterclaims could be viewed as a sign of weak argumentation, this absence of rebuttals could also be reasonably justi<sup>fi</sup>ed by attributing it to the presence of well-substantiated claims or incontrovertible grounds. The absence of discussion of social context, however, is more problematic as it brings into question the suitability of the grounds to support what is clearly an organization or social key claim – business/IT alignment.

To elucidate the importance of including social context in the argument structures, we borrow from Habermas' (1984a, 1984b) theory of communicative action. This theory is chosen because it posits an approach to determining the reasonableness of argument forms and because it is a widely adopted theoretical lens in the IS literature. According to this theory, an argument is deemed reasonable based on three ontological positions dealing with the objective, social, and subjective views of the world. According to Habermas, claims of fact, truth or ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy are reasonably made with regard to objective/physical phenomena. In this realm, claims can be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by multiple observers using procedures that render social values and individual idiosyncrasies irrelevant. Claims pertinent to social phenomena, however, are only veri<sup>fi</sup>able by testing the level of agreement or con<sup>fl</sup>ict that the claims have with principles that govern relationships between, and the rights of, individuals within a society. The validation process in this realm is not irrational, but presupposes an open forum for discussion that is free from coercion or control. Rational discourse in this sphere of reasoning addresses questions such as, what is good or bad, or right and wrong in an organization and is well suited to make recommendations about what courses of action should or should not be taken (Klein & Hirschheim, 2001). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, however, show that the rhetoric or justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for both BPR and SOA discourses has focused almost exclusively on the instrumental means-end rationality – logic that we posit is not ideal for rational decision making in the social sphere. The social aspect of the argument is often overlooked or marginally mentioned in the form of possible threats to successful implementation with little detail provided. This omission of social context also helps to explain the absence of counterclaims in the BPR and SOA argumentation. While it is easier to make incontrovertible truth claims about objective/ physical phenomena, social claims are intrinsically subjective and subject to alternative points of view. The fact that early ERP discourse was inconclusive (subject to valid rebuttal) may also explain the longevity of the discourse as con<sup>fl</sup>icting views implied the need for further discussion and consensus building. It is instructive that we found no articles suggesting that future research should be performed in the realm of the social context for SOA and BPR; rather, the focus of all SOA and BPR articles was the technical ad vantages that these new innovations provide.

The onset of a management fashion is characterized by the devolution of reasonably well de<sup>fi</sup>ned technical interventions into imprecise and sometimes dubious social constructions (Zbaracki, 1998). This breakdown in reasoning occurs when “rational” techniques are extended to ambiguous social purposes that are not necessarily amenable to these methods (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999). In the social arena, reason is attained more by negotiation and argumentation than by de<sup>fi</sup>nition (Habermas, 1984a, 1984b). As is evidenced in the maps, however, the reasonableness of both SOA and BPR discourses was imposed by prede<sup>fi</sup>ned parameters such as lower costs and increased <sup>fl</sup>exibility. Missing from these maps is the notion that cost could also be viewed as a social construct that could mean different things to different stakeholders in the implementation of this innovation. The reasonableness of these claims in their social context consequently could be challenged due to the absence of the “shared understanding” that is critical to Habermas' (1984a, 1984b) (1987) concept of communicative rationality. Reason, according to Habermas, is not to be found in a single subject, but in the relationship of subjects. The notion of intersubjectivity however is glaringly missing from the BPR and SOA argument maps, but prominent in the ERP discourse. This lack of social discourse or argumentation could arguably be associated with the demise of BPR; likewise, its presence in the ERP discourse might help explain the longevity of ERP's trajectory in academic discourse. Similarly, the absence of social argumentation in the early stages of SOA discourse may be an alarming sign of a potential emerging fad.

In addition to the development of the argument maps, we also performed a trend analysis of Google searches of the term SOA during the last seven years (from 2004 to 2011) in order to determine if the interest in SOA has decreased. As a comparison, we performed the same analysis with the term “Cloud Computing”, which is a much touted current technological solution in the business world. The graph depicting the trends for SOA and Cloud Computing is shown below.

The top part of graph shows that the interest in SOA (represented by Google searches) peaked in 2007 and has since declined to the 2005 level. Curiously enough, SOA started its decline when Cloud Computing started to rise. The lower part of the graph shows a similar trend for news related to SOA and Cloud Computing.

The decline in interest in SOA while Cloud Computing gains ground, coupled with the lack of reasoned arguments found in the argument maps, reveal symptoms of an emerging management fad in which academics have had a major role, as suggested by Baskerville & Myers, 2009.

## 6. Conclusion

The argument mapping technique used in this paper facilitated the discovery of interesting differences between early ERP discourse and both BPR and SOA early discourses. These differences in early argument structures provide clues that help explain the divergent ERP and BPR discourse trajectories as well as provide grounds for evaluating discourse of emergent innovations such as SOA. The main insight derived from comparisons of the ERP and BPR argument structures was that, unlike the well balanced early ERP discourse which included both social and technical argumentation, the nascent BPR and SOA discourses focused almost entirely on technical function with minimal attention paid to social context. This difference may help clarify Abrahamson and Fairchild's (1999) observation that attributes the upswings of management fashions to “unreasoned discourse”. Based on the differences between BPR and ERP argument maps, we can describe “unreasoned discourse” not as unintelligent argumentation, but rather incomplete argumentation.

We characterize BPR and SOA's early argument structure as “unreasoned" because it omits the social sphere of argumentation. This is the reverse of the problem that is common in the political sphere, in which social context often overrides concerns of functional feasibility. An example of this would be an election year decision to cut taxes while at the same time increasing the rate of government spending. The difference between these two unreasoned forms of argumentation, however, is that while well-argued social claims could be debunked by providing “hard” or “objective” evidence of the technical infeasibility of these claims, it is much more dif<sup>fi</sup>- cult to discredit well-grounded technical claims based on social or “subjective” concerns. The social aspects of a claim are, by nature, the most debatable or contentious aspects of the discourse. As is evident from the nonfaddish trajectory of ERP discourse, the introduction of social considerations at an early stage of the discourse may help moderate the adoption of a new IS innovation and replace the sudden and short-lived bursts of interest with a more gradual and enduring application of the innovation.

A key premise for the arguments in this paper is the notion that management fashions are not neutral, but problematic. While we agree with Baskerville and Myers' (2009) recommendation that “IS academics should be more proactively engaged in the fashion setting process and in the evaluation of IS fashions." we nevertheless assert that the role of academics should not be to merely participate in the propagation of these fads, but rather to provide critical feedback that could detour these fashions from their faddish trajectories. If discourse surrounding IS innovations does not include a discussion of the social context and is not subjected to rigorous scrutiny at an early stage of its inception, this failure could lead to disillusioned stakeholders who will be inclined to discredit even legitimate IS claims based on their negative experience with these fads. Hirschheim, Porra, and Parks's (2003) longitudinal study of Texaco's IT department provides an illustrative example of this concern. In this study, Texaco IT management did not do enough to stop rampant discourse about how IT could solve everything. This resulted in lost con<sup>fi</sup>dence in IT and the subsequent relegation of the department to “little importance,” even when independent benchmarking showed it to be a top-performing IT department. Faddish discourse, consequently, by creating unreasonable expectations and irrationally skewing perceptions of IT innovation, remains deleterious even in light of <sup>fi</sup>nd ings such as Wang's (2010) study which suggests a relationship between fashionable (or more aptly de scribed “innovative”) organizations and enhanced organizational reputation, increased executive pay, and long terms gains in performance. Academics could play a crucial role in mitigating this anomaly by improving the discourse and providing more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained, scholarly analysis at an early stage that would validate reasonable claims about an innovation and simultaneously prevent detrimental "<sup>fl</sup>ighty" claim from taking off.

Thus. the main contribution of academic analysis to IS innovation discourse would be to expand the limited boundaries of discussion (or unreasoned discourse) that characterize faddish argumentation. We suggest that academics, by virtue of occupational disposition as well as formal training, are best suited to identify the missing elements and provide critical input into the discourse. The challenge for academic research would be to get involved early enough to have an impact on the trajectory of a management fad. As Baskerville and Myers (2009) noted, however, academic involvement tends to appear very late<sup>3</sup> in the management fashion setting process. This typically results in academics performing post-mortems of the phenomena rather than conducting diagnoses that could lead to remedial approaches to the discourse. A technique such as argument mapping, however, could be very useful in not only determining what went wrong with a particular IS discourse such as BPR, but could also help identify the early warning signs and perhaps deter potential fads. Indeed, based on the insights obtained from comparisons of ERP, BPR and SOA argument maps, we recommend that future research test our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by using this technique to eval uate whether or not emerging IS discourses are likely to degenerate into fads or if they are more likely to attain a degree of permanence and provide long-lasting bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to its various stakeholders. We also suggest broader applications of the argument mapping technique in academic research as a useful tool for general discourse or argument evaluation.

An example of a broader application of the argument mapping technique is its ability to visually portray discourse trends over time. Evidence from the BPR-SOA argument maps, for example, shows that although the BPR phenomenon emerged in the early 1990s, it appears that an imbalanced focus on technical function is a persistent shortcoming that continues to encumber our evaluations of new information system innovations. While SOA may not be a reincarnation of BPR, it is apparent that we continue to apply BPR thinking to evaluate SOA. The issue with this, as suggested earlier, is not that BPR thinking was wrong, but that it was incomplete. The argument maps consequently suggest that an enduring feature of management fashion is the lack of pluralism in the early discourse. It is apparent from the ERP argument maps that when the discussion is broadened to include multiple perspectives or competing paradigms, that faddish claims tend to lose some of their glimmer. While previous studies (e.g., Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Mingers, 2001; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Robey, 1996) have highlighted the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty and importance of pluralism in IS research, this paper adds to this stream of research by suggesting that the failure to have multiple perspectives at an early stage of research results in “unreasoned” discourse and is an essential contributor to Baskerville and Myers' (2009) observation that the academic literature follows a similarly faddish trajectory (on its upswing) as the practitioner literature.

## 7. Limitations

The argument maps developed in this article were based on a literature review of academic journals. As mentioned in the methodology section, the practitioners' views were represented by the analysis of articles pub lished in applied journals such as IBM Systems Journal, Communications of the ACM, Sloan Management Review, Harvard Business Review, and California Management Review. Although we expect most practitioners to agree with the material published in these journals, we recognize that further study on trade publications should be included in future studies.

Although the lens adopted by this paper provides a useful characterization of management fashions at their inception, we recognize that this representation is not all encompassing as there are aspects of management fashions that are not captured by our methodology. While this paper focuses on the role that argumentation or reasoning plays in the development of management fashions, we acknowledge that there are other compelling theorizations of management fashions that challenge the notion that this phenomenon has anything to do with reason or rational argumentation (Czarniawska & Joerges, 1996; Doorewaard & van Bijsterveld, 2001; Westrup, 2003). Indeed, a potential criticism of our discourse based analysis is that its focus on transient terminology or labeling such as “BPR” or “SOA” may mask or obscure non-faddish and enduring underlying practices, such as process-centered business practice in the case of BPR, or modular/integrated architecture in the case of SOA. Although therefore this paper posits that a study of transient terminology or labeling lends insight to our understanding of management fashions, it nevertheless concedes that there are enduring aspects of both innovations that are not captured by its chosen lens.

Similarly, while we adopt a negative conceptualization of management fashion based on a premise that ascribes merit to the longevity of a discourse, we recognize that there are alternative theoretical lenses that would allow for neutral or indeed positive portrayals of this phenomenon. Nigel Thrift (2005), for example, observes that innovation times are reducing, and therefore, worthwhile innovations may have all the characteristics of a fashion. In periods of rapid technological change, it is conceivable that transient discourses would be more advantageous to organizations than the prolonged discourses advocated by this paper.

We also acknowledge that the method and theories used in this paper cannot predict the onset of management fashion as there is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient evidence to establish a causal relationship between argument structure and management fashion. We recognize that our sample of two discourses – one enduring (ERP) and one more ephemeral (BPR) – is quite small. Indeed, although BPR and ERP were coined at the same time and share many goals and objectives, they are not exact parallels and do not represent highly controlled examples that would enable us to make claims about causation. Clearly, more innovations need to be evaluated to see if indeed unreasoned discourse is visible in those IT innovations that have been relegated to the scrap heap.

Furthermore, research is needed to conduct a more detailed deconstruction of the three studied dis courses with the intent of more carefully eliciting the “hidden meanings and unacknowledged biases” (Beath & Orlikowski, 1994) that we propose could be linked to the development of management fashions.

## 8. Implications for practice

Practitioners are constantly bombarded with all sorts of claims that such-and-such IT innovation will revolutionize business. The dilemma they face is in how to assess, at an early stage, whether such claims are mere hype or worthy of further scrutiny and possible investment. Our approach of applying Argument Mapping to the discourse surrounding such IT innovations might permit the equivocal practitioner to better assess the likelihood of such an IT innovation to catch on and change the IT/business landscape. Any approach that can help the practitioner discern likely hype from reality should be welcomed. We hope our research <sup>fi</sup>ts this practical need.

Our study also highlights to practitioners the importance of broadening the scope of discussion during early phases of innovative discourse. It is apparent from our research that the main discursive failing of early management fashion discourse is that it is constrained to a particularly narrow depiction of the innovative practice. While uncertainty is the hallmark of early stages of an innovative idea and organizations may try to navigate this ambiguity by focusing on the tried and true “bottom-line” implications of an innovative idea, our research suggests that this is an important stage to broaden the scope of discussion to include what might otherwise be considered tangential or “side-line” discourses. Indeed, while it may be more manageable to limit early discussion to “key” stakeholders, this study suggests that the earlier the views of “other” stakeholders are added to the discussion the lower the likelihood will be of buying into a fad.

## 9. Implications for future research

Many areas for future research remain. These include examining whether or not the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study could be applied to other innovative discourses, both within and external to the IS domain. This paper has also only examined the argument structures of innovative discourses during their upswings. An evaluation of the argument structures that characterize a management fashion in its downswing would facilitate an evaluation of the claim that “real learning” occurs in this phase of fashion discourse. Additionally, the ability of the argument mapping tool to generate useful insights for this paper suggests its application in other research contexts where it could be used to support, add insights, or as a contrast to other dialectic modes of analysis.

## Appendix

Description of methodology used to analyze articles

In order to properly describe the methodology used to develop the argument maps for SOA and BPR, we will take a step-by-step deconstruction of the arguments included in a paper to demonstrate how claims, grounds, warrants and other argument components are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

The article chosen for this purpose is “Impact of service orientation at the business level,” (Cherbakov et al., 2005). This particular article was chosen because it presents one of the most complex and complete arguments for SOA, including one of the few statements regarding social implications of SOA adoption. Less sophisticated articles are easier to map, however, due to their simplicity, they do not provide the optimal conditions to show the power of analysis that can be obtained by using argument mapping.

Following the methodology depicted by Fletcher and Huff (1990a,b), the deconstruction of the argument occurs in 4 stages.

• First Pass: Read through the whole document, identifying topics, arguments, and the most obvious key claims.

• Second Pass: Mark all claims, and identify grounds for each claim.

• Third Pass: Within each argument, identify sub claims, elaborations and reiterations.

• Fourth Pass: Provide implicit warrants wherever they are not obvious.

## First Pass

A comprehensive read of the document yields an obvious claim that is included in the abstract, as well as in the introduction. Relevant sections of the abstract and introduction are included below, with the claim in underlined text.

“In the current business environment in which companies are under increasing pressure not only to increase revenue but also to respond quickly to changing market conditions, companies will be successful only if they transform themselves and become on demand businesses. In this paper we describe the changes needed to effect this transformation, and in particular, we describe the important role plaved by componentization and by service orientation. We discuss the way componentization enables a business to operate in a value net, a network of partnerships with customers and suppliers supported by real-time information <sup>fl</sup>ows and information technology systems. We also describe the need for service orientation to achieve seamless integration of business components.”

One can observe, the clear intent of the authors to associate SOA with <sup>fl</sup>exibility, and the concept of ITbusiness alignment (expressed in the article as seamless integration of business components) in order to succeed in today's fast-paced market. This claim is reiterated in the Introduction section (page 654), as shown below.

“What is described here is a business that is able to recognize change as it is occurring and react appropriately, ahead of the competition, and keep pace with the demands of its customers, value-net partners, and employees alike. In trying to achieve this state, the business will need to leverage technology to the fullest. We call such a business an “on demand business”. Fundamentally, becoming an on demand business is equivalent to achieving total business <sup>fl</sup>exibility. Two important enablers contribute to the realization by an enterprise of this vision of on demand—componentization and service orientation.

The notion of componentization allows an enterprise to deconstruct, analyze, and then reconstruct into value nets, in which partnerships with customers and suppliers operate in a network supported by real-time information <sup>fl</sup>ows and integrated IT systems. The process of deconstruction/reconstruction is realized through business components, which correspond to distinct business functions. In the on demand environment, the component-based <sup>fi</sup>rm links its components ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and seamlessly both internally and across the <sup>fi</sup>rm's boundaries with best-of-breed components provided by external partners.

Nonetheless, componentization by itself is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient. Interactions between business components need to be seamlessly and tightly integrated across the value net. The need for <sup>fl</sup>exibility across the value net requires that the component network be <sup>fl</sup>exible; that is, the enterprise can “in-source” an outsourced component and vice versa; replace, on demand, a current partner with a different partner; change the terms of the contract between the two components, and so on.

The key to seamless integration between business components is service orientation. As de<sup>fi</sup>ned above, each business component serves a unique purpose and provides one or more business services for consumption by other business components. The component that consumes a business service offered by another business component is oblivious to how the provider creates the business service. Service interactions between business components are governed by business-level agreements and contracts, which cover items such as cost structures, service levels, and so on. The information technologies that evolved in support of service orientation, <sup>fi</sup>rst Web services and then service-oriented architecture (SOA), have proven to be well-suited to business transformation in the enterprise.”

Therefore, one can conclude that the main claims of this article are the following

1. Companies will be successful only if they become an on-demand business

2. In order to become an on-demand business, companies should adopt SOA, because SOA is key to seamless integration between business components.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst claim is nothing new. Even the literature on BPR provided plenty of calls for more <sup>fl</sup>exibility in the organization to respond more quickly to market changes. Even the concept of componentization is akin to BPR. Therefore, for the purpose of this study, we will focus on the argument of claim number 2.

## Second Pass and Third Pass

Because we are only interested in the claims regarding the argument for the adoption of SOA, we can combine the second and third pass by identifying all relevant claims, and the grounds provided to support them. The claims associated with SOA adoption and implementation can be found in the section, “The service oriented enterprise” on page 654 and 655.

The componentization of the business leads to an increase in the number of interacting elements, which means that more effort is invested in logistics. Complexity can be controlled by designing business functions as well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned services. Thus we see the emergence of the service-oriented enterprise.

Transforming an enterprise into a service-oriented enterprise involves business challenges that are more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to overcome than the technological challenges associated with implementing an SOA infrastructure. The concept of a service-oriented business is simple to understand; the realization is complex and requires research and innovation in many areas. In this section we describe some of the steps required to realize a service-oriented enterprise.

Thus far, we have the following logical chain for the argument expressed in this article.

1. Flexible organizations that can quickly adapt to market changes will succeed (on-demand enterprises).

2. SOA is key to the success of an on-demand enterprise.

3. SOA adoption involves business challenges that are more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to overcome than the technological changes associated with implementing SOA infrastructure.

The two grounds provided for claim 1 are:

“most CEOs expect their enterprises to reach revenue growth by becoming more responsive” – Page 653.

“Successful enterprises now focus on creating value nets, in which partnerships with customers and suppliers operate in a network supported by real-time information <sup>fl</sup>ows and integrated IT systems.” – Page 656.

The above quote can be considered a sub-claim, supported by the following grounds.

“Ever increasing customer demands for faster delivery and more choices, combined with e-commerce technologies, made the older supply chain obsolete.” – Page 656.

The grounds provided for claim 2 are:

“Complexity can be controlled by designing business functions as well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned services. Thus we see the emergence of the service-oriented enterprise.“ – Page 654.

“The information technologies that evolved in support of service orientation, <sup>fi</sup>rst Web services and then service-oriented architecture (SOA), have proven to be well-suited to business transformation in the enterprise.” Pages 654–655.

The grounds found for claim 3 are:

Sub-claim 1: “If organizational structures remain unchanged, the realization of the enterprise's SOA will be suboptimal.” – Page 658.

Supported by the following ground: “The transition to a service-oriented enterprise requires loosely coupled organizations that are based on the role de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of service consumers and service providers and the understanding of their relationships. Although the relationships within each unit may remain hierarchical, the relationships between units are not; they are based on the provision and consumption of services.” – Page 658.

Sub-claim 2: “In most effective enterprises, funding of IT development and operation will also change.” – Page 658.

Supported by the following ground: “Because services are enterprise assets, funding models need to shift from being based on lines of business to being based on the enterprise or, perhaps, on consumption.“ – Page 658.

## Fourth Pass

As stated by Fletcher and Huff (1990a,b), the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of warrants can be extremely challenging, as most of the time writers do not include them explicitly in their arguments. The purpose of the warrants is to show what the logical connection between the claims and the grounds is. In most cases, this connection is obvious, and therefore warrants can be omitted.

In the analysis of this article, we found that the logical connection between grounds and claims are straightforward, and thus there is no need to infer any warrants.
