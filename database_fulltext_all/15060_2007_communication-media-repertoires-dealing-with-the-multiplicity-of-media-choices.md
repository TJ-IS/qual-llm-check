## Introduction

Organizations increasingly rely on information and communication technologies (ICT) to support flexibility in processes and organizing mechanisms, and to facilitate innovation and responsiveness. For example, use of ICT has enabled individuals to work in teams with geographically distributed coworkers. In addition to increasingly working in distributed settings, individuals must often participate in multiple concurrent team structures. Consequently, employees often use various communication media to collaborate with colleagues in various locations, although the use of communication media can vary greatly across the team structures and work processes. The number of these technologies available to employees constantly increases (e.g., Blackberries™, pagers, mobile phones, intranets, instant messaging), in addition to e-mail, telephone, voice mail, and face-to-face (FTF) communication. As a result, the use of these multiple media in complex work environments can have significant implications for productivity and efficiency of individuals and organizations.

In this research, we explore how workers use combinations of communication media, either simultaneously or sequentially, to complete an interaction with their sometimes-distributed colleagues. Several contingency theories, such as media richness and social influence, have been proposed to understand how and when different communication media are selected. In addition, many studies have investigated the influence of different contextual factors on media use. These studies usually focus on selection of one communication medium, primarily e-mail, or on comparisons between two media, often face-to-face versus some electronic medium. However, the focus on selection or use of a single communication medium limits our understanding of current media usage in organizations, and may not capture the complexities of combining media. We believe the time has come to study not only media choice but also the usage of multiple media in the performance of work activities. We propose the concept of communication media repertoire, adapted from genre repertoire (Orlikowski and Yates 1994), to investigate patterns of use of multiple communication media for specific communication purposes. We define a communication media repertoire as the collection of communication channels and identifiable routines of use for specific communication purposes within a defined community.

Our study takes place in the sales divisions of two Fortune 100 information technology companies. We use a case study approach to identify communication media repertoires used at each firm, and compare the patterns of media usage for specific communication purposes for individuals in each firm. Based on the study's findings, we propose a framework for investigating the use of multiple communication media in organizations through examination of communication media repertoires. In developing the proposed framework, we identify two types of contextual factors affecting communication media repertoire usage, institutional and situational conditions, and discuss the importance of distinguishing them in future research. The framework highlights the role of these conditions, as well as consequences of repertoire usage, on individuals' perceptions of the media. We also highlight the dynamic nature of the development of communication media repertoires within a work community. As individuals within the community use different media for different purposes, they develop rules or norms for communicating, thereby forming communication media repertoires.

## Background

Communication is central to the organizing process and many studies have looked at the role of communications in the functioning of organizations. Increasingly, information and communication technologies (ICT) are integrated into communication-based organizational work activities. As a result, investigating the use of new and more traditional communication media to support organizational communications is an active research area.

## Previous Research on Media Choice

Several theories have been proposed to explain how and when different communication media are used. They can generally be classified as media trait theories and social influence theories (Carlson and Davis 1998). Because many excellent reviews of these theories have been published in recent years (Carlson and Zmud 1999; Fulk and Boyd 1991; Kock 2004; Te'eni 2001), we only briefly discuss them here.

An active area of research has focused on media trait theories. In general, media trait theories assume that different technologies have relatively static and objective characteristics. The choice of a communication medium is assumed to be rational; users match the characteristics of a particular medium to a specific task or communication objective. However, findings from studies using these theories have been inconsistent (Carlson and Zmud 1999; Lee 1994; Te'eni 2001). Studies have shown that these characteristics are more dynamic than initially conceptualized. For example, the same medium can be considered rich or lean depending on situational factors such as norms and rules for interaction within a particular work environment (Fulk et al. 1990) or contextual factors (Carlson and Zmud 1999; Carlson and Davis 1998; Lee 1994). Carlson and Zmud (1999) propose channel expansion theory as an extension of media trait theories. This theory suggests that an individual's experience with media, topic, and a communication partner influences perceived richness of a medium, and perceptions about the media change over time.

Another active area of research has been on the influence of contextual factors (i.e., circumstances related to the specific communication event), which have been found to significantly influence the decision to use a communication medium. For example, urgency of the communication event influences the choice of medium (Steinfeld and Fulk 1986; Trevino et al. 1987). Based on their investigations, Jones, Saunders, and McCleod (1989) propose that media usage in decision making is influenced by the characteristics of the decision being made (e.g., importance and urgency), and the user's decision-making role. Similarly, Saunders and Jones (1990) investigate a number of contextual factors (e.g., number of concurrent decisions to make, time pressures, perceived importance of the decision, value premises). They advocate the influence of institutional factors, such as the organization's information environment and established interaction patterns, on media usage for decision making. Other contextual factors that have been found to influence media usage choices are physical proximity (Steinfeld and Fulk 1986), accessibility (Culnan 1984, 1985), familiarity with technology and task (Carlson and Zmud 1999; Fulk 1993), group size (Miranda and Saunders 2003), and the level of job pressure and task routineness (Fulk 1993). Researchers also argue that understanding of contextual factors is socially constructed (Fulk 1993; Fulk et al. 1990; Miranda and Saunders 2003; Webster and Trevino 1995).

Social influence theories emphasize the role of a social actor in using various media (Eisenberg and Contractor 1990; Fulk et al. 1987; Steinfeld and Fulk 1986). Media usage provides observable cues that are processed by the communicators and in turn influence their choice of media (Walther 1992, 1995). For example, physical and material characteristics of different media are combined with the social and institutional context of the user to influence the construction of meaning and therefore the use of specific media (Yoo and Alavi 2001).

## Summary of Previous Research

The results of prior research suggest that the capabilities and appropriateness of use of a communication medium are perceived differently under different circumstances. However, there are many questions still to be answered about how different perceptions and usage patterns develop. The choice of media in previous studies is usually isolated from the ongoing work practices of users. This limits our understanding of how and when employees use the variety of different media available to them, even as the effective use of electronic media has become critical for effective performance of work activities. Previous research does address the choice to adopt a single technology, often e-mail (Lee 1994; Markus 1990, 1994), but also video conferencing (Kraut et al. 1999), groupware (Phillips and Brown 1993), and on-line information service (Lamb and Kling 2003), from other alternative communication media. Much research investigates situations when various media are used most effectively, beginning with Daft, Lengel, and Trevino (1987).

Conversely, relatively few studies examine the on-going, routine use of the multiplicity of media available to employees in performing organizational work. For example, how does having an array of media to choose from affect media usage patterns? Does the understanding of the capabilities of a specific medium vary depending on how other media are used? Are employees combining media to enhance interactions with colleagues? As the number of media available to employees increases, we must understand the effects of these new media not only on organizational tasks and performance, but also on media behaviors. While the increasing organizational use of new media, such as instant messaging, is being investigated (Nardi et al. 2000; Rennecker and Godwin 2003), the emphasis is often on the use of the new medium itself. Little research has explored how use of these new technologies is assimilated into the employee's existing set of media behaviors. Markus (1994) is an exception, but this study took place in only one firm, and, perhaps more importantly, it was conducted prior to the emergence of many communication technologies that are now commonly used in most organizations, such as use of intranets, mobile phones, and instant messaging.

Particularly important for understanding the routine use of a range of communication media in the performance of work activities are community norms, or socially established regulating behaviors. Previous research has shown the influence of communication norms and rules for interaction in the usage of media within work groups (Fulk et al. 1990; Kraut et al. 1998; Markus 1994). Norms of behavior also shape the understanding of the medium, and the utility that is gained from its use (Kraut et al. 1998, Te'eni 2001). Communication media users develop an understanding of a communication medium that influences their perception of capabilities of the medium and consequent usage behavior (e.g. Lee 1994; Markus 1994). However, little research has investigated how usage behaviors and perceptions develop, or how norms might differ between organizations. That is, how and when individuals routinely and adaptively use the variety of media available to them for performing organizational work needs to be better understood (Barry and Fulmer 2004).

## Multiple Media Usage: A Repertoire Perspective

The goal of our research is to investigate how and when different media are used in an organizational setting when employees are faced with an array of communication media from which to choose. To investigate these questions, we adopt the notion of a repertoire from Orlikowski and Yates (1994), who found a collection of communication genres (e.g., memo, proposal, dialogue, and a ballot genre system) were understood and used by members of the organization being studied.

They termed this collection a genre repertoire. Similarly, we posit that organizational members select a communication medium or a combination of media from their communication media repertoire for use in interactions with colleagues. We consider the repertoire to include the collection of communication media used by organizational members.

The concept of a repertoire also includes the existence of socially established rules regulating the use of different genres within the community (Orlikowski and Yates 1994). Genre repertoires focus on both the form and the medium of communication; however, we limit our focus to the specific communication medium being used. We draw on a technology-in-practice perspective whereby rules regulating use of technology are developed through recurrent use of the technology (Orlikowski 2000). From this perspective, employees develop an understanding of how and when to appropriately use particular media for organizational communication purposes such as coordinating or sharing knowledge. Studying the use of communication media embedded in and integral to the performance of organizational work has the advantage of providing a richer understanding of media usage (Lamb and Kling 2003; Saunders and Jones 1990). The communication media repertoire provides a foundation from which we can explore the use of communication media embedded in organizational work activities.

We argue that a repertoire perspective provides an alternative conceptual frame for examining media usage in situations where individuals select a medium or multiple media within an organizational setting to accomplish a communicative purpose. This perspective also provides a mechanism for comparing media use across different organizational communities (Orlikowski and Yates 1994). We posit that individuals within a community will have socially established norms to guide media usage patterns, and that these norms may differ between communities. Thus, our research question is

How, when, and with what implications do communication media repertoires differ between user communities?

## Methodology

In the absence of existing models for examining the use of a variety of communication media embedded in organizational work activities, we use a multiple case study approach to build theory (Eisenhardt 1989; Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991; Strauss and Corbin 1990; Yin 1994). Eisenhardt (1989) proposes several steps in the process of theory building from case study research. ## Research Sites: Selecting Cases

Guided by our research question, our sampling frames had to offer enough similar aspects that there would be some comparable properties (Miles and Huberman 1994; Yin 1994). Our requirements included organizations where employees worked in teams with some level of geographic distribution, and where several different communication media were available for use within the teams. After contacting a number of organizations, we selected sales divisions of two Fortune 100 companies in the IT industry (referred to as MyCo and IntOrg).

## MyCo

As shown in Figure 1, the sales division at MyCo is divided into two functional areas: a sales side and a technical side. The technical side is further divided into two functions: technical specialist and consultant. On the sales side, account representatives are responsible for identifying, creating, and cultivating new service opportunities, as well as attending to dedicated accounts, depending on their size. Account representatives identify the need for particular solutions or technologies, and then bring in a technical specialist who educates the client on the technology and how it will work in the client's environment. Internally, the technical specialist is charged with advising and educating other employees in the division on technology developments. The consultant is also technically oriented and works closely with the client to provide post-sales services. The consultant is primarily concerned with current products and technologies, whereas the technical specialist is more oriented toward emerging technologies. MyCo recently adopted an “office-free” environment. Consultants and account representatives are not assigned dedicated workspaces at the offices. They can reserve workspaces in a “hoteling” fashion. They are encouraged to spend more time at customer sites and may work at home. Technical specialists are assigned dedicated office space, but also may work at home if appropriate.

## Data Collection: Crafting Instruments and Protocols

Data collection took place at the convenience of the participating organizations. The primary data sources were semi-structured interviews (Myers 1997) with managers and professionals within the divisions. We also collected publicly available financial, size, and industry data, and existing paper documents, including physical layout diagrams of the workplaces, organizational charts, job descriptions, and policy manuals. Finally, we observed the physical work environments, technologies, and applications available in each company. The research team consisted of the two authors and a graduate research assistant.

## Interviews: Entering the Field

We started our research with the premise that individuals in both companies use several communication media to support communications. Based on initial interviews with senior management and prior literature on communication purposes in organizations (Watson-Manheim and Béanger 2002), we started with five communication purposes to be explored. These are summarized in Table 2. While this categorization provided a basis for questioning respondents about their use of different communication media, we did not assume it was an exhaustive list and remained open to additional purposes emerging from the interviews. No additional purposes were identified in the interviews, where respondents were asked to provide details about their experiences working in various teams. Particular emphasis was placed on (1) understanding their communication media use, (2) identifying the communication purposes for which these were used, (3) identifying other factors such as incentives, training, or culture affecting media use and effectiveness, and (4) obtaining their perceptions of communication effectiveness. ## Data Analysis and Theme Identification

Based on the key constructs, we developed an initial list of coding categories. This list was refined after the first interviews, and refined again after interviews were completed to reflect both the knowledge gained from the interviews and the additional knowledge of published research (Miles and Huberman 1994). Appendix B presents the sample coding categories. Our unit of analysis was the individual. Once the research team agreed on the list of categories, each member separately coded the same interview file. We compared results and discussed differences until agreement was reached on the categories, meanings, and future coding procedures. Each researcher then coded two interview files using a revised coding template, which provided not only structure but also flexibility for coding new or unexpected findings (Miles and Huberman 1994). After these two files were coded, further discussions were held until coders achieved complete agreement. Next, the researchers separately coded each interview. The coding resulted in summary files.

A thematic analysis was performed. Using the previously coded files, both authors analyzed the data to identify key themes, or patterns, of media usage. Themes were identified when several respondents either within or across organizations discussed a similar concept (see Appendix C).

## Results and Discussion

## Respondents

The divisions of each company are similar in size. We interviewed 18 employees per site, representing approximately 20 percent of the employees in the divisions. Some interviews could not be used in the data analysis because they were with office managers and executives to gather additional information (e.g., organization charts, job descriptions, and demonstrations of intranet applications and other available software). One tape was damaged and one individual had to leave before the interview was over. The resulting samples include 13 individuals for MyCo and 17 individuals for IntOrg. Table 3 indicates their demographics.

Employees in both organizations are members of multiple teams concurrently—one work unit team and multiple project teams. Work unit team members usually have similar jobs and report to a common manager. Employees at MyCo and IntOrg are also members of multiple short-term project teams (Cohen 1993) with various life spans. The teams have specific objectives and their members work interdependently to meet these objectives. Often, at least one member is located in a different geographic area (virtual teams), with some members reporting to different functional areas within or outside the division.

## Physical Layouts

There are observable differences in the physical layouts and atmosphere of each company workplace. At MyCo, there is a “cube farm” with some dedicated and many non-dedicated (hoteling) low-wall offices. The physical layout encourages individuals to meet informally. There is a play area, with games, free soft drink dispensers, snack machines, kitchen appliances, and tables. Yet, because many of MyCo employees work remotely, the office appears largely empty. Lockers are available for individuals’ personal effects. Individuals often report selecting hoteling desks near the printer so as to meet other MyCo employees working at the office while they are there.

At IntOrg, the physical layout also includes a cube farm, but the layout is much more traditional with dedicated high-walled cubicles, often grouped close to the work unit manager. The permanent offices for many of these employees are on-site at a customer location and everyone is expected to be almost always available to customers. Being at a customer location, IntOrg employees have access to several conference rooms where meetings can be held both formally and informally. This is particularly important to IntOrg employees as some of IntOrg's competitors also have offices located at this customer site. There is an implicit understanding that employees work on-site and be accessible as much as possible although they are allowed to work at home.

The extent to which individuals work remotely differs significantly between the two companies. At MyCo, a hoteling arrangement is in place and individuals are encouraged to perform telecommuting or mobile work. On average, individuals interviewed at MyCo report working 2.8 days per week remotely, either at home or at a customer site. Individuals at IntOrg, however, are much less distributed, reporting an average of 0.6 day per week of work at a remote location, and a significant portion of this is performed at a remote telework center (office). At IntOrg, a core part of the division is located at the customer site and all but two employees have permanent desks assigned. In contrast, 9 of the 13 employees at MyCo have no permanent desks.

## Communication Media Usage

In both organizations, a wide variety of technologies are available to employees. The technologies are similar in functionalities. However, we found differences in self-reported usage behaviors in the two firms. MyCo employees report an average of 87.1 E-mails per day while 51.1 are reported at IntOrg. Fifty-four percent of respondents at MyCo report e-mail as the primary organizational communication medium, while there is little agreement on a primary organizational communication medium at IntOrg. There are significant differences in expectations for e-mail response time: 2 to 4 hours at MyCo, 24 hours at IntOrg. However, MyCo employees are also more likely (46 percent) to perceive that e-mail creates information and work overload for them, compared with 29 percent at IntOrg. Finally, 43 percent of IntOrg respondents report spending a significant portion of their day in face-to-face meetings, compared to zero at MyCo.

Communication media repertoires at the two firms included both single and combined media usage. There are similarities and differences in the types and combinations of media used for specific communication purposes as reported by respondents (see Table 4).

## Coordination

Coordination is the process through which interdependence of tasks among individuals is managed (Thompson 1997) and task uncertainty is reduced (Van de Ven et al. 1976). Communication between individuals is necessary for the efficient coordination of work activities (Barry and Crant 2000; Kraut and Streeter 1995). In the two firms, participants discuss two types of coordination activities, which we categorize as simple (e.g., logistical coordination) and complex (e.g., shared understanding of task and objectives). Simple coordination includes task assignment, task follow-up, and meeting scheduling. Employees report differences in media preferences for simple coordination. Employees at MyCo most frequently report e-mail as the main medium for coordination followed up by either face-to-face interactions or phone conversations. This is not surprising given the e-mail culture suggested by the respondents and the distributed nature of the workforce.

It's all the prework done all over e-mail and then physically, when we need to get together then we will schedule the time, book out a conference room or what-have-you. (MyCo Respondent)

IntOrg employees, however, report using chat or pagers to check availability before contacting someone by phone.

[Chat] serves a big need in particular if...you need some quick answers and it's kind of nondisruptive. The other party is on the phone, they can still, if they're monitoring that, they see it. That's very beneficial. I use it all the time. (IntOrg Respondent)

Opportunity-tracking software is used extensively at IntOrg and is frequently reported as useful for logistical coordination. However, the software is used in a complementary fashion to other media.

It's just a vehicle; I think it's just a repository to keep track of things. You know, names, due dates, tasks and that kind of thing. And, from then on out it's a matter of follow-up with the owners of those tasks, and you do that with either telephone calls, emails or whatever is appropriate. (IntOrg Respondent)

Complex coordination includes developing a shared understanding of issues (e.g., task objectives), or “be on same page” type of communication. Employees in both organizations report using scheduled face-to-face meetings and teleconferencing to accomplish this purpose. In both organizations, complex coordination is also addressed by combining media. In particular, employees report using features of e-mail to support a subsequent coordination activity (e.g., provide a record of pre-meeting interaction).

Through e-mail sometimes things get shaped and changed.. into quite exactly what you wanted to express. And then we typically get a conference call together.... (MyCo Respondent)

## Knowledge Sharing

Individuals exchange and combine knowledge through communication events (Nonaka 1994). Knowledge sharing involves shared learning activities and collaborative problem solving. Employees at MyCo report combining media in a sequential fashion from phone to e-mail for knowledge sharing, while only one IntOrg employee reports such a combination. Using e-mail as follow-up to phone conversations can serve to document the exchange, or clarify the information.

If you talk to me on the phone I probably, after you tell me something, I'll say, great! can you send that to me as an e-mail? (MyCo Respondent)

At IntOrg, instead of e-mail, employees report using the phone to clarify knowledge exchanged during teleconferences.

If we have a conference at 1 o'clock, we're done by 2. Well usually at 4 o'clock somebody calls me, "[name], I was confused by what he said in the conference call." (IntOrg Respondent)

We found evidence at both firms of media being used concurrently, either to conduct parallel discussions or perform additional work (i.e., multitasking). This was more prevalent at IntOrg, with only one employee at MyCo reporting such usage. In particular, employees report using chat or instant messaging functions with other team members while participating in a conference call.

When you do so many conference calls, it's real helpful to be able to chat with people while you're on those conference calls that...you may or may not have to be participating actively and I find it helpful to get other things done. (IntOrg Respondent)

Single communication media were also used for knowledge sharing, although usage varied significantly between the two firms. While both firms have periodic face-to-face meetings, only employees at MyCo perceive these also to be useful for knowledge sharing.

I don't like to use the team meeting just to present information unless there is a specific topic that is complex. I do believe you can use e-mail, phone, and conference calls for those sorts of things, and to bring everyone together I think people get bored after an hour of just hearing information and having that information dumped on them. So I like them to roll up the sleeves and let's work on the problem and do some brainstorming on some issue that we see. (MyCo Respondent)

At IntOrg, individuals are more likely than MyCo employees to report ad hoc meetings as being most useful to share knowledge. This is not surprising since IntOrg employees are physically collocated, allowing ad hoc meetings to occur more easily.

In the informal ones [meetings], I have a lot of questions. I mean, I want to see what they have done in terms of pricing or putting a proposal in front of a customer....I want to learn from them. So, I typically ask to get together so that we can look over something and I can ask questions. (IntOrg Respondent)

## Information Gathering

Information gathering involves methods of finding specific information needed for the performance of work activities. Two main strategies of information gathering were identified in our interviews, which we characterize as personalization and codification strategies (Hansen et al. 1999). The preferred strategy at both companies is personalization, or direct contact with the person who has the needed information. Personalization strategies depend on direct person-to-person contact because the information is closely tied to the individual; networks of people are more important than computers for sharing the information (Hansen et al. 1999).

I think because it's a big company, it's sort of largely still driven on who you know. You know, so there's a very big informal network and you kind of get to know the people that have specialties in different areas. (IntOrg Repondent)

The personalization strategy was also evident in the media combinations reported by employees for information gathering, for example combining e-mail with phone calls.

If I can't find the information myself, I tend to send an e-mail to somebody at my virtual team that may know the answer. From there, if I think that that is maybe not appropriate, I will send an e-mail to my manager or pick up the phone and call someone who may know the answer. (MyCo Respondent)

The reason is e-mail takes the time bias out of communication, because if I can't find someone to talk to I can at least ensure that they have heard what I had to say. Then we will link up later, either through an e-mail response or we will schedule a meeting, or meet over lunch but generally to get the first initial message out on e-mail is very effective. (IntOrg Respondent)

The second major information gathering strategy is codification, or accessing information through electronic resources, most often by searching the company intranet or e-mailing colleagues. This strategy is more prevalent at MyCo. Unlike personalization, codified information is conducted on a “person-to-document” basis, where information is stored in a computer system and available for reuse by many employees (Hansen et al. 1999). At MyCo, the intranet is especially important for accessing codified information. One potential reason is that employees at MyCo have incentives to make information available, and can set up internal Web pages for this purpose. They feel that much of the information they need is available somewhere on the intranet.

We've got web pages, which is the directory on-line, and we've got an entire porthole for [X] software, which is what I sell. Where a laundry list of our products, who we compete with, how we position, how we price, what we demo, write-ups that we've had in the past. (MyCo Respondent)

However, workers at MyCo also report difficulty finding specialized information on the intranet without first knowing the name of the specialist. This is due to the large amounts of codified information available on the intranet. As a result, using a personalization strategy is still key to finding codified information if it is available.

It's just because it is so widely scattered. I mean, again with all the hyper high technology people that are around here, they are really quick in setting up their own internal website. Which is great that they all have the ability to do that. But how do you know that that is out there? How do you know that I have my own internal web site that has this great series of white papers on how to apply [MyCo] strategies? You don't! Unless you and I happen to be talking. (MyCo Respondent)

## Relationship Development

Relationship development is the process by which individuals are socialized into the organization and then create their networks of contacts within the organization (Barry and Crant 2000; Gabarro 1990). Informal relationships and communication are important in transmitting organizational culture and knowledge across different functional and hierarchical levels (Stohl 1995). At both firms, developing personal relationships is important for effective performance in a variety of ways as discussed previously. In addition, through networks and/or relationships with other workers, individuals get assigned to projects or know whom to select for their own projects. Even with the available advanced technologies, including quasi-face-to-face settings such as videoconferences, employees at both firms still prefer meeting in person to develop relationships.

Typically you try to keep it in front of people and to...build relationship[s], I guess....[So] you go to meetings. (IntOrg Respondent)

[The biggest challenge] is getting to know team members....I have problems with people that I didn't know before...I don't see or talk to—because I don't have the opportunities...it just takes time meeting with teammates and actually go on some calls...and then all of a sudden you start working together, because you just work on this call, and once you do that—lunch—and then [you know them]. (MyCo Respondent)

MyCo employees also report using a combination of e-mail and face-to-face or telephone to develop relationships because they are more geographically dispersed, as illustrated by the following quote.

Technology, I guess it's not going to do much to build personal relationships....I don't know—when I started I kept e-mailing somebody quite a lot and never talked to him. But then I went out with him in [training sessions]...and then when I did talk to him it was as if we've known each other. It's like if you haven't seen somebody for a long time and you never met before but you always e-mailed...and so in a way it is not all negative—technology. I think there [are] ways you can access people that you could have never accessed them before because of that. (MyCo Respondent)

Using e-mail is not sufficient for relationship development, but combined with either meetings or phone conversations it provides enough of a personal touch to overcome the limitations of e-mail alone.

## Conflict Resolution

Conflict resolution is the process by which disagreements between individuals are addressed, handled, and ultimately resolved. The source of conflicts differs between the two companies, and conflicts are discussed less often at MyCo than at IntOrg. At MyCo, conflicts are primarily attributed to personality differences, while at IntOrg conflicts are attributed to conflicting objectives among team members and conflicting directions on tasks.

Employees at the two firms use media differently in resolving conflict. At IntOrg, face-to-face interaction is viewed as the only method of resolving conflicts, which often includes escalation to higher management at both firms. Individuals at MyCo, however, report using e-mail prior to telephone or face-to-face interactions for conflict resolution.

I would say phone or face-to-face is where it goes after it leaves e-mail. Typically you won't see many e-mail escalation of conflict that really gets knock down, drag 'em out because its documented and you can't delete it once its been sent and people are very cautious about that. (MyCo Respondent)

[When there's a conflict,] you talk and you get them out on the table quickly...while in a face-to-face meeting It can be confrontational So the immediate challenge is just getting people to talk. Sometimes, you know, you can hide behind e-mails. You know I often will pick-up the phone and say, "You know what. This e-mail that you sent, don't send me any more like that. Call, come get me, let's talk." (IntOrg Respondent)

## Discussion: Shaping Hypotheses

## Influence of Institutional Factors

Our results indicate areas of common understanding between the two groups of employees as to how and when certain communication media repertoires are used. However, there are also a number of differences in the perception of suitability of different media for certain purposes. While the two work units have a number of similarities in terms of the work being done (selling IT products and services) and the communication technologies available for use, there are also significant institutional differences: the physical structure of the two organizational work units; the social structure, especially interpersonal trust; and incentives for use of different media. These differences appear to influence employees' perceptions of different communication media, and their appropriateness for use in combinations.

## Physical Structure of Workplace

As discussed previously, workers at IntOrg are located in cubicle offices, often clustered by formal reporting groups. Most employees have dedicated offices at the customer location. While a few employees take advantage of the opportunity to work at home, it is generally understood that they should work on-site to be available to their customer. IntOrg employees spend a significant portion of their day in meetings and are concerned about the inefficiency of these meetings. Consequently, they employ combinations of media that make their time more efficient. Specifically, they report using chat or project management software while being on teleconferences with non-collocated colleagues. IntOrg employees find it relatively easy to conduct scheduled and ad hoc face-to-face meetings, and expect such meetings to be held often. However, they stress the value of capabilities of face-to-face meetings (e.g., the ability to look a coworker in the eye) as reasons for using this medium.

On the other hand, most MyCo employees do not have assigned offices and report working at home more often (2.8 days per week). They also spend significant time visiting different customer locations. For all communication purposes, e-mail is included in at least one of the identified media combinations (see Table 4). The lack of physical collocation and mobility results in the extensive use of e-mail, an asynchronous medium. MyCo employees report a smaller number of face-to-face meetings and find it more difficult to participate in face-to-face meetings than at IntOrg. Meeting face-to-face often requires restructuring of an entire day as one employee recounts.

So if I have meetings here, I usually come right here in the morning and stay here all day....I try to arrange the types of things that I do around just being here all day. Not that I couldn't do the same things at home, because I could, but I am not going to come here just for an hour, because I live an hour away, and then turn around and go home. (MyCo Respondent)

Thus meetings at MyCo are scheduled ahead of time and few unscheduled ad hoc meetings are reported. As a result, MyCo employees use the structured and scheduled formal monthly meetings for multiple purposes such as complex coordination and sharing knowledge. These meetings are seen as efficient for knowledge sharing among work unit team members.

[The formal meetings are] the touch point where you get your peers together and you can share experiences and it gives our managers a chance to communicate to us what is most important to get done over the next months, what we can expect, and if there is something major coming up....Usually it is all day...[but] that is about the only time we see each other for the entire month....[These meeting are pretty] efficient for sharing knowledge [with others]. (MyCo Respondent)

Conversely, while knowledge sharing does occur in formal meetings at IntOrg, employees prefer informal meetings for knowledge sharing. This is in part due to the perceived inefficiency of the large number of formal meetings they attend each day. The preference is also influenced by the close proximity of team members, which creates opportunities for such meetings to easily occur.

I think I am probably on the lighter side of that [formal meetings], too. Scheduled formal meetings—about three a day—so 15 a week. Informal—that depends; you just catch them. My group is right around me, maybe at least five a day informal. And that is something that is preferential to me. I would much rather have someone catch me and say, “I need ten minutes of your time some time now or soon.” (IntOrg Respondent)

## Interpersonal Trust

Interpersonal trust has important implications for conducting organizational work activities. Trust is especially critical for effective performance of collaborative work which is heavily communication dependent (Paul and McDaniel 2004). There have been a large number of studies investigating trust in the workplace (e.g., McAllister 1995) and in virtual teams (e.g., Jarvenpaa and Tractinsky 1999; Paul and McDaniel 2004; Zolin et al. 2004). Studies on virtual teams have examined non-collocated teams who are using electronic media for communication, generally investigating how and when trust is formed and how trust can be maintained in this environment. Our findings add a new dimension to this research, as we found evidence that levels of trust at MyCo and IntOrg influenced behavior and usage patterns of the media.

We draw on the definition of interpersonal trust from McAllister (1995): “the extent to which a person is confident in, and willing to act on, the basis of the words, actions, and decisions of another” (p. 25). Thus, if trust levels are low, people express less confidence in the promises made, the reliability of information shared, or other statements made by colleagues. We find significant differences in the level of confidence the employees at the two companies had in their colleagues. These differences impact the media combinations used for information gathering at the two companies. At MyCo, individuals report using e-mail followed by face-to-face or telephone meetings. While IntOrg respondents also identify e-mail as the first step, we find a pattern of redundant media to ensure that the intended recipient effectively receives the communication. So for example, as indicated in the quote below, employees might send an e-mail then follow up with a voice mail, and then with a text message. This is consistent with task closure theory (Straub and Karahanna 1998), which suggests that individuals feel the need to achieve closure to a communication segment. However, in this case, e-mail is insufficient to achieve that closure, requiring instead a serial combination of communication media from one repertoire.

From getting responses and using [e-mail]....It's starting to fail me because then I have to go and do the face-to-face. I have to make the additional phone calls which all of sudden we've got these very redundant things happening because the initial entry point is failing. (IntOrg Respondent)

Communicating with a variety of media enables employees to switch from one medium to another when they do not receive a response to a medium in a timely fashion. The prevalence of redundant media use at IntOrg is also likely related to the e-mail response time differences in the two organizations (i.e., 24 hours at IntOrg and 2 to 4 hours at MyCo). Employees at IntOrg do not perceive e-mail to be a reliable medium for interaction in part because they do not have confidence that they will get a response from colleagues. Moreover, they discuss media preferences in ways that indicate skepticism in the reliability of information being given, another indication of low levels of trust that MyCo respondents do not express. For example, face-to-face communication is preferred to e-mail in the following quote:

I like to look in their eyes and say, "What is this?" And if they start stammering, they don't know. I don't know that from an e-mail. (IntOrg Respondent)

Low levels of trust at IntOrg also appear to lead to defensive communication behaviors such as copying e-mail to colleagues and management to show “how busy I am,” and deliberately omitting involved people from a distribution list.

It's kind of hard. You get the idea that you are not supposed to know something....Sometimes it's deliberate; yeah, we want you out of the loop, [and at other times], then you get everyone copied on something. (IntOrg Respondent)

Both groups recognize the physical features of the e-mail but interpret the capabilities enabled by those features differently. MyCo employees do not discuss the need to assess reliability of information being given by the person, nor manipulation of distribution lists for impression management. In fact, several employees cite the documentation feature of e-mail as a reason for their use of e-mail for knowledge sharing.

The most efficient way would probably be e-mail because you can provide more information...if you are trying to share content with them or provide information to them it might be easier to receive it if it is written. (MyCo Respondent)

High trust levels at MyCo may have been influenced by management actions. While IntOrg employees extensively use project management software for simple coordination activities, MyCo employees perceive e-mail as most suitable for this purpose. Employees at MyCo do not use available project management software and prefer to keep individual task lists. This behavior is supported by management.

Since basically I am their manager I am tracking everybody's but basically they track their own. I basically trust that they know their business better than I know their business. They maintain it their own way. I just maintain a list of the things that I communicated so that I don't lose track. (MyCo Respondent)

## Organizational Incentives to Use Communication Media

Implicit and explicit incentives appear to contribute to the different ways communication media are used in the two organizations. Research suggests that strong social and organizational encouragement for use of e-mail influences the decision to use this medium (Markus 1994). Employees at both firms report combining e-mail with other media to achieve communication purposes. This finding is much more prominent at MyCo, where e-mail is consistently combined with other media across all purposes reported by employees (Table 4). Conversely, there is no medium used consistently in combinations at IntOrg, nor is a primary communication medium identified.

One thing that I think is a trait of [MyCo]...is just that people default to send e-mail first as the primary medium of operation. (MyCo Respondent)

Compensation structures may influence the differences in repertoire usage in the two organizations. Both groups have fixed salaries with some level of commission. As can be seen from Figure 1 (see “Research Sites” under the “Methodology” section), MyCo employees report directly to a unit manager and have relatively standardized commissions. On the other hand, IntOrg employees work in a matrixed organizational structure with multiple reporting relationships and significant negotiation of commissions across projects. These are often cited as a significant source of conflict between employees.

I guess I would say what causes conflicts the most is ...internal measurement issues. Because different teams...have different measurements...some are cost centers, where they don't really care about the profit that they make on a specific engagement. This can tend to be the biggest area of conflict. (IntOrg Respondent)

Explicit incentives also influence differences in usage and understanding of the single intranet. There is extensive information about products, white papers, and other material available on both organizations' intranets. Both MyCo and IntOrg employees have access to the intranet and report using it; however, MyCo employees indicate that use of the medium is more prevalent in their work activities. Only at MyCo do employees express a common preference for using the intranet for information gathering. This different behavior likely stems from different incentives for technology use in the two companies (Orlikowski 1993). MyCo employees are given specific performance incentives to share best practices by putting information on the intranet—a codification strategy of knowledge sharing through the intranet in which knowledge is codified and stored so that it can be accessed by anyone at the company (Hansen et al. 1999). Consequently, while employees in both firms believe the intranet is a source of valuable information, MyCo employees are more likely to use it to both share and gather information.

If you are talking about incentives, the other incentive is that you get recognition for the fact that you develop what would be considered a best practice. And it is especially nice when whatever you may have developed is now going to be used, not only across your states but the region and then ultimately company wide—that is a big recognition. So the company, at the top-level, recognizes the need for sharing information and they reward you based on that. (MyCo Respondent)

We have discussed these institutional factors separately for ease of analysis and presentation; however, they are not separable in practice (Orlikowski 2000). For example, the relative ease of having face-to-face interactions in combination with low levels of interpersonal trust influenced the rules of behavior reported by IntOrg employees (e.g., more face-to-face meeting are expected since they are necessary to overcome lack of trust).

## Task Characteristics

In both organizations, face-to-face meetings are preferred over e-mail for complex tasks (e.g., planning or development of documents) that require interactivity between coworkers or complicated explanations where questions may be asked. However, both groups also agree that for coordinating these meetings (i.e., simple logistical tasks), e-mail is the preferred medium to use. Further, use of e-mail in combination with meetings provides documentation of the exchanges. Workers at IntOrg express a preference for e-mail to record interactions that can be retrieved later. MyCo workers also identify this functionality of e-mail as useful in knowledge sharing (e.g., threads of conversations could be followed to gain understanding of a conversation).

## Message Characteristics

We find some agreement in both firms that face-to-face or phone interactions are preferred to e-mail when there is sensitive information to communicate. Workers also express the view that certain information (e.g., very lengthy messages) is more readily interpreted when it can be seen (versus heard). Thus, in this situation they use e-mail in combination with face-to-face or phone conversations.

## Urgency

At both MyCo and IntOrg, employees report varying media choices if the message is urgent (i.e., a quick response is needed). Employees mention the difficulty of conveying a sense of urgency in e-mail because there are too many e-mails and some people do not respond to them. Consequently, media usage routines in the repertoires at each firm differ when communication becomes urgent. For example, instead of using e-mail alone for simple coordination, MyCo employees might use e-mail first and then the telephone to convey an urgent request for information.

I send an e-mail request and if it is urgent for like tomorrow, I call them up as well. (MyCo Respondent)

## A Framework for Investigating Communication Media Repertoires

We have defined a communication media repertoire as the collection of communication media and identifiable routines of use for specific communication purposes within a defined user community. Using a repertoire perspective in our research has allowed us to move away from the question of why a communication medium is chosen for use (as previous media trait and social influence theories have done) to investigate how and when an array of communication media are used in the ongoing performance of work activities. This perspective provides a powerful tool for investigating media usage in an organizational context and the resulting consequences. As an example of this, our research has brought to light the use of combinations of media, which has received little academic investigation (for an exception, see Nardi et al. 2000) even though this is a common occurrence at both MyCo and IntOrg.

To further investigate this phenomenon we propose the framework shown in Figure 2. We suggest that communication media repertoires are constituted and reconstituted through media usage in the performance of ongoing, routine work activities. Figure 2 makes explicit the central role of usage practices in understanding the repertoires in the community. We propose that usage practices are directly influenced by (1) the existing repertoire of practices, (2) structuring conditions, and (3) perceived consequences of use. Usage practices are indirectly affected by usage norms in the community.

The influence of the repertoire is not deterministic but rather influences usage practices by providing a set of alternative usage routines understood by the community to be appropriate for accomplishing a purpose. The repertoire can be composed of a single routine or multiple usage routines. In this framework, we aim to capture the inherent duality of organizational routines (Feldman and Pentland 2003). While there are rules and expectations for media use, actual usage practices may deviate from expected behavior. We propose that at the time of action the communication media repertoire, as well as the individual understanding of the situation and structuring conditions, influence media usage decisions. The existing repertoire of practices provides a frame through which usage decisions are made. However, changes may be made based on the understanding of the specific situation and events at the time of the usage decision.

As discussed previously, structuring conditions have two components: institutional conditions and situational conditions. Institutional conditions constrain or facilitate the set of possibilities of media usage that users perceive as appropriate for use. So for example, for knowledge sharing, employees at MyCo find scheduled face-to-face meetings most useful, while at IntOrg, employees report using scheduled and ad hoc face-to-face meetings. Employees at MyCo do not consider ad hoc face-to-face meetings as a possibility for knowledge sharing due largely to the physical structure of their workplace. As shown in Table 6, an institutional norm at MyCo is to hold few face-to-face meetings relative to IntOrg. Institutional conditions then influence the preferences that individuals express for media use and consequently the development of usage norms (shown in Table 6). The process of development of the norms is depicted in Figure 2.

Institutional conditions lead to clear distinctions of expected behavior between the two firms and are more prescriptive of actual behavior than situational conditions. Situational conditions appear to influence which practice may be invoked at a particular point in time. So continuing with the above example, if the need to share knowledge is urgent and all parties are present, an ad hoc face-to-face meeting may be held at IntOrg. Alternatively, if the meeting is urgent, employees who need to meet are paged to come to a scheduled meeting, an expected norm at IntOrg (see Table 6). Whether this practice is invoked, however, depends on the individuals' interpretation of the situation; for example, whether a meeting does qualify as urgent. These variations are based on individuals' interpretations of the situation and their perception of the capabilities that communication media afford in the given situation.

The performance of any routine has an “improvisatory nature” (Feldman and Pentland 2003, p. 102) and changes to the routine are always an option for the individual. For example, for complex coordination, employees at both MyCo and IntOrg report using two different combinations of media: (1) face-to-face followed by e-mail/phone followed by additional face-to-face and (2) e-mail followed by a phone call (see Table 5). In this case, an employee may invoke one of these two combinations depending on her understanding of the specific situation, but she may also alter the routine to fit the situation. These changes can range from minor adjustments (e.g., seeing someone in the hall and conveying the message in person instead of making a phone call) or major adjustments (e.g., deciding a phone call has not produced adequate results and an additional face-to-face meeting must be held to emphasize the importance of an issue). Thus, variations may be somewhat ad hoc or they may be expected norms in the user community (see Table 6).

As depicted in Figure 2, consequences of usage reflexively influence usage decisions. Individuals use media to accomplish a purpose and observe consequences of usage. These observed consequences influence perceptions of the capabilities of the media and the appropriateness of use (or usage sequence in the case of media combinations) for accomplishing a purpose (Grint and Woolgar 1997; Orlikowski 2000). An individual may then replicate or vary usage based on her interpretation of usage consequences. Through repeated use of media by members of the group, expectations of usage are developed within the user group and norms of behavior become routinized (Orlikowski 2000). The inclusion of routines in the repertoire construct allows for adaptation in response to changing conditions (Orlikowski and Yates 1994). By repeatedly reacting to specific circumstances, new routines are developed and maintained, and existing ones adapted (Feldman and Pentland 2003). In this way, variation can ultimately lead to the formation of a new routine in the repertoire.

An example from our research illustrates the improvisational nature of media usage practices. At IntOrg, the pager was described as the primary medium for urgent communications (see Table 6). In practice, understanding of what exactly was meant by urgent and when to actually use the pager varied significantly. While most employees agree that the pager was to be used when there was an immediate need to respond to a customer request or concern, the pager was also seen as a way to insure a timely response under a variety of circumstances. Consequently, some employees report that the pager was overused, making it difficult to get a timely response and often causing them to use an alternative medium in combination with the pager. These isolated examples did not emerge as an alternate norm of media usage in urgent situations; however, this case provides an example of the dynamic nature of the repertoires and how actual usage practices and observations of consequences can ultimately lead to changes in norms of usage.

Finally, we briefly discuss the direct relationship between usage norms and structuring conditions. We have discussed the direct influence on usage decisions of structuring conditions and the indirect influence of norms, through the development of communication media repertoires. We also propose that usage norms can influence the understanding of structuring conditions, thereby again indirectly influencing usage decisions. For example, we identify differences in interpersonal trust as an institutional condition that influences the development of different communication media repertoires in the two organizations. This leads to differences in institutional norms, specifically, the need to verify task closure when using asynchronous media (as shown in Table 6). While task closure is assumed at MyCo, at IntOrg the receiver is expected to verify task closure when using asynchronous media. If no verification is received, it is appropriate to use multiple media until a response is received. Ultimately, redundant usage may create an environment where it is routine that individuals will not answer their e-mails or voice mails, knowing that others will ask many times before it is deemed important, therefore further exaggerating the climate of distrust in the group. Alternatively, if explicit norms are developed for task closure, the climate of distrust may be mitigated, ultimately leading to changes in usage practices.

## Implications

This research has implications for the future study of electronically mediated work as the use of the multiplicity of ICTs becomes more prevalent in organizations. The proposed framework serves as a starting point for exploring the use of multiple communication media in organizations. In particular, a common change in institutional conditions in organizations is the introduction of a new medium of communication and the development of usage patterns for that technology. For example, research has shown that the introduction of instant messaging into an organization leads to the development of new usage structures and routines for that technology (Rennecker and Godwin 2003). Our framework supports this view but also highlights the importance of examining how the new medium is integrated into the usage routines of the community, and how it creates changes in the understanding of usage of other communication media in the repertoire. Of course, the framework needs to be explored in longitudinal studies, and needs to be tested against previous media choice findings.

We investigate media repertoires in two different organizational settings, allowing us to explore the role of institutional factors on shaping repertoires. As a result, we propose that institutional conditions influence the development of norms of communication media usage in organizational communities, both directly and indirectly, by influencing perceptions of capabilities of media. Findings also suggest that usage preferences are influenced by situational conditions, or the circumstances in a specific situation. While previous research has identified a number of contextual factors that influence media choice, our study suggests that drawing a distinction between the influence of institutional factors and situational factors may be beneficial to a better understanding of organizational media usage. As demonstrated in the previous section, while institutional conditions may influence media usage patterns, particular situational conditions, such as urgency, may attenuate the influence of these institutional conditions.

Results from research on media choice provide a limited basis for understanding different perceptions of media. Most research has focused on differences in perceptions of “richness” and has attributed these differences to such factors as previous experience with media or communication partners (e.g., Carlson and Zmud 1999; Lee 1994). Our research indicates the need for a more comprehensive explanation with attention to the conditions under which media is used and the role of the media in work practices, which we explore by examining the purpose for the communicative interaction.

In addition, the explanatory power of findings from previous studies is somewhat limited by the research settings since they have generally not compared differences between media use in units of different organizations. Rather, they focused on media choice by employees in one organization (Lee 1994), or by students (Carlson and Zmud 1999; Yoo and Alavi 2001), although there are notable exceptions (Carlson and Davis 1998; Daft et al. 1987; Jones et al. 1989; Maznevski and Chudoba 2000). Field settings for future research are important to develop a better understanding of how media is used in performing routine work activities. It is important to include the purpose for communication when studying media use in organizational work activities. Much more work needs to be done to understand how communication media are being integrated into work activities. Field settings do allow deeper exploration of the influence of institutional level variables on perceptions of media characteristics. The set of available communication media are similar in our research settings, but differences in available media in other organizations may influence employees' perceptions of media capabilities, and influence usage preferences and behaviors. Research in organizations with different physical conditions where employees may not easily engage in face-to-face communication (e.g., global virtual teams) may lead to different findings.

More research is needed to better understand use of multiple media. Media choice theories could be extended to support not only choice but also ongoing usage preferences in the context of multiple media, and communication media repertoires might be the lens that allow this extension. Focusing on choice of a single medium limits understanding of media use in multiple media environments as people are often using combinations of media. One medium may be dominant but accompanied by complementary use of other media as we find in our study. Therefore, we believe caution should be exercised when questioning users about the choice of a medium. While users may discuss one medium for use, closer examination of our data reveals the use of multiple media to accomplish specific communication purposes. In addition, there may be interrelationships between media that influence both usage preferences but are obscured by focusing on the choice of a medium. Adopting a repertoire perspective focuses attention on the sequential or combined use of the variety of communication media available in organizations.

An interesting finding is the use of chat (instant messaging) in combination with phone calls or conference calls at IntOrg. Use of instant messaging in organizations is fairly new, although researchers have started to investigate such use (Nardi et al. 2000), albeit often in isolation. While we did not collect data on the actual type of instant messaging system used, it could be interesting for researchers to investigate this since different types of conferencing systems (e.g., Skype™, MSN™, etc.) allow users to switch from instant messaging to video or voice over IP conferences, providing a multiplicity of media in one application. Future research on such systems could be undertaken using communication media repertoires.

Our findings and proposed framework also highlight the need for additional research to better understand how perceptions and understanding of media capabilities are developed, and both influence and are influenced by communication behaviors. We highlight the importance that perceived capabilities of a medium appear to play in its selection, possibly more so than the material characteristics. As Grint and Woolgar (1997, p. 138) argue, “the relevance of technology lies in actor interpretive activities rather than in any objective account of its capacities or effects.”

For practitioners, the repertoire perspective calls attention to the complexity of the current communication environment in organizations. We highlight the importance of understanding the impact of the use of multiple communication media on the productivity of employees. For example, the findings suggest that using communication media in a dysfunctional manner, such as when usage patterns reflect low levels of interpersonal trust, can lead to decreased effectiveness in communication, wasted effort, and frustration for employees, and wasted resources in the organization. To circumvent or alleviate such negative outcomes, management must understand the sources of trust issues and work to resolve them. They may also provide trust building exercises or training to employees and managers in the use of the media.

While communication technology can facilitate more efficient communication and increase the geographic scope of interactions, large repertoires of communication media may actually increase the complexity of work practices. As communication technology is increasingly integrated into work practices, employees face increased diversity of team memberships, multiple communication medium availability, and differing local conditions. To reduce this complexity, organizations could encourage teams or work units to create communication protocols early in the team's life so that individuals can build a shared understanding of when to use which communication media or what the choice means, and increase the productivity of all team members by reducing duplicate or unnecessary exchanges.

## Limitations

Limitations result from the choices made for this research. Our data was collected from the sales divisions of two different companies in the high tech industry. The selling tasks were described as more complex at IntOrg than at MyCo, which may have contributed to some of the differences in the communication practices that emerged from the data. However, the sales process also includes a number of similarities across the two firms, which may influence the common perceptions that we find. A comparison of a work unit in a research and development division of a firm with a sales division, or even sales divisions in two different industries, may have resulted in different preferences for media usage. We also interviewed individuals at one point in time; our data is then limited by the memory and possible biases of the employees. When the employees were asked what media they used in a particular situation, they may not have described their actual behavior but their espoused behavior—what they would like to do or assume they will do (Argyris and Schon 1974).

An additional limitation stems from the lack of direct measurement of individual characteristics. In some instances, individual preferences or individual characteristics might override some situational and institutional factors in influencing repertoire usage. Similarly, we did not specifically ask whether respondents use the same repertoire as that of the sender when they receive a request or communication. Researchers may find the matching of repertoire selections between the sender and the receiver to be an interesting avenue for future research. Finally, we recognize the need for longitudinal studies to investigate communication practices from a process and actual work practice perspectives.

## Conclusion

This study investigates how individuals use the variety of communication media available to them in the process of performing organizational work. In today's organizations, individuals work in complex environments: they are members of multiple teams with differing membership; they have access to an increasing number of communication media, and often combine the use of electronic media with face-to-face communication; they must develop and navigate different norms of communication media usage in these different teams to perform communication-based work. While previous research has focused on selecting one communication medium for a specific task or given certain factors, our results indicate that looking at the richness of the media or the equivocality of the tasks is not enough. Comparing the use of an electronic medium to the use of face-to-face is also insufficient, and not realistic in many firms today. Our results indicate that individuals faced with multiple choices of communication media actually make use of a repertoire of communication media rather than making discrete media choices, and develop norms of media usage over time. This research is only a first step in understanding the implications of the multiplicity of media choices in the current work environment. Our proposed framework for studying communication media repertoires provides a foundation for future investigation of the use of multiple communication media across a variety of organizational settings.

## Partial Interview Protocol

The questions below represent a sample of the questions that guided the interviews with team members. Further probing was performed as necessary. Adapted protocols were used for managers, office managers, and executives.

Background Information. Describe your formal role and work performed as a member of the team. Does this vary? Do you have an informal role you play as well? How long have you worked in this job? With this company?

Work Setting. What is your work setting? Office? Location? How often are you at another location? Do you have a choice in location? Are there consequences/impacts from selecting a different work location? On average, what percentage of time do you spend at each location?

Team Membership. Describe the teams of which you are a member (or recent ones). How is team membership determined? How are goals determined and agreed on and accepted? How are resources obtained? Difficulties? Does the project team maintain the same membership over the life of the project? What is the average life of a project?

Communication. How would you describe communication within the teams of which you are a member? What kind of things do you need to communicate about? How frequently are formal meetings held? What are the most common reasons for having a formal meeting? How are formal meetings set up? Are formal meetings efficient? What would make them more efficient? What kind of informal communication do you have? Generally, how is information distributed to team members? How do you generally get the information you need? Is this satisfactory? Was it productive? How do you generally get information that is not formally available, for example, that does not exist in brochures, such as pending promotions or other organizational changes?

Communication Media. Think of the most recent instance where you had to quickly obtain information or solve a problem that required getting information from several colleagues. Describe this incident and how you communicated with your colleagues. How do you decide whom to include or ask? What are preferred communication media within the company? Which communication technologies are best for obtaining expert information? Other types of information? Which of these technologies allow you to be more productive? Do you use different technologies for obtaining information from outside your work group/unit? How often do you use these technologies? Which do you use most often? Is that similar to what other team members do? Describe the technology support provided by your company. Describe an incident when technology helped you successfully in your job. Describe an incident when technology failed in supporting you in your job.

Team Outcomes. How well do you think [type of] teams work together? What factors would you say are most important to success? What factors present the most challenge? What do you feel are the biggest challenges and problems faced by individuals like you who work given your distributed work environment? In a team?
