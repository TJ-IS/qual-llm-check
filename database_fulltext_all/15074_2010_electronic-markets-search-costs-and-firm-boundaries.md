# Electronic Markets, Search Costs, and Firm Boundaries

Ramesh Sankaranarayanan

School of Business, University of Connecticut, Storrs, Connecticut 06269, rsankaran@business.uconn.edu

Arun Sundararajan

Leonard N. Stern School of Business, Kaufman Management Center, New York University, New York, New York 10012, asundara@stern.nyu.edu

W<sup>e</sup> <sup>study</sup> <sup>how</sup> <sup>interorganizational</sup> <sup>systems</sup> <sup>(IOS)</sup> <sup>such</sup> <sup>as</sup> <sup>electronic</sup> <sup>markets</sup> <sup>and</sup> <sup>other</sup> <sup>enabling</sup> <sup>information</sup> technologies that facilitate broader interfirm transactions affect the extent of outsourcing in firms. We do so by modeling firms in a three-tier value chain consisting of buyers, intermediaries, and suppliers, who can interact using IOS that lower the procurement search costs associated with finding appropriate trading partners. In the context of complex business-to-business (B2B) search, we study how decreasing search costs affect a firm’s decision to insource or outsource the procurement function, depending on whether the search process is information intensive or communication intensive. Variation in search costs changes the transaction costs of interaction between firms, as well as the contracting costs associated with outsourcing, owing to changes in the costs of moral hazard for delegated search. We study these effects in a new model that integrates search theory into the principal-agent framework, and establish that the optimal outsourcing contract has a simple “al or nothing” performance-based structure under fairly general assumptions. Our model predicts that when B2B search is information intensive, IOS will facilitate an increase in outsourcing, market-based transactions, and a reduction in the vertical scope of extended enterprises. In contrast, when B2B search is primarily communication intensive, IOS will lead to tighter integration and an increase in the vertical scope of the extended enterprise. Our research suggests that the nature of the information technologies and of the business activities supported by IOS are crucial determinants of the organizational and industry changes they induce, and our results have important implications for a variety of industries in which both technological and agency issues will influence the eventual success of global IT-facilitated extended enterprise initiatives.

Key words: outsourcing; electronic commerce; interorganizational information systems; electronic markets; economics of IS; information systems and organizational change; IT impacts on industry and market structure; IT-enabled supply chains; IT and new organizational forms; search costs; moral hazard; agency theory; analytical modeling

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade, interorganizational systems (IOS) that facilitate electronic procurement (e-procurement) and other marketplace services associated with electronic markets have matured and are facilitating an array of new interfirm transactions and enterprise forms. These changes raise new variants of classic questions pertaining to organization design. The IOS enable the rise of efficient intermediaries, to whom firms can outsource their procurement processes. However, because the underlying technologies are available to the firms themselves, the firms could insource the procurement process to a greater extent as well.

In this paper, we study how IOS associated with electronic markets affect procurement-related search costs and agency costs. We examine the consequences for a firm’s decision to outsource (or insource) its procurement processes, and its implications for the scope of the firm. We also capture how changes in these transaction costs may ripple down the value chain, affecting the price dispersion of industrial intermediate goods.

Our work is anchored in a rich and varied literature in information systems on several important themes, such as the role of IT and IOS in firms (Brynjolfsson et al. 1994, Sambamurthy et al. 2003), the role of electronic commerce in decreasing search costs (Bakos 1997), and agency costs. Gurbaxani and Whang (1991) have recognized early-on the broad interplay between interorganizational systems, transaction costs, and firm size. Prior researchers have advanced a view of electronic markets as technology-enabled governance mechanisms (Malone et al. 1987) or as coordination mechanisms (Malone and Crowston 1994). These papers point to the need for building a rigorous theoretical understanding of the interplay of information technology, transaction costs, and firm scope and size, and our work addresses this need. Brynjolfsson et al. (1994) have found evidence that IT investments are empirically associated with declining firm size. Hitt (1999) finds that increased use of IT is associated with significantly reduced vertical integration. Our paper analyzes a specific type of IT investment—interorganizational systems—and examines whether there is any theoretical basis to expect a change in the scope of a firm. Prior work has shown how decreasing search costs could lead to lower prices (Bakos 1997), or in dynamic environments could lead to higher prices (Campbell et al. 2005). In our model, decreasing search costs lead to lower prices, but in this context we focus on firms’ decision to insource or outsource procurement. Bakos (1997) has argued convincingly that electronic markets decrease search costs, but focuses on the implications for market participants’ incentives to invest in electronic markets, and on the allocational efficiencies resulting from reduced search costs. Unlike Bakos (1997), who focuses on consumer search, we focus on industrial search. Unlike consumer search, where consumers search for themselves, industrial search is more complex and increasingly involves the use of search intermediaries. This raises agency issues, and we study one such agency issue: moral hazard arising from delegated search.

In our work, we examine how IOS-enabled decreasing search costs can affect firm scope by changing a firm’s propensity to outsource its procurement function. In our model we integrate search theory (Stahl 1989, 1996) with principal-agent theory (Holmstrom 1979), which to our knowledge has not been done before. We model delegated search by an agent (“intermediary,” here a procurement service provider) on behalf of a principal (“firm”), in the context of decreasing search costs for both firms and intermediaries. Because of advances in e-commerce technologies and IOS, decreases in search costs could be (a) proportionate, if search is more communication intensive, or (b) block decreases, if search is more information intensive. We find that when search costs decrease by the same proportion (i.e., proportionate decrease) for firms and intermediaries, firms prefer to insource the procurement function more (resulting in an increase in firm scope). When search costs decrease by the same amount (i.e., block decrease) for firms and intermediaries, firms prefer to outsource the procurement function more (resulting in a decrease in firm scope).

Consider the following example. ICG Commerce, a growing procurement services provider, was hired by a pharmaceutical company that wanted to procure and install 49,000 PCs throughout its organization worldwide. The requirements included associated service and leasing agreements, disposal of old PCs, and loading software onto each PC. The pharmaceutical company could have carried out the procurement using its in-house team, but outsourced the procurement to ICG Commerce. On behalf of the company, ICG used e-sourcing tools to identify and evaluate an initial set of suppliers for their quality of PCs, ability to coordinate worldwide delivery as per schedule, and service capabilities. After shortlisting suppliers, ICG used online reverse auctions to manage final price negotiations. To ensure that suppliers’ quotes in the auctions were comparable, ICG standardized specifications and requirements. The company realized savings of \$16.9 million (17.3%) over the term of the leases (ICG Commerce website).

In the scenario above, the intuitive conclusion might be that the pharmaceutical company should retain its procurement in-house, and actually lower its usage of procurement intermediaries like ICG, because information technologies associated with electronic markets reduce search costs for the pharmaceutical company, especially for products like PCs whose specifications seem easy to codify. The availability of reverse auction technologies accentuates this lowering of search costs. However, the observed outcome was one in which the firm outsourced, and reduced its organizational scope instead. This is not an isolated example—a recent survey of senior executives by Accenture found that 9% of companies surveyed used procurement service providers (PSPs) rather than in-house procurement divisions for direct materials and services, whereas 20% of them use PSPs for indirect materials and services. In a 2005 survey by A. T. Kearney and CAPS Research, Monczka et al. (2005, Figure 4.5) found that between 9% and 20% of companies outsource procurement activities related to maintenance, repair and operating supplies (MRO) and 11% to 17% of them outsource procurement activities related to indirect materials, whereas 4% to 9% of companies outsource procurement related to direct materials. Thus, with decreasing search costs associated with the spread of electronic procurement technologies, firms seem to be using search intermediaries to a greater extent in some situations. This is especially true for indirect/MRO inputs rather than direct inputs.

The answer to this puzzle lies in three observations. The first is that B2B search is complex and nuanced, involving both the passive acquisition of information and iterative communication. Second, the nature of this search—that is, the mix of passive information acquisition and iterative communication—determines the extent to which information technology alters the relative search costs of firms and their potential intermediaries. Third, a change in search costs can also alter the agency costs associated with hidden action (moral hazard) that characterizes transacting with any intermediary.<sup>1</sup>

B2B search is complex. Unlike consumer search, the activities associated with B2B procurement of intermediate industrial goods are numerous, often involving the acquisition of a substantial amount of complex product information, and the need for iterative communication to discover true price and product/delivery specifications. There are fixed costs associated with engaging in such search, relating to defining processes for identifying suppliers, and criteria for supplier evaluation and selection, which often require collaborative decision making between the divisions of an organization using the inputs.

B2B search can therefore be mapped on two dimensions: information-intensive search and communication-intensive search: (a) information-intensive search involves collecting passive published information sources providing generic information (of common interest to all buyers), such as catalogs, trade magazines, and prior records; and (b) communicationintensive search involves collecting buyer-specific information that requires active communication with colleagues, salespersons, previous customers provided as references by a supplier, consultants, and so on.<sup>2</sup>

The search costs related to the example above include defining evaluation criteria, locating and selecting the initial shortlist of suppliers, preparing requests for proposals (RFPs) and sending them out to suppliers, evaluating the RFPs returned by suppliers, which involves understanding exactly what PC specifications and terms for installation/ maintenance/disposal each supplier is offering, comparing these offers (often via internal discussions), and so on. Some of the variable information acquisition costs (costs that are a function of the number of suppliers) are via passive information acquisition, and others require iterative communication, although for a standardized product like a PC, the acquisition and processing of information is more likely to be information intensive. Other costs are independent of the number of suppliers (preparing supplier evaluation criteria through a collaborative process) and are more analogous to fixed costs.<sup>3</sup>

Deploying a specific technology could decrease search costs, both for a firm and potential intermediaries, in two basic ways (or a combination thereof): as a proportionate decrease, or as a block decrease.

(a) Proportionate decreases: Some information technologies decrease search costs by lowering the cost (or time taken) to perform a specific task in the search process. One such task is communication. When using secure e-mail instead of a courier service, the cost per iteration of communication goes down. An experienced intermediary who requires fewer communication cycles with suppliers (compared to a firm with less prior experience) will thus see their search cost decrease by a smaller total amount, although the decrease as a proportion of the original search cost is likely to be the same for the buyer and intermediary. In our example, if the pharmaceutical company needs to determine each potential PC supplier’s ability to deliver to its worldwide locations, it would have to communicate with each supplier iteratively to find this out—the buyer asks some questions, to which the supplier responds, based on which the buyer asks more questions, and so on. Suppose it takes the buyer (pharmaceutical company) on average five iterations per supplier, but it takes the intermediary only three iterations, on account of the intermediary’s superior process efficiency and experience. It is much faster and less expensive to carry out each iteration of this communication by e-mail or fax than by sending documents via courier services. Thus, the cost per iteration decreases by the same amount for the buyer and intermediary, but the number of iterations of communication itself is not likely to change. There may be some communication associated with the search in our example—to elicit buyer-specific details from potential suppliers about capabilities at its installation locations and maintenance terms, for example—but this is relatively minimal.

(b) Block decreases: Some technologies eliminate specific tasks in the search process. In the context of search, one such task is browsing through catalogs and other data sources. When catalogs move online, browsing can be replaced by automated filtered search. For example, to shortlist potential PC suppliers and products from catalogs, a paper catalog might require extensive browsing, wherein someone must go through an entire catalog to identify desirable suppliers and products. However, an online catalog eliminates the need for browsing by letting users specify supplier and product criteria to a filter and retrieve only relevant information. Whether a buyer or intermediary uses such an online catalog, the search cost decreases by the same block amount for both. Because the PC is a fairly standardized product, information acquisition and processing are likely to be mostly information intensive (it is possible to get PC product specifications via online catalogs, and read reviews and opinions in trade magazines). Thus, the availability of enabling information technologies lowers the buyer and intermediary’s search costs of obtaining passive published information by a shared (block) amount. The intermediary has a lower search cost than the buyer to begin with, and her cost advantage is preserved even after the availability of Internet based technologies. This lowering of search costs across the board also puts a downward pressure on suppliers’ prices for PCs and their price dispersion. In such situations, where there is a block reduction in search costs, our model finds (ceteris paribus) that there will be an increase in outsourcing to intermediaries to perform the search, as was the case in our example.

This is because our model also accounts for the agency costs associated with moral hazard, the third observation we highlighted earlier. A buyer who hires an intermediary to carry out procurement search is unaware of how thoroughly the intermediary searches on its behalf. If the intermediary puts greater effort into the search, the buyer benefits from lower prices, but the intermediary incurs a greater cost. Incentives are generally aligned (although imperfectly) using contracts that typically involve performancebased service-level agreements (SLAs). The net reduction in search costs also reduces these related agency costs, thus resulting in a net additional benefit from outsourcing.

## 2. Model

In the model, there is a set of firms (buyers), each of whom has a unit demand for an industrial input (henceforth called the raw material) for use in its business. The raw material is supplied by a set of m competing suppliers. Each supplier chooses a (potentially different) price per unit of raw material. We model the decision process of a single buyer. Because of potential price dispersion, the buyer is forced to engage in costly search, determining each potential supplier’s price until she discovers an acceptable price for the raw material. To keep things simple, we think of this as a quality adjusted price, which takes into account different delivery terms, quality differences, and so on, and we can thus easily define a “lowest” hedonic price.

As is customary in the search literature, we assume that a small fraction $\mu$ of potential customers for the raw material are “shoppers” who have a zero search cost and so will always search exhaustively for the lowest price before buying. The other fraction $1 - \mu$ of potential customers consists of the set of all buyers, each having a positive search cost $c _ { P } .$ The presence of shoppers simply ensures a smooth distribution of raw material prices, and they play no further substantive role in the model (refer to Figure 1).

A buyer decides between procuring the raw material using its in-house procurement team (insourcing) and outsourcing the procurement of the raw material to an intermediary. If the buyer insources procurement, she searches for the best-priced raw material from among the suppliers. The costs involved in insourcing therefore include (i) search costs of evaluating each potential supplier, at the rate of $c _ { P }$ per supplier evaluated, and (ii) direct costs of buying the raw material at price p.

The buyer may outsource procurement to a specialist intermediary who, by virtue of greater process efficiency, has a lower search cost $c _ { A }$ . The buyer benefits from the intermediary’s lower search cost, but incurs hidden-action moral hazard costs when hiring the intermediary: The buyer can observe the raw material price $p$ obtained by the intermediary (we assume that the price $p$ is auditable, so that the intermediary cannot misrepresent this price), but cannot observe the number of candidate suppliers that the intermediary samples before buying the raw material. To induce the intermediary to search optimally, the buyer offers the intermediary a contract $[ p + w ( p ) ]$ , that reimburses the intermediary for the price $p$ of the raw material, and additionally specifies a performance-based incentive payment $w ( p )$ , which can be any function nonincreasing in $p .$ The buyer’s optimal contract must be designed to ensure participation from the intermediary (individual rationality), and to induce the intermediary to search optimally (incentive compatibility).

The intermediary is assumed to be risk averse, with preferences represented by the utility function u	x , where x is the intermediary’s payoff $w ( p )$ . u	x is twice differentiable, strictly increasing, and strictly concave. Denoting the first and second derivatives of the utility function by $u _ { 1 } ( x )$ and $u _ { 1 1 } ( x )$ , we have $u _ { 1 } ( x ) > 0 , u _ { 1 1 } ( x ) < 0 .$ . Moreover, the utility from payoff x is never greater than x, i.e., $u _ { 1 } ( x ) \leq 1$ . Furthermore, $u ( 0 ) = 0$ . The outside opportunity of the intermediary is denoted a constant $\underline { { u } } \geq 0$ . ## 3.1. Insourcing

We first consider the buyer’s cost of insourcing, where the buyer procures the raw material directly from a supplier. Each supplier chooses (as indicated above in Equations (1) and (4)) a symmetric mixed-strategy pricing strategy $f ( p )$ with support $p \in \left[ r _ { 0 } , \bar { p } \right] .$ , and corresponding distribution function $F ( p )$ . The following lemma, based on Stahl (1989), establishes that the optimal search strategy for the buyer is to search until a price below a cutoff price $r _ { P }$ is found, or all suppliers have been exhaustively sampled.

Lemma 1. The buyer’s optimal search strategy for the raw material is to search until she obtains a price less than or equal to a cutoff price $r _ { P } ,$ and if this cutoff is not achieved, to sample all suppliers. The cutoff price $r _ { P }$ depends on the buyer’s search cost $c _ { P }$ as follows:

$$
c _ {P} = \int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {P}} F (p) d p.\tag{5}
$$

Given Lemma 1, the equilibrium pricing strategy of the suppliers should place zero probability on any value of $p > r _ { P }$ because no buyer (or trader) will purchase from a supplier whose price is higher than $r _ { P }$ In other words, in the suppliers’ equilibrium strategy, no supplier should price higher than $\bar { p } = r _ { P }$ . This in turn implies that the first supplier encountered by the buyer will offer a price less than the buyer’s cutoff price $r _ { P } ,$ and the buyer does not search for a better price.

Given this pricing strategy, the ex ante expected cost to the buyer from insourcing (denoted by $K ^ { \mathrm { I } }$ where superscript I stands for “Insourcing”) is simply

$$
K ^ {\mathrm{I}} = \int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {P}} p f (p) d p\tag{6}
$$

because the first supplier sampled by the buyer is bound to have a price below the cutoff price $r _ { P } ,$ and the first search is costless for the buyer. Integrating (6) by parts and using (5) yields $K ^ { \mathrm { I } } = ( r _ { P } - c _ { P } )$ , which, using result (3) above, simplifies to

$$
K ^ {\mathrm{I}} = c _ {p} \bigg (\frac {((1 - \mu) / (2 \mu)) \log ((1 + \mu) / (1 - \mu))}{[ 1 - ((1 - \mu) / (2 \mu)) \log ((1 + \mu) / (1 - \mu)) ]} \bigg).\tag{7}
$$

## 3.2. The Costs of Outsourcing

We now consider the buyer’s decision to outsource procurement. The buyer’s problem is to design the cost-minimizing contract $p + w ( p )$ , while inducing incentive-compatible search on the part of the intermediary, and ensuring the intermediary’s participation. Once the contract $p + w ( p )$ is specified, the intermediary will sample suppliers until the expected gain from an additional search is less than the expected increase in expected utility. Therefore, given any contract in which $w ( p )$ is nonincreasing in $p ,$ there will be a cutoff price at which the intermediary stops searching. We formulate the buyer’s problem for each such arbitrary cutoff price $r _ { A }$ at which the buyer may wish to induce the intermediary to stop searching (we will subsequently model the consequences of choosing the optimal $r _ { A } ^ { * } )$ . Denote this contract for an arbitrary $r _ { A }$ as $p + w ( p , r _ { A } )$ . Given any such $r _ { A } ,$ the buyer must design $w ( p , r _ { A } )$ so that $r _ { A }$ is indeed the cutoff price at which the intermediary stops searching, which leads to the following incentive-compatibility constraint (see Online Appendix B, §7.2 for derivation of this constraint)<sup>6</sup>:

$$
[ \mathrm{IC} ]: \int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {A}} [ u (w (p, r _ {A})) - u (w (r _ {A}, r _ {A})) ] f (p) d p \geq c _ {A}.\tag{8}
$$

Equation (8) ensures that the buyer structures the incentive pay $w ( \cdot , r _ { A } )$ so that if the intermediary first discovers a price $p \geq r _ { A } ^ { * }$ , thereby realizing a payment of at most $w ( r _ { A } , r _ { A } )$ , the expected incremental payoff to the intermediary from a second search, which is $[ u ( w ( p , r _ { A } ) ) - u ( w ( r _ { A } , r _ { A } ) ) ]$ when the second-search price is $p ,$ measured by $f ( p )$ over the range $[ r _ { 0 } , r _ { A } ] ,$ is no less than the search cost incurred by the intermediary for the second search. This makes the choice of doing the second search incentive compatible: That is, the intermediary searches again if her first search resulted in her getting a raw material price $p \geq r _ { A }$ , and not otherwise.

The contract must also ensure the intermediary’s participation. The formulation of this participation constraint, and of the buyer’s objective function, are constructed by assigning the appropriate probability to each possible outcome for a cutoff price of $r _ { A }$ —the details of this are available in Online Appendix $\scriptstyle \mathrm { \mathrm { B } , }$ §7.1. The structure of the contract derived in Proposition 1 is illustrated in Figure 3. Despite the fact that the outcome set (price of the raw material obtained) is continuous, the nature of the intermediary’s task (search) naturally lends itself to a partition of this space into favorable and unfavorable outcomes. This is because even when the payoffs are continuous, the decision rule for whether to search again is based on a deterministic cutoff price. Proposition 1 shows that this induces the buyer to structure a contract that explicitly makes the payoff from the task of the intermediary discrete. Additionally, the intermediary (agent) can take one of two discrete actions (search once, twice), rather than having a continuous action space (as is the case in moral hazard models in which the agent chooses effort). This provides some intuition for why the optimal contract is discontinuous and flat, rather than varying smoothly with observed outcome $p .$ If the number of suppliers is $m > 2 ,$ , then the set of possible actions for the buyer is one of m such actions. ## 3.3. The Nature of the Insourcing-Outsourcing Trade-Off

The decision to outsource is monotonic in the intermediary’s search cost $c _ { A }$ in the following sense—there is generally a continuous lower range of value of $c _ { A }$ over which the buyer outsources, and a continuous higher range of values of $c _ { A }$ over which the buyer insources.

Proposition 2. For any buyer search cost $c _ { P } > 0 ,$ , the following results hold:

(a) $I f c _ { A } = c _ { P } ,$ then the buyer always prefers insourcing to outsourcing.

(b) The buyer’s costs from outsourcing increase monotonically with $c _ { A }$

Therefore, if there exists a threshold value $c \in ( 0 , c _ { P } )$ at which the buyer is indifferent between outsourcing and insourcing, then the buyer outsources when $c _ { A } < c _ { , }$ , and insources when $c _ { A } > c .$

Figure 5 illustrates the results from Proposition 2. At each high-enough value of $c _ { P } ,$ , there is a corresponding value c of $c _ { A }$ at which the buyer is indifferent between outsourcing and insourcing. We term this locus of all such points $( c _ { P } , c _ { A } )$ such that the buyer is indifferent between outsourcing and insourcing as the “Locus of Indifference.” At each fixed value of $c _ { P } ,$ the buyer outsources for $c _ { A } < c ,$ and insources for $c _ { A } > c$ . The locus of indifference is increasing and convex for case (a); and is increasing with constant slope for case (b); in either case its slope is always less than one (these properties of the locus of indifference are established in the proof of Lemma 2 in Online Appendix B, §7.5). The locus of indifference also lies entirely below the $4 5 ^ { \circ }$ line, consistent with part (a) of the proposition.

## 3.4. Decreasing Information-Intensive Search Costs

We now focus on our main question: how the relative search-cost changes associated with the IT deployments in B2B marketplaces change the incentives of buyer firms to outsource procurement. Recall that nonpersonal information sources provide general information that all potential buyers are likely to find useful, and search involving such information is termed information intensive. Many marketplace technologies substantially lower the cost of acquiring and evaluating this kind of information. Examples include online product catalogs, Internet-based supplier portals, and product/price comparison engines. The deployment of such IT systems are likely to reduce the procurement search costs of all market participants by a constant amount.

Our next proposition relates this kind of change in direct procurement search costs across the value chain to an increase in the likelihood of outsourcing.

Proposition 3. When search is information-intensive, B2B information technologies cause the procurement search costs of both the buyer and intermediary to reduce by the same constant amount —that is, from their original values $c _ { P } , \ c _ { A }$ to new values $( c _ { P } - \tau ) , ( c _ { A } - \tau )$ . This kind of constant or block change results in an increase in outsourcing and a more loosely coupled extended enterprise.

Thus, if search is information intensive, the deployment of IT that reduces search costs will result in lower organizational scope, and a more loosely coupled extended enterprise (see Figure 6(a)). Some intuition for this proposition can be obtained by considering an extreme case, when $\tau = c _ { A }$ . This represents a reduction in search costs that takes the intermediary’s search costs to zero. Because $c _ { P } > c _ { A } .$ , the buyer still has a positive search cost after the reduction, and consequently, finds it less costly to outsource.

A constant reduction in search costs is usually observed in highly standardized procurement processes such as straight rebuy situations, which require the evaluation of relatively few vendor and product attributes, using impersonal sources of information, and involve fewer decision makers.

## 3.5. Decreasing Communication-Intensive Search Costs

As discussed in §1, our analysis of the industrial procurement process indicates that certain kinds of information acquisition often involve a significant amount of iterative communication: between sales people and vendors, and between the several departments<sup>7</sup> within the buyer firm who are involved in the evaluation of a supplier proposal. The interorganizational systems in electronic markets reduce this cost of iterative communication significantly. These information systems make each iteration of communication less expensive, faster, less error prone, and richer. This decreases the number of iterations required, probably by some fixed fraction, and also decreases the cost of each iteration, and therefore results in a proportionate reduction in both $c _ { P }$ and $c _ { A }$

Proposition 4. When search is communication intensive, B2B information technologies cause the search costs of both the buyer and intermediary to reduce by the same constant proportion k < 1—that $i s ,$ from their original values $c _ { P } , ~ c _ { A }$ to new values $k c _ { P } , k c _ { A }$ . This kind of proportionate change results in a reduction in outsourcing and a more tightly coupled extended enterprise.

## 3.6. Shared Processes and Process Efficiency Advantages

Our next section examines two additional cases beyond those of Propositions 3 and 4. Sometimes, a precursor (or parallel activity) to the adoption of an electronic market may be the implementation of a common technology platform that spans many business processes, the associated reengineering of these processes (Sanders et al. 2001), and the adoption of best-practice shared processes for functions like procurement across firms in the marketplace. If search cost advantage is embedded in a superior procurement process, process reengineering could result in the convergence of the buyer and intermediary’s search cost. In that case, it follows from Proposition 2 that an electronic market that causes the search costs of both the buyer and the intermediary to converge to a common value results in a decrease in outsourcing.

The intuition for this result is straightforward: If the rationale for outsourcing is to take advantage of the intermediary’s efficiency in the procurement process, and the electronic market leads to a negation of this efficiency advantage, this negates the basis for outsourcing in the first place.

## 4. Discussion and Conclusions

Increasingly, advances in interorganizational systems such as B2B electronic markets and other enabling technologies such as auctions, catalog software, eRFQ and eRFP software, etc., are providing firms with a broader array of options with regard to the procurement function. Because these technologies are generally available to all firms in an industry, they can help firms achieve greater efficiencies and lower search costs in their procurement function. However, expert intermediaries also have access to these technologies, and may be able to provide a better value proposition to firms looking to outsource procurement.

Our paper studies this trade-off with a model that integrates search theory with agency theory. We analyze the costs of bilateral contracting between the buyer and intermediary. In this framework, we formulate and compare the buyer’s cost of insourcing procurement of a raw material and outsourcing procurement to the intermediary. Our results show that the resulting changes in the governance of procurement depend critically on the nature of the procurement search process, and related capabilities of the enabling information technologies.

Often, procurement-enabling technologies decrease search costs by (i) reducing the cost of acquiring generic published information, and (ii) facilitating faster and less costly communication of specific information. In light of our results, the use of information technology in predominantly information-intensive versus communication-intensive search can have opposing effects on the extent of outsourcing. Returning to our introductory example, for MRO and indirect materials, with a lower dependence on the firms’ product design and other supplier relationships, search tends to be more information-intensive and standardized, whereas for direct materials, search tends to be more communication intensive and less standardized. Procurement outsourcing has been adopted with a faster pace for MRO and indirect materials than for direct materials, which is consistent with our model’s predictions.

A natural direction for future research would be toward confirming this direction of change empirically. This would involve a structured analysis and collection of data relating to the effects of marketplace technologies on procurement search costs, although our model provides the necessary theoretical basis and testable predictions for a first study, such data is likely to have immense value in studying other questions of importance to IS research as well.

There are several other contexts featuring both costly search, contracting under asymmetric information, and the delegation of costly search to an intermediary. Our model provides a first explanation for why these contracts are often “all or nothing.” Our context is therefore part of a larger class of problems that investigate how differing search costs, and correspondingly, different search behavior across agents, can affect the decision-making process of each of these agents (Reinganum 1982). It would be interesting to extend the model developed in our paper to inform this question further.

Our model has a number of limiting assumptions, which could be relaxed and explored in further research. For example, we assume a unit demand for each buyer, which could be relaxed to the case when buyers have a downward-sloping demand curve with varying price elasticities. We model the case of two suppliers, but in real-world markets, there are multiple suppliers. In our model, buyers and intermediaries are homogeneous in their search costs. Considering heterogeneous search costs could be a fruitful direction for further research.

To summarize, we have studied how IOS such as electronic B2B markets, and other procurementenabling technologies such as online auctions, electronic catalogs, eRFP systems, etc., affect the desirability of outsourcing procurement, and could thereby impact the scope of the extended enterprise. We highlight the complexity of B2B search processes, and model three key effects that declining search costs induce: a change in the transaction costs of all firms; a change in the contracting costs associated with outsourcing procurement; and a change in the price dispersion of input commodities. Integrating the nature of B2B search with the nature of procurement technologies used, our model predicts that when search is information intensive, procurement technologies will facilitate an increase in procurement outsourcing and a reduction in organizational scope, whereas when it is primarily communication intensive, procurement technologies will lead to a decrease in procurement outsourcing.

r _ {A} ^ {*} F (r _ {A} ^ {*}) - \int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {A} ^ {*}} p f (p) d p = \int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {A} ^ {*}} F (p) d p.\tag{29}

$$
\begin{array}{l} \int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {A} ^ {*}} F (p) d p \\ = \left(\int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {A} ^ {*}} w ^ {\mathrm{H}} (r _ {A} ^ {*}) f (p) d p + \int_ {r _ {A} ^ {*}} ^ {r _ {P}} w ^ {\mathrm{L}} (r _ {A} ^ {*}) f (p) d p\right) - w ^ {\mathrm{L}} (r _ {A} ^ {*}), \end{array}\tag{30}
$$

which implies that

$$
[ w ^ {\mathrm{H}} (r _ {A} ^ {*}) - w ^ {\mathrm{L}} (r _ {A} ^ {*}) ] = \frac {1}{[ F (r _ {A} ^ {*}) ]} \int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {A} ^ {*}} F (p) d p.\tag{31}
$$

Substituting the expressions for $w _ { 1 } ^ { \mathrm { H } } ( r _ { A } ^ { * } )$ from $( 2 8 ) , r _ { A } ^ { * } F ( r _ { A } ^ { * } ) -$ $\begin{array} { r } { \int _ { r _ { 0 } } ^ { r _ { A } ^ { * } } p f ( p ) \ d p } \end{array}$ dp from (29), and $[ w ^ { \mathrm { H } } ( r _ { A } ^ { * } ) - w ^ { \mathrm { L } } ( r _ { A } ^ { * } ) ]$ from (31) into (25) and rearranging terms yields the simplified firstorder condition

$$
\frac {[ 2 - F (r _ {A} ^ {*}) ]}{[ F (r _ {A} ^ {*}) ]} \int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {A} ^ {*}} F (p) d p = \frac {[ 2 - F (r _ {A} ^ {*}) ]}{[ F (r _ {A} ^ {*}) ]} \frac {c _ {A}}{u _ {1} (w ^ {\mathrm{H}} (r _ {A} ^ {*}))},\tag{32}

which in turn implies that

$$
c _ {A} = u _ {1} (w ^ {\mathrm{H}} (r _ {A} ^ {*})) \int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {A} ^ {*}} F (p) d p.\tag{33}
$$

\frac {d r _ {A} ^ {*}}{d c _ {A}} = \frac {1}{u _ {1 1} (w ^ {\mathrm{H}} (r _ {A} ^ {*})) w _ {1} ^ {\mathrm{H}} (r _ {A} ^ {*}) \int_ {r _ {0}} ^ {r _ {A} ^ {*}} F (p) d p + u _ {1} (w ^ {\mathrm{H}} (r _ {A} ^ {*})) F (r _ {A})}.\tag{34}

The proofs of Propositions 3 and 4 use the following lemma, which is proved in the extended online appendix.

Lemma 2. Denote the set of values $( c _ { P } , c _ { A } )$ for which the buyer is indifferent between outsourcing and insourcing as the “locus of indifference.” If one expresses this locus as a function $c _ { A }$ of $c _ { P } ,$ then this function is strictly convex, or $d ^ { 2 } c _ { A } / \dot { d } c _ { P } ^ { 2 } > 0$

Proof of Proposition 3. Assume the converse. Therefore, there exists $\tau > 0$ such that $( c _ { P } ^ { * } , c _ { A } ^ { * } )$ lies on the locus, whereas $( c _ { P } ^ { * } - \tau , c _ { A } ^ { * } - \tau )$ lies above the locus. This implies that $d c _ { A } / d c _ { P } > 1$ at $( c _ { P } ^ { * } , c _ { A } ^ { * } )$ , and from Lemma 2, this implies that $d c _ { A } / d c _ { P } > 1$ for each $c _ { P } > c _ { P } ^ { * }$ , which in turn means that at some point, $c _ { P } = c _ { A }$ on the locus, contradicting Proposition 2(a). The result follows. <sup></sup>

Proof of Proposition 4. Lemma 2 implies that a straight line from the origin to $( c _ { A } , c _ { P } )$ on the locus intersects the locus at exactly one point, this one, and because the locus is convex, the result follows. <sup></sup>
