# Digital product innovation within four classes of innovation networks

Kalle Lyytinen,\* Youngjin Yoo<sup>†</sup> & Richard J. Boland Jr.\*

\*Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH USA, email: Kalle@case.edu, and <sup>†</sup>Temple University, Philadelphia, PA USA

Abstract. The increased digitization of organizational processes and products poses new challenges for understanding product innovation. It also opens new ho rizons for information systems research. We analyse how ongoing pervasive digiti zation of product innovation reshapes knowledge creation and sharing in innovation networks. We argue that advances in digital technologies (1) increase innovation network connectivity by reducing communication costs and increasing its reach and scope and (2) increase the speed and scope of digital convergence, which increases network knowledge heterogeneity and need for integration. These developments, in turn, stretch existing innovation networks by redistributing control and increasing the demand for knowledge coordination across time and space presenting novel challenges for knowledge creation, assimilation and integration. Based on this foundation, we distinguish four types of emerging innovation networks supported by digitalization: (1) project innovation networks; (2) clan innovation networks; (3) federated innovation networks; and (4) anarchic innovation networks. Each network involves different cognitive and social translations – or ways of identifying, sharing and assimilating knowledge. We describe the role of <sup>fi</sup>ve novel properties of digital infrastructures in supporting each type of innovation network: representational <sup>fl</sup>exibility, semantic coherence, temporal and spatial traceability, knowledge brokering and linguistic calibration. We identify several implications for future innovation research. In particular, we focus on the emergence of anarchic network forms that follow full-<sup>fl</sup>edged digital convergence founded on richer innovation ontologies and epistemologies calling to critically re-examine the nature and impact of modularization for innovation.

Keywords: digitization, innovation, networks, knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows, cognition, socia integration

## INTRODUCTION

‘Don’t reinvent the wheel’ is common wisdom for making best use of innovation efforts. But today, in order to capture maximum bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the transformative power of digitization, it be comes increasingly necessary to reinvent even the most basic elements of familiar products.

Consider, for example, the design of a City Car by the MIT Smart Cities Project (Mitchell, 2007) (Figure 1). It incorporates reinvented wheels, each containing their own motor, steering and suspension, integrated through an ‘intelligent’ <sup>fl</sup>y-by-wire software bus. With its decentralized power train, it can be folded and stacked to reduce storage space. Being more fully digitized, it can become an integrated part of an intelligent transportation service for a city. For example, the design includes intelligent mapping displays with contextual, location-based information that respond to different needs of particular types of travel, such as daily commuting, holiday sight seeing or running local errands, as well as particular types of drivers, such as young adults, the elderly or business travellers. The car would be obtained from ‘stacks’ located around the city for short-term use with a mobile payment device. The foldable car is also designed to be an actor in larger markets of energy and <sup>fi</sup>nance. It would buy electricity from the power grid and store it in its batteries for later use or sell it back as prices go up. The car is also designed to operate as a market agent in balancing the use of road space and parking capacity within the city, using pricing mechanisms and auctions to buy driving rights in different areas at different times. Furthermore, the City Car is an open design with its components available to third-party innovators. Its hybrid architecture echoes the design of the internet (Yoo et al., 2010), with intel ligence placed at the edges of a network.

The City Car is a striking example of how digitization in product innovation brings novel het erogeneous resources together, blurs industry boundaries and enables new socio-technical ecologies. The design of the City Car involved an intense pooling of diverse knowledge and physical resources inside and outside of MIT as experts from urban planning, architecture, industrial design, material science, electrical engineering, mechanical engineering, software engineering, energy policy, economics and management came together in ‘reinventing the wheel’ of the familiar car. Wakes of innovations were instigated when actors from many disciplines began revisiting their assumptions of a taken-for-granted product and its ecology, revealing the novel opportunities for creating value afforded by increased digitization. ## THE CHALLENGES OF DIGITIZATION

Increasing the level of digitization in our everyday socio-economic system involves representing, processing, storing and communicating the widest possible range of matter, energy and informa tion comprising our world, using strings of ones and zeroes (Yoo et al., 2010; Yoo 2012; Tilson et al., 2010). Digitization makes it possible to radically recon<sup>fi</sup>gure the design and production of nearly all industrial-age products – now called the Internet of Things (Ashton, 2009; European Commission, 2009). Advances in digital technologies decouple form from function and media from content creating opportunities for generating new products (Yoo et al., 2010; Tilson et al., 2010; Jönsson et al., 2009). With these decouplings, digitization also couples loosely previously unrelated products into new ones – like the <sup>fi</sup>tness monitoring services enabled by connecting Nike’s running shoes and Apple’s computing products (Yoo, 2010; 2012). Such radical recon<sup>fi</sup>gurations of familiar products are not limited to simple sports gear. Just as steam engines and assembly lines radically changed how organizations innovated in the early parts of the 19th century – when production shifted from craftwork to mass production – digital technologies now enable similar transformations in the art of invention and innovation.

Information system research has historically focused on the impacts of digital technologies in organizations, especially on their process and structural effects (Fulk and DeSanctis, 1995; Swanson, 1994; Zammuto et al., 2007); i.e. they have viewed digital technologies as an operand resource (Nambisan, 2013; Fichman et al., 2014).<sup>1</sup> Initially, these studies focused on the uses of information technology within the con<sup>fi</sup>nes of an organization’s internal or immediate interfacing processes – for example, they would ask, how do information technologies in<sup>fl</sup>uence the structure and behaviour of teams, organizational units or supply chains? Now, as we witness the emergence of a new breed of digitally fostered innovation related to products in all spheres of life (Yoo, 2010; Tilson et al., 2010), information systems (IS) scholars must ask how digital technologies in<sup>fl</sup>uence the structure and process of innovation itself – as an operant resource – in the context of product innovation (Nambisan, 2013; Yoo 2012). We de<sup>fi</sup>ne digita product innovations as signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly new (from the perspective of a particular community or market) products or services that are either embodied in information and communication technologies or enabled by them. Examples include new enterprise platforms (enterprise resource planning and customer relationship management), new consumer products (smartphones and Amazon’s Instant Video service) and existing products substantially en hanced by the addition of digital technology (e.g. digital infotainment systems in automobiles)

Indeed, the construction of robust digital infrastructures like the Internet of Things and globa wireless networks, continued miniaturization and cloud computing challenge us to rethink the scope of our theories and empirical analyses associated with product innovation (Nambisan, 2013). To wit, Zammuto et al. (2007) note that an organization’s ability to innovate quickly by combining digital components and services with traditional products will have profound implica tions for how we must think about organizing for innovation.

In this essay, we address one piece of this challenge and consider how the ‘new materiality afforded by digital technologies affects organizational and inter-organizational knowledgerelated capabilities to innovate with products. We will cover the discovery and development phases of product innovation and address questions like the following: What is the nature of the core IT functionality underpinning the digital product innovation? What complementary products and services are needed to <sup>fl</sup>esh out the ‘whole product solution’? What other elements (organizational or technical) comprise a sound innovation system incorporating the digital product? By exploring these questions, our overarching aim is to address a broad question facing IS researchers: what are the implications of pervasive digitization on how product innovation is likely to take place in the future and especially how it related to new forms of knowledge creation and integration?

## Networks of innovation

We adopt a network view of innovation and conceptualize both the processes and outcomes of product innovation as constellations of heterogeneous networks that embody different forms of knowledge. In this view, no innovation is borne out of a single idea of a single innovator, which gradually diffuses to a market after its invention. In contrast, we view innovations emerging from the ebb and <sup>fl</sup>ow of fragile, heterogeneous and dynamic knowledge in ‘trading zones’ (Galison, 1997) shared between heterogeneous actors and their tools in complex socio-technical networks; the <sup>fl</sup>ows in these networks involve a series of fragile and uncertain knowledge transla tions where ambiguous, con<sup>fl</sup>icting ideas, representations and material artefacts become assembled through the interaction of diverse actors into new knowledge combinations of resources.

Drawing on this view, we formulate a framework to distinguish four types of product innovation networks – each with alternative innovation dynamics, outcomes and technological capabi lities. We argue that these are distinct digitally enabled forms of organizing for innovation (see also Puranam & Alexy, 2014). We then use this framework to draw a series of implications for digitization of product innovation. We underline the socio-technical nature of innovation net works due to the critical role that digital technologies and cultural context play in shaping these networks and their innovation behaviours.

The four types of networks we will unpack are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant sources of digital product innovation (Hansen, 1999; Powell et al., 2005; Uzzi & Spiro, 2005), which rarely emerge from the linear development of a single idea into a new product. In contrast, digital product innovations grow ‘fractally’ through a non-linear process where socio-technical networks assemble ideas, technologies, tools, actors and know-how (Latour, 1987; Tuomi, 2002; Van De Ven et al., 1999). Consequently, over the last decade, innovation scholars have begun to move away from their initial ‘push’ and ‘pull’ models based on images of an innovation diffusing through an environment or responding to a market demand (Cooper & Zmud, 1990). Instead, they increasingly draw on images of ‘<sup>fl</sup>uids’ or ‘wakes’ to describe the dynamics of innovation networks (Tuomi, 2002; Van De Ven et al., 1999) to better capture the distributed, emergent nature of digital product innovation. Examples of such images abound in <sup>fi</sup>elds as diverse as biotechnology (Van De Ven et al., 1999); architecture, engineering and construction (AEC) (Boland et al., 2007); aerospace (Argyres, 1999); automobiles (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2013); and digital music (Tilson et al., 2013). These studies point out that digitally enabled product innovation emerges through webs of social and technical interactions, stemming from recombinations of earlier innovations and enabled by critical complementary resources and knowledge (Arthur, 2009). This results in innovation processes that cut across multiple organi zational and user community boundaries. These processes share characteristics of non-linear dynamic systems in that they are sensitive to path dependence and are ‘neither stable and predictable, nor stochastic and random’ (Van De Ven et al., 1999). These innovation processes result from cooperative and competitive connections in which varieties of actors and functions dynamically create an infrastructure that can facilitate or constrain innovation (Van De Ven et al., 1999).

Initial notions of distributed systems of innovation focused on social distribution (Van de Ven et al., 1999). Recently, several organizational innovation scholars have recognized the importance of the digitization of innovation processes themselves and the ways that increased com munication speed and lowered communication cost mitigate against the tyranny of ‘space’ (von Hippel, 2005a; Nambisan, 2013). Thus, a wide range of recent IS studies have focused on the ways that IT can enable product innovation by adding business value, shortening time to market, supporting emergent collaborations across time and space or providing new complementarities (Nambisan, 2013). Most of these studies, however, have focused on internal organizational processes and have a limited view of the role of digital technologies in product innovation.

A better understanding of how digital technologies can in<sup>fl</sup>uence product innovation and its processes requires us to adopt a richer conceptualization of digital technologies and their role in innovation networks (Yoo et al 2010: Tilson et al 2010: Kallinikos et al 2013: Fichmar et al., 2014; Nambisan, 2013). In the spirit of social shaping of technology, we recognize diverse and evolving roles and meanings that digital technologies can play in innovation practices (Bijker, 1995; Latour, 1987). We also acknowledge the messiness, ambiguity, multiplicity and volatility of digital product innovations as they evolve over time (Tuomi, 2002; Tilson et al., 2010; Yoo, 2010). The innovation networks for digital technologies are, therefore, likely to be dynamic, uncertain and equivocal, as well as subject to political and power in<sup>fl</sup>uences. Accordingly, scholars need to pay special attention to the social, communal and networked features of digital product innovation (von Hippel, 2005b) and examine questions such as whether increased network heterogeneity promotes product combinations, fosters learning or thwarts diffusion (Tuomi, 2002). To better understand the effects of heterogeneity, scholars need to attend more carefully to the types and forms of interactions mediated by digital technologies (Carlile, 2002; Yoo et al., 2012) and examine how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence network structures and processes (Obstfeld, 2005). Scholars must ask to what extent digital technologies in<sup>fl</sup>uence networks of innovation and how they shape the way innovation knowledge is created and shared across communities (Nambisan, 2013).

To summarize, recent IS innovation research has come a long way towards developing a more robust understanding of the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of digital technologies on product innovation. Yet continued increases in the speed and scope of pervasive digitization have created an increasingly dynamic and complex set of social processes in digital product innovation, which require continuing research efforts. This challenges IS innovation scholars to better theorize and empirically assess the transformative power of digital technologies on innovation networks. An important step in addressing this intellectual challenge is to bridge the gulf between scholars who study innovation within and through social networks and scholars who study digitally enabled innovation processes. For this purpose, we need richer vocabularies that can highlight the multifaceted role of digital technologies in creating new spaces for product innovation. We also need theoretical frameworks that explicitly recognize the role of social and technological heterogeneity in structuring different product innovation networks. To do so, we will <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss innovation processes as distributed innovation networks and the roles digital technologies play in shaping such networks.

## Digitization and innovation networks

We discuss two principal ways in which digitalization is shaping product innovation. They are (1) digital connectivity, which reduces communication cost and increases its speed, and (2) digital convergence, which results in increased knowledge heterogeneity and combinability.

## Digitization as operand resource for connectivity

Digital technologies as operand resources have historically expanded the reach of innovation processes by overcoming the ‘tyranny of space’, or the historically bounded, socio-technical limits for traversing space and reducing execution time. This is manifested in new organizational forms like virtual teams, open innovation forums or crowdsourcing. These forms can affect process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies or collaboration abilities or expertise organizational expertise or resource sourcing (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 2000; Majchrzak & Malhotra, 2013). These new organiza tional forms also affect the possibilities for process tracing, which, in turn, affect actor, process or task transparency (Puranam & Alexy, 2014). All of these potential effects result from improved digital connectivity across time and space with radically reduced cost.

A case in point is the rapid growth of open-source innovation (Puranam & Alexy, 2014) that has followed the phenomenal success of several open-source software projects like Linux and Apache (Crowston et al., 2012; Fitzgerald, 2006). These organizational forms show how thousands of voluntary contributors – in this case programmers – with emergent and minimum levels of hierarchical control can innovate and deliver reliable, robust and scalable products (von Hippel & von Krogh, 2003; Puranam & Alexy, 2014). The success of these open organiza tional forms is dependent on standardized knowledge-sharing practices and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient uses of suites of digital artefacts that offer access, connectivity and transparency. In this regard, the open digital development platforms consisting of software libraries, con<sup>fi</sup>guration and testing environments, communication platforms such as Twitter or IRC, and knowledge-sharing plat forms such as Stack Over<sup>fl</sup>ow, YouTube and so on offer continuous shared access to code and related representations (for a detailed analysis of their use, see e.g. Lindberg et al., 2013).

This increased connectivity helps maintain a sense of a community, negotiate proposed designs and requirements and record reasons for choosing speci<sup>fi</sup>c solutions (Stewart & Gosain, 2006; Scacchi, 2009). The extended forms of connectivity also allow global sourcing and coordination of knowledge and skills to deliver highly complex software products. The continued success of open forms of digital innovation is not limited to software products. Companies like General Electric and Proctor & Gamble have leveraged Internet-based innovation communities to <sup>fi</sup>nd new ideas and solutions (Chesbrough et al., 2006; von Hippel, 2005a). Several companies like IBM (innovation jams) or Lego (mindstorms) have developed strategic platforms to source or generate innovations by engaging in digitally supported crowdsourcing (Majchrzak & Malhotra, 2013). These processes are radical departures from conventiona top-down and sequential innovation methods and draw upon iterative, bottom-up patterns of collaboration for exploration and development of ideas and solutions.

## Digitization as operant resources for convergence

Today, a wide range of digital capabilities can be embedded as operant resources into products and thereby create novel functionalities (Yoo et al., 2010). This is an outcome of the extreme <sup>fl</sup>exibility of digitizing (Yoo et al., 2010; Kallinikos et al., 2013) and its generative ability (Zittrain, 2008), i.e. the capacity of a self-contained (digital) system to generate new outputs, structures or behaviours endogenously through the participation of uncoordinated third-party actors without deliberate planning from the originator of the system (Wikipedia, http://en.wikipedia. org/wiki/Generativity). Generativity results from combining information available about the product, product environment or the network to which the product is connected to stimulate the co-production of new ideas. Such progressive digitization is often referred to as digital convergence (Tilson et al., 2010) in which diverse information is transformed into a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed digita format that connects previously unrelated knowledge in ways that can be manipulated and analysed in changing the product features. The so-called quadruple play (combining broadband internet, phone, TV services and apps in mobile internet) has for example resulted in the convergence of media content, storage and distribution mechanisms and has generated signi<sup>fi</sup>cant digital product innovations such as smartphones with apps, YouTube or Net<sup>fl</sup>ix. The possibilities of digital convergence are, however, not limited to media products (Yoo et al., 2010; Henfridsson et al., 2014). As we observed with the example of the City Car, most products that used to be non-digital are now increasingly composed of digital components, which enable them to interact with other products through digital devices (e.g. your refrigerator talking to your phone) or to use the internet to monitor or service them (Jönsson et al., 2009) or to control external behaviours of other products or learn about and respond to the environment in which they operate. This has resulted in a ‘second wave’ of product innovation exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by the City Car or the Driverless Car (for more examples, see Brynjolfsson & McAfee 2014). Digital convergence also offers organizations new ways to differentiate the user experience associated with their products.

Digitization is, however, not just constrained to <sup>fi</sup>nal, operant resources – products. The activities that create innovative products – the operand resources – are also becoming increasingly digitized (Yoo et al., 2010; Henfridsson et al., 2014). For example, in the AEC industries, new three-dimensional (3D) digital tools have allowed designers to digitize not only the building drawings but also myriad other aspects of construction management such as site measures survey data, fabrication data, cost estimates, data about risk and time, important and unique construction knowledge and so on. Again, by connecting previously unconnected data in their network of design and construction activities, digital convergence has created a space for enacting new types of innovation in what had been a stable, traditional set of industries. Each time a new connection is made between previously unconnected knowledge communities, the actors are better able to bring their unique capabilities into new productive networks (Berente et al., 2007).

Overall, digital convergence results in the increased heterogeneity of knowledge resources and tools necessary to innovate. This rich set of resources can be a positive aspect of an inno vation network, but signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in business models, identities, intellectual property, business practices and technologies among actors can thwart the opportunities for innovation and pose challenges for the development of effective infrastructures for digital innovation.

## Interactions between the two forms of digitization

We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two dimensions of increasing digitization that will shape future product innovation networks:

1. Digitization as an operand resource spurs digital connectivity, which, ceteris paribus, reduces communication cost and increases its speed and reach. This ampli<sup>fi</sup>es the distribution of control and resources and enables coordination and collaboration among innovation participants.

2. Digitization as an operant resource spurs generativity through increasing digital convergence, thereby increasing knowledge and resource heterogeneity among innovation network participants.

The boundaries between operand and operant dimensions are not as clear-cut as we portray them here. However, it is important to keep these distinctions clear for analytic and theoretical reasons. First, these two forms of digitization have separate and distinct impacts on product innovation networks: the operant form affects the scope, reach and cost of communications and control in the existing types of network links. Its main in<sup>fl</sup>uence is in the overall topology of communication across space and time and the related dynamics of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows. To the contrary, the operant form stimulates new types of links between previously unconnected nodes and thereby changes the nature and content of relations in the innovation networks. The operant dimension of digitization introduces new types of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows and network structures and increases ambiguity in the type of knowledge that is likely to <sup>fl</sup>ow in the network. There is much to learn about how organizations can change the ways they manage product innovation by emphasizing one or the other dimension of digitizing.

In the past, digital product innovation research has focused on the cost, speed and associated forms of distributed control driven by operand dimension of digitization. These issues do not directly affect consumers (other than in lowered product cost or increased product develop ment cycles), because these innovations are related to the back-of<sup>fi</sup>ce functions, or the invisible parts of companies (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 2000). In contrast, digital product innovation driven by the operant dimension of digitization, with its increased heterogeneity of knowledge, changes the meaning of products and thereby directly affects the consumer experience (Verganti 2009). The operant dimension of digitization is the product innovation that rede<sup>fi</sup>nes products, changes business models or generates new business logics (Yoo et al., 2010). It is important for researchers to analyse how these two dimensions interact and create different types of innovation spaces and alternative classes of innovation networks.

## TRANSLATIONS IN DIGITAL INNOVATION NETWORKS

Innovating in any network involves translations – both cognitive and social processes of translations, in which cognitive and social elements emerge in the network that did not exist when the innovation process started. Innovation involves cognitive translations whenever actors capture knowledge with representations that are exchanged and translated by different actors within the network before materializing in a new product (Altshuller, 1984; Simon, 1996). Similarly, innovation involves social translations whenever obtaining, transforming and sharing innovation knowledge involves negotiation, sense-making and sense-giving, in which actors’ understandings of the meaning of products and their own identities and relationships are rede<sup>fi</sup>ned through interaction (Verganti, 2009; Gal et al., 2008). The intertwining and mutual dependence of cognitive and social translations is inevitable in any innovation processes. We argue that digital product innovation is likely to differ from earlier product innovations because of the complexities of managing new cognitive and social processes that emerge from their richer communications and increased knowledge heterogeneity. We will next examine the translations required by digital innovation networks in more detail.

## Cognitive translations

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a cognitive translation as a generative process whereby innovation knowledge is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, produced, re<sup>fi</sup>ned, integrated and evaluated partially through digital means in its movement towards an innovation ‘closure’ of being stabilized into a new product. Pervasive digitization of several representational technologies is germane to creating new innovation networks including CAD/CAM systems, product and project management platforms, customer feedback systems and crowdsourcing platforms (Majchrzak & Malhotra, 2013; Yoo et al., 2006). As digitization increases, the variety of cognitive translations within the networks also increases (Yoo et al., 2012). The participants are thereby likely to improve the range, accuracy, speed and scope of these translations (Carlile. 2002: Carlo et al., 2012a: Yoo et al., 2006) and must be open to novel ways of organizing and carrying out cognitive translations, such as automatic checking and updates or crowdsourcing (Argyres, 1999; Majchrzak & Malhotra, 2013; Yoo et al., 2006).

During cognitive translation, participants will use digital tools and digital representations to assist in creating and re<sup>fi</sup>ning innovation knowledge by mapping the knowledge into representations and sharing them with others in the network (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995). Cognitive translations include knowledge from available representations both inside and outside the network to make it known to the participants (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995). The dynamic of the cognitive translations form a generative dance of knowledge identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, sourcing, creation, dissemina tion and validation.

As the operant dimension of digitization increases the knowledge heterogeneity of innovation networks (Argyres, 1999; Majchrzak & Malhotra, 2013; Yoo et al., 2006), the semantic distance of knowledge elements necessary for product innovation grows. Increasing knowledge heterogeneity increases the ambiguity of innovation, posing novel challenges in negotiating and making sense of the increased diversity of knowledge (Bailey et al., 2012). It also calls for new ways to connect and translate knowledge. Therefore, cognitive translations leading towards in novation closure rarely form a linear process. Instead, iterative, fractal and messy processes are common, marked by an ebb and <sup>fl</sup>ow of knowledge and peppered with actors’ surprises and disappointments (Boland et al., 2007).

## Social translations

As noted, innovation takes place within a web of social relationships, and an innovation process, by necessity, transforms the social space of the actors in the innovation network. These transformations are denoted here as social translations; they take place at the boundaries of communities, in what Galison (1997) calls trading zones, where actors reveal their interests and mutually adjust to each other’s perspectives. Social translations are also involved in the interactions between people and artefacts during innovation (Hutchins, 1995). The artefacts are used to make observations, carry out calculations and render representations that shape and reshape a participant’s role during cognitive translations, and by doing so, they also in<sup>fl</sup>uence a participant’s social position. Accordingly, when the artefacts and their use change (e.g. because of digitization), the roles of the actors, their identities and their relationships will also change (Boland et al., 2007; Carlile, 2002; Gal et al., 2008).

Social translations involve constant interaction and political positioning among innovation network participants. Their perspectives are often in con<sup>fl</sup>ict, but they still need to <sup>fi</sup>nd a way to modify and align their interests into temporary dialectic synthesis. In this regard, social translations are inherently political mechanisms that are required for innovation to proceed (Boland et al., 2007; Galison, 1997). Although cognitive translations are viewed as dynamic and progressing, social translations are, in contrast, often a series of back-and-forth movements into positions within a social space that in<sup>fl</sup>uences the participant’s subsequent behaviour.

Overall, the more radical the innovation, the more alien any one participant’s ideas can be to others, and the more dynamic the ebb and <sup>fl</sup>ow of knowledge and perspectives is, the more lim ited a participant’s ability to translate ideas becomes. Thus, when two or more participants come together through unlikely connections borne out of serendipity, accidents or bold initiatives, those who occupy a ‘structural hole’ (Burt, 1992) or operate as a ‘weak link’ (Granovetter, 1973) are likely to face boundaries rife with tension (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995; Carlile, 2002). As the poo of actors diversi<sup>fi</sup>es, it also becomes less likely that the new participants will join the innovation network, owing to growing differences in their interests. As the diversity grows, negotiating each one’s interests in the network while protecting the current social order becomes increasingly challenging. Therefore, social translations by necessity involve tension and con<sup>fl</sup>ict and call fo translations that can overcome the tension by evolving into a Hegelian synthesis, or deadly con<sup>fl</sup>ict, or generating a tension dialectic or dialogic (Hargrave & Van De Ven, 2006) where each side assumes the position of the other, enabling an interplay between the two. Accordingly, dialogical translations can present a never-ending series of tensions between different voices in a dynamic, iterative process of perspective making and perspective taking (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995). The innovations originating from dialogical relationships are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the highly dy namic structure of the networks. For example, in AEC, tensions between design and construction communities serve as a primary source of innovation. New digital representations can stimulate new dialogical connections and new sets of social translations can take place through that dialogue, growing the network and expanding the scope of innovation (Boland et al., 2007).

## Interactions between translations

Although we have treated cognitive and social translations separately for analytic purposes, they remain tightly intertwined in practice. The cognitive translation constrains and is constrained by the social translation, and vice versa. The cognitive schemata, mental models and scripts that underlie generative processes of innovation are socially constructed and conditioned (Barley & Tolbert, 1997). The act of cognitive translation is inherently social. At the same time, a cognitive translation involves more than one actor and is embedded in ongoing social interactions. As a new product idea moves forward to become a reality, a growing number of actors from diverse communities become involved. For example, in a typical construction project, an architect’s radical ideas for a building’s geometry and materials captured in architectural drawings (a translation from an idea to a representation) must be later translated into detailed engineering draw ings (a translation from a representation to another representation) by integrating a new set of understandings and ideas. In this process, engineers not only map one type of representation to another but also inject their own knowledge, thereby expanding the density of knowledge em bedded in the representational artefact. Similar translations take place as engineering drawings are used to create shop drawings, and eventually, fabricated components are integrated into the building through the labour of contractors and subcontractors. In this series of transformations, cognitive and social translations interact and are essential in shaping the innovation and moving it ahead. Often, the completion of one’s cognitive translations requires an input from others, meaning that one’s cognitive translations are enmeshed in social translations with other actors. This suggests a recursive image of multiple independent, yet interconnected, cognitive translations forming a vibrant innovation network that is brought together by a series of socia translations. Next. we will discuss how recent developments with digital technologies have reshaped innovation networks and in<sup>fl</sup>uenced associated translations.

## FOUR CLASSES OF INNOVATION NETWORKS

## A typology of innovation networks

The two dimensions of digitization will next be used to de<sup>fi</sup>ne four ‘archetypes’ of digital product innovation networks. Each is a distinctive way of organizing an innovation process and engag ing in its social and cognitive translations. These archetypes are presented here as ‘ideal types – abstracted from the details of their unique contexts to emphasize generalized patterns of how product innovation unfolds under different conditions of digitization.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst dimension – that of digital connectivity through operand resources – considers the distributed nature of coordination and control that characterizes each of the four innovation network types. This dimension is primarily about who controls the resources and knowledge of the innovation network. The value may be lower cost, better quality or greater novelty (Attewell, 1992). At one end of the continuum, with innovations created in a vertically integrated <sup>fi</sup>rm, we see a centralized control over actors and resources. Here, the vertically integrated <sup>fi</sup>rm has sole ownership over the results through patents, trademarks or trade secrets. Typically, this is a top-down process that is envisioned and launched by managers using a dedicated research and development function. An example of this is the traditional hierarchical product development process run by large companies like the old AT&T and General Electric. On the other extreme, we see minimal formal control over innovation actors and resources during the innovation process and little control over innovation processes and ownership rights. The innovation emerges as a decentralized community or ‘bazaar’ without a formal hierarchy over its participants and outcomes. An example of this type of innovation network is open-source soft ware development. The process is run by voluntary contributors, and the results are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a ‘common good’ guaranteed by licenses such as General Public License and related Creative Common principles (http://creativecommons.org/). A less extreme but still looser innovation form can be found in open industry alliances that work jointly with minimal control, with ownership arrangements based on cross licensing or open access to the results (e.g. fuel cell research or the human genome project).

The second dimension of our typology is the level of heterogeneity within operant resources that need to be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and mobilized during product innovation. At one end, we see innovation networks that have a single, relatively homogeneous body of discipline or domain-based knowledge, managed and maintained through a single digital platform. One example is the suite of tools used by Linux and Apache communities to design, implement, test and maintain associated codes. At the other end, we see innovation networks with a heterogeneous body of multidisciplinary knowledge supported by a hodgepodge of tools and artefacts. A good example of this type of innovation network is the design of the City Car. Although the innovation process relied on a ‘core’ of 3D modelling technologies, a bulk of the innovation involved localized technologies, tools and knowledge resources. The high level of heterogeneity includes highly diverse technologies and tools that need to be integrated and coordinated, often through ad hoc gateways, through human bricolage or by expanding the digital capabilities that support convergence (such as semantic standards founded on XML).

They can change the nature of the products in an industry or even create new industries. The four forms of innovation networks make use of different sets of digital capabilities and participate in distinct types of information infrastructures. We also note that because of the development of extensive and relatively lowcost information infrastructures. the form of innovation networks is shifting towards ‘east' ano ‘south’ – meaning less centralized control and more heterogeneity. ## The project innovation network

In the project innovation network, cognitive and social translations take place within the context of a centralized hierarchical control. Typically. the proiect type of innovation network is supported by relatively homogeneous knowledge resources from a single discipline and uses standardized digital tools like CASE tools, CAD/CAM tools and so on. Innovation networks that focus on particular subsystems, such as a power train embedded in a larger product like a car, fall into this type (Henderson & Clark, 1990). Process innovations to decrease the quality variance of a product, such as capability maturity model, TQM or ISO9000, are supported by a homogeneous set of tools, which are also good examples of project innovation networks.

## The clan innovation network

In the clan innovation network, cognitive and social translations take place among relatively homogenous actors who share a single discipline or closely related ones. Actors in a clan network share an interest in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of product, which is relatively well understood among the participating actors. Consequently, the actors access a common set of tools, share a common vocabulary and have largely complementary and overlapping knowledge as re<sup>fl</sup>ected in their shared product concept. These actors, however, are neither under the sway of hierarchical managerial control, nor are their innovation outcomes controlled by a coherent central process. Exemplars of this type of innovation network are buoyant open-source communities (Crowston et al., 2012; Fitzgerald, 2006). Here, volunteer programmers are not bound by a centralized control but act based on their self-interest and initiative, which is often driven by strong social ethos and social bonding. Programmers share their work through relatively homogeneous technological platforms, and they conform to strong community standards that de<sup>fi</sup>ne structure, form and quality of the outcome (‘what counts as good code’). The reduced communication costs of their digital infrastructures enable a distributed approach to programming and largescale sharing of work (von Hippel & von Krogh, 2003). As a result, innovations originating within clan networks are likely to be economically ef<sup>fi</sup>cient, owing to the volunteer efforts of many participants and the strong community standards of good programming. However, this requires that incentives to join and contribute work to the network can be created through social bonding, reputation systems and the like (Stewart & Gosain, 2006). Hence, social translations that are enabled by shared digital platforms are often critical in coordinating complementary knowledge resources and in negotiating product goals and tasks.

Although clan-based innovation does not rest on traditional hierarchical control, the network structure often adopts a centralized meritocracy where the leading elite (core) defines the criteria for the expected innovation quality and its direction. The core also controls the architecture and the deliverables expected of it. Typically, a small number of competent actors form the inner core of the network and are surrounded by an external ‘belt’ or periphery of followers (Berquist & Ljungberg, 2001; O’Reilly, 1999). Much of the work in the centre is about standardizing the work<sup>fl</sup>ow, generating rules for participation, creating vision and guiding the process and direction of work. Contributions by the periphery need to be constantly assessed and integrated by the core. However, this structure is fundamentally different from a centralized control system in that individuals volunteer for the clan network: they choose their contributions based on their own interests and are free to choose their own work domain or even leave the network (Malone, 2004). The impacts of digitization in a clan form primarily concern mobilizing the distributed actors and coordinating their knowledge resources to gain ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and quality without resorting to bureaucratic structures.

## The federated innovation network

A federated innovation network has members who operate under centralized control. ofter within a single organizational hierarchy (or within an alliance). Yet, even with such boundaries of the network, the innovation work draws on knowledge from diverse knowledge communities across multiple disciplines and is concerned with integrating it within some type of uni<sup>fi</sup>ed digita or knowledge platform, where the <sup>fi</sup>rm or innovating unit can exercise architectural control over the key elements of the innovation. Classic examples of federated innovation networks can be found in large-scale manufacturing work such as the aerospace or automotive industries (e.g. Argyres, 1999; Bailey et al., 2012). The automotive industry is an example of a platform industry (King & Lyytinen, 2004; Hendfrisson & Yoo, 2014; Anderson et al., 2008) that needs to mobilize and integrate diverse knowledge communities to innovate, ranging from transportation engineering, mechanical engineering, material engineering, electrical and electronic engineering, manufacturing, logistics and distribution to industrial design. Yet these communities, whether they are a part of the car manufacturer or participate as suppliers, are under tight control by the car company, which often determines the product platform, manufacturing architecture and branding. The product is tightly de<sup>fi</sup>ned and controlled in all aspects by the company, through a process of modularization. In this modular structure, each community contributes to the development of well-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed components and interfaces that will be assembled by the manufacturer acting as an integrator.

Other examples of the federated form of innovation network can be found in <sup>fi</sup>rms that provide integrated solutions and services (Sawhney et al., 2004) including IBM, Accenture and UPS. In these <sup>fi</sup>rms, interactions with customers are through a consulting arm, which adopts the role of a boundary spanner and cuts across structural holes within the <sup>fi</sup>rm and across its market ecology, to dynamically orchestrate knowledge from diverse communities. Some traditional multi-division industrial <sup>fi</sup>rms like ABB or Rockwell Automation are also shifting towards this structure as they face unprecedented demands to deliver integrated solutions instead of providing separate systems or components. However, in order to orchestrate such solutions, new forms of innovation networks need to be invented.

Currently, a maior company relving on this form of innovation network is Google (Chen et al.. 2007). Google’s strategic goal is to use digital technologies as a shared platform to allow continuous innovation in a large number of user communities and to integrate knowledge communities into an innovation ecology. Its main strategy is to leverage open standards and open-access principles in a quest to offer services that allow users to search digital content in any form. To this end, they carefully decide in which areas they will retain architectural control (like search optimization or data centre management) and where they will allow openness in order to integrate the innovations of separate knowledge communities who are developing services like Google Earth, Google Map, YouTube or Android. Google’s income depends on un derstanding users’ ‘search’ behaviour and offering services based on that knowledge, thus connecting the marketing and advertising communities to their ecology. They actively seek to utilize their digital platform for integrating and innovating with diverse communities. Their digita infrastructure allows Google to quickly integrate newly acquired services into their core offering. In addition, Google uses a bottom-up approach to innovation in which most employees are entitled to use up to 20% of their time to explore ideas based on the digital platform.

In federated innovation networks, actors from internal communities interact with their externa reference groups for knowledge resources, values and expertise, as part of maintaining their unique skills and competencies. The challenge for innovating in hierarchical organizations with this form of network is that the internal communities can share ‘too little’ inside the organization, owing to their strong social networks in outside communities. At the same time, organizational boundaries prevail in the managerial, legal and <sup>fi</sup>nancial sense. Therefore, these organizations must maintain loose couplings horizontally across different knowledge communities, while maintaining tight integration at the platform level, which can become challenging (Baldwin & Clark, 2000). Social translations in the federated form can be highly problematic as actors come from a multitude of knowledge communities that can be engaged in hostile competition. The im pact of digitization in this type of innovation network is primarily about the novelty that results from the combination of heterogeneous digital resources including diverse types of digita repositories, search capabilities and social networking tools. Therefore, cognitive translations in federated innovation networks are likely to be emergent and ambiguous. Another important aspect of digitization in federated innovation networks is the criticality of information infrastruc ture. This can encourage key innovators to make their components become central control points in the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s information infrastructures (Tilson et al., 2010). The <sup>fi</sup>rm acting as the integrator – typically the <sup>fi</sup>nal manufacturer – can extract economic rents by controlling the ar chitecture. At the same time, if any of the sub-components become a bottleneck, it is the <sup>fi</sup>rm that produces this component who will extract the largest economic rents. Therefore, during the emergence of the infrastructure, innovators seek to create a viable platform strategy by creating sticky and non-imitable knowledge or seeking to control key components within the infrastructure.

## The anarchic innovation network

In anarchic innovation networks, the structure and dynamics of product innovation are the most complex. Control of the process, structure and outcomes is distributed throughout the network, and at the same time, the knowledge resources are highly heterogeneous. The knowledge resources needed for innovation are not known a priori, and cognitive translations are therefore ambiguous and emergent. Many teams that work on radical products or services transcending traditional industry boundaries operate in these types of innovation networks. For example, in the AEC industry, a radical construction project such as Gehry’s work on the Guggenheim

Museum in Bilbao involved multiple autonomous <sup>fi</sup>rms from different trades providing varying knowledge resources and unique tools to be pooled for the innovative task they faced. Each <sup>fi</sup>rm followed their own unique logic and innovation trajectory while participating in a small and dedicated community of practice established in their trading zone (Boland et al., 2007). Their innovation trajectories and knowledge had to be interwoven during the innovation so that each community would in<sup>fl</sup>uence the other neighbouring communities’ cognitive and social translations. Similar types of innovation networks can be found in turbulent markets such as new mobile services (Tilson et al., 2010; Yoo et al., 2005). In these markets, a myriad of previously unconnected actors (phone operators, software companies, content providers, hardware device manufacturers, advertising companies, etc.) weave together their perspectives, busi ness models and technological frames to establish new services and develop service arrange ments (Hargrave & Van De Ven, 2006). Scienti<sup>fi</sup>c communities like the one that created the new particle accelerator (HDR) in CERN are also examples of an anarchic innovation network (Galison, 1997; Tuertcher et al., 2014).

The key challenge in anarchic networks is controlling a wide range of actors who often have different and possibly con<sup>fl</sup>icting interests with highly distinct knowledge bases. No one has control over the <sup>fi</sup>nal product architecture, the digital infrastructure that supports the innovation or the rules of engagement (Tuertscher et al., 2014). In addition, the heterogeneous knowledge resources available are invested and contested, making knowledge leakages unlikely (Carlile, 2002). A speci<sup>fi</sup>c form of collective action is required in cases like this to motivate actors and to overcome traps like the ‘prisoner’s dilemma’ (Hargrave & Van De Ven, 2006). In anarchic innovation networks, cognitive and social translations can often become problematic. First, the network is composed of diverse and evolving tools and capabilities, making cognitive translations fragile and hard to implement. In addition, as new actors join, the richness and the socia and technological heterogeneity will expand cumulatively along the ‘rolling edge’ of their ability to control it (Berente et al., 2007). The challenges associated with cognitive translations in anarchic innovation networks are more pronounced than with federated networks, because translations are combined with issues of control and incentives. The social translations are also challenging, because they need to be based on dialogical connections in which knowledge and insights <sup>fl</sup>ow across multiple communities through the use of information and communication technologies and speci<sup>fi</sup>c trading zones (Tuertscher et al., 2014). New types of ‘boundary objects’, or translation devices, are required as part of the infrastructure to support discussions of rules of engagement, building trust and sharing visions (Star & Ruhleder, 1996; Tuertscher et al., 2014). These connections must remain dialogical while the network continues to grow and adds new actors and tools. Thus, the infrastructure needs tight control while at the same time it needs to be <sup>fl</sup>exible and open – a requirement that is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to meet with the information infrastructures of today (Tilson et al., 2010).

## Design requirements for product innovation infrastructures under digital connectivity and convergence

Drawing on the four innovation network archetypes presented earlier, we next identify <sup>fi</sup>ve requirements for information infrastructures that can promote and sustain digital produc innovation in settings with increasing levels of digital connectivity and digital convergence (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010). In each of the four types of innovation networks, the nature and control of the infrastructure are different, which uniquely shapes them. We also note the chal lenges of moving from one type of network to another. Because of increasing digitization, we observe the general trend of project innovation networks to move towards ‘east’ and ‘south’. We also note that challenges to carry out such moves are both technological and institutional and call for both <sup>fl</sup>exibility and stability in the infrastructure and its control.

Generally, each infrastructure for product innovation is there to mediate both cognitive and social translations, despite their different and often opposing demands. In the case of cognitive translations, the infrastructure needs to provide a stable and well-organized set of capabilities to accommodate the search, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, integration and analysis of diverse knowledge representations, which the innovators need at their disposal while they translate one representation to another during the innovation process. This is especially challenging in federated and anarchic networks because of their growing technical and social heterogeneity. In these networks, innovators must explore diverse alternatives for representation and knowledge sharing, requiring that they engage with multiple, often non-compatible representations while exploring their innovation options. We label this <sup>fi</sup>rst requirement as representational <sup>fl</sup>exibility.

At the same time, representations need to retain some level of semantic coherence for each innovation community through (meta) model-based control. For example, in the AEC industry, when architects translate 2D drawings into 3D digital models, or vice versa, the ‘intelligence embedded in the model as parametric relationships among elements of the building often be comes lost. The translated representation simply conserves the shape of the source model, without retaining any of the semantics of the obiects being represented in it. A new breed of digital repositories, through their rule-based and model-based controls, allow such heterogeneous models to retain some level of coherence and enable them to ‘intelligently’ map one object world to another (Argyres, 1999). That is, if a building surface needs to be constructed with concrete of a certain strength and cost, the next family of models retains these properties throughout the design process and associated cognitive translations. We label this second requirement as semantic coherence. It posits that representations referring to the same semantic ‘core’ domain can be easily organized and manipulated as a group. This challenge is important in both federated and anarchic networks where one faces high levels of heterogeneity, but at the same time, the solutions need to be organized on a common ground in order to yield a cognitive closure while being translated across communities.

Cognitive translation processes in federated and anarchic networks are also non-linear and emergent. Therefore, various model instances need to be traced during the innovation process to observe where, how and why views converged or diverged. Therefore, it is important to be able to trace ‘through’ heterogeneous processes during innovation and across the innovation network. We label the third requirement as temporal and spatial traceability.

For social translations, in federated and anarchic networks, the infrastructure needs to act as a ‘social’ broker that detects and organizes action around structural holes. Hargadon & Sutton (1997) observed that in order to support innovation, brokering needs to be combined with an organizational memory that maintains a common ground for knowledge sharing and helps <sup>fi</sup>nd who knows what. Human knowledge brokers are known to connect to previously non-connected communities and through weak ties reduce the distance among actors, thereby helping networks to converge (Watts, 1999). In situations like this, information infrastructure needs to amplify such processes as the networks grow and become more heterogeneous. Our fourth representational requirement is thus knowledge brokering.

Finally, in dialectic and dialogical connections, which are typical in federated and anarchic networks, associated information infrastructures must permit partisan representations between innovators as they negotiate at network boundaries. In the case of dialectic connections, the information infrastructure needs to offer ‘rough’ pragmatic boundary objects that help parties to seek synthesis (Carlile, 2002). In the case of dialogical connections, information infrastruc tures need to meet as a requirement of linguistic calibration that can support an ongoing discourse by mapping between varying vocabularies and storing their discursive context for later reference in order to maintain a common ground (Rossi et al., 2004).

## DISCUSSION

We have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several important changes in product innovation networks driven by digitization. First, digitization generates new types of innovation networks where heterogeneous knowl edge <sup>fl</sup>ows more freely and where unexpected innovation ecologies mushroom around novel products. Second, the pervasive use of digital technologies in product innovation as an operant resource, along with the use of digital tools as operand resources in the development of those products, pushes organizations to pursue more radical innovations. Rapid digital convergence has. for example. already transformed mobile phones from a single-purpose communication device to a multipurpose and <sup>fl</sup>exible computing platform. As the world around us becomes more digitized, there will be even more opportunities to transform familiar products into some thing alien and novel (Verganti, 2009). In order to pursue such opportunities, most product organizations are rethinking and reshaping the boundaries that de<sup>fi</sup>ne their innovation networks. For example, MIT’s City Car shows that product innovators need to rethink innovation networks in ways that radically expand the boundaries of a product category. As more organizations, stakeholders and industries explore such opportunities, they will be moving towards forms that resemble anarchic innovation networks.

The digitization of innovation also requires us to rethink how we study and theorize product inno vation. In this paper, we explored just two questions related to this: (1) what are the impacts of increased digitization of products on the ways in which innovation activities are organized and (2) what are the possible ways to con<sup>fi</sup>gure digitally enabled product innovation infrastructures to allow for their full innovation potential to be realized? To address these questions, we developed a framework that identi<sup>fi</sup>es challenges associated with product innovation opportunities and explored design aspects of information infrastructures that can support fully <sup>fl</sup>edged product innovation.

To address the first question, we articulated product innovation networks as socio-technica assemblages in which heterogeneous and uncertain knowledge is produced, transformed and stabilized. In such networks, innovations emerge through a series of generative processes tha involve cognitive and social translation. To address the second question, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four types of innovation networks by identifying two drivers of digital innovation: (1) digitization as operand resource for connectivity and (2) digitization as operant resources for convergence. On one hand, we saw digital connectivity increasing the distribution of control and need for coordination within innovation networks. On the other hand, we saw digital convergence increasing knowledge het erogeneity during innovation. These two drivers allowed us to conceptualize four archetypes of in novation networks: project, clan, federated and anarchic. Furthermore, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve attributes of digitally enabled product innovation infrastructures: representational <sup>fl</sup>exibility, semantic coher ence, temporal and spatial traceability, knowledge brokering and linguistic calibration. In the following, we will discuss the implications of our framework for future research on product innovation.

The proposed framework informs product innovation research on at least four fronts: (1) it indicates a need to reconsider the ontology and epistemology of innovation; (2) it argues that we need to analyse innovation dynamics more deeply; (3) it calls on scholars to modify pre-digita theories of industrial organization; and (4) it provides a guide for analysing socio-technica networks and identifying emerging patterns of innovation.

## Epistemology and ontology of innovation

The very idea of innovation networks suggests that the organization of the network – who or what is in the network, and how does he, she or it interrelate and behave – has a lot to do with the content of the innovation network – what the actors think they are innovating about. That is, the social space of an innovation network shapes and is shaped by the cognitive space and its movement. In short, the ontology of the innovation cannot be separated from its epistemology: what actors innovate about is a mirror of who and what is enrolled in the network and what she or it knows' and can do. Thus, the uncertainty, ambiguity, diversity and dynamics of innovation networks should be a main focus of future product innovation studies. The idea of multiple distinc forms of innovation networks challenges our traditional way of measuring innovation with a single construct. If innovation is viewed as distributed and emergent, it is not clear where, when and how one can measure it and its progression. The concept becomes even more challenging when one compares different innovation networks, as what is being innovated changes radically as different networks are used. For example, the future of mobile phones emerging from the innovation network around Google with its vibrant and open-ended communities will be different from that of a competing innovation network that resembles more traditional forms from the telecommunication industry and their tight centralized controls.

Shifting our focus away from the innovation artefact to the innovation network allows us to better appreciate the inherent uncertainty and volatility involved in digital product innovation. It also highlights our need for better ways of observing and classifying innovation networks and their behaviours to better re<sup>fl</sup>ect the critical properties of distributed and emergent innovation. These indices should capture and re<sup>fl</sup>ect not only the epistemology of innovation networks but also their ontology, i.e. what the innovation is about and what types of knowledge is being relied on.

## Innovation dynamics

As companies increasingly embrace digitized components in their products, they will move towards more distributed and heterogeneous innovation network forms. We have witnessed the emergence of federated innovation networks in very traditional industries like AEC (Boland et al., 2007) and of anarchic innovation networks in mobile services (Yoo et al., 2010; Tilson et al., 2010). We have also observed a shift to distributed forms as evidenced by the successfu open-source movements in the software and electronics industries. Currently, other industries are following this trend as they embrace digitization in their products and services – for example, through implementing Internet of Things, digital product libraries, 3D printing and big data. The automotive industry is facing new challenges as car components are being rapidly transformed from mechanical parts to ones enabled by or based on digitization (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014). As car manufacturers come to terms with the new digitized car components, they need to formulate digital product architectures that integrate these components and their related knowledge communities (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014). Once these architectures are established, it will open up new vistas of product innovation based on integrating digital content and internet-based products into cars as seen in the City Car or Driverless Car. This means, however, that automotive companies will need to open up their platforms and rede<sup>fi</sup>ne their relationships with contractors and suppliers. This will result in a shift from a tightly controlled, vertically integrated <sup>fi</sup>rm into a more loosely coupled set of product innovation networks (Yoo et al., 2010; Henfridsson et al., 2014). Accordingly, we expect that car companies will be slowly pushed to ‘east’ from a federated innovation network to an anarchic network.

The introduction of digital components into products, then, no matter how innocent the original intent of the innovation network might be, is likely to lead to fundamental and continuing recon<sup>fi</sup>g uration of the innovation networks, eventually moving organizations towards anarchic forms. Digita product innovation pushes innovators to increasingly connect and reconnect to actors across a myriad of organizations and communities, thereby amplifying knowledge heterogeneity and values, introducing peculiarities in vocabularies and behaviours and sharpening differences in ownership and governance (Berente et al., 2007). Over time, this will lead to a continuing expansion and recon<sup>fi</sup>guration of innovation networks, making them more like anarchic networks.

The four archetypes of innovation networks that we have presented are not intended as a static classi<sup>fi</sup>cation that merely sorts out product innovations into different types of antecedent structures. Instead, the analysis suggests a continuing “southeastward” movement in the content and scope of product innovation. Depending on where the <sup>fi</sup>rm is currently located in the innovation space, different firms will take different routes during this shift. It is likely that we will see new ecologies of product innovation in which organizations seek to <sup>fi</sup>nd appropriate niches as they navigate this dynamic, evolving landscape. An important future research agenda is to probe the processes through which innovation networks become recon<sup>fi</sup>gured and the ways that entering actors, technologies and innovations shape them. As Boland et al. (2007) note, such a shift will not happen teleologically, but rather though gradual and locally emergent evolutions.

To better highlight differences between the four innovation contexts and to articulate conditions under which organizations can and will move towards anarchic innovation networks, we need further empirical and theoretical research on innovation network dynamics and their structural antecedents. In addition, we need to examine to what extent organizations simultaneously engage in multiple different types of networks, and how the intensity and proportion of these engagements affects the level and nature of their innovation work. To this end, we need to formulate new constructs, operationalizations and measures that will enable us to study variations and changes in the innovation scope, dynamics and speed both within networks and across organizational populations (Berente et al., 2007).

## Theories of industrial organization

Anarchic innovation networks represent a radical shift from the dominant organizing logics of the industrial age that were based on modular thinking (Baldwin & Clark, 2000; Yoo et al., 2010; Tuertscher et al., 2014). Invention of modular structures enabled <sup>fi</sup>rms to simultaneously have loose horizontal couplings and tight vertical integration. The emergence of anarchic networks is already challenging the role of vertical integration in several industries such as mobile phones or TV. In the future, anarchic networks with their increased knowledge heterogeneity may replace horizontal loose coupling with rapid and tighter horizontal integration (adjustment) among various business components. Zammuto et al. (2007), for example, envision a radically different future where innovation opportunities will be rapidly exploited by dynamically recon<sup>fi</sup>guring digitally enabled business components. Yoo et al. (2012) suggest that the digitalization will introduce layered modular structures that will enable massive and parallel experimentations of recombination of granular digital components from previously unrelated industries. Such <sup>fl</sup>exible, real-time organizational innovation is not based on traditional tight integration among loosely de<sup>fi</sup>ned modularized subsystems. Instead, it requires strong social norms (ease of social translations), as we witness in open-source communities, coupled with new types of information infrastructures. As a result, we suspect that progressive digitization will replace traditional industrial organizational forms based on modular designs with more organic, anarchic networks within and across industries, which will rely on tight and swift horizontal integrations along with looser vertical couplings enabled by digital information infrastructures.

## Networks and emerging innovation patterns

Social network analyses that have dominated one corner of innovation research have been strongly embraced recently as an important way to study innovation and associated knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Gupta et al., 2007). In the past, such analyses focused solely on the social communication links but forewent other types of (mediated) connections, especially those enabled by the affordances of the information infrastructures. Yet these othe connections deeply effect the morphology and dynamics of innovation networks, as witnessed by the success of open-source software development, where ‘talk’ is largely replaced by ‘work on the code’. Our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the four types of innovation networks raises the question of how far we can get in explaining innovation processes and outcomes with ‘pure’ social constructs and associated methodological tools. In our opinion, received social network models signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly under-theorize the role of digital infrastructures as critical connection and translation ‘platforms’. Material characteristics of new and emerging technologies and the ways they shape and are shaped by social relationships among heterogeneous actors need far better and careful theorization (Zammuto et al., 2007).

In the future, digital tools and capabilities need to be conceptualized ontologically both as nodes and as connections in extended types of network analyses (Kane & Alavi, 2007).

Recently, some virtual team research has used network analysis to reveal the impact of the reach and intensity of computer-mediated connections in building social networks (Ahuja & Carley, 1999). But at the same time there is virtually no theorizing about digital technologies as nodes and translators. We need to be cautious, however, that we do not rush blindly to just add digital technologies as additional nodes and connections in the network analyses. Digita artefacts have varying capabilities and will enter either as nodes or connections in network models depending on their functionality and role. Thus, differences in their capabilities need to be carefully distinguished. This will require a richer and more nuanced vocabulary of digital artefacts and their capabilities for shaping product innovation and how they connect to human actors and other artefacts while organizations build increasingly complex innovation networks. This can be accomplished by exploring the topologies and the behaviours of innovation networks with richer and more re<sup>fi</sup>ned vocabularies and analytic tools.

Traditional network models do consider power and con<sup>fl</sup>ict in networks, but in a limited way. The attention to power and con<sup>fl</sup>ict should be expanded if we want to understand clan and anarchic in novation networks. We need to depart from the received idea of power that draws upon the notion of a hierarchical position with a control over and access to resources – the capability of an agent to in<sup>fl</sup>uence another to follow the agent’s will. Power, in contrast, needs to be re-conceptualized in the context of distributed and autonomous agents with varying capabilities that are interacting with both human and digital artefacts. Theories explaining collective action (Hargrave & Van De Ven, 2006) or Foucault’s (1980) work on disciplinary power offers useful starting points.

## CONCLUSION

Rich forms of digital technologies have become an essential element of everyday life, making it critical to examine how the increased digitization affects the way organizations innovate. Our paper takes some steps in this direction by proposing a framework of digital product innovation networks and exploring some characteristics of digital information infrastructures that can support highly digitized forms of innovation networks. We further propose that the movement towards anarchic forms of innovation networks is an inevitable journey for organizations that want to embrace the generative capacity of digitally induced product innovation. This is a journey that is likely to challenge familiar ideas and beliefs about products, structures, relationships and identity. Innovators need to challenge the taken for granted by making the familiar alien. Future theoretical and empirical work needs to expand, revise and validate the proposed model with rigorous empirical analyses of the dynamics and behaviours of innovation networks and of the various organizational forms that condition the emergence of radical products in all walks of life.
