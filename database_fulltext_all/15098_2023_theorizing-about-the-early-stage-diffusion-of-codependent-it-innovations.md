# Theorizing about the Early-Stage Diffusion of Codependent IT Innovations

Srikanth Parameswaran,<sup>1</sup> Rajiv Kishore,<sup>2</sup> Xuanhui Yang,<sup>3</sup> Zhenyu Liu<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Binghamton University, State University of New York, USA, sparames@binghamton.edu <sup>2</sup>University of Nevada, Las Vegas, USA, rajiv.kishore@unlv.edu <sup>3</sup>Xiamen University, China, xxhhyang@gmail.com <sup>4</sup>Xiamen University, China, zhenyliu@xmu.edu.cn

## Abstract

In this paper, we focus on the early-stage diffusion of codependent IT innovations, which are a type of innovation in which the overall innovation consists of two complementary parts that are adopted by two different adopter communities but where both parts need to be jointly adopted by the two coadopter communities for successful diffusion of the overall innovation. Using innovation diffusion, organizing vision (OV), and institutional entrepreneurship theories as the key theoretical lenses, and an in-depth case study reconstructed using 20 years of discourse surrounding Walmart’s campaign in the early stage of diffusion of the RFID-in-retailing technology, we develop a fourphase process model for the early-stage diffusion of codependent IT innovations. We make three specific contributions to the IS discipline, specifically to the literature on IS innovation adoption and diffusion. First, we add the notion of coadopter relative advantage and posit that the organization in the coadopter community with a higher coadopter relative advantage that perceives the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage will emerge as an institutional entrepreneur (IE) and will influence the early-stage diffusion of the codependent IT innovation. Second, we add the notion of an internal-external influencer and posit that the IE may be an actor who is internal to the overal adoption phenomenon, which involves two different coadopter communities, but external to the coadopter community with a lower coadopter relative advantage that adopts the innovation component. Third, we divide the early-stage diffusion process into four phases—emergence, structuralization, evolution, and chasm—and identify the institutional entrepreneurship strategies used and the OV functions enacted by the IE during each phase. We propose that the IE for a codependent innovation will: (1) use the rationale development strategy and enact the interpretation OV function during the emergence phase, (2) use the resource mobilization strategy and enact the mobilization OV function during the structuralization phase, (3) use the relationship development strategy and enact the legitimation OV function during the evolution phase, and (4) use all the three institutional entrepreneurship strategies and enact all the three OV functions during the chasm phase.

Keywords: Codependent IT Innovation, Early-Stage Innovation Diffusion, Coadopter Relative Advantage, Internal-External Influencer, Institutional Entrepreneurship, Organizing Vision, Innovation Diffusion Theory, RFID Technology

## 1 Introduction

Compared to the extensive research on the diffusion of single-whole innovations in the IS field, scant attention has been paid to the diffusion of a class of innovations termed multiproduct innovations in the prior literature (Bucklin & Sengupta, 1993), which we call codependent innovations. Distinct from single-whole innovations such as personal computers, spreadsheets, ERP systems, and database technologies, codependent innovations are a type of information technology (IT)- based innovation in which the overall innovation consists of two complementary parts that are adopted by two different adopter communities, where both the parts need to be jointly adopted by members, or coadopters, of the two adopter communities for successful diffusion of the overall innovation (Bucklin & Sengupta, 1993). The radio frequency identification and detection (RFID) technology used in retailing for supply chain management, the focus of the current study, is an example of a codependent innovation. There are two main parts to this RFID-in-retailing innovation<sup>1</sup>—the RFID chip (also known as an RFID tag) and the RFID reader—which are adopted by different adopter communities. Suppliers of consumer packaged goods attach RFID tags to their products before shipping them to retailers. Retailers install RFID readers in their supply chain, including warehouses, trucks, retail stores, etc., to track these packages. RFID technology promises to benefit the overall supply chain of retailing only if both retailers and suppliers jointly adopt their respective parts.

Innovation diffusion theory (IDT) posits two key notions that explain the diffusion of single-whole innovations. The first notion is the innovation’s relative advantage, as perceived by the adopter community. Potential adopters perceive a relative advantage in adopting an innovation if they assess that the innovation’s benefits outweigh its costs, given their existing practices (Akhlaghpour & Lapointe, 2018; Rogers, 2003; Sun et al., 2018). The earlier that adopters perceive a relative advantage, the earlier they adopt the innovation. The second notion is the role of influencers, both external and internal to an adopter community, in influencing the adoption of the innovation at various stages of diffusion (Akhlaghpour & Lapointe, 2018; Bass, 2004; Strang & Soule, 1998). In the early stages of the diffusion process, external influencers, such as mass media, research institutes, technology vendors, and consulting firms, communicate the innovation to potential adopters, shape their perceptions, and fuel the early adoption of the innovation (Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1990; Jeyaraj & Sabherwal, 2014; Rogers, 2003). Later on, these early adopters become internal influencers within the adopter community and fuel more widespread adoption (Jeyaraj & Sabherwal, 2014; Rogers, 2003). While the notions of relative advantage and external/internal influencers are the primary explanations for the diffusion of single-whole innovations in the literature, scholarship on the diffusion of codependent innovations has been rather scarce.

Codependent innovations challenge both of the key IDT notions discussed above. Consider the notion of relative advantage: Regarding codependent innovations, the costs and benefits that the two adopter communities will gain from the adoption of their unique part of the codependent innovation are likely to be different and unique to that community. Further, due to these differences, the relative advantage gained from the codependent innovation for the two coadopter communities is likely to be asymmetric, with one community enjoying a higher relative advantage from the codependent innovation, especially during the early stages of the innovation diffusion process (Gaukler et al., 2007). Therefore, the overall interest of coadopter communities in adopting their respective parts of the innovation is also likely to be asymmetric in the early stages of the diffusion phenomenon.

In the RFID-in-retailing example, retailers can expect immediate benefits from enhanced product visibility, traceability, availability, and lower ongoing costs due to the one-time fixed cost of installing RFID readers and associated systems. On the other hand, in the early stages, the supplier community realizes no immediate benefits but incurs higher ongoing costs due to the recurring cost of affixing RFID chips onto products sold to retailers, a cost that is not incurred by retailers. As such, in the early stages of diffusion, the retailer community is likely to have more favorable opinions about the RFID-in-retailing innovation than the supplier community and is likely to adopt its part of the innovation sooner than the supplier community. In contrast, in single-whole innovations, there is a single adopter community with a single cost-benefit calculus (Agarwal & Prasad, 1997; Junglas et al., 2019; Moore & Benbasat, 1991). For example, organizations adopting ERP systems calculate relative advantage from this technology over current technology based on similar benefits (e.g., seamless business process integration) and costs (e.g., software licensing, workforce training) as those incurred by other members of the adopter community (Su & Yang, 2010). In essence, while the traditional notion of relative advantage considers heterogeneity in perceptions of relative advantage, such heterogeneity is in the perceptions of members within a single adopter community based on a single set of costs and benefits; however, heterogeneity in the perceptions between two different coadopter communities, which is based on two different and unique sets of costs and benefits, is not considered.

In addition, the successful diffusion of a codependent innovation requires the joint adoption of two different parts of the innovation by two different coadopter communities. This implies that the realization of a coadopter community’s relative advantage from adopting their part of the codependent innovation is contingent on the other coadopter community adopting their part of the codependent innovation. Therefore, even if a coadopter community perceives a high relative advantage, its members will not be able to realize this relative advantage by adopting their part of the codependent innovation if members of the other coadopter community resist adopting their part of the codependent innovation due to their lower relative advantage, especially during the early stages of the diffusion process. In other words, a high relative advantage for one community, which is a strong predictor of diffusion in the traditional innovation diffusion literature, may be insufficient to kickstart the diffusion of codependent innovations overall. In the RFID-in-retailing example, retailers’ relative advantage in adopting RFID readers will bear fruit only if suppliers also adopt RFID tags and affix them on product packaging or palettes. As discussed above, members of the supplier community might resist the adoption of this innovation due to a lower or negative relative advantage, especially during the early phases of the diffusion phenomenon, in turn reducing the likelihood of realization of the retailers’ relative advantage from the RFID codependent innovation (Dutta et al., 2007; Gaukler et al., 2007). For this reason, we also need to extend the traditional notion of relative advantage to the notion of a coadopter relative advantage to explain how the early-stage diffusion process for codependent innovations unfolds.

Next, we consider the role of influencers. External influencers who typically play an influential role in the early stages of the diffusion process in the context of single-whole innovations might find it hard to influence the adoption of the part of the innovation that yields a lower relative advantage to the relevant coadopter community. In the context of the RFID-inretailing innovation, external influencers might find it difficult to influence the adoption of RFID tags by the supplier community due to their higher costs and lower relative advantage vis-à-vis the supplier community. On the other hand, members in the coadopter community adopting the innovation component with a higher relative advantage might have the incentive to serve as influencers for the coadopter community adopting the innovation component with the lower relative advantage, as the former community will benefit from the codependent innovation only when the latter community also adopts the Innovation. In the context of the RFID-in-retailing innovation, retailers might have an incentive to serve as influencers for the supplier community that is adopting RFID tags because retailers will benefit from this overall innovation only when suppliers adopt their part of the innovation. So, are these influencers internal or external influencers? Traditional IDT would suggest that retailers as influencers for the supplier community are internal influencers because they belong to one of the two adopter communities. However, we argue that retailers are external to the supplier adopter community that is being influenced to adopt the innovation component with a lower relative advantage, i.e., the RFID tag.

Therefore, we ask: How does the diffusion process unfold during the early stages of codependent innovations? Specifically, which actors play the role of influencers during the early-stage diffusion process for codependent innovations? And what actions do these influencers engage in to influence the early-stage diffusion process for these types of innovations? These questions are important to address because new codependent IT innovations continue to be developed and implemented in various industries, as IT continues moving toward more open and decentralized systems and as interdisciplinary advancements in fields including nanotechnology, computer science, and biomaterials continue pushing the horizons of these types of IT innovations. Further, many of these innovations are targeted for adoption by very large adopter communities, such as the recent Europay, Mastercard, and Visa (EMV) chip-embedded credit card payment system, which is expected to be adopted by hundreds of millions of consumers and millions of retailers, banks, credit card companies, and other organizations. Finally, these innovations will likely transform industries, organizations, and the way that individuals live and work in fundamental ways, as can already be seen with the RFID and EMV-credit card technologies in the supply chain and retailing arenas. However, the successful diffusion of such innovations necessitates that all parties (e.g., individuals, retailers, and processing networks for credit cards) jointly adopt these innovations in order for the benefits to flow to different parties in the value chain. As with the RFIDin-retailing diffusion discussed above, the diffusion of the EMV-chip credit card payment technology has also been slow and complicated. A CNBC report showed that in its early stages, only 37% of US businesses had adopted EMV credit card payment technology by March 2016, despite the deadline of October 1, 2015 that was set for adoption of this payment technology by all parties concerned and despite significant penalties for the failure to adopt (Dimyan, 2016).

This paper is an attempt to fill the current research gap through an in-depth case study of the early stages of the diffusion of RFID technology in retailing. We use innovation diffusion theory (IDT) (Akhlaghpour & Lapointe, 2018; Rogers, 2003; Strang & Soule, 1998), organizing vision theory (OVT) (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997), and institutional entrepreneurship theory (IET) (Hardy & Maguire, 2017) as the key lenses for this study. First, IDT deals with the notions of relative advantage and influencers (i.e., external vs. internal) in explaining innovation diffusion (Akhlaghpour & Lapointe, 2018; Rogers, 2003; Strang & Soule, 1998). The notion of the coadopter relative advantage helped us conceptualize a potential coadopter’s assessment of a codependent innovation. The notion of internal-external influencers allowed us to conceptualize the source of influence, which is internal to the codependent innovation’s adopter community but external to the coadopter community that has a lower coadopter relative advantage. Second, IET studies why and how proactive actors who may benefit from new business practices “create new institutions or transform existing ones” (Maguire et al., 2004, p. 657). Thus, this theory is aligned with our goal of theorizing the role of proactive actors who may benefit from a codependent innovation to act as influencers and the strategies they use in the early-stage diffusion of codependent innovations. Finally, OVT deals with the public discourse surrounding IT innovations created by actor communities with a vested interest in the diffusion of the IT innovation. At the institutional-field level, an organizing vision (OV) interprets and legitimizes the IT innovation and mobilizes appropriate resources for the diffusion of that IT innovation, functions that may lead to the development of an institutional field surrounding the focal IT innovation (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997; Wang & Ramiller, 2009). OVT provides us with a theoretical lens for analyzing the key OV functions that influencers enact through their actions during the earlystage diffusion process in the context of codependent innovations in the institutional field.

We contribute to the IS innovation diffusion literature by studying codependent innovations with two complementary parts, and examine how they diffuse during the early stages of the innovation process.

Based on the case of the adoption of RFID-in-retailing, we develop a process model of the early-stage diffusion process for codependent innovations and examine the different roles of the institutional entrepreneur (IE) during the different phases of the early-stage diffusion process. There are three specific ways in which we make this contribution. First, we add the notion of coadopter relative advantage and posit that the member of the coadopter community that perceives the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage between the two different adopter communities will emerge as an IE and will influence the early-stage diffusion of the codependent innovation. Second, we add a nuanced understanding that the key influencers in the early stages of the diffusion process need not be external to the adopter community, as suggested by the traditional innovation diffusion literature. We add the notion of an internalexternal influencer and posit that the IE as an influencer may be an actor internal to the overall adoption phenomenon involving two different adopter communities but external in terms of the actor’s influence on the focal coadopter community. Third, we divide the early-stage diffusion process into four phases—emergence, structuralization, evolution, and chasm—and identify the institutional entrepreneurship strategies used and the OV functions enacted by the IE during each phase. Our contributions also have important practical implications for all the entities involved in the institutional field surrounding a codependent IT innovation.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Codependent Innovations and Innovation Diffusion Theory

Codependent innovations differ from single-whole innovations according to two aspects. First, codependent innovations consist of two complementary parts that are adopted by two different adopter communities (e.g., RFID in retailing). On the other hand, single-whole innovations are adopted as a whole by a particular community of individual or organizational adopters. The adopters stand to benefit from the adoption of “single-whole” innovations both directly from their use of the innovation as well as indirectly from any network externalities that may result from the diffusion of the focal innovation in the adopter network (Katz & Shapiro, 1985, 1986; Rogers, 2003). Enterprise resource planning (ERP) is an example of a single-whole innovation. While there may be several parts of ERP, including software, hardware, database, services, etc., all of these parts are adopted by a single adopter community (Wang & Ramiller, 2009).

Second, there is a need for joint adoption of the two different but complementary parts of a codependent innovation by two different adopter communities in order for the benefits to flow in the value chain. Joint adoption occurs when an entity in one adopter community adopts one part of the codependent innovation, and another entity in the other adopter community adopts the other complementary part of the codependent innovation. For example, the RFID-in-retailing innovation will be beneficial to the overall supply chain, i.e., both retailers and their suppliers, only when there is joint adoption of this codependent innovation, with suppliers adopting RFID tags and the retailers adopting RFID readers (Dutta et al., 2007). Retailers can better manage their supply chains using RFID technology only when their suppliers collectively tag all their products, because only then can retailers read the RFID tags to identify each product as it flows through their supply chains in order to accurately estimate product arrival times, shortages, stock-outs, etc. Suppliers can benefit by directly tracking shipments, which can obviate the need to investigate chargebacks from cargo theft. Further, if retailers share their data on sales, inventory, and shipments collected from RFID readers at the point-of-sale, store shelves, and warehouses, etc., suppliers can replenish out-of-stock items faster, based on sales data, and verify the timeliness of promotions schedules, based on information about when products reach the store shelf (Wailgum, 2006; Whitaker et al., 2007). These supply chain benefits will not be realized if either the retailers or their suppliers do not adopt their complementary parts.

These two aspects of codependent innovations are intriguing and have important implications for advancing the two key notions in the IDT literature: (1) relative advantage and (2) the role of influencers. The first notion of relative advantage is well established as one of the most important innovation attributes in the broader innovation diffusion literature (Agarwal & Prasad, 1997; Rogers, 2003). This construct has also emerged as one of the most dominant predictors of the diffusion of IT innovations (Akhlaghpour & Lapointe, 2018). Relative advantage “is a ratio of the expected benefits and the costs of adoption of an innovation” (Rogers, 2003, 233). However, relative advantage is not an objective, invariant innovation attribute, and different adopters in a community of adopters perceive an innovation’s relative advantage at different levels given their current situation and future expectations (Moore & Benbasat, 1991). Because different adopters have different perceptions of the relative advantage of the innovation, they adopt it at different times. This phenomenon is the essence and basis of the innovation diffusion process and the associated S-shaped diffusion curve (see Figure 1), which is essentially the cumulative normal distribution curve. The S-shaped curve shows the cumulative number of adopters as a function of time (Rogers, 2003). During the early stages, the diffusion curve rises slowly because only a few venturesome adopters, also called innovators, see the relative advantage from adopting the innovation (Libai et al., 2008; Moore, 2014; Rogers, 2003). Next, the innovation is embraced by early adopters, who are visionaries and do not require much evidence of the innovation’s relative advantage. As the diffusion process continues to evolve, the innovation is adopted by the “early majority,” followed by the “late majority,” and finally by “laggards” according to the adopters’ understanding of the relative advantage to be gained from the innovation based on both external and internal communication influencing the adoption phenomenon. The percentage of adopters from the overall adopter community in each of these adopter categories may vary for different innovations, but the generally accepted percentages based on the assumption of a normally distributed diffusion process are 2.5% innovators, 13.5% early adopters, 34% early majority, 34% late majority, and 16% laggards. Once a certain percentage of the adopter community, somewhere between 10% and 20%, has adopted the innovation, the peer influence process accelerates the S-curve until all other adopter categories—early majority, late majority, and laggards—adopt the innovation. The diffusion is said to be in an early stage until a majority of adopters embrace the innovation. In the S-curve, the early stage ends when the early majority starts adopting, after which the growth stage begins.

Scholars have argued that there is a chasm between the early and growth stages of the diffusion (Clarysse et al., 2014; Moore, 2014). The validation provided by earlystage adopters does not serve as a good reference for the adopters in the early majority (i.e., growth-stage) due to the psychographic profile differences between the adopters in these groups (Moore, 2014; Xu et al., 2017). Until the innovation fits the pragmatist orientation of a typical early majority adopter, diffusion reaches a standstill, creating what scholars call a “chasm” between the early and the growth stages of diffusion (Moore, 2014; Xu et al., 2017). Indeed, the innovations that fail to appeal to the early majority adopters do not cross the chasm (Davidson et al., 2015; Moore, 2014; Purdy & Gray, 2009). Only those innovations with a confirmed relative advantage and applicability to the early majority adopter category will cross this chasm to achieve mainstream adoption in the growth stage (Moore, 2014).

In the context of codependent innovations, there are two different coadopter relative advantages for the two different adopter communities adopting two different but complementary parts, which confer different sets of benefits and costs to their respective coadopter communities. For example, in the case of the diffusion of the RFID-in-retailing innovation, suppliers and retailers are the two different adopter communities (i.e., the coadopter communities) adopting RFID tags and RFID readers, respectively.

Hence, we add nuance to the notion of relative advantage and introduce the notion of coadopter relative advantage in this paper. We define coadopter relative advantage as the extent to which one part of a codependent innovation (e.g., RFID reader) offers a relative advantage (over prior technology) to its coadopter community (e.g., the retailer community) contingent upon the other coadopter community (e.g., the supplier community) adopting the other part of the codependent innovation (e.g., RFID tags). We explore whether and how a member of the coadopter community that perceives a higher degree of coadopter relative advantage influences the early-stage diffusion of a codependent innovation.

We clarify and emphasize that there will be heterogeneity in perceptions of coadopter relative advantage among members of both coadopter communities, including the community with a lower coadopter relative advantage, such that some members of each community are likely to perceive their respective coadopter relative advantage to be fairly high while others perceive it to be quite low. In fact, the diffusion of a codependent innovation will occur only if some members in each of the two coadopter communities perceive their respective coadopter relative advantage to be high and thus adopt their respective complementary parts of the codependent innovation. For example, in the case of the RFID-in-retailing innovation, the retailer community is overall more likely to have a higher coadopter relative advantage as compared to the supplier community, especially during the early stages. Suppliers bear RFID tagging costs from the start, which are immediate and recurring, whereas the benefits from RFID-in-retailing are long-term and not immediate. In contrast, retailers incur an initial fixed cost of installing RFID readers but they realize supply-chain benefits right from the earliest stages. Therefore, the retailer community perceives a higher coadopter relative advantage than the supplier community in the early stages (Gaukler et al., 2007). However, this does not mean that there is no heterogeneity in how different members of the supplier community view their coadopter relative advantage. Indeed, some suppliers might perceive a higher coadopter relative advantage as they foresee a long-term reduction in their supply-chain costs by investing in RFID tags in the early stages, despite knowing that their adoption of RFID tags will also benefit retailers and likely to a higher extent. The same is true of the retailer community. Essentially, it is these innovators and visionaries in the two coadopter communities, i.e., retailers and suppliers, who initiate and spur the early-stage diffusion of the RFID-inretailing codependent innovation.

The second notion is the role of influencers, both external and internal to an adopter community, in influencing the adoption of the innovation at various stages of diffusion by serving as communication channels (Bass, 2004; Strang & Soule, 1998). The classical innovation diffusion process unfolds for singlewhole innovations fueled by some innovators and early adopters who adopt based on information obtained from external influencers, e.g., a technology vendor, a consultant, etc. External influencers, such as mass media channels (e.g., television, radio, brochures, and magazines), rapidly disseminate generic information about the innovation to a large number of potential adopters (Jeyaraj & Sabherwal, 2014). However, the early majority does not adopt the innovation based solely on information from mass media. Early adopters become internal influencers within the adopter community by sharing more specific, experiential information about the innovation with adopters in the early majority category with whom they share an interpersonal relationship (Jeyaraj & Sabherwal, 2014). In the case of codependent innovations, the coadopter community that sees the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage may act as an influencer for the other coadopter community with a lower coadopter relative advantage. In the case of RFID-in-retailing, a retailer may influence the supplier adopter community to foster joint adoption of this codependent innovation at a rapid clip. This finding is in consonance with the extant literature on diffusion. However, a nuance we add here is that of an internal-external influencer. In the case of RFID-in-retailing, a retailer is an internal influencer when we consider the overall innovation diffusion phenomenon, which involves two different coadopter communities coadopting two different but complementary parts of the codependent innovation. However, a retailer is an external influencer in terms of the influence on the coadopter community of suppliers, who are not likely to be highly motivated to adopt RFID chips due to a lower coadopter relative advantage for them as compared to that for the retailer community. Therefore, we add a nuance to the notion of the influencer, and define an internal-external influencer as a member of one coadopter community (e.g., the retailer community) who adopts one part of a codependent innovation (e.g., RFID readers) but influences members of the other coadopter community (e.g., the supplier community) who are adopting the other part of the codependent innovation (e.g., the RFID tags).

When organizations explore innovative IT solutions, scholars recommend an institutional perspective to learn how institutions develop, produce, and diffuse new IT solutions (Hinings et al., 2018; Nielsen et al., 2014; Wang & Ramiller, 2009). Organizations seldom exist as silos. They are embedded in an institutional field, its interorganizational relationships, and the material and linguistic objects that both constrain and enable their efforts to innovate with IT. An institutional field is a set of actors formed by the same regulatory processes or sharing the same meaning systems (Nielsen, Mathiassen, & Newell, 2014). These actors include adopters, vendors, consultants, governments, investors, journalists, research institutions, and professional associations. Using an institutional perspective, we borrow from two streams of research. In the first stream, we focus on the institutional entrepreneurship literature. In the second stream, we focus on the OV, which is the IT discourse created and employed by an interorganizational community of actors with varying material interests (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997).

## 2.2 Codependent Innovations and Institutional Entrepreneurship Theory

We borrow from IET and conceptualize influencers as institutional entrepreneurs (IEs). IET explores why and how proactive organizations with strategic interests try to initiate changes that bring about new institutions or transform existing ones (Leca et al., 2008). This theory views IEs as actors trying to bring about an institutional change while being institutionally embedded. If actors are institutionally embedded, their norms and collective beliefs will be institutionally determined (Battilana et al., 2009). How, then, do IEs initiate institutional change in spite of the institutional field’s pressure toward stasis (Battilana et al., 2009)? This central problem in IET is called the “paradox of embedded agency” (Seo & Creed 2002). IEs can bring about institutional change by certain conditions that trigger them to sense an opportunity for institutional change. These conditions include institutional field conditions like the extent of institutionalization of existing practices, regulatory forces, and states of crisis (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). These conditions can increase uncertainty or create problems for an actor and consequently persuade actors to seek ways to reduce problems (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). Moreover, an actor’s relative power among the actors in the institutional field and/or the availability of financial, political, or social resources at their disposal could grant them the power to influence the field.

To study the role of influencers in the diffusion of codependent innovations, we focus on the influencing strategies used by an IE to affect the diffusion of an IT innovation within an adopter community in the institutional field. Based on their synthesis of the IET literature, Hardy and Maguire (2017) proposed that IEs utilize three broad strategies to promote change: (1) rationale development: IEs construct and communicate rationales to persuade other actors to support the institutional change; (2) resource mobilization: IEs mobilize resources to reward or punish other actors to persuade them to support the institutional change; and (3) relationship development: IEs establish relationships to garner support for the proposed change (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). Rationale development activities may include but are not limited to telling stories about their (un)successful implementation of an innovation, using frames to get the community to focus on the innovation, and producing, consuming, and distributing knowledge pertaining to the innovation (Battilana et al., 2009; Phillips et al., 2004). Resource mobilization activities may include but are not limited to actors funding research activities for developing an innovation, implementing negative consequences like penalties on dependent actors, materially rewarding supporters, and using formal authority in legal and professional spheres to authorize new practices or create new rules (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). Relationship development activities may include but are not limited to conducting research studies for developing an innovation in collaboration with another actor, joining a standards body as a member, and forming alliances and partnerships (Hardy & Maguire, 2017).

## 2.3 Codependent Innovations and Organizing Vision Theory

We borrow from OVT to conceptualize how institutional change happens around a codependent IT innovation. OVT concepts of the public discourse around an IT innovation, and OV functions that foster diffusion provide a framework to analyze the diffusion of codependent innovations. Analyzing the public discourse surrounding a codependent innovation would help us understand how different adopter communities communicated and perceived the coadopter relative advantage. OV is a collective (to some degree), convergent, institutional field-level idea of an IT innovation that is based on actors’ knowledge about and experience using the IT innovation (Wang & Ramiller, 2009). Actors in the innovation community such as adopters, vendors, research analysts, consultants, and journalists develop a set of ideas about the application of an IT innovation in organizations.

An OV diffuses an IT innovation through three functions: (1) interpretation by conveying the uses and usefulness of the innovation to the community (Davidson et al., 2015), (2) legitimation by backing the innovation and demonstrating its applicability to accepted business problems (Davidson et al., 2015; Kaganer et al., 2010), and (3) mobilization by motivating actors to participate in the diffusion process (Amadoru et al., 2021; Davidson et al., 2015). Interpretation in the institutional field will be evident through research and development activities to identify the usefulness of the innovation, activities funding research institutes to better understand an innovation, and using public discourse to generate buzzwords (Kaganer et al., 2010; Liao, 2016; Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). Mobilization in the institutional field will be evident through vendors displaying new products in forums, powerful actors coercing other actors to adopt the innovation, actors deploying or calling other actors to deploy resources for implementing the innovation, and actors using discourse to outline how to diffuse the innovation (Amadoru et al., 2021; Swanson & Ramiller, 1997; Wang & Swanson, 2007). Legitimation in the institutional field will be evident in actors using authority and power to attach legitimacy to an innovation, using success stories to convey why other actors need to embrace the innovation, setting standards for the innovation, and using discourse to show how the innovation fit into norms (Kaganer et al., 2010; Marsan et al., 2020; Paré et al., 2020; Swanson & Ramiller, 1997; Wang & Swanson, 2007).

## 3 Theoretical Integration

## 3.1 Innovation Diffusion Theory and Organizing Vision Theory

OVT and IDT share similarities and differences. Both theories discuss the role of adopters, vendors, consultants, research institutes, standards organizations, and mass media. Actors in IDT, including adopters, act as communication channels interacting with potential adopters to transmit the innovation (Akhlaghpour & Lapointe, 2018). Potential adopters are also placed in the social system category to conceptualize the adopter community (Hu et al., 1997). OVT focuses on an interorganizational community consisting of actors with material interests in an innovation. However, the interactions represent this community’s public contemplation of new technology to make sense of the innovation at the community level, in contrast to a relatively private sensemaking effort discussed in IDT (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997).

The relative advantage concept is also applicable in both theories. According to IDT, potential adopters assess the relative advantage of an innovation based on information provided by external and internal influencers. In OVT, potential adopters make an adoption decision based on the business problematic linked to the OV and discussed by way of public discourse around the innovation, which in turn helps them assess the costs and benefits (i.e., the relative advantage) associated with the innovation (Marsan et al., 2012; Miranda et al., 2015; Ramiller & Swanson, 2003; Swanson & Ramiller, 1997).

The mechanisms in IDT are the influencers’ actions and the relative advantage perceived based on adopter profiles (Akhlaghpour & Lapointe, 2018). OVT mechanisms are mobilization, interpretation, and legitimation. In contrast to IDT mechanisms, OVT counterparts have broader outcomes because they foster diffusion by shaping the institutional field around an IT innovation. Thus, while both theories focus on the adoption and diffusion of technology as outcomes, OVT also focuses on commercial opportunities, community structure, and technology invention and adaptation as outcome variables (Nielsen et al., 2014; Swanson &

Ramiller, 1997). Moreover, the mechanisms posited by the two theories are related. For instance, an OV’s mobilization function forges a network of adopters to bring about strong ties needed for IDT’s internal influence mechanism (Strang & Soule, 1998).

Both theories discuss the influential roles different actors play in different phases based on their perceived benefits from the diffusion of an innovation. While OVT discusses which actors dominate the discourse at each phase (Wang & Ramiller, 2009), IDT posits that external influencers initially influence potential adopters, which is followed by influence based on firsthand experience by internal influencers (Jeyaraj & Sabherwal, 2014). Taken together, these two theories can better illuminate the diffusion process by (1) identifying the sources that shape the relative advantage—influencers and the business problematic linked to the OV, (2) highlighting the role of influencers—directly influencing potential adopters and shaping the OV, (3) conceptualizing the mechanisms fostering diffusion, and (4) explaining multiple outcomes at the adopter and the institutional levels.

## 3.2 Innovation Diffusion Theory and Institutional Entrepreneurship Theory

IDT and IET share both similarities and differences as well. Both theories focus on the interorganizational community surrounding an innovation, with IET focusing more on an IE that engages proactively with the adopter community for an innovation’s widespread adoption. Both theories discuss different actors playing an influential role at different phases based on their perceived benefits from an innovation’s diffusion.

Both theories also focus on the role of influencers and their strategies as mechanisms that drive the widespread adoption of an innovation. The influencers in IDT act as communication channels transmitting the relative advantage to potential adopters. Despite the influencer’s efforts, some innovations do not diffuse due to the existing unsupportive institutional arrangements, which reduce the benefits and increase the cost of adoption (i.e., lower the relative advantage). To address this, IET discusses the role of influencers, also called as IEs, who bring out the new institutional arrangements needed to foster innovation diffusion (Wang & Swanson, 2007). The IE’s actions go far beyond just communicating the relative advantage with potential adopters. They build relationships, mobilize resources, and develop rationale (Hardy & Maguire, 2017) to develop, test, fund, regulate, legitimize, and use the technology (Wang & Swanson, 2007), essentially increasing the relative advantage for potential adopters.

The mechanisms for innovation diffusion are also related in the two theories. For instance, IET suggests that we can expect vendors to fund media houses to campaign for an innovation and to author special sections in business magazines discussing the future of an industry (Wang & Swanson, 2008). Such actions create a market for the innovation at an institutional level. For potential adopters, these actions convey the relative advantage to be gained from adopting an innovation, thereby acting as the IDT’s external influence mechanism.

Regarding outcome variables, both theories also focus on the adoption and diffusion of a new technology. In addition, IET also focuses on institutional field change. The two theories taken together can better illuminate the diffusion process by conceptualizing the role of actors in (1) directly influencing potential adopters (as in IDT) and (2) developing an institutional field when current institutional arrangements do not support an innovation (as in IET).

## 3.3 Institutional Entrepreneurship Theory and Organizing Vision Theory

OVT and IET also share some similarities and differences. First, both theories highlight the role of the actor community in influencing institutional change even though they do so differently. On the one hand, OVT designates roles to each actor in the community that collectively creates the OV, namely vendors, adopters, research institutes, consultants, etc. (Davidson et al., 2015; Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). On the other hand, IET discusses one proactive actor embedded in the adopter community of the institutional field who occupies a position of power within that community and serves as an IE for institutional change (Hardy & Maguire, 2017; Marsan et al., 2020).

Both theories also focus on institutional change mechanisms. The OV in OVT interprets, legitimizes, and mobilizes to diffuse the IT innovation and make it the new paradigm (Amadoru et al., 2021; Kaganer et al., 2010). The IE in IET interprets the field, develops rationale, mobilizes resources, and develops relations to effect institutional change (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). While in OVT, diffusion mechanisms are performed by the institutional field-level construct OV on the actors in the field, in IET, individual actors (organizations or individuals) perform their actions to influence other actors in the institutional field toward institutional change.

The mechanisms in both theories are related. When an IE’s actions build rationale and develop relationships to understand an innovation, it represents the OV function of interpretation (Marsan et al., 2020). An IE using their resources to back an innovation and building rationale by publishing case studies represents the OV function of legitimation (Kaganer et al., 2010). When an IE builds relations to structure the community and mobilizes resources to activate the market, it represents the OV function of mobilization (Wang & Swanson, 2007). In a similar vein, OVT argues that, like an IE, certain opportunistic actors try to command the OV to suit their interests (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997).

Both theories also focus on the dynamics of the diffusion process. While OVT identifies the actors who are likely to dominate the OV at each phase of the IT innovation diffusion (Amadoru et al., 2021; Liao, 2016; Wang & Ramiller, 2009), IET discusses the actions performed by an IE at each stage of an institutional change (Morrill, 2006; Purdy & Gray, 2009).

Scholars have also integrated OVT and IET by arguing that IEs can enact an OV function when they perform actions that interpret, legitimize, and mobilize an IT innovation (Kaganer et al., 2010; Marsan et al., 2020; Paré et al., 2020; Wang & Swanson, 2007). Through these actions, ambitious players in an interorganizational community can carve out a preferred institutional environment where they can benefit from innovation diffusion and institutionalization. In line with this thought, Wang and Swanson (2007) studied the diffusion of professional services automation and proposed that IEs promote IT diffusion by (1) perceiving the opportunity for institutional transformation based on the likelihood of IT diffusion, (2) using discourse to incorporate compelling success stories from adopters and vendors to legitimize the innovation, and (3) mobilizing the community by urging the members to focus their attention on the innovation.

## 3.4 Moving from One Institution to the Next

We now integrate the three streams of literature from IDT, OVT, and IET and discuss how they collectively inform how an institutional field around an incumbent technology moves to a new state to foster the diffusion of a different new technology. The incumbent technology is the well-accepted and widely adopted means for solving a business problem. However, this technology is never perfect. New technologies are launched to address the incumbent technology’s problems and, in many cases, provide additional applications for the user (Fayard et al., 2016). Some actors may see a tremendous advantage in launching a new technology. But launching it requires massive and enduring new institutional arrangements necessitating an institutional field change, which faces major resistance from other actors in an institutional field (Marsan et al., 2012). The three theories discussed above inform this change process by which an institutional field moves from a prior state to a new state, as well as the role the key influencers play during this process. Below, we discuss how each perspective informs the institutional change process.

According to IDT, actors who see an advantage in a new technology serve as communication channels and transmit the innovation directly to the potential adopters to enhance the adopter’s relative advantage and foster adoption (Jeyaraj & Sabherwal, 2014). The diffusion is initially confined to venturesome and visionary adopters based on the innovation’s potential communicated by external influencers (Rogers, 2003). Subsequently, the innovation diffuses in the mainstream market once benefits are confirmed by members of the adopter community, i.e., the internal influencers (Rogers, 2003). Thus, the new technology becomes the dominant technology in the industry, replacing the incumbent one.

IET posits that actors with vested interests in the innovation strive to bring about institutional-level arrangements through rationale development, resource mobilization, and relationship development (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). Such actions promote diffusion through legitimacy, authorized practices, regulations, rules, interactor relationships, resource pools, community attention, norms, mandates, and rewards (Battilana et al., 2009; Hardy & Maguire, 2017; Purdy & Gray, 2009). Consequently, a new institutional field emerges surrounding an innovation whose adoption is taken for granted and advantageous.

According to OVT, opportunistic actors shape the discourse surrounding an innovation (Munir & Phillips, 2005) and enact the OV functions of interpretation (Amadoru et al., 2021; Kaganer et al., 2010), legitimation (Kaganer et al., 2010; Marsan et al., 2020; Paré et al., 2020; Wang & Swanson, 2007), and mobilization (Wang & Swanson, 2007) to promote the diffusion of the innovation. Such actions promote the material realization of the innovation through market offerings and support infrastructure, interactor relationships, standards, adoption manuals, technology development, and research. These actions motivate the community by promoting a common understanding of the technology’s uses, usefulness, legitimacy, and future (Davidson et al., 2015). Hence, potential adopters obtain institutional support for their adoption and draw upon the common understanding to shape their perception of the innovation’s business use cases (Marsan et al., 2012; Miranda et al., 2015; Ramiller & Swanson, 2003).

## 4 Research Design

## 4.1 Research Site Selection

We chose Walmart’s promotion of RFID in retailing as our research site because it is well-suited to this study for several reasons. First, RFID technology is an example of a codependent innovation. There are two main parts to this innovation, the RFID tag and the RFID reader. These two parts were adopted by two different adopter communities, suppliers and retailers, respectively. Second, because of the uncertainties in RFID adoption, including the retailer mandate, technology issues, and cost-benefit absorption, the criteria used to justify RFID investment in retailing were based on cost-benefit calculations and a mental image of the diffusion trajectory (Rogers, 2003). Therefore, both the individual calculus as well as collective beliefs about diffusion helped organizations consider their options when choosing whether or not to adopt RFID. This exemplifies the roles of the coadopter relative advantage and OV in the diffusion of RFID. Third, powerful initiators and vested interests characterized the process of early-stage RFID diffusion in retailing. The mandate by Walmart and other retailing giants were the most remarkable events that shaped the OV (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997) and the diffusion of RFID-in-retailing. The strategies of these influential actors with vested interests exemplify the role of institutional entrepreneurship in the early-stage diffusion process of RFID in retailing. Fourth, RFID diffusion was not just about upgrading technology; it was also about changing the institutional environment (Riggins & Slaughter, 2006) as well as transforming existing supply chains (Soon & Gutiérrez, 2008) and business models of retailing (DiMaggio, 1988, p. 15), because it would become an essential IT infrastructure in retailing after it was adopted. These four reasons make the diffusion of RFID-in-retailing directly pertinent and quite valuable to enhancing the understanding of the early-stage diffusion of codependent innovations.

## 4.2 Research Method

We used the case study research method, which is an empirical inquiry that analyzes the phenomenon under question in a real-life context (Myers, 1997). The case study method is best suited when studying processes (i.e., “why” and “how” questions), when the focus is on contemporary events and when the boundaries between the context and the phenomenon under study are not apparent (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 2008). Since we were studying the contemporary events and the process through which IEs influence the early-stage diffusion of a codependent innovation in an institutional field, we found that the case study methodology best suited our purpose. In our case study, we employed the interpretive philosophical perspective (Walsham, 1993). This philosophical perspective posits that reality can be understood only through social constructions such as language, consciousness, tools, documents, shared meanings, and other artifacts (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1993). Further, this perspective gave us the freedom to use theory as an initial guide (IDT, OVT, and IET in our paper) while also being open to the data collected from the case. Thus, the interpretive tradition allowed us to iterate between our data and the theory and, in the process, helped us refine, expand, or abandon the initial theory we started with (Walsham, 1995).

We used a case study reconstructed using 20 years of discourse surrounding RFID diffusion and promotion by Walmart in retailing. Based on the data (described in detail below) we collected from the discourse surrounding RFID technology, we used the narrative strategy to explicate the process of RFID diffusion. The narrative strategy involves the construction of a story from raw data (Bartis & Mitev, 2008; Langley, 1999; Myers, 1997; Pentland, 1999), which in our case is the data from the RFID discourse. Time is a key anchor point in this strategy (Langley, 1999). In this strategy, the researcher develops a chronology of events that serves to organize data and present theory (Langley, 1999). Using this strategy, we developed a chronology to uncover the timing and sequence of events championed by certain actors that led to the diffusion of RFID. Then we focused on Walmart to present a narrative of the early-stage diffusion of RFID in retailing.

## 4.3 Data Collection

For our analysis, we collected data about RFID-inretailing from the internet, disseminated through the following channels: trade press, academia, online journals, white papers, and analysis reports. We chose internet-based sources for the following reasons: First, internet-based data sources have been used to capture the status of RFID diffusion in research (Fish, 2006; Quaadgras, 2005). These sources are timeless and have paid great attention to Walmart’s RFID strategy. Second, most of the internet reports are based on official interviews conducted by journalists from leading media outlets. Although we did not conduct interviews in person, we used the interviews that are publicly available. Finally, text on the internet is an essential part of the RFID discourse that RFID players use as a reference.

We conducted a systematic effort to access publicly available information about RFID diffusion on the internet from 1999 to the end of 2019, thereby amassing 20 years of public discourse on RFID diffusion. We monitored several sources to understand industry events. Table 2 summarizes our data sources and the purpose of each particular source of data. We collected reports on RFID in retailing published in several online news services, including RFID Journal, The New York Times, The Wall Street Journal, InformationWeek, and Computerworld, among other similar sources. We also subscribed to several RFID-specific blogs including RFID Update, RFID Weblog, and RFID Gazette. We acquired white papers and analysis reports from consulting companies including IBM Business Consulting, A.T. Kearney, AMR Research, and others. We also obtained information from the RFID tracks of leading IS conferences (e.g., ICIS, HICSS, and INFORMS). Finally, we kept records of public statements on RFID released by Walmart<sup>2</sup> and other major players in retailing, such as Best Buy, Target, P&G, and Intermec to triangulate the validity of news reports. These sources formed a cross-reference network to ensure that our data not only represented the discourse surrounding RFID in retailing but were also detailed and comprehensive. All the data were stored in a qualitative research database for further review, retrieval, and interpretation.

We analyzed our data in three steps. First, using data from the above sources, we developed a chronology of key events in RFID diffusion in retailing as they evolved. The time horizon of the list ranged from December 1999 when the future of RFID in retailing was framed for the first time to the end of 2019 when RFID diffusion crossed the chasm. Thus, this list provides a comprehensive snapshot at different points in time as the diffusion of RFID in retailing unfolded. In particular, we focus on the key events of our case that relate to actions of the four key actors—RFID research institutes, retailers, suppliers, and RFID vendors—that pertain to the various institutional entrepreneurship strategies and functions of OV in the context of early-stage diffusion of codependent innovations along with the development of the RFID-inretailing institutional field. In our second step of data analysis, we used our theoretical concepts as sensitizing instruments to analyze our research questions (Nielsen et al., 2014; Patton, 2014). We pooled all the text documents and analyzed our data using the recursive pattern of interpretive qualitative research (Klein & Myers, 1999; Miles et al., 2018; Miles & Huberman, 1994; Nielsen et al., 2014; Walsham, 1993).

We identified four distinct phases heuristically based on the substance of the discourse and inspired by the distinct issues that needed to be addressed during those phases for the successful diffusion of RFID in retailing. We identified the cutoff points for the four phases of early-stage diffusion based on the coadopter relative advantage of RFID-in-retailing, specific OV functions enacted and IE strategies used by the IE at different points in time, and the extent of joint adoption of the RFID technology by retailers and their suppliers. The four periods range from 1999, when the RFID idea surfaced, to 2019, when RFID diffusion crossed the chasm. The issues to be addressed in the four RFID phases we identified are congruent with the issues and the discursive activities we identified by synthesizing the work on IDT (Rogers, 2003), and on phase models from IET (Morrill, 2006; Purdy & Gray, 2009) and OVT (Nielsen et al., 2014; Wang & Ramiller, 2009).

In each of these phases and in each article, we searched for the theoretical concepts we identified during our review of theoretical foundations. We read each article in our data line by line, coded them, and summarized them based on these concepts and their relationships (presented in Table 1) (Wang & Swanson, 2007). Table 1 provides coding examples for the concepts of interest. To identify the actors who played the role of an IE, the IE strategies they used, and the OV function they enacted, we focused on the actions of the four key organizational actors including RFID research institutes, retailers of consumer packaged goods, suppliers of consumer packaged goods, and RFID vendors because we found that these four actors were deeply involved in the early-stage diffusion and shaping of the RFID-in-retailing institutional field. Table 4 presents the actors who were part of the RFID-inretailing institutional field during the four phases of our study. We identified the actions performed by each actor in these four phases and coded which of the three IE strategies (rationale development, relationship development, and resource mobilization) the actor performed, and which of the three OV actions (interpretation, legitimation, and mobilization) the IE enacted by performing these actions. To attribute an action to an actor, we determined the cited contributor for each coded text using the categories of research institutes, retailers, suppliers, and vendors (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 2001). In the cases where the coded text belonged to white papers and analysis and press reports, we recorded the category as the organization that published these articles. To code<sup>3</sup> rationale development we coded all actions performed by an actor where they used the discursive realm to promote the IT innovation. To code resource mobilization, we coded all actions performed when actors used financial, political, social, and discursive resources to either gain support for the IT diffusion or to lever against the detractors (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). To code relationship development we coded all actions performed by an actor when they developed new relationships, such as collaborations, coalitions, and alliances, to get other actors to help diffuse the IT innovation (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). To code<sup>4</sup> enacting interpretation we coded all the actions the IE performed to convey the essential features of the innovations and their usefulness (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). To code enacting mobilization, we coded all of actions the IE performed to motivate and involve other actors in materially realizing the innovation (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997; Wang & Swanson, 2007). To code enacting legitimation, we coded all of the actions the IE performed to develop, test, refine, and legitimate the innovation (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997; Wang & Swanson, 2007).

## 5 Case Analysis and Theoretical Framework

## 5.1 Institutional Change from UPC Bar Codes to RFID in Retailing

Barcode technology has been the dominant institutional logic for supply chain management in retailing for the last four decades (Harford, 2017). A barcode consists of machine-readable black and white stripes printed on each product package (Harford, 2017). It encodes the universal product code (UPC) that uniquely identifies a product (Dodge & Kitchin, 2014). Upon scanning the code using a laser scanner, a user can look up a product’s type and price from the stock database (Dodge & Kitchin, 2014). Retailers use barcode scanners to more accurately and efficiently track and automate inventory. With just half a cent spent on printing each barcode label, suppliers can utilize inventory and sales data from retailers to improve their demand forecasts and better plan their production (McCathie, 2004). By encoding traceability information on the barcode, suppliers can enhance shipment tracking, quality control, and product recalls (McCathie, 2004). However, barcodes have limitations, such as a minimal information-holding capacity, line-of-sight optical scanning requiring human involvement, and low durability (McCathie, 2004). For supply chain management, this could mean that barcodes are laborintensive, prone to human errors, restricted in traceability and visibility, incapable of item-level tracking, and sensitive to environmental conditions (McCathie, 2004).

Compared to barcodes, RFID technology is a significant advancement in representing and communicating product information. RFID tags consist of an embedded circuit that encodes product information (such as price, unique identification, stock number, batch number, date of production, etc.) and an antenna that broadcasts this data (Dodge & Kitchin, 2014). An RFID reader can query the information transmitted by the tag from a considerable distance, even if the RFID reader is not in the line of sight. The RFID technology addresses all the limitations of the barcode technology. It is durable, reliable, accurate, carries rich information that is updateable throughout the supply chain, eliminates line-of-sight scanning, affords item-level tracking and simultaneous automatic scanning, and requires less labor (McCathie, 2004).

RFID’s superior capability offered retailers and suppliers a coadopter relative advantage over the barcode technology based on different sets of benefits and costs. Retailers could benefit from RFID through enhanced product visibility, traceability, and availability, accurate sales estimates, reduced out-ofstocks, fewer expired products, increased labor productivity, faster replenishment rates, and faster unloading times (Kaplan, 2018b; McCathie, 2004). The costs of RFID adoption for the retailers were a fixed cost of installing RFID readers and the supporting IT infrastructure. Suppliers could benefit from RFID from promotions tracking, shipments tracking, theft prevention, accurate sales data from a retailer, targeted recalls, and counterfeit protection (McCathie, 2004). The costs of RFID adoption for suppliers are the recurring cost of attaching RFID tags to each product. However, their benefits are not immediate. Only economies of scale in the RFID tag production resulting from huge demand can reduce the costs for suppliers. In addition, suppliers bear the costs of tagging as they are most likely to attach the RFID tags in almost all supply chain implementations because item-level tags need to be attached to the carton wrapping of consumer packaged goods or sewn into clothing material (Basker & Simcoe, 2021; Gaukler et al., 2007). It is not economically feasible to tag at the retailer location because the tagging and encoding equipment must be present at each location (Basker & Simcoe, 2021; Gaukler et al., 2007). In essence, although RFID-in-retailing offered a coadopter relative advantage to both retailer and supplier communities, the coadopter relative advantage was systematically higher for the retailer community beginning with the early stage.

We noted earlier that an organization in the coadopter community that perceived the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage (i.e., the retailer community) sensed the opportunity of an institutional change from UPC bar codes to RFID in retailing. Among all the retailing players, Walmart perceived a high coadopter relative advantage in adopting RFID-inretailing from a very early stage. Walmart’s strategy is to offer low prices to customers, reflected in its slogan “Everyday Low Prices” (Hyde, 2019). Cutting operational costs through innovative retail supply chain technologies has been one way Walmart can provide low prices to customers—in fact, it was Walmart that pioneered the diffusion of barcode technology (Hyde, 2019). Given all the potential benefits discussed above, Walmart sensed that RFID technology would help them provide lower prices to their customers. Walmart felt that it could leverage its market dominance, cuttingedge expertise, and power over suppliers to diffuse RFID. More importantly, Walmart perceived lower costs with higher benefits compared to suppliers who had higher costs with no immediate benefits (Kaplan, 2018b). Thus, Walmart perceived a high degree of coadopter relative advantage in RFID and focused on introducing RFID as a next-generation supply chain technology in retailing.

Walmart faced several challenges in introducing RFID in retailing. Barcodes had been firmly embedded in retailing for four decades. It was the supply chain management standard around which retailers and suppliers trained their personnel and built their infrastructure and processes (McCathie, 2004). For suppliers, barcodes were easy to use and affordable (McCathie, 2004). It was a mature technology. Despite that, there were ongoing innovations to advance the technology (McCathie, 2004). Universally accepted and understood standards existed for encoding labels and the design and development of barcode equipment (McCathie, 2004). Most importantly, all actors accepted the benefits of barcode technology in retailing. Thus, introducing RFID-in-retailing was an institutional fieldshaping project for Walmart.

Introducing RFID meant that Walmart had to tackle resistance from suppliers because Walmart’s coadopter relative advantage from adopting RFID readers would only bear fruit if suppliers started affixing RFID tags to their product packaging. In the early stages, suppliers might have perceived risk in embracing a revolutionary technology for one retailer only. Typically, one supplier supplies the same products to multiple retailers, many of them large ones (Malone, 2012). If the rest of the retailers do not adopt RFID, the supplier has to implement the new RFID technology for Walmart but continues using the barcode technology with perhaps even a larger share of their business (Malone, 2012). Such an investment might be too much of a risk because the supplier’s returns from RFID may be for naught if a newer technology surpasses the RFID technology and retailers leapfrog to the new technology (Goldenberg & Oreg, 2007; Guo & Chen, 2018; Huang, 2011; Malone, 2012). For example, retailers might leapfrog to wireless sensors by sensing their superiority over RFID technology (Mayberry, 2005). Because RFID provides no short-term benefits and higher costs for suppliers, research institutes might find it challenging to obtain funding from suppliers. Vendors might hesitate to develop and market RFID solutions until a critical mass of retailers and suppliers adopt the RFID tags and readers (Basker & Simcoe, 2021; Gaukler et al., 2007; Malone, 2012). Such a stalemate would deter standardsetting bodies, resulting in a lack of accepted standards to move the technology forward. Given these tensions inherent to bringing about an institutional change in support of RFID in retailing, we conducted a research project on Walmart’s efforts to influence the early-stage diffusion of the RFID-in-retailing innovation.

## 5.2.1 Phase 1: Emergence

The emergence phase in the early-stage diffusion process for a codependent innovation is when an OV around a new technology such as RFID starts taking shape, and a community begins to form around this vision (Wang & Ramiller, 2009). It is during this phase when concrete ideas for the application of a technology in commercial endeavors start being developed (Greenwood et al., 2002). External influencers— research institutes and inventors—are accordingly expected to take center stage in the institutional field surrounding the innovation (Liao, 2016; Rogers, 2003; Wang & Ramiller, 2009) and are expected to play the role of an IE during this phase. At this point in the innovation process, it is these entities who have the most knowledge about and the most interest in the potential applications of the new technology for solving business problems. Therefore, it is these players who are expected to promote and spur the community discourse toward the potential applications and benefits of the innovation in the business world (Rogers, 2003; Wang & Ramiller, 2009). These entities perform research and development activities to show the feasibility of the innovation and its business value, draft standards, and develop hardware and software for the innovation. They team up with adopters who fund these entities’ activities. Vendors also observe these entities to understand how they can build and sell these innovations (Nielsen et al., 2014; Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). Such events, championed by research institutions, signal the emergence of an institutional field. But in the case of RFID diffusion, we found that it was Walmart, the coadopter of one complementary part of the innovation that played the role of an IE and led the emergence of the RFID-inretailing institutional field.

Until October 1999, the RFID-in-retailing institutional field did not exist. There was neither an actor community involved in the RFID-in-retailing innovation nor was there a public discourse around this innovation. Early in the RFID development, it was Walmart that realized the benefits of RFID. Walmart envisioned RFID as the next-generation supply-chain technology through which it could find efficient ways to reduce its operational costs and build newer trade models (Haberman, 1999). Suppliers were excited about RFID as a tool for tracking shipments and managing cargo theft but were concerned about the high cost of tagging (Violino, 2002). In spite of the massive costs of installing RFID readers and the supporting infrastructure, Walmart decided to leverage RFID, given its cutting-edge IS expertise and prior experience in pioneering several important supply chain management technologies (Violino, 2002). So, Walmart perceived the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage.

Walmart focused on building a case for RFID’s use in retail in the emergence phase because Walmart’s initial RFID pilots with IBM in the early 1990s faded out (Violino, 2002). RFID in retailing was never commercialized due to the high cost of technology, low sales volumes, lack of open standards, and IBM’s financial troubles (Roberti, 2005a). The Auto-ID Center, a nonprofit research institute (Roberti, 2002) located at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) (Roberti, 2002) was founded with support from suppliers, Walmart, other retailers, and standard-setting organizations. Walmart was the first retail member of the Auto-ID Center (Ashton, 2003; Violino, 2002). By funding and collaborating with the Auto-ID Center, Walmart played an influential role in the Auto-ID Center’s quest for the RFID rationale (Roberti, 2002). Walmart conducted both public and secret pilots (Roberti, 2003b, 2014) with interested suppliers to test RFID technology (Gilbert, 2003; Semilof, 2001). Walmart created a buzz in the public discourse by using terms such as “smart shelves” (Gilbert, 2003).

Sanjay Sarma, a professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and a co-founder of the Auto-ID Center, mentioned four turning points in RFID diffusion, one of which happened during the emergence phase and used Walmart as the IE. Referring to Walmart joining the Auto-ID Center, he wrote:

In 2001, Wal-Mart joined the Auto-ID Center, and this move would prove to be a very important event. For a company renowned for its innovative but grounded attitude to join a research effort meant that the research was getting close to practicality. (Garfinkel & Rosenberg, 2006; Wehr, 2005)

Walmart coordinated the knowledge-building activities during this phase even though research institutes and inventors played a key role by serving as a hub for creating knowledge about RFID. The Auto-ID Center was looking at all possible applications of RFID, but Walmart pioneered the idea and tested the use of RFID in retailing. Referring to Walmart’s planned trials (which were later shelved) (Violino, 2003b), Kevin Ashton, a Procter & Gamble executive and executive director of the Auto-ID Center, said:

Until now, the Auto-ID Center’s tests have involved identifying and tracking the location of shipping containers in warehouses. The tests starting this month at Wal-Mart and Tesco will be among the first major trials of the technology developed with the Auto-ID Center to track individual items on store shelves ... It’s the last Russian doll. It’s the hardest thing, and it’s what we’ve been working toward. (Gilbert, 2003)

The role of vendors was minimal at this stage because they were still analyzing the feasibility of building and selling RFID technology. The other efforts in this period included RFID journal startups (Roberti, 2003a), RFID improvement efforts of scholars and universities (Lee et al., 2004), RFID startups (Impinj, 2000), and business media selling the idea of RFID (Anderson, 2002; 2004; Bacheldor, 2004; Collins, 2003; Johnson, 2005; Jones et al., 2005; Kevin, 2005). Overall, Walmart’s actions in funding research institutes and conducting trials in collaboration with research institutes and suppliers reflect the institutional entrepreneurship strategy of rationale development.

All of Walmart’s activities, such as research funding, standards setting, and pilot testing, were geared toward providing an understanding of the RFID innovation and its uses in retailing. Walmart’s efforts focused on two main questions: What is RFID good for? How does RFID work? Through these efforts, Walmart accumulated and aggregated common knowledge about the emerging RFID-in-retailing institutional field. Walmart’s actions led to buzzwords in the public discourse. For example, when Walmart planned pilot tests, one headline from a reputed online news magazine said, “Major retailers to test ‘smart shelves’” (Gilbert, 2003). Another expert called RFID tags “license plates” (Nelson, 2001). Chana Schoenberger of Forbes titled her article “RFID: The Internet of Things.” Interpreting Walmart’s pilots, she wrote, “Stores have eyes. Now they’re getting ears and brains. Soon tiny wireless chips stuck on shampoo bottles and jeans will track all that you wear and buy” (Schoenberger, 2002). Walmart’s actions during this phase represent an actor enacting the OV function of interpretation.

There were also a few instances where Walmart’s actions represented enacting mobilization and legitimation. Walmart mobilized the actors in the institutional field by increasing their awareness about RFID and encouraging them to act on this awareness. However, the project was only getting close to practicality. Walmart championing the RFID project lent legitimacy to RFID, but the legitimation of RFID in retailing was not fully there. Not convinced of the merits of RFID, Andy Robson, CHEP’s business development manager for supply chain information services, said: “Certainly, we believe the Intermec/Marconi/Savi solution that we’re now working with is the best available in the market, but because it’s in a pilot context, we still believe there is further and better technology coming down the line” (Douglas, 2002; Gilbert, 2003). This leads to our first proposition:

Proposition 1: The IE for a codependent innovation will use the strategy of rationale development and enact the OV function of interpretation during the emergence phase of the early-stage diffusion process for that codependent innovation.

The extent of joint adoption at the end of this phase could be best described as one restricted to pilot tests by powerful retailers with research institutes and interested suppliers. Suppliers would not adopt RFID technology until the cost of tagging was reduced and they saw evidence of RFID benefits. Vendors would not achieve economies of scale in production for them to be able to provide low-cost RFID solutions until there was sufficient joint adoption. One CNET report aptly summarized the extent of diffusion:

A major barrier to the broad adoption of smart-shelf technology in the thin-margin businesses of mass-market retail and consumer commodities has been the high cost of the equipment. For the tags alone, manufacturers can expect to pay 30 cents per tag at a minimum, according to one developer, Alien Technology in Morgan Hill, Calif., which is supplying Gillette with its tags. At that price, it’s unfeasible to embed the tags on, say, a 50-cent bar of soap. That’s why Alien Technology and others are working to lower the cost of tags to 5 cents to 10 cents over the next several of years. Even so, Ashton said widespread adoption of RFID could take until 2010—if it ever does occur. (Gilbert, 2003)

## 5.2.2 Phase 2: Structuralization

The structuralization phase in the early-stage diffusion process for a codependent innovation starts after the initial discourse in the innovation community during the emergence phase has led to the formation of the fundamental ideas and notions about the potential applications of the new technology for solving business problems. While discourse will continue in the community throughout the innovation life cycle, now is the time when the various actors in the innovation community need to take action to further the diffusion agenda (Morrill, 2006; Purdy & Gray, 2009). During this phase, the vendors who produce the technologies that are part of the innovation are expected to play the role of an IE as external influencers because they want to sell their products to the adopter community (Rogers, 2003; Wang & Ramiller, 2009). Based on the guidelines from research institutes and inventors, technology vendors build and test their products (Nielsen et al., 2014). Using marketing campaigns, vendors promote the benefits of the technology and try to sell the innovation to adopters through marketing avenues such as tech fairs, press releases, and advertisements (Paré et al., 2020; Rogers, 2003; Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). Vendors also team up with research institutes, consultants, and adopters to learn how to improve the technology and its production (Nielsen et al., 2014). The innovation is realized during this phase in the form of vendor-developed products that are ready to be adopted by prospective adopters. Adopters, some of which experiment with the vendors’ technology (Rogers, 2003), also contemplate the widespread adoption of the innovation based on community discourse. But in the case of RFID diffusion, we found that it was Walmart, the coadopter of one complementary part of the innovation, that played the role of the IE and championed the structuralization of the RFID-inretailing institutional field.

The ongoing pilots and other interpretation activities helped Walmart reassess the costs and benefits of RFID (InformationWeek, 2001; TalkBusiness, 2004; Murphy, 2003). In this phase, Walmart further crystalized the benefits of RFID—better product tracking and inventory management—perceived in the prior phase (TalkBusiness, 2004). As a downstream site in the retail supply chain, Walmart did not have to bear the recurring cost of RFID tagging (Dutta et al., 2007). Walmart just needed to bear the fixed costs of installing RFID readers and the supporting IT infrastructure. Being the largest retailer, Walmart had suppliers depending on them for their business (Roberti, 2003a). Suppliers were enthusiastic about shipment tracking, promotions management, and reducing out-of-stocks (InformationWeek, 2001; Murphy, 2005). However, suppliers had concerns about immature technology (TalkBusiness, 2004; Violino, 2003c), the risk of RFID becoming obsolete (Violino, 2003c), disruption to current operations (Violino, 2003c), lesser reliability compared to barcodes (Wired, 2003; Violino, 2003c), the high cost of tags (Violino, 2003c), and the lack of short-term returns given huge investments in RFID tagging (TalkBusiness, 2004; Wehr, 2004). So, Walmart perceived the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage.

Walmart understood that actions on its part were needed to get suppliers to adopt RFID tags in order for its coadopter relative advantage to bear fruit. At a retailing conference in June 2003, Walmart issued a mandate asking its top suppliers to adopt RFID technology, which was a substantial push for the diffusion of RFID-in-retailing innovation (RFIDJournal, 2003, 2004b). Walmart focused on pallet- and case-level RFID in its mandate with the objective of improving the inventory accuracy in its warehouses as opposed to item-level tagging, which would have meant disruption to its in-store operations and even heavier investments in installing readers for store shelves and points of sale (O’Connor, 2014; Roberti, 2010a). At the same time, using its massive resources, Walmart deployed RFID systems in almost 12 distribution centers and 1,000 stores. With the tagged cases and pallets, Walmart conducted several pilots to evaluate RFID in real scenarios (Whitcomb & Gallagher, 2004). Eight consumer packaged goods manufacturers participated in an RFID trial in Walmart’s distribution centers in April 2004 to assess the technology in the real world (Whitcomb & Gallagher, 2004). Walmart joined the board of directors of EPCglobal, an organization chosen by actors in the retail industry to develop standards for RFID (WalmartFact, 2005). Walmart urged vendors to exhibit their products in industry conferences, collaborate on pilot tests, and to give price discounts and technology support to suppliers. To ensure continued RFID diffusion, Walmart reduced the number of pallets that required tagging and extended the mandate deadline when suppliers complained about the lack of benefits (Wailgum, 2004). Overall, on one hand, Walmart substantially raised the demand for RFID technology through mandates. On the other hand, Walmart encouraged the supply, affordability, and maturity of the technology through vendor motivation, investments in research, and standardsetting efforts. Walmart’s actions during this phase reflect the institutional entrepreneurship strategy of resource mobilization.

Although vendors took the required steps at this stage, Walmart played the influencer role in the diffusion of RFID during this phase. In fact, RFID vendors built their RFID plans around Walmart, mainly through testing and cost cutting. Sun Microsystems, which built a Walmart-compliant test center, was one such example (Sun, 2003). Suppliers had to comply even if they did not want to because they were highly dependent on Walmart. As Pete Abell, co-founder of the EPC Group Ltd., an independent consulting company, frames it, “You can’t [ignore the mandate] if 10 percent to 40 percent of your business is going through Wal-Mart” (Roberti, 2003a). Even with research institutes and standard-setting bodies, it was Walmart that played an influential role. Sanjay Sarma identified the mandate as the final turning point in RFID diffusion:

The final, and perhaps most powerful, step in the gathering momentum behind RFID was the Walmart “mandate” announced in the middle of 2003… Starting from there, the expansion would encompass the entire supply chain. Nothing riveted attention more than the largest corporation on the planet making an announcement of this magnitude. (Garfinkel & Rosenberg, 2006; Wehr, 2005)

In the structuralization phase, Walmart actively contributed to the discourse by communicating its RFID adoption strategy. “By 2006, we will roll it out with all suppliers,” said Walmart spokesman Tom Williams in 2003 when Walmart first spelled out its mandate (Roberti, 2003a). Such statements sketching the diffusion plan set the tone and trajectory for the actors in the structuralization phase. Walmart shaped the public discourse not only through official statements about RFID mandates, but also in more tangible ways, such as involving resource commitment in its pilot tests and participating in EPCglobal. When some of Walmart’s suppliers were not able to meet the January 2005 adoption deadline, Walmart tried to lower the proportion of tagging to 65% and postponed the deadline to keep its RFID initiative alive (Sliwa, 2004 ). Also, when suppliers complained about the lack of benefits, Walmart kept downplaying the cost of tagging to avoid any hindrance to the diffusion (Vijayan & Brewin, 2003). Walmart indirectly shaped the discourse by urging its suppliers to organize a forum to share the lessons learned from the field operation.

The mandate effectively mobilized the actors in the institutional field surrounding RFID. Walmart’s influence on suppliers and other retailers meant that vendors envisioned a large and sustainable market for RFID. This market potential attracted vast sums of capital that were invested in RFID start-ups. Soon after Walmart imposed the mandate, other major retailers such as Target (RFIDJournal, 2004a), Best Buy (Roberti, 2004), Albertsons (PRNewswire, 2004), Tesco (IDTechEx, 2005a), and Metro (RFIDUpdate, 2005) issued similar mandates and RFID pilot requests. Giant manufacturers also became eager to carry out RFID pilot tests. This market potential also stimulated RFID vendors to release new products (Capgemini, 2004) and provide a substantial discount for suppliers who purchased RFID tags in bulk, with discounts in some cases ranging up to 70% (Swedberg, 2007a). The competition among RFID vendors also emerged because of Walmart’s strategic actions. Vendors started soliciting input from suppliers in devising their solutions and began offering attractive discounts. The slap-and-ship<sup>7</sup> solution was invented to attract suppliers who wanted to comply with Walmart’s mandate at the lowest cost (Swedberg, 2007a). Small suppliers also began seeking affordable RFID compliance solutions. The standard-setting body, EPCglobal, was set up and began to release standards to meet Walmart’s pace (RFIDUpdate, 2004). The media started following the RFID compliance movement in a more concerted way and started guessing the result of the 2005-Sunrise mandate (IDTechEx, 2005b; Feder, 2004; Hill, 2004 ; Incucomm, 2005).

Based on the above discussion, Walmart’s actions during this phase represent an actor enacting the OV function of mobilization. “The fact that the largest company in the world is publicly adopting EPC open standards should give companies confidence that the day of a single, interoperable RFID system is close at hand,” said Kevin Ashton, executive director of the Auto-ID Center (Vijayan & Brewin, 2003). Walmart’s mandated diffusion model provided an answer for how RFID diffusion can happen in retailing. Walmart’s actions denoting interpretation and legitimation were less prevalent in this phase. Rather than focusing on what RFID technology could do, Walmart was keen on mobilizing the field to get RFID adopted. It did so by focusing on the feasibility and success of its mandate. At the end of this phase, reflecting on the RFID strategy, Linda Dillman, executive vice president and chief information officer for Walmart, summed up the mandate: “I don’t have to stand up here and debate whether it will happen, because it did” (IDTechEx,

2005b). Walmart’s market power downplayed the concerns about the mandated diffusion model. For example, when confronted with the lack of common protocols and standards around RFID, Keith Mahoney, Walmart’s food logistics controller, said, “We could not allow the lack of them to hang up the project” (Vijayan & Brewin, 2003). This leads to our second proposition:

Proposition 2: The IE for a codependent innovation will use the strategy of resource mobilization and enact the OV function of mobilization during the structuralization phase of the early-stage diffusion process for that codependent innovation.

The extent of joint adoption at the end of this phase was characterized by suppliers adopting RFID tags purely to satisfy retailers’ mandates. Suppliers faced problems such as the immaturity of RFID standards and the high costs of RFID products. For small suppliers, tight budgets and lack of expertise made compliance impossible, even by the deadline. The retailers, including Walmart, installed RFID equipment in their stores and distribution centers. In March 2005, Walmart made a statement to summarize its mandate: 127 suppliers sent almost 5 million tags to Walmart (Whitcomb & Gallagher, 2005). Walmart itself deployed 14,000 sets of RFID equipment in its 104 stores and 35 Sam’s Club locations (IDTechEx, 2005b). Patrick Ervin, Vice President of Business Development, Retail Supply Chain Vertical, Alien Technology’s aptly summarized the extent of joint adoption:

It’s clear that Wal-Mart is more committed than ever. They’re going to another 200 vendors. They’re adding store sites and they’re increasing the number of SKUs that they’re tagging. They have a large investment and they’re not backing off. (RFIDJournal, 2005)

## 5.2.3 Phase 3: Evolution

The excitement and action that was generated during the structuralization phase may taper when implementation complexities and technology glitches are found during early implementations of the technology, and if the return on investment (ROI) is not in line with what was touted. This dissonance between vision and reality marks the transition of the early-stage diffusion process into the new evolution phase. Now, is the time to focus on increasing the normative appeal of the innovation and convincing the innovation community that these early problems are nothing to be disheartened about and that the ROI can be generated for all parties by making suitable improvements to technologies, implementation methods, and organizational processes and practices (Marsan et al., 2020; Nielsen et al., 2014). Accordingly, during the evolution phase, suppliers are expected to play the role of an IE due to their own vested interests at this point in terms of reducing their own costs and even gaining advantage over their competitors from the innovation that has now been accepted as a valid innovation in the field (Rogers, 2003; Wang & Ramiller, 2009). Suppliers are expected to work with academia and research institutes to devise standards and improve the innovation’s ROI (Purdy & Gray, 2009; Tolbert & Zucker, 1983). Suppliers are also expected to convey the results of pilot tests to the community to attract more retail customers and more retail business from current customers touting the benefits of the RFID technology. As more suppliers foster the innovation, vendors can offer RFID tags at lower costs. However, contrary to these expectations, we found that in the case of RFID diffusion, it was Walmart, the coadopter of one complementary part of the innovation, who played the role of an IE and led to the evolution of the RFID-inretailing institutional field.

Among the adopters, it was again Walmart that had the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage from RFID diffusion. Walmart already significantly invested in the RFID project by installing RFID equipment in stores, distribution centers, and Sam’s Club locations. Walmart could get more out of the investments only when more suppliers adopt RFID because there would be more data to capture, share, and leverage within and across organizations (Malone, 2012). Suppliers also saw benefits in RFID. The benefits for suppliers included maintenance of business with Walmart, and long-term supply chain optimization if the RFID technology sustains and matures. In addition, a supplier might lose its competitive advantage by getting technologically obsolete if other suppliers start adopting RFID and reduce their supply chain costs. However, compared to the structuralization phase, suppliers sensed even higher costs and the lack of ROI in an immature technology that is primarily going to benefit the retailers. Walmart understood further actions were needed to convince suppliers that there is a good ROI from adopting RFIDin-retailing technology because its coadopter relative advantage was contingent upon suppliers also adopting RFID tags.

Walmart’s actions during this phase reflect the institutional entrepreneurship strategy of relationship development. First, Walmart took several steps to identify and assess the ROI in RFID implementation for suppliers. Walmart commissioned a research study with the University of Arkansas. The result of the study— reduction in out-of-stocks, faster replenishment rate, and reduction in manual orders—encouraged the entire community (WalmartFact, 2005). This finding coupled with internal assessments shifted Walmart’s RFID focus from distribution-center use to in-store use (Songini, 2007b) and expanded its RFID deployment plan (WalmartFact, 2006). Second, Walmart collaborated with another retailer, Target for a joint pilot to share EPC data with suppliers (Roberti, 2005d). Third, Walmart pursued actions to mature, standardize and spur demand for the technology. Walmart upgraded its facilities to next-generation RFID standards and asked suppliers to upgrade to these RFID standards citing better performance and lower cost (O’Connor, 2006c). They polled suppliers to learn about their readiness in adopting the new standards (O’Connor, 2006c). Inspired by this move, some RFID chip manufacturers began introducing new RFID products. “We wanted to kickstart the industry, so to speak, which would help spur demand for our silicon,” said Impinj president and chief executive officer William Colleran during the unveiling of next-generation tags and readers (O’Connor & Roberti, 2005). However, these actions never established the ROI for suppliers. Except for a few suppliers like Kimberly-Clark, Unilever, and P&G, who declared benefits from RFID (KimberlyClark, 2006; Songini, 2007a), most suppliers still suffered from unstable technology, high costs, implementation problems, and few benefits (Fabris, 2007; Wailgum, 2004).

There was less cooperation from other actors in the institutional field. Proactive suppliers began withdrawing radical RFID implementation plans, and many smaller suppliers only minimally adopted RFID. The installed base resulting from Walmart’s mandate was not big enough to achieve economies of scale since most suppliers took a passive attitude and purchased as little as they could (Fabris, 2007). Many vendors began postponing new product releases, citing the costs involved in producing equipment according to new standards and minimal adoption from suppliers (O’Connor, 2006b; Roberti, 2005b). “It’s a classic Catch 22 situation. In order for prices on radio frequency identification (RFID) tags to fall to what has been described as the ‘magic 5-cent’ mark, manufacturers need to see more wide-spread adoption of the technology”, wrote Mel Duvall, a veteran business and technology journalist (Duvall, 2007a). Other retailers retreated from an aggressive implementation plan even after they had confirmed the benefits of RFID through pilot tests (Duvall, 2007b). Complaints about the RFID mandate became a public secret (Gardner, 2004; Hudson; McWilliams, 2007). Rapid introduction of newer standards (O’Connor, 2006a), patent wars, royalty declaration issues, and a short time gap to transition between standards made the above challenges more daunting (O’Connor, 2006b; Roberti, 2005b).

Walmart understood that some concrete actions were again needed to ensure that their RFID initiative did not fizzle out. It saw the potential benefits of RFID for itself but also understood the hesitation of other members of the RFID community, particularly those of suppliers and RFID vendors, in investing in RFID due to a lack of ROI. To break this deadlock, Walmart came up with three initiatives to continue extending RFID diffusion. In the middle of 2007, it further pushed the agenda of RFID-in-retailing by unveiling the three new initiatives: mandating RFID use on entire specific product categories, deploying whole-process RFID in Sam’s Club, and applying RFID on weekly product promotions (O’Connor, 2007). The initiative in Sam’s Club was rolled out in January 2008 (RFIDUpdate, 2008). During this phase, Walmart pursued its RFID initiative using its technical capabilities, resources, and dominant power to provide legitimacy to the RFID-inretailing innovation and to continue promoting the diffusion of this codependent innovation when doubts about it started emerging.

Walmart’s actions during this phase reflect an actor enacting the OV function of legitimation. Walmart’s actions focused more on answering the following question for the actor community: How can the value of RFID be realized? The community asked for financial justification for their RFID adoption. Walmart projected the next-generation Gen 2 technology as the answer to the financial justification (RFIDUpdate, 2004). Walmart hosted a chief information officer summit and announced the change from Gen 1 to Gen 2 tags. Simon Langford, Walmart’s manager of RFID strategies, called the new tags a “breakthrough” (O’Connor, 2006c), thereby shaping the discourse. To add legitimacy, he quashed the current reports about ROI and added, “I think a lot of the [tag] studies that have been done prior [to the introduction of near-field tags] are invalid, and they need to be done again” (O’Connor, 2006c). He concluded by saying, “The whole [playing] field has been changed now” (O’Connor, 2006c). By forcing the Gen 1 to Gen 2 standards change, Walmart promoted chip manufacturers to design and display their products at major summits (O’Connor, 2006c). The promotions were aimed at how the new products would reduce the cost of RFID. During one of their product demonstrations, RFID manufacturer Impinj’s president and chief executive officer William Colleran mentioned, “An RFID reader is no more complicated than an 802.11 Wi-Fi router. … And we’ve seen the price of Wi-Fi routers fall from many hundreds of dollars to less than \$100” (O’Connor & Roberti, 2005). Walmart tried hard to lead the whole community toward novel scenarios in which the benefits of RFID might be realized. At the beginning of 2008, at an annual member conference, Walmart spelled out three new initiatives: A mandate to Sam’s Club’s suppliers, applying RFID in weekly promotions, and pilots to test if RFID can improve sales across an entire product category (O’Connor, 2007). Dubbing this move as a “change in focus,” Carolyn Walton, Walmart’s VP of IT said: “We’re coming at [RFID] from a different angle” (O’Connor, 2007). This leads to our third proposition:

Proposition 3: The IE for a codependent innovation will use the strategy of relationship development and enact the OV function of legitimation during the evolution phase of the early-stage diffusion process for that codependent innovation.

In January 2008, in order to take a firmer stance on RFID, Walmart followed up its new mandate by imposing deadlines, and for the very first time, it charged a service fee of up to \$3 per nontagged pallet (RFIDJournal, 2009b). Compared to how they reacted to Walmart’s 2003 mandate, suppliers did not show great urgency, considering how accommodating Walmart had been for noncompliant suppliers in the prior mandate (RFIDJournal, 2008a). Suppliers wanted to evaluate the benefits of the new mandate requirements (RFIDJournal, 2008a). Vendors were getting demand for RFID tagging mainly from Walmart and Sam’s Club suppliers (RFIDJournal, 2008b). However, in January 2009, Walmart significantly relaxed its mandate requirements, reducing the fee and indefinitely postponing the deadline after considering the lack of benefits for suppliers and the time required to comply (RFIDJournal, 2009b). Sam’s Club established a supplier council to help suppliers identify benefits from each type of RFID tagging (RFIDJournal, 2009b). As another big blow to Walmart’s campaign, P&G withdrew from the pilot programs it was conducting with Walmart (RFIDJournal, 2009a; SCDigest, 2009)—an event that industry experts felt might have informed the community about the lack of benefits of RFID technology (FierceRetail, 2009). Overall, Walmart overestimated its ability to drive institutional change—it overpromised but underdelivered by setting up a very ambitious timeline for its RFID vision.

At the end of the evolution phase, all actors were dormant with respect to their RFID activities. Walmart chose not to unveil any new initiative until it worked closely with the suppliers and found RFID benefits for them. Suppliers, vendors, and research institutes were waiting for the next definitive move from Walmart. The extent of joint adoption at the end of this phase could be best described as one where retailers (including Walmart) were cautious about the further installation of RFID systems in their distribution centers. Suppliers adopted the minimum required technology. Compared to what it had originally envisioned, Walmart installed fewer RFID systems in its own distribution centers and influenced fewer suppliers to embrace RFID. One industry expert said:

Five years into the RFID revolution, it’s clear that implementation is occurring more slowly than Walmart originally envisioned…. Today, 600 of Walmart’s 60,000 suppliers (plus 750 Sam’s Club suppliers) have deployed RFID. On Walmart’s end, RFID has been rolled out at 1,000 of the roughly 4,000 Walmart and Sam’s Club stores in the United States. Walmart’s original goal was to have 12 of its roughly 120 distribution centers outfitted for RFID by 2006. Today, only five are set up for RFID, as the company has shifted its RFID focus to in-store implementations. (Webster, 2008)

Walmart abandoned the RFID mandate considering the cost of alienating the suppliers and the flak it received from the press (Roberti, 2010b). Walmart did not want to force suppliers with additional expenses given the global financial crisis (Roberti, 2010b). The innovation diffusion literature shows a chasm between the early stage and the growth stage of the diffusion process (Moore, 2014). It, therefore, does not come as a surprise to know that the diffusion of RFID in retailing suffered a similar fate (Malone, 2012; Roberti, 2017). The diffusion went into an abyss and near death only to rise again.

## 5.2.4 Phase 4: Chasm

The innovator suppliers’ success stories to legitimize the innovation do not serve as good references for suppliers in the growth stage because the latter are risk averse, price sensitive, fear change, and look for incremental performance improvements (Moore, 2014; Rogers, 2003; Xu et al., 2017). These adopters buy whole products that are easily implementable, backed by mature technology, and sold by leading vendors. Innovations often do not cross the early stage, which results in a chasm because growth-stage adopters are a self-referencing group; they will not adopt until the ROI is confirmed by adopters from their category (Xu et al., 2017). During this phase, vendors are the external influencers and act as IEs because they can gain substantial profit and growth by winning over growth-stage adopters (Mola et al., 2022). They devote resources to finding a use case for a niche adopter segment, around whom they develop the innovation and its support infrastructure (manuals, service partners, and additional products) (Mola et al., 2022; Moore, 2014). They establish relationships with adopters to convince them and to directly sell their innovation to them, often working closely with them for installation (Gombault et al., 2016; Moore, 2014). They forge tactical alliances with other vendors and consultants for delivering sales support and related products (Marsan et al., 2020). They dominate the public discourse to convey their market leadership, the innovation’s ROI, and the availability of customized solutions tailored to suit each adopter (Marsan et al., 2020; Swanson & Ramiller,

1997). Eventually, vendors build a marketplace and a support infrastructure around the innovation that appeals to the mainstream adopters. Once acquired, these growthstage adopters show loyalty to the innovation by standardizing it in their operations, appreciating it, and influencing other suppliers, thereby accelerating diffusion (Akhlaghpour & Lapointe, 2018). However, contrary to these expectations, we found that in the case of RFID diffusion, Walmart and other retailers, the members of one complementary part of the innovation, who played the role of an IE in the early phases also helped the RFIDin-retailing institutional field cross the chasm (Moore, 2019).

After two years in oblivion, Walmart took a new approach. Based on pilot tests, Walmart realized that RFID benefits lie in item-level tracking in stores, particularly in the apparel section (Roberti, 2010a). Itemlevel tracking uniquely identifies a product and distinguishes product varieties. Walmart was able to perform frequent, efficient, and cheaper inventory management, and trace a product from the backroom to the store shelf and checkout, leading to inventory accuracy, on-shelf availability, and granular product visibility in higher orders of magnitude than manual counting and barcoding (Roberti, 2010a). This new approach played to the strengths of RFID, as opposed to the pallet-level tagging enforced by Walmart in its earlier mandates. Pallet-level tagging focused on maintaining inventory accuracy for shipments arriving at warehouses, where the accuracy was already at 99% (O’Connor, 2014). It is at the stores where accuracy is impacted due to various factors such as customers, nonrepeatable processes, employee distractions, weather conditions, and buying patterns (O’Connor, 2014). Other retailers like Macy’s, Zara, Target, and JC Penney conducted pilots and understood the benefits and future use cases of itemlevel RFID (Studio98Test, 2009; University of Arkansas, 2009; Kay, 2010; O’Connor, 2009). Speaking about the benefits of item-level RFID at a retailer conference, Peter Longo, president of logistics and operations at Macy’s said, “no matter how good the bar code is, it is woefully short of what you can do with RFID” (Roberti, 2011). Regarding the benefits from Macy’s RFID trials, he stated: “Inventory accuracy went from 70 to 75 percent to 95 to 99 percent. … That’s practically stunning. We see that as the most important benefit” (Roberti, 2011). Summarizing his views on the technology’s future, he said: “A year ago, we used to talk about whether to use RFID or not” and added, “The debate is over. Our position is that the technology is here, and it is time. RFID is no longer a jazzy technology being tested by academics and consultants. It’s not acceptable to have 30 percent inventory inaccuracy” (Roberti, 2011).

Suppliers had higher benefits and lower costs in this phase compared to the previous phases. Unlike barcodes, itemlevel RFID benefits suppliers by letting them track promotions, automate assembly, lower chargebacks from reduced shrinkage and shipping errors, derive sales insights using retailer’s real-time sales data, and increase sales due to product availability in the retail sales floor (Kay, 2010; Roberti, 2011; Swedberg, 2011a). The costs were lower because of the technology maturity (Wailgum, 2010), the emergence of established vendors (Kay, 2010; Roberti, 2010a), and the retailer’s willingness to share the tagging costs, subsidize the sensor costs and work closely with the former in RFID installation (Bustillo, 2010; Roberti, 2010a). Suppliers could see the benefits this time due not to the cost of losing business with influential retailers for noncompliance but due to RFID’s superiority compared to the barcode and their cheaper cost (Bustillo, 2010; Kay, 2010). Suppliers sensed that they would lose out on the competitive advantage if they did not invest in RFID (Kay, 2010).

Although retailers and suppliers had their respective coadopter relative advantage, it was again the retailers that had the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage. The pilot studies were retailer initiated and retailer centric. The supplier benefits were primarily industry predictions based on the retailer pilots, with limited references within the supplier community (Kay, 2010). Suppliers had the cost of maintaining two supply chains—building a new one for retailers beginning their RFID rollouts and another for retailers using barcodes. Moreover, the cost-sharing arrangements and the supplier-side installation were still in flux (Kay, 2010). Thus, despite their optimism, suppliers looked up to retailers for the concrete actions needed for the coadopter advantage to materialize for both parties. As an expert in RFID in retail aptly summarized:

Simply put, this is an instance where everyone is a winner. Even apparel suppliers currently concerned about whether retailers will pay an appropriate share of tagging costs would have difficulty disputing the fact that RFIDbased processes are superior to barcodebased processes. (Kay, 2010)

The actions of Walmart and the other retailers represent all three IE strategies. Retailers’ actions reflect the institutional entrepreneurship strategy of rationale development. When RFID diffusion went to a standstill, the retailers conducted pilots and identified RFID’s niche—in-store item-level tracking in the apparel section—in the retail supply chain and attracted the field’s attention (Bustillo, 2010; Studio98Test, 2009; University of Arkansas, 2009). Retailers formed and co-chaired industry groups that served as knowledge hubs drafting detailed tagging guidelines and sharing them publicly on their websites (Swedberg, 2010). These resources helped adopters in their RFID rollouts. Retailers shared their success stories and lessons learned in conferences (Anderson, 2016), research studies, and case studies, which served as a reference for other retailers and spurred RFID adoption (McKevitt, 2016b; Swedberg, 2016b;

Warry, 2015; Zaino, 2016). Retailers continued to make a case by trying more RFID use cases such as closed-loop supply chains, omnichannel sales, smart dressing rooms (Moore, 2019; Swedberg, 2011c), nonreplenishment items (Swedberg, 2014a), pick-to-the-last-unit programs (Swedberg, 2016c), one-hour delivery (Swedberg, 2016a), and smart product displays in showrooms (Swedberg, 2016d).

Retailers’ actions also reflected the institutional entrepreneurship strategy of relationship development. In collaboration with research institutes, retailers conducted pilots (O’Connor, 2009), identified use cases, developed standards (Swedberg, 2011c), and tailored technology for specific use cases (Roberti, 2016a). Retailers worked closely with suppliers by troubleshooting (Swedberg, 2011a), providing tagging guides and standards manuals (Beck, 2018), and involving business managers in decision-making (Roberti, 2010a). Retailers got the best price on tags for suppliers by forecasting the volume of tags to be bought for a particular product category and sharing the tagging costs with a cost-of-goods model (Roberti, 2010a). In the process, retailers gained the supplier’s trust, which had earlier been lacking (Kaplan, 2018a). Retailers formed industry groups with other retailers and invited participation from vendors and suppliers (Swedberg, 2010).

Retailers’ actions also reflected the IE strategy of resource mobilization. Retailers invested in RFID rollouts by installing RFID readers, redesigning processes, and building data handling capability (Bustillo, 2010). Early adopters like Walmart and Macys served as opinion leaders for the early majority to join the RFID bandwagon (Bustillo, 2010; Kriz, 2018; Thau, 2017). They invested in research and development activities by funding research centers (Studio98Test, 2012), sharing data (Beck, 2018; Lopez, 2018), conducting field trials (Swedberg, 2010), and piloting new use cases (Moore, 2019; Swedberg, 2011c). They channeled their human resources by training employees, appointing ambassadors (Greengard, 2015), creating specialized job positions such as chief omnichannel officer (Prince, 2013), and showing senior leadership commitment (Zaino, 2017). Retailers brought in vendors to sponsor such RFID programs, letting them have a say in standards (Swedberg, 2011c). By using their power in the retailing field, they publicly backed RFID (Bustillo, 2010), issued mandates to suppliers (Anderson, 2016; Zaino, 2017) and influenced standards development (Edwards, 2012). To get suppliers’ support and cooperation in RFID adoption, retailers invested in significant outreach efforts and provided resources (McBeath, 2018).

Vendors played a vital role in this phase. For instance, the RAIN RFID alliance formed by vendors (such as Google, Impinj, Intel, and Smartrac) was a pivotal event aimed at accelerating RFID adoption (RAINRFID, 2014; Greengard, 2015). Vendors kept advancing the technology (Cook, 2014; Swedberg, 2013; Warry, 2015), built the support infrastructure (GS1, 2009b; Greengard, 2015), and tailored solutions to suit adopters (Woodie, 2016). With the increase in RFID demand, vendors earned profits, achieved economies of scale in production (Warry, 2015), and offered solutions at affordable prices (Kriz, 2018; Swedberg, 2016b). Industry leaders emerged in the RFID market (Zaczkiewicz, 2016). They published case studies for potential adopters (Kay, 2010), piloted new use cases (Zaino, 2016), offered new RFID solutions and services (Woodie, 2016), and collaborated for standards development, research (Swedberg, 2011c), and installations (Prince, 2013). Vendors gained experience with RFID implementations, learning best practices, and common pitfalls (Kay, 2010; McBeath, 2018).

Suppliers primarily played a supporting role by embracing RFID (Bustillo, 2010; Kay, 2010), fulfilling tagging requirements (Zaino, 2017), collaborating on research (Swedberg, 2011b), and actively participating in industry groups (Swedberg, 2011c). There are also instances where suppliers played an influential role. A Johnson & Johnson representative was elected as the vice-chairman of the Board of Governors of GS1 EPCglobal, a nonprofit RFID standards organization (GS1, 2012). Realizing RFID’s future, some suppliers even tagged products for retailers who had yet to adopt RFID, even influencing those retailers resisting RFID (Zaino, 2017). Suppliers like Nike (Cosgrove, 2019), Reebok (Swedberg, 2016d), Macy’s (Swedberg, 2014a), and Rebecca Minkoff (Swedberg, 2014b) tried novel use cases and announced positive outcomes (Roberti, 2016b; Wollenhaupt, 2018).

Research institutes collaborated with other actors to publish ROI studies, case studies, manuals, and future use cases (Beck, 2018; Studio98Test, 2009; University of Arkansas, 2009; GS1US, 2018). With support from other actors, they lead standards development, upgradation, and ratification (GS1, 2012). They supported adopters by providing education, training, tools, and community support (Edwards, 2012). Besides, they invested resources by opening new dedicated RFID research centers (Studio98Test, 2012). Finally, they performed mergers with other institutes to better collaborate on standards development (Edwards, 2012).

The retailer’s actions in this phase indicate that they enacted all three OV functions. When RFID adoption came to a standstill, the pilot studies shifted the field’s attention from pallet-level to item-level RFID (Bustillo, 2010; Kay, 2010). By doing so, retailers provided a revised understanding of RFID’s usefulness, which encouraged other retailers to test and adopt RFID and motivated suppliers to cooperate. Retailers kept the field enthusiastic throughout the chasm by visualizing and testing new use cases in different product segments, often using buzzwords such as “buy online pickup in store,” “ship from store”

(Hardgrave, 2016b), “one-hour delivery” (Swedberg, 2016a), and “smart dressing rooms” (Moore, 2019) to frame the use cases. These actions represent an actor enacting the OV function of interpretation.

In the prior phases, RFID was a technology solution looking for a business problem. However, the IE’s ROI studies in the chasm established item-level RFID as a technology solution for actual business problems, many of which remained unsolved by barcodes until that point (Bustillo, 2010; Kay, 2010; O’Connor, 2009). As a result, coadopters focused on tailoring RFID to their operations instead of pondering whether RFID would deliver ROI, signaling a mindset change compared to prior phases (Greengard, 2015). Retailers’ public announcements about extensive RFID rollout plans and their rationale lent legitimacy to item-level tracking in retail (Bustillo, 2010; Greengard, 2015; Kay, 2010). For instance, one headline stated: “Macy’s to RFID tag everything” (Anderson, 2016). Suppliers envisioned RFID as a mainstay of retailing due to such public backing by retailing giants (Bustillo, 2010; Greengard, 2015). To lend further legitimacy, retailers played a leadership role in standards development and maturation (Swedberg, 2011c). They participated in big conferences (Anderson, 2016) and announced their post-implementation success (McKevitt, 2016b). These actions represent an actor enacting the OV function of legitimation.

Retailers fostered joint adoption by installing RFID and simultaneously issuing mandates to suppliers (Bustillo, 2010; Kay, 2010), working closely with them in fulfilling the tagging requirements (Anderson, 2016; Roberti, 2016a). To activate all actors in the institutional field, they forged social networks to obtain sponsorship from vendors, involvement from suppliers, and funding for research institutes (Swedberg, 2010). Through research studies and industry groups, they created knowledge (e.g., websites, social media, manuals, recommendations, and case studies) for the would-be coadopters (Swedberg, 2011c). Retailers regularly announced RFID roadmaps and successes using compelling and future-oriented frames such as “a very big part of our future” that nudged other actors to identify the commercial opportunities in RFID (Prince, 2013). Some of the articles headlined “RFID ready for a lift-off,” (Kay, 2010), “Why RFID is (finally) here to stay,” (Woodie, 2016), and “Macy’s: Inventory will be 100% RFID-tagged by 2017” (McKevitt, 2016a). Speaking of changing the future of RFID, one vendor said, “I don’t see it [RFID] slowing down at this point. Acceleration has been pretty consistent the last two years” (Woodie, 2016). These actions reflect an actor enacting the OV function of mobilization.

Proposition 4: The IE for a codependent innovation will use all three IE strategies and enact all three OV functions during the chasm phase of the early-stage diffusion process for that codependent innovation.

Advancements in RFID technology and other fields made RFID more useful. RFID technology advanced, matured, and became cheaper (Wailgum, 2010), meaning that choosing a tag for a specific application became easier (Cooduvalli, 2017). Big data analytics afforded the number-crunching ability not available in the early phases (Lopez, 2017). The rise of cellular tracking technologies rendered RFID’s privacy issues trivial in the minds of consumers (Woodie, 2016). ERP systems evolved to ingest real-time data and are hence suited for RFID (Wollenhaupt, 2018). The proliferation of handheld devices and mobile apps meant that retailers could employ handheld readers for their inventory count (Michel, 2015). Technologies like drones, Wi-Fi, and the Internet of Things complemented RFID (Baydakova, 2018). Adhesives became more resilient to environmental conditions, improving read rates (Moore, 2019). With the popularity of social media, omnichannel sales—where customers buy online and pick up at stores—emerged as a new RFID use case (Swedberg, 2019).

The joint adoption of RFID increased between 2010, when retailers introduced item-level tracking, and 2019 (Hardgrave, 2016a). A rising number of retailers moved from pilots to deployment (Hardgrave, 2016a) during this time, with the retail sector driving the majority of sales volume for the vendors (Wollenhaupt, 2018). The apparel segment within the retail industry continues to dominate RFID adoption, with other segments gradually catching up. The forecasts for RFID adoption by academic scholars and industry pundits paint a bright future (Kriz, 2018; Thau, 2017). There is consensus that RFID fell into the “chasm” after Walmart’s mandate (Kriz, 2018; Roberti, 2017; Thau, 2017). Experts opine that RFID is at the tipping point and ready for prime time, with mainstream adoption on the horizon (Kriz, 2018; Roberti, 2017; Wollenhaupt, 2018). Other experts feel that RFID adoption has now reached the early majority (Swedberg, 2019). The discourse around RFID has moved from technology issues to business cases (Warry, 2015).

Our overall case analysis discussed above shows that the key influencers in the early stages of the codependent innovation diffusion need not be an actor external to the adopter community, as the traditional innovation diffusion literature suggests (Bass, 2004; Jeyaraj & Sabherwal, 2014; Strang & Soule, 1998). Walmart, an actor internal to the overall adoption phenomenon but external to the coadopter community of suppliers, perceived the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage in RFID and played the IE role throughout the early-stage diffusion process. During the chasm, the other retailers played the role of an IE as well. Although Walmart and the other retailers are internal to the overall adoption phenomenon, which involves two different adopter communities, they are external to the coadopter community of suppliers resisting adoption of RFID tags due to high recurring costs and lower perceived benefits, as compared to retailers. Therefore, for the diffusion of RFID-in-retailing, Walmart acted as an internal-external influencer in the first three phases, and the other retailers joined in during the chasm. This leads to our fifth proposition:

Proposition 5: Instead of external influencers of a codependent innovation playing the role of an IE, the internal-external influencers who perceive the highest coadopter relative advantage of the codependent innovation will play the role of an IE in the early-stage diffusion process for that codependent innovation.

## 6 Discussion

## 6.1 Key Insights about the Processual Dynamics

Our findings show that the organization in the coadopter community that perceives the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage between the two different coadopter communities influences the earlystage diffusion of a codependent innovation. In our RFID casework, retailers perceived the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage throughout the early stage and influenced the diffusion of the RFID-inretailing innovation. This notion is similar to the notion of relative advantage in prior research on single-whole innovation diffusion, which posits relative advantage as the key predictor of adoption decisions (Akhlaghpour & Lapointe, 2018; Rogers, 2003). In line with this literature, retailers perceive relative advantage from RFID adoption due to orders of magnitude improvements in inventory management, product tracking, and reducing operational costs. Suppliers perceive relative advantage in RFID due to better shipment tracking and theft prevention. However, the notion of relative advantage perceived by each adopter community is limited in explaining diffusion outcomes in codependent innovations because two different adopter communities are adopting two different parts of the innovation with different relative advantages, and members from both communities need to adopt their respective parts for their respective relative advantages to bear fruit. In the case we examined, suppliers voiced concerns over the recurring costs of attaching RFID tags, despite their enthusiasm for future benefits. But retailers perceived a higher coadopter relative advantage because of the fixed costs of installing RFID readers and the supporting infrastructure, while suppliers bore the high and ongoing RFID tagging costs. The coadopter relative advantage was thus higher for retailers than for suppliers beginning with the early stage, specifically for Walmart, who factored into its calculus of coadopter relative advantage its dominance over suppliers and its prior experience in supply-chain revolutions. Therefore, it adopted RFID readers. However, retailers cannot benefit from RFID without joint adoption of the RFID technology by suppliers. Thus, Walmart attempted to influence the suppliers who resisted RFID due to a lower coadopter relative advantage for them from this technology. Therefore, we add the notion of coadopter relative advantage to IDT to answer who plays the role of influencers during the early-stage diffusion process for codependent innovations.

Our findings reveal the role of an internal-external influencer in the early-stage diffusion of codependent innovations. In our RFID case, Walmart played this role in the early-stage diffusion of the RFID-inretailing innovation. In contrast, prior research on single-whole innovations suggests that external actors play the influencer role in the early stages of the diffusion process (Bass, 2004; Jeyaraj & Sabherwal, 2014; Strang & Soule, 1998). The notion of the internal-external influencer is similar to the notion of internal and external influencers in prior research on single-whole innovation diffusion. Walmart is an internal influencer as a member of the adopter community. Walmart influenced other retailers by sharing RFID benefits found by its pilots and success stories gained as an adopter. Walmart is also an external influencer in terms of its influence on the coadopter community of suppliers who must adopt RFID tags in order for the benefits of RFID adoption to accrue to any party. Walmart acted as an external influencer for suppliers by extolling the benefits of RFID, framing RFID as the retailing industry’s future technology, and preparing resources (such as manuals and tagging guides). Walmart was able to disseminate information to many suppliers due to the sheer number of suppliers tied to them and their use of mass media outlets and conferences. Despite its similarity to the notion of influencer in the IDT on single-whole innovations, Walmart’s role adds nuance to the influencer concept because it influenced the diffusion of RFID, a new class of innovation that we term codependent innovation. Walmart is an internalexternal influencer because it is internal to the overall RFID adoption phenomenon (as the adopter of RFID reader) but external in terms of its influence on the coadopter community of suppliers adopting the RFID tags. Therefore, we add the notion of internal-external influencer to IDT to answer the question of who plays the role of influencer during the early-stage diffusion process for codependent innovations.

Based on our analysis of the early-stage diffusion of RFID in retailing, we show how the diffusion process unfolds during the early stages of a codependent innovation. We divided the early stage into four phases: emergence, structuralization, evolution, and chasm. In each phase, we identified the actor that played the role of an IE, the institutional entrepreneurship strategies they used, and the OV functions they enacted.

## 6.1.1 Phase 1: Emergence

In the emergence phase, which lasted from 1999 to 2003, Walmart, as the internal-external influencer, performed IE actions by developing and testing the RFID technology. Our findings contrast with prior IDT literature that posits that during this phase, external influencers (Rogers, 2003), such as research analysts and their firms, play the role of IE by seeking opportunities for and developing IT innovations (Greenwood et al., 2002; Liao, 2016; Morrill, 2006; Wang & Ramiller, 2009). They collaborate with innovative adopters to research, experiment, and test the IT innovation (Morrill, 2006; Nielsen et al., 2014; Rogers, 2003). However, our case analysis shows that Walmart performed these actions by funding the Auto-ID Center and conducting pilots with interested suppliers, representing the IE strategy of rationale development. Moreover, Walmart’s actions reflected their enactment of the OV function of interpretation. Further, they dominated the discourse by using frames such as “smart shelves” and by discussing the RFID’s emergence and applicability and its future in retailing, calling it the next-generation supply chain technology. In contrast, prior work on OVT suggests that in this phase, research institutes dominate the discourse to present the new IT as superior to current practices, thereby attracting community attention and shaping the benefits for potential adopters (Liao, 2016; Wang & Ramiller, 2009). At the end of this phase, diffusion was restricted to pilot tests conducted by retailers (mainly Walmart) in collaboration with suppliers.

## 6.1.2 Phase 2: Structuralization

In the structuralization phase, which lasted from 2003 to 2005, Walmart as the internal-external influencer performed IE actions by mobilizing the retailing field toward RFID diffusion. Our findings contrast with prior IDT literature that posits that in this phase, external influencers (Rogers, 2003), such as technology vendors, perform the actions of an IE by producing and releasing products (Wang & Ramiller, 2009). Vendors work with visionary adopters by deploying resources to realize the innovation (Purdy & Gray, 2009) and learn the new technology to solve business problems (Fayard et al., 2016). However, our case analysis shows that Walmart influenced RFID diffusion by deploying RFID systems in their retail stores and distribution centers and mandating suppliers to adopt RFID tags. To activate the market, retailers influenced vendors to reduce prices, offer discounts, and display their products, indicating the IE strategy of resource mobilization. Walmart’s actions reflected their enactment of the OV function of mobilization. Further, they dominated the discourse by issuing and summarizing their mandate and providing constant updates on their mandate and RFID deployment. In contrast, prior work on OVT suggests that in this phase, vendors dominate the discourse through their marketing campaigns to shape the benefits for potential adopters (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997; Wang & Ramiller, 2009). At the end of this phase, the diffusion can be characterized by retailers aggressively adopting RFID systems and suppliers adopting only the minimum required RFID tags to fulfill Walmart’s mandate.

## 6.1.3 Phase 3: Evolution

In the evolution phase, which lasted from 2005 to 2008, Walmart as the internal-external influencer performed IE actions by focusing on developing and conveying the ROI of RFID to other actors. Our findings contrast with prior literature on IDT that posits that in this phase, the adopters of an innovation, i.e., the internal influencers (Rogers, 2003), are expected to perform IE actions to get more adopters on board in order to benefit from network externalities. Suppliers’ actions focus on perfecting the innovation to reduce costs and leveraging it to gain an advantage over their competitors because the innovation has now been validated (Fayard et al., 2016; Wang & Ramiller, 2009). However, our case analysis shows that Walmart focused on adding legitimacy to RFID use in retailing by sharing data with suppliers and conducting pilots in real scenarios and standards development in collaboration with research institutes, indicating the IE strategy of relationship development. Walmart’s actions reflected their enactment of the OV function of legitimation. Further, they dominated the discourse by framing RFID ROI, standards maturity, technology maturity, and backing the technology. In contrast, prior work on OVT suggests that in this phase, suppliers dominate the discourse to incorporate their success stories and justify a potential adopter’s investment in innovative IT (Wang & Ramiller, 2009). Despite these efforts, RFID diffusion came to a standstill due to a lack of coadopter relative advantage for suppliers. Other retailers did not follow an aggressive implementation, limiting RFID adoption to pilots. Vendors did not influence because they had few suppliers and had to bear the costs of updating standards. Our results show evidence of what scholars call a chasm between the early and the growth stages of diffusion (Moore, 2014; Xu et al., 2017).

## 6.1.4 Phase 4: Chasm

In the chasm, which lasted between 2008 and 2019, as the internal-external influencers, Walmart and other retailers performed IE actions by focusing on a business problem that best suited the strengths of RFID. Compared to their focus on pallet-level RFID in the initial phases, Walmart and the other retailers focused on item-level RFID in the apparel category, which conferred a coadopter relative advantage for retailers and suppliers. In this phase, they worked closely with suppliers for sharing costs and implementation, simultaneously transforming their own sales floors by installing RFID readers. Our findings contrast with prior literature that posits that vendors act as external influencers and perform the actions of an IE during the chasm to shape the relative advantage for the pragmatic potential adopters (Moore, 2014). They deploy resources to develop a complete product and set up support services, working closely with the adopter and forging other tactical alliances (Gombault et al., 2016; Marsan et al., 2020; Moore, 2014). However, our case analysis shows that retailers performed such actions, essentially utilizing all three IE strategies. Retailers’ actions reflected their enactment of all three OV functions. They dominated the discourse to pitch item-level RFID as a new direction, sketch their implementation plans, visualize novel use cases, showcase the maturity of standards and technology, and share success stories. In contrast, prior work on OVT suggests that in this phase, vendors dominate the public discourse to demonstrate ROI and market leadership and position the innovation in a niche market segment (Moore, 2014). Overall, retailers helped RFID in retailing cross the chasm, which is critical to deeming the early-stage diffusion successful, consequently moving the diffusion to the growth stage (i.e., the mainstream markets).

## 6.2 Theoretical Contributions

Understanding codependent innovation diffusion is critical to tapping into the transformative potential of this class of innovations. A case in point is the RFIDin-retailing innovation, which promised a supply-chain revolution in retailing and involved massive investments and process changes from all actors in retailing. But its diffusion fell into a chasm and needed almost a decade to enter the growth stages of diffusion (Kriz, 2018; Roberti, 2017). There is a lack of understanding of how codependent innovations (Bucklin & Sengupta, 1993) diffuse in the IS innovation diffusion literature, compared to the extensive research on the diffusion of single-whole innovations (Angst et al., 2010; Wang & Swanson, 2007). To address this, we study codependent innovations with two complementary parts and examine how they diffuse during their early stage. Our paper makes three distinct contributions to the IS literature by expanding the scope of IDT to the codependent innovation context. Table 7 summarizes our key contributions.

First, we add the notion of coadopter relative advantage to the IDT literature and posit that the organization in the coadopter community that perceives the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage between the two different adopter communities will emerge as an IE and will influence the early-stage diffusion of the codependent innovation. By doing so, we provide an enhanced understanding of the notion of relative advantage in IDT from two perspectives.

(1) Prior work conceptualizes relative advantage by assuming that all members of the adopter community draw from the same set of costs and benefits (Junglas et al., 2019). This conceptualization is insufficient to explain the diffusion of codependent innovations because it cannot explain the asymmetry in relative advantage between the two different coadopter communities and how this asymmetry affects the diffusion of the codependent innovation in its early stages. We offer a nuanced conceptualization of relative advantage that we term coadopter relative advantage. Each coadopter community has its own coadopter relative advantage, which is calculated based on the unique set of benefits and costs in adopting their respective part of the innovation; however, the realization of this advantage is contingent upon joint adoption of the entire codependent innovation. This also helps explain why codependent innovations generally favor one of the two coadopter communities. While we challenge the adequacy of the notion of relative advantage in IDT in the context codependent innovations, we also add further nuance to this notion, given its limitations in understanding the costs and benefits perceived by the two coadopter communities in codependent innovations (Agarwal & Prasad, 1997).

(2) The conceptualization of prior work is limited in explaining how diffusion unfolds in the light of a hindrance to joint adoption due to the difference in the relative advantage for the two different coadopter communities. Our conceptualization of coadopter relative advantage factors in the adoption likelihood of the other coadopter community, and that calculation determines who performs the role of an IE to influence diffusion. We propose that the organization in the coadopter community with a higher coadopter relative advantage that perceives the highest degree of coadopter relative advantage will influence the diffusion by acting as an IE because their higher coadopter relative advantage will bear fruit only if the other coadopter community also embraces and adopts their part of the codependent innovation. Through this understanding, we add to the prior work in IDT that posits relative advantage as the key notion in explaining the diffusion of single-whole innovations (Akhlaghpour & Lapointe, 2018). Our conceptualization of coadopter relative advantage aligns with prior work on relative advantage where the focus is on different perceptions within a community resulting from the technology component, i.e., comparing the new technology with the incumbent one (Moore & Benbasat, 1991).

In addition, our conceptualization focuses on the relative advantage differences between the two coadopter communities. Further, we show that another adopter’s adoption likelihood (or lack thereof) is factored into the calculation of relative advantage and determines whether an actor plays the role of an influencer. Moreover, we add a new understanding that a high relative advantage by itself is insufficient to foster the diffusion of codependent innovations. Our notion adds value to researchers studying the diffusion of codependent innovations, as they may direct their efforts to develop instruments for measuring the coadopter relative advantage for the different coadopters involved. Further, our work offers a novel construct that researchers can theorize as predicting codependent innovation diffusion. Scholars focusing on strategies like mandates and incentives (as we did in our paper) (Rogers, 2003), and interventions in the IS design, promotion, and surrounding processes need to focus on this novel construct as a dependent variable when studying codependent innovation diffusion.

Second, we add the notion of an internal-external influencer to the IDT literature and posit that the IE as an influencer may be an actor internal to the overall adoption phenomenon, which involves two different adopter communities, who is, however, external in terms of their influence for the coadopter community that has a lower coadopter relative advantage and thus a lower motivation to adopt their part of the codependent innovation. By doing so, we provide an enhanced understanding of the notion of influencers in the IDT from two perspectives.

(1) the concepts of internal or external influencers are inadequate in conceptualizing the form of influence needed for the diffusion of codependent innovations. External influencers might find it hard to persuade adopters due to the asymmetric coadopter relative advantage and the consequent stalemate in codependent innovation diffusion. A member of the coadopter community with the higher degree of coadopter relative advantage might influence the coadopter community with the lower degree of coadopter relative advantage. While this member, as an adopter in the overall codependent innovation adoption phenomenon, is an internal influencer, this member is also external in terms of their influence on the coadopter community with the lower degree of coadopter relative advantage. To conceptualize this influence, our work proposes a new type of influencer in terms of internal-external influencer and adds a new understanding to the influence processes in IDT research on communication channels (Loh & Venkatraman, 1992; Strang & Soule, 1998).

(2) We add a new understanding that internal-external influencers play the role of an IE in all the phases of the early-stage diffusion process for codependent innovations. In contrast, prior research on single-whole innovations suggests that external actors play the influencer role in the early stages of the diffusion process (Bass, 2004; Hu et al., 1997). Our notion adds value for researchers studying the diffusion of codependent innovations, who might develop new instruments to measure internal-external influence in the context of codependent innovation diffusion. Further, researchers can theorize the effect of diffusion interventions involving the internal-external influencer to speed up the diffusion process because these influencers are most likely to obtain buy-in from potential adopters. Such interventions can be compared with those involving external influencers, whose influence might slow down the diffusion. Our work aids researchers in theorizing the timing and the nature of influence at various phases in the early-stage diffusion so that communication channels are utilized in the ideal sequence as the diffusion of codependent innovations progresses from one phase to another.

Third, we divide the early-stage codependent innovation diffusion process into four phases—emergence, structuralization, evolution, and chasm—and identify the institutional entrepreneurship strategies used and the OV functions enacted by the IE during each phase. This contribution provides a more nuanced understanding of influencers’ actions in the IDT from two perspectives.

(1) We add a new understanding that influencers need to engage in institution building actions to promote the diffusion of the new class of innovation termed codependent innovations. The diffusion of codependent innovations requires influencers to change the existing unsupportive institutional arrangements, which lowers the coadopter relative advantage and deters the key actors’ attention and resources from the new technology. However, prior work posits that external and internal influencers motivate potential adopters by relying on the communication of generic and experiential information, respectively (Angst et al., 2010; Jeyaraj & Sabherwal, 2014). We add to this line of work and posit that influencers foster diffusion by building new institutions by using institutional entrepreneurship strategies and by enacting OV functions. We also add to the growing body of work that integrates OVT and IET to explain the diffusion process by extending it to the context of codependent IT innovations (Paré et al., 2020; Wang & Swanson, 2007). In contrast to prior work focusing on the strategies used by vendors and other external influencers (Kim & Miranda, 2018; Paré et al., 2020; Wang & Swanson, 2007), we focus on the strategies used by adopters, specifically internal-external influencers, who enact different OV functions to spur the innovation process.

(2) We add a finer-grained understanding of the diffusion phenomenon by identifying the four unique phases during the early-stage diffusion process and discussing which specific institutional entrepreneurship strategies to use and OV functions to enact to address the distinct issues that arise in each of those phases to foster the diffusion of codependent innovations. Based on our RFID-in-retailing casework, we propose that the IE for a codependent innovation will: (a) use the rationale development strategy and enact the interpretation OV function during the emergence phase, (b) use the resource mobilization strategy and enact the mobilization OV function during the structuralization phase, (c) use the relationship development strategy and enact the legitimation OV function during the evolution phase, and (d) use all the three institutional entrepreneurship strategies and enact all the three OV functions during the chasm phase. By doing so, our work extends prior work on phase models in the IT diffusion literature that looks at dynamic patterns in effective communication channels (Loh & Venkatraman, 1992; Strang & Soule, 1998) and in an actor’s discursive (Wang & Ramiller, 2009) and material practices in innovating with IT (Nielsen et al., 2014). We extend this work to the context of codependent IT innovations.

In addition, our work adds to the small but growing body of work on the strategies used by actors to enact the OV functions of interpretation (Liao, 2016), legitimation (Kaganer et al., 2010; Marsan et al., 2020), and mobilization (Amadoru et al., 2021; Wang & Swanson, 2007). Further, compared to prior work that primarily focuses on legitimation strategies (Kaganer et al., 2010; Paré et al., 2020), our work also focuses on interpretation and mobilization strategies (Amadoru et al., 2021). Our work affords researchers additional mechanisms—i.e., institutional entrepreneurship strategies and enacting OV functions—with which to theorize the role of influencers in fostering the diffusion of codependent innovations. Our four-phase process model serves as a baseline for future researchers to test and expand the understanding of the process of codependent innovation diffusion in different contexts.

Our theoretical contributions offer originality through several revelatory (i.e., surprising) insights into our understanding of the diffusion of codependent innovations, offer scientific utility through a clear presentation of the research model, present boundary conditions for further theory building and testing, and provide practical utility through the presentation of the managerial implications in a subsequent section (Corley & Gioia, 2011). We made our best efforts to adhere to the key considerations of theory development outlined by Rivard (2014) by identifying a gap, defining and extending the key theoretical concepts driving our phenomenon, explicating our contributions, and improving the presentation clarity by using various tools like figures, tables, and propositions. In terms of theoretical integration, our work falls into the type “close areas of research, compatible underlying assumptions” in the typology proposed by Okhuysen and Bonardi (2011). All three theories—IDT, OVT, and IET—represent close research areas because they examine a common phenomenon—the diffusion of new practices. Further, these three theories also have compatible underlying assumptions, as they all assume multiple actors with motives and self-interests and clarify that an innovation might not benefit everyone.

We believe that our theoretical insights are generalizable (see Appendix A for more details) within the boundary of codependent innovations in retailing settings involving big players. In terms of the Lee and Baskerville (2003) generalizability framework, ET generalizability (from empirical statements to theoretical statements) applies to our study. To further strengthen the face validity of our findings, we show our model’s applicability in the case of another codependent innovation in retail. We analyzed the early-stage diffusion of EMV technology in US retailing. EMV chip-based credit\debit cards and EMV chip readers are the two complementary parts. The two different adopter communities who adopt this innovation are banks who issue EMV cards to their customers and merchants and retailers who install chip readers at point-of-sale terminals, respectively. We found that the early-stage diffusion of this technology followed the process model we discerned in our study of RFID diffusion in retailing. In the early stages of EMV card diffusion, banks perceived a higher degree of coadopter relative advantage than retailers and played the role of an IE. Merchants in the US resisted EMV cards mainly due to costs associated with implementation. As a countermeasure, banks announced a deadline beyond which EMV noncompliant merchants would be held liable for fraud committed by consumers at the point of sale—a move very similar to the mandates issued by Walmart during the structuralization phase (Pettey, 2015). Banks utilized public discourse to position EMV technology as a solution for security breaches and to outline EMV transition deadlines (Visa, 2019; Orem, 2019). These efforts led to the joint adoption of EMV cards by banks and chip readers by retailers (Orem, 2019).

## 6.3 Practical Implications

Our theoretical contributions have important practical implications for organizations’ IT innovation practices. First, organizations engaging in the adoption and diffusion of a codependent IT innovation should constantly perform internal analyses of the costs and benefits of the innovation over existing technology/practices and the external analysis of costs and benefits to the members of the other coadopter community. This analysis will inform the organization whether they need to carry out the role of a “visionary” organization championing an innovation. Second, visionary organizations can engage in three types of strategies: (1) developing the rationale for the innovation by means of press statements, online news releases, white papers, conferences, promotional activities, buzzwords, and keynotes; (2) mobilizing resources by using penalties, financial clout, investments in new equipment, research funding, political resources for lobbying, and reward systems for supporters of the innovation; and (3) developing relations through coalitions, alliances, research collaborations, think tanks, standard-setting bodies, and/or governmental organizations. Third, our notion that an IE can enact certain OV functions informs organizations that their actions have industry-level diffusion implications. In other words, their actions can, directly and indirectly, activate the industry to perform interpretation, mobilization, and legitimation functions and thereby aid IT diffusion. Organizations interested in leading the community toward interpretation should engage in research and development to understand innovations, fund research institutes, and use public discourse to come up with buzzwords. Organizations interested in leading the community toward mobilization need to help vendors manufacture IT solutions, urge them to display new products in forums, mandate the adoption of other actors, call others to deploy resources to implement the innovation, and use discourse to outline implementation plans. Finally, organizations interested in leading the community toward legitimation need to endorse innovations, help set standards, manage the discourse, legitimize the innovation, and promote success stories. Fourth, our four-phase model for early-stage diffusion of a codependent innovation shows organizations how to time their strategies when innovating with codependent innovations. In the emergence phase, the IE needs to work closely with research institutes by funding them and drafting standards. In the structuralization phase, the IE needs to influence the coadopter community and vendors simultaneously and use various techniques, including mandates, to help achieve economies of scale. In the evolution phase, the IE should ensure that adopters get a good ROI, vendors save on costs, and research institutes develop new standards to improve constituent technologies. In the chasm phase, the IE needs to find a niche target segment for the innovation, work closely with the coadopter community for sharing costs and implementation, and collaborate with vendors and research institutes for technology maturation and standards development. Finally, the IE interested in codependent innovation diffusion needs to perform actions to enact the institutional functions of interpretation, mobilization, and legitimation during the emergence, structuralization, and evolution phases, respectively. In the chasm phase, the IE needs to perform actions to enact all three OV functions.

## 6.4 Limitations and Future Work

Our narrative and interpretation of RFID diffusion are based on our analysis of internet-sourced discourse. While internet sources can play an essential and key part in the discourse surrounding an innovation, they could be restricted in the extent to which they represent the diversity of opinions by each actor (Wang & Ramiller, 2009). However, internet-based resources are the biggest discursive arena and have also been used in prior RFID (Fish, 2006; Quaadgras, 2005), innovation diffusion (Davidson et al., 2015), and institutional entrepreneurship (Garud et al., 2002) studies. We collected data from diverse, reputed sources to ensure the credibility of our findings. Future research could focus on other sources like industry expositions, social media sources, and personal interviews to gain an insider perspective, and use analytical tools like text mining and linguistic analysis to gain additional insights (Amadoru et al., 2021; Davidson et al., 2015; Miranda et al., 2015).

Also, while we studied the actions of all actors in the retailing field, we analyzed OV around codependent innovations primarily from a Walmart-centered perspective. As a potential extension to OV research in codependent innovations, scholars could explore the OV shaping activities of other adopting parties and the tensions in the conversations surrounding coadopter relative advantage. Further, although suppliers in our case study initially complied with the mandate, they eventually abandoned it because they would have been no worse off had they followed the mandate and adopted RFID (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). Moreover, retailers were unable to develop relationships in the evolution phase to the extent needed to collectively fulfill the mandates (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). Future studies could extend IET with the notion of mandates by an IE to bring about institutional change and research the extent to which they can be effective.

We propose three additional directions for future research. First, future research can explore how differential levels of other important innovation attributes, like compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability, play a role in diffusing codependent innovations (Rogers, 2003; Sun et al., 2018). Experts have opined that one of the barriers to RFID diffusion is the complexity inherent in RFID deployment—a process involving the integration of complicated IT infrastructure and business process changes for which there is no cohesive solution available in the market (Kaplan, 2018b; Ricci, 2010). Studying the role of relative complexity between two different adopter communities and how it impacts their adoption decisions is an exciting avenue for future research.

Second, future studies could also extend our framework to include the later stages of codependent innovation diffusion and study who plays the influencer role in later stages. Future studies might also delve deeper into the mechanisms of internal-external influence. For instance, IDT posits that prestige and competition influence adopters to imitate other adopters (Jeyaraj & Sabherwal, 2014; Strang & Soule, 1998). Given that an internal-external influencer might not be an actor from the same adopter community, it would be interesting to study how such factors influence imitation.

Third, future research could also focus on the structure of the OV. Business use cases are one of the key components of an OV (Miranda et al., 2015). We expect the structure of an OV for codependent innovations to be different because two different adopter communities are involved, and the overall innovation tends to favor one coadopter community. In the case of the RFID-in-retailing diffusion, we noticed that the business use cases focused on retailers fueled by their interests. Studying the structure of OV in codependent innovations could yield a nuanced structure of OV (Miranda et al., 2015). As another fruitful future research avenue, codependent innovations may add further nuance to the notion of community reception of an OV (Davidson et al., 2015; Ramiller & Swanson, 2003; Swanson & Ramiller, 2004). The business use cases, legitimating claims, and implementation guidelines and challenges are centered around one coadopter community and thus cannot be readily received by another coadopter community. Although the business use cases are centered on one coadopter community, studying the profile and experience of actors in the other coadopter community could explain those actors’ reception of the OV (Marsan et al., 2012). A related concept in IET is an actor’s social position in the institutional field (Battilana et al., 2009), which confers upon them financial, political, or social resources and grants them the power to influence the field (Hardy & Maguire, 2017). Future research could explore the dynamic patterns in an actors’ profile, experience (Marsan et al., 2012), and social position in the institutional field at different phases of the diffusion of codependent innovations (Battilana et al., 2009).

## Appendix A: Generalizability

The EMV technology is another example of a codependent innovation. The complementary parts are the EMV chip credit\debit cards and the chip readers. The two different adopter communities are banks who issue EMV cards to their customers and merchants and retailers who install chip readers at point-of-sale terminals. Our analysis shows that the early-stage diffusion of EMV technology in US retailing followed the process model we discerned in our study of RFID diffusion in retailing. EMV cards create a unique transaction code for every transaction at a point of sale, which makes these cards resistant to counterfeit frauds that magnetic strip-based cards are prone to (Kossman, 2017). Before its implementation in the US, EMV card implementations in other countries had established massive benefits in decreasing counterfeit fraud (Visa, 2019; Kossman, 2017). Banks perceived a higher coadopter relative advantage with EMV cards than retailers and acted as IEs in the early stages of EMV card diffusion. EMV cards benefit banks the most because they are liable for customer losses due to counterfeit fraud from in-store transactions; retailers and merchants, especially consumer-oriented ones, face a loss of reputation when a security breach happens at their store (Chandler, 2014). However, EMV adoption would require banks to reissue EMV credit cards to all customers and set up the supporting IT infrastructure, and would require retailers to install chip readers and associated software in all point-of-sale terminals (Chandler, 2014). For retailers, EMV cards can frustrate customers because the checkout process takes longer than before (i.e., using magnetic stripes). With security breaches becoming commonplace in pointof-sale terminals in the US, banks took a leadership role in introducing EMV cards in the US by announcing their migration plans (David & Best, 2014). Even in Europe, where EMV cards first originated, it was banks\credit card companies who developed a rationale by inventing the EMV technology and drafting the standards (EMV, 2019; Olson, 2017). When merchants in the US resisted EMV cards mainly due to costs associated with implementation, banks issued a shift in liability deadline after which the EMV noncompliant merchants would have to bear the costs of counterfeit frauds incurred by customers at the in-store point-of-sale—a move very similar to mandates issued by Walmart at the structuralization phase (Pettey, 2015). This strategy was pivotal in influencing other banks to issue similar notices and bringing retailers on board. Banks developed relationships by working closely with vendors and merchants to conduct pilots and make the EMV technology easy to use for customers (Kossman, 2016). Banks utilized the OV by positioning the technology as a solution for security breaches, outlining EMV transition deadlines, and publishing research on fraud reductions (Visa, 2019; Orem, 2019). These efforts lead to the widespread joint adoption of EMV cards by banks and EMC card readers by retailers (Orem, 2019).

By showing our model’s applicability in the case of another codependent innovation in retail, we strengthen the face validity of our findings. However, it is important to note that the US was one of the last countries adopting EMV technology, which was already a mature and standardized technology in European retailing before it reached the US markets. On the other hand, RFID in retailing was in its infancy, with businesses developing the technology and identifying the use cases in retail. Also, retailing in Europe has fewer banks and credit card companies than the thousands in its US counterpart.

Although we limited our data collection and analysis to a single case study, we believe that our theoretical insights are generalizable within the boundary of codependent innovations in the retailing context with large players. In terms of the Lee and Baskerville (2003) generalizability framework, ET (from empirical statements to theoretical statements) generalizability applies to our study. We demonstrate the generalizability of our model to a new setting by analyzing the early-stage EMV technology diffusion in US retailing. Our analysis shows that the early-stage diffusion of EMV technology in US retailing followed the process model we theorized in our study of early-stage RFID diffusion in retailing. It is important and ethical to judge and report whether the conditions in settings from which the theory is generalized from (RFID technology) are similar to new settings (EMV technology) to which theory is generalized (Lee & Baskerville, 2012). Thus, in the previous paragraph, we outlined how this new setting (EMV in the US) is different from RFID in retailing, based on which we theorized our process model.
