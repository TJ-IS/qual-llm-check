# Investigating Firm Strategies on Offering Consumer-Customizable Products

Zheyin (Jane) Gu

University of Connecticut, Storrs, Connecticut 06269, jane.gu@business.uconn.edu

Giri K. Tayi

State University of New York at Albany, Albany, New York 12222, gtayi@albany.edu

This study examines firm strategies on offering such consumer-customizable products. Our analysis shows that a monopolistic firm obtains a greater profit from offering a consumer-customizable product than from offering a preconfigured standardized product only if consumers are sufficiently capable to conduct the customization task; otherwise, it is more profitable for the firm to offer a standardized product. Moreover, consumers obtain a greater surplus when the firm offers the customizable product. Interestingly, when the firm offers both products, its effort in enhancing consumer customizability (e.g., offering free consumer training) always benefits both the firm and consumers, but its effort in increasing the value of the standardized product (e.g., offering more functions) can hurt both the firm profit and consumer surplus. Our theoretical results explain many interesting business practices and provide useful insights for marketing practitioners.

## 1. Introduction

The idea of consumer customization has been around ever since the futurist Alvin Toffler (1980) predicted its emergence in the 1970s, but business implementations of this idea have not been very successful until recently with information technology (IT) products. For example, Apple is famous for allowing consumers to customize the appearance and functionality of its computers and mobile phones. Probably the most ubiquitous consumer-customizable product is the platform-based mobile device, which consumers customize by downloading and installing apps. And most recently, in January 2015, Google announced a plan to launch a modular smartphone, Project Ara, which allows a consumer to pick components from a range of selections including battery, camera, and screen, and piece them together to make her own phone. As commented by Project Ara’s head product designer, “You could change the design all the time. 0 0 0 Conceptually it’s the first time a major electronic object, your phone, will not only be designed but also manufactured, if you wish, by the end user” (Dezeen Magazine 2015). Other successful examples of consumer-customizable products in the IT industry include Facebook and Pandora. As shown in these examples, “consumer customization” for a product is conducted by consumers for themselves so that the product will best suit their own unique needs, which is distinct from “product customization” as discussed in existing studies, which is conducted by firms for consumers, as in the case of the Mini Cooper (e.g., Dewan et al. 2003, Syam et al. 2005, Ghose and Huang 2009). The purpose of this study is to examine firm strategies on offering a product that consumers can self-customize. In particular, we examine the following questions: First, what are the benefits for a firm to offer a consumer-customizable product? Second, is it always profitable for a firm to offer a consumercustomizable product over a standardized product? And finally, what are the strategic implications of a firm offering a consumer-customizable product on consumer surplus?

The benefits of offering a consumer-customizable product can be illustrated by an interesting life example of Steve Jobs (Isaacson 2011, p. 132):

Chris Espinosa found a way to satisfy Jobs’s design demands and control freak tendencies. 0 0 0 Espinosa kept refining [the calculator] in response to Jobs’s critiques, day after day, but with each iteration came new criticisms. So finally one afternoon, when Jobs came by, Espinosa unveiled his inspired solution: “The Steve Jobs Roll Your Own Calculator Construction Set.” It allowed the user to tweak and personalize the look of the calculator by changing the thickness of lines, the size of buttons, the shading, the background, and other attributes. Instead of just laughing, Jobs plunged in and started to play around with the look to suit his tastes. After about ten minutes he got it the way he liked. His design, not surprisingly, was the one that shipped on the Mac and remained the standard for fifteen years.

In the information systems and marketing literature, “product customization” generally refers to the manufacturer practice of interacting with a client, understanding the client’s preferences, and then producing a product that suits the client’s needs. This strategy is illustrated by the initial effort of Steve Jobs’ design team when they tried to design a calculator that Steve (as the user) liked. In this case, the firm internalizes the cost of product customization, and the success of customization depends on effective communication between the firm and its client. In contrast, our study focuses on “consumer customization,” wherein a firm offers a product that the client can self-customize. This strategy is illustrated by the later effort of Steve Jobs’ team when they provided the “Calculator Construction Set” to Steve so that he could design the calculator as he liked. In this case, the firm outsources the customization task and the related costs to consumers, and the success of customization depends on consumers’ customizing capability. Besides saving on customization costs, by offering a consumer-customizable product a firm also avoids the cost and hassle of communicating with clients, and therefore can satisfy consumers’ peculiar needs better and more efficiently. This notion is perfectly illustrated in the Steve Jobs’ calculator example.

Offering consumer-customizable products is particularly beneficial for a firm selling experience products about which consumers have diverse tastes. Consumers learn about their fit with an experience product only after a period of use. For example, a consumer may find out whether a smartphone fits her needs only after having experienced its various functions. Moreover, consumers may have time-varying preferences (Hoeffler and Ariely 1999) or may be uncertain about their own preferences at the time of purchase (Lynch Jr and Ariely 2000). In these situations, offering a consumer-customizable product grants consumers flexibility to adjust the product to best suit their needs when they are ready to do so.<sup>1</sup> Furthermore, when consumers have diverse tastes, it is too costly and thus infeasible for the manufacturer to customize the product for each individual consumer. For example, each individual person can have distinct habits of using computers or smartphones. In this case, by offering a customizable product the firm allows each individual consumer to enjoy a good fit with the product through self-customization, as opposed to offering a standardized product that may fail to suit most consumers’ needs.

Interestingly, whereas in the IT industry offering consumer-customizable products has become fairly common, in other industries firms’ effort in offering consumer customization is still rare and much less successful. Some examples in the non-IT industries include Kraft, Hallmark, M&Ms, Wrigley, and Nike. One recent attempt was Coca-Cola’s launch of the “freestyle machine,” which lets consumers make their own beverages by mixing more than 40 ingredients, including lemon, orange, raspberry, Coke, and Dasani (York and Cancino 2011). Beyond the easy explanation that offering consumer customization for IT products is easier and less costly owing to their digital nature, we are interested in investigating other factors that cause such differences in the practice of offering consumer customization between the IT and non-IT industries. Moreover, even within the IT industry we also see variations in firm strategies on offering consumer-customizable products. We would also like to investigate factors that have caused such variations.

In understanding firm strategies on offering consumer customizable products, it is important to note that consumers differ in their capability to make a customizable product best suit their own needs. Depending on consumers’ skills, experiences, and knowledge about their own preferences, the results of consumer customization can differ considerably, as can be evidenced from the work of a professional and an amateur carpenter. Consumers with poor customizing capability are likely to desire a standardized product preconfigured by the professionally skilled manufacturer over a product that needs their own effort. Overall consumer customizing capability may vary across markets. For example, in general, consumers are more experienced with IT products such as computers and electronics and therefore are more capable of customizing these products than with many non-IT products such as apparel and beverages. Moreover, an individual consumer may develop different customizing capability for different product categories. Whereas a tech guru is likely to have higher customizing capability for IT products than for a beverage, a cook, on the other hand, may have higher customizing capability for beverages than for IT products. Although consumers can enhance customizing capability with any product category through learning, with limited time and cognition capacity, they naturally choose to develop such capability only in key categories more important to their lives.

Consumer heterogeneity in customizing capability has important implications for firm strategies on offering customizable products. In particular, each of the two product offering strategies has its unique advantage, and the heterogeneous customizing capability of consumers affects the relative strengths of their advantages. On one hand, when the firm offers a standardized product, the value of the product is uniform to all consumers, enabling the firm to fully exploit consumer surplus using a uniform price. Nonetheless, the highest price the firm can charge and the maximum profit the firm can obtain is bounded by the manufacturing attributes of its product. On the other hand, when the firm offers a customizable product, consumers’ perceived values of the product differ, and increase with their customizing capability. The firm can set a higher price for the same product when consumers have a higher customizing capability, but has to make a trade-off between collecting a greater demand and acquiring a higher profit margin.

## 1.1. Summary of Results

Our analysis shows that for a monopolistic firm, offering a customizable product is more profitable than offering a standardized product only if the overall customizing capability of the consumer market is sufficiently high; otherwise, offering a standardized product is more profitable. This insight might explain why offering consumer-customizable products is more popular in the IT industry. Owing to the rapid advancement of digital technologies in the past decades, a large population of consumers has accumulated a high level of knowledge and experience about IT products. The extensive digital experiences and knowledge make consumers more interested in and also more capable of customizing IT products. Note that consumers are not just individual people; they can also be individual institutions such as companies and organizations.

We also consider the case where the monopolistic firm is capable of offering both the customizable and the standardized product. We find that offering both products is more profitable than offering either, if the overall consumer customizing capability and consumers’ cost of customization are not too high.

Under this condition, demand is split between consumers with high customizing capability, who buy the customizable product, and consumers with low customizing capability, who buy the standardized product. Consumer surplus when the firm offers both products is greater than when the firm offers the standardized product only, but is lower than when the firm offers the customizable product only. Interestingly, we find that when the firm offers both products, an increased overall consumer customizing capability enhances both firm profit and consumer surplus, but an increased value of the standardized product can hurt both the firm and the consumers.

## 1.2. Related Literature

Our study follows a long tradition of research on product fit and monopolistic competition, which dates back to the early works of Hotelling (1929), Chamberlin (1948), and later Shaked and Sutton (1982), and continues in the information systems and marketing literature. Although in recent years advances in IT have provided consumers with unprecedented price and product information, consumers’ intrinsic and idiosyncratic needs continue to challenge marketers to facilitate the revelation of product fit information and assist consumers in finding suitable products. Consumer uncertainty on product fit can be resolved through seller-induced learning, such as money-back guarantees (Davis et al. 1995), demonstrations (Heiman et al. 2001), or sampling (Gu and Xie 2013). Gu and Liu (2013) show that a big retailer can manipulate consumers’ fit search process through store layout designs. Consumer fit uncertainty may also be resolved through experiential learning. Jing (2011a) studies a dynamic scenario in which first-period buyers of a durable product inform second-period consumers about the product specifics. In a similar setup, Jing (2011b) examines how the seller can promote social learning so that the same number of first-period buyers informs more secondperiod consumers about the durable product. Our study makes a novel contribution to this research by demonstrating that a firm can resolve consumer fit uncertainty by allowing consumers to self-customize the product according to their own tastes, which increases the product value for a broad set of horizontally differentiated consumers at little cost on the firm side. The concept of consumer self-customization is new to the literature and makes a distinctive impact on firm strategies and consumer welfare. The firm strategy of facilitating consumer self-customization constitutes an important new dimension of product differentiation and design that digital technologies make possible, which adds to the traditional strategies of increasing product quality or investing in one set of product features at the cost of others. Although the current study does not explore all aspects of consumer self-customization, we aim it to serve as the starting point for this new and exciting research direction.

By examining firm strategies on offering products that consumers can self-customize, our study complements the literature on firm strategies to customize products for consumers. Dewan et al. (2003) consider a firm that offers a variety of products that spread around the Salop circle of consumer tastes and examine how the costs of providing such products affect firm pricing and market entry deterrence. Syam et al. (2005) consider a model in which two competitive firms have to decide which of two product attributes to customize for consumers after a costly interaction, and find that both firms customize an identical attribute but not both. Ghose and Huang (2009) investigates a duopoly market where one or both firms tailor their product qualities and prices based on consumers’ willingness to pay. Other research examines customized pricing for the same product (for a review, see Arora et al. 2008). For example, Choudhary et al. (2005) examine the competitive implications of personalized pricing when a firm knows a consumer’s willingness to pay for a product. Whereas these studies model how firms can internalize the customization task, in our study the same task is outsourced to consumers. This outsourcing of customization is particularly feasible with IT products.

Our study is also related to the literature on cocreation. Cocreation is the process during which consumers create value together with the company (Prahalad and Ramaswamy 2003). Recent technical advances in information and communication technologies provide the opportunity for consumers to engage directly in an organization’s innovation process through virtual customer environments (Di Gangi and Wasko 2009). In an empirical study, Nambisan and Baron (2007) show that customers’ perceptions of interaction-based benefits will shape their future participation in product support in the virtual customer environments, and customers’ interactions will also generate changes in their affective states, and these, in turn, influence their attitudes toward the firm. Kohler et al. (2011) generate a set of design principles for virtual cocreation systems through synthesizing the insights gained from action research. It is important to note that cocreation typically occurs during the product development process, when the manufacturers involve consumers in product development activities such as generating and evaluating new product ideas; elaborating, evaluating, or challenging product concepts; and creating virtual prototypes (Kohler et al. 2011). In contrast, consumer customization that we study occurs during the product consumption process, after the product development stage. Our study is also related to the product line literature. Offering more products helps a firm to serve consumers with different needs (Villas-Boas 2004, Klemperer 1992, Randall et al. 1998), but the information load involved in product search may cause poor-quality choices or a failure to purchase (Huffman and Kahn 1998, Iyengar and Lepper 2000, Lurie 2004, Kuksov and Villas-Boas 2010). By offering a consumer-customizable product, the firm helps alleviate the cognition cost consumers incur in searching for the product that best suits their needs.

## 3. Analysis

We solve the game through backward induction by first solving the firm’s optimal strategies in cases $\begin{array} { r } { \dot { N , } } \end{array}$ $M ,$ and MN separately and then comparing the firm’s payoffs in the three cases to derive its optimal product offering strategy.

## 3.1. Firm Strategy in Case N and Case M

We first consider case N , where the firm offers a standardized product only. In this case, all consumers buy the standardized product as long as they obtain a nonnegative utility, $U ^ { N } = \alpha - p ^ { N } \geq 0$ . The firm’s optimal strategy is to serve the entire market and fully exploit consumer surplus by charging $p ^ { N * } = \alpha .$ . At this price, the firm’s maximized profit is $\pi ^ { N * } = \alpha ,$ , and consumer surplus is $S ^ { N * } = 0$

We next consider case M, where the firm offers a customizable product only. In this case, a consumer buys the customizable product if and only if $U ^ { M } =$ $V ( { \bf \dot { \boldsymbol { \theta } } } ) - p ^ { M } - c \geq 0 .$ , which is satisfied when her customizing capability is sufficiently high

$$
\theta \geq \underline {{\theta}} ^ {M} (p ^ {M}) = \Gamma (p ^ {M} + c),\tag{3}
$$

where $\underline { { \theta } } ^ { M }$ is the customizing capability of the marginal consumer. The market is partially covered if $\bar { m } < \underline { { \theta } } ^ { M }$ and fully covered otherwise. We derive the firm’s profit function as

$$
\pi^ {M} (p ^ {M}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {1 - \Gamma (p ^ {M} + c)}{1 - m} p ^ {M} & \text {if} m <   \underline {{\theta}} ^ {M}, \\ p ^ {M} & \text {if} m \geq \underline {{\theta}} ^ {M}. \end{array} \right.\tag{4}
$$

When $m < \underline { { \theta } } ^ { M }$ , taking the first order condition of $\pi ^ { M }$ with respect to $p ^ { M }$ , we obtain

$$
\frac {\partial \pi^ {M}}{\partial p ^ {M}} = \frac {1 - \Gamma (p ^ {M} + c)}{1 - m} - \frac {p ^ {M}}{1 - m} \Gamma^ {\prime} (p ^ {M} + c) = 0.\tag{5}
$$

We let $\tilde { p } ^ { M }$ denote the interior solution to Equation (5), which satisfies

$$
\tilde {p} ^ {M} = \frac {1 - \Gamma (\tilde {p} ^ {M} + c)}{\Gamma^ {\prime} (\tilde {p} ^ {M} + c)}\tag{6}
$$

and sustains if

$$
m <   \underline {{\theta}} ^ {M *} = \underline {{\theta}} ^ {M} (\tilde {p} ^ {M}) = \Gamma (\tilde {p} ^ {M} + c).\tag{7}
$$

When $m \geq \underline { { \theta } } ^ { M }$ , all consumers buy the customizable product, and the firm’s optimal strategy is to charge the highest price that renders such a demand. We summarize the firm’s optimal pricing strategy in the following lemma.

$$

$$

$$
\pi^ {M *} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {(1 - \Gamma (\tilde {p} ^ {M} + c))}{1 - m} \tilde {p} ^ {M} & i f m <   \underline {{\theta}} ^ {M *}, \\ V (m) - c & i f m \geq \underline {{\theta}} ^ {M *}. \end{array} \right.\tag{9}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} S ^ {M *} = \int_ {\max \{\underline {{\theta}} ^ {M *}, m \}} ^ {1} (V (\theta) - p ^ {M *} - c) f (\theta) d \theta \\ = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {1}{1 - m} \int_ {\underline {{\theta}} ^ {M *}} ^ {1} (V (\theta) - \tilde {p} ^ {M} - c) d \theta & \text { if } m <   \underline {{\theta}} ^ {M *}, \\ \frac {1}{1 - m} \int_ {m} ^ {1} (V (\theta) - V (m)) d \theta & \text { if } m \geq \underline {{\theta}} ^ {M *}. \end{array} \right. \end{array} \tag {10}\tag{10}

This contrasts with the case when the firm offers the standardized product, where consumers are left with zero surplus, $\mathbf { \dot { S } } ^ { N * } = 0$ . We examine how the firm profit $\pi ^ { M * }$ and consumer surplus $S ^ { M * }$ vary with the overall consumer customizing capability m in the market and obtain the following proposition.

<sup>Proposition</sup> <sup>1.</sup> When the firm offers a customizable product and the overall consumer customizing capability m increases, (1) the firm profit always increases, and (2) the consumer surplus increases only $\dot { i } \dot { f } m < \underline { { { \theta } } } ^ { M * }$ and otherwise decreases.

<sup>Proof.</sup> See the appendix.

Interestingly, Proposition 1 suggests that the firm always benefits from enhanced customizing capability of a consumer market, but consumers are worse off when their customizing capability is already high and continues to increase. With higher overall consumer customizing capability (a larger m), consumers generally perceive a higher value from the customizable product and therefore have a higher willingness to pay. This benefits the firm by allowing it to acquire a greater demand without lowering price if $m < \underline { { { \theta } } } ^ { M * } ,$ or to charge a higher price without losing demand if $m \geq \underline { { \theta } } ^ { M * }$ . Whereas in the former case consumers also gain as more consumers buy the product, in the latter case consumers are worse off since they are paying a higher price for the same product. In practice, many IT companies provide free training classes and offer free samples, which enhance consumer customizing capability. Our result thus suggests that when a firm offers only a customizable product, such activities always benefit the firm, but may hurt consumers.

Comparing the equilibrium firm profit and consumer surplus in case N and in case M, we obtain the following proposition.

<sup>Proposition</sup> <sup>2.</sup> (1) The firm obtains a greater profit from offering a customizable product than from offering a standardized product if the overall consumer customizing capability is sufficiently high

$$
m > \tilde {m} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 - \frac {1 - \Gamma (\tilde {p} ^ {M} + c)}{\alpha} \tilde {p} ^ {M} & i f \alpha <   \tilde {p} ^ {M}, \\ \Gamma (\alpha + c) & i f \alpha \geq \tilde {p} ^ {M}; \end{array} \right.\tag{11}
$$

otherwise, offering a standardized product is more profitable.

(2) Consumer surplus is always greater when the firm offers a customizable product than when the firm offers a standardized product.

<sup>Proof.</sup> See the appendix.

Proposition 2 shows a monopolistic firm may be worse off by offering a customizable product rather than a standardized product. Offering either product has its unique advantage, and the relative strengths of their advantages depend on the overall consumer customizing capability m. On one hand, when the firm offers the standardized product, consumers’ willingness to pay for the product is homogeneous, which allows the firm to fully exploit consumer surplus while serving the entire market. This constitutes a unique advantage compared with the case where the firm offers the customizable product, where consumers’ heterogeneous willingness to pay forces the firm to make a trade-off between charging a higher price and covering a larger market. When m increases, the customizing capability of the entire market is improved. Consumer heterogeneity in customizing capability is reduced, weakening the benefit of offering the standardized product. On the other hand, when the firm offers the customizable product, consumers’ willingness to pay for the product increases with their customizing capability, which allows the firm to charge a higher price for the same product. This constitutes an advantage compared with the case where the firm offers the standardized product, where consumers’ willingness to pay is bounded by the product’s manufacturing attributes. When m increases, more consumers have high customizing capability, strengthening the benefit of offering the customizable product. Collectively, offering a customizable product brings the firm a profit advantage over offering a standardized product when m is sufficiently high. Since consumers’ customizing capability is generally higher for IT products, our result may explain why offering customizable product is more common in the IT industry than in non-IT industries. Proposition 2 also shows that consumers always obtain a greater surplus when the firm offers a customizable product. This is because the firm cannot fully exploit consumer surplus when their willingness to pay for the product is heterogeneous. Our result thus suggests an interesting win–win situation when the firm offers the customizable product.

## 3.2. Firm Strategy in Case MN

In addition, an increased value of the standardized product shifts consumer demand from the customizable product to the standardized product, reducing total consumer surplus. Our result suggests that it is not always profitable for a firm to invest in enhancing the value of the standardized product it offers, such as offering more product functions, when the firm also offers a customizable product.

## 5. Conclusion

Our study makes the first attempt to examine firm strategies on offering a product that consumers can self-customize to satisfy their idiosyncratic needs. Our analysis shows that it is more profitable for a monopolistic firm to offer a consumer-customizable product than to offer a standardized product if the overall customizing capability of the consumer market is sufficiently high; otherwise, offering a standardized product is more profitable. We also consider the case where the firm is capable of offering both a customizable and a standardized product and show that offering both products is more profitable than offering either if the overall consumer customizing capability and consumers’ customization cost are both low. Interestingly, when the firm offers both products, enhanced overall consumer customizability always benefits both the firm and consumers, but increased value of the standardized product can hurt both firm profit and consumer surplus. Furthermore, the total surplus of consumers when the firm offers both products is higher than when the firm just offers the standardized product, but lower than when the firm offers the customizable product.

Although our study focuses on the monopolistic case, it is interesting to consider a competitive market where each of two competing firms decides whether to offer a customizable product or a standardized product. Our preliminary results show that in equilibrium the two symmetric firms adopt differentiated product offering strategies, with one firm offering the customizable product to serve consumers with high customizing capability and the other firm offering the standardized product to serve consumers with low customizing capability. This result is interesting because it shows that, facing market competition, the two symmetric firms are likely to adopt asymmetric product offering strategies. This is because in a market characterized by consumer heterogeneity in customizing capability, offering different types of products (customizable or standardized) creates product differentiation and consequently reduces price competition between the two firms. The strategic differentiation in firms’ product offering strategies can also be achieved when the two competing firms offer products with different levels of customizability. This insight may explain the variation in firm product offering strategies in the IT industry.

A limitation of the current study is that we do not consider consumer learning on customizing capability or market dynamics related to consumer learning. There are many interesting future research directions to pursue in this regard. For example, a consumer may improve their customizing capability for a firm’s product through experiencing that product or through experiencing the firm’s other related products. Moreover, early buyers who acquire customizing capability for a product may share such knowledge with late buyers through online tutorials or word of mouth. Such experiential learning and social learning on consumer customizing capability can have important implications for a firm’s dynamic marketing strategies including sequential product launches, dynamic pricing, and product line design. Another interesting future research direction is to examine strategies of two-sided platforms, where developers on one side produce customizability that consumers on the other side can consume. We leave these interesting issues for future explorations.
