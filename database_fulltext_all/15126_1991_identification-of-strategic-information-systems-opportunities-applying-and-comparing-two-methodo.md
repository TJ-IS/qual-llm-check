## Abstract

Much has been said about opportunities for the strategic use of information technology by organizations aiming to gain a competitive advantage. However, not much is known about the actual process by which opportunities for the use of strategic information systems are identified. While various planning methodologies have been proposed, there is at present a paucity of information on empirical results obtained from applying them, and on their effectiveness, efficiency, and specificity. This article presents the results of a field experiment aimed at applying and comparing two well-known methodologies for identifying information systems opportunities from a competitive advantage perspective—Porter's value chain and Wiseman's strategic thrusts methodology. An instrument was prepared to operationalize each methodology, which was then applied in two matched sets of 10 medium-sized enterprises. Both methodologies were found to be effective in generating a significant number of ideas for information systems worthy of implementation. Similarities and differences are analyzed and discussed in terms of the number, estimated implementation costs and duration, managerial level, and decision to implement the applications identified by the two methodologies. These applications are also classified from the perspective of both Porter's and Wiseman's framework. The results seem to indicate that while there is an overall similarity between the two methodologies, there are certain differences that show the more outward orientation of the strategic thrusts framework and its greater attractiveness for organizations in unstable environments.

Keywords: Planning methodologies, strategic information systems, competitive advantage, interorganizational systems, electronic data interchange

## Introduction

In the past, information systems have concentrated on internal functions of the organization such as accounting, inventory, and managerial control. At present many of these systems have spread beyond the boundaries of the organization because of the establishment of electronic links among customers, suppliers, and competitors (Bergeron and Buteau, 1988; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Porter, 1985b). Interorganizational information systems allow major improvements in organizational effectiveness and constitute one of the most important competitive weapons available to the organizaitons (Ives and Learmonth, 1984; Notowidigdo, 1984).

Much has been said concerning the opportunities created by the strategic use of information technology. Unfortunately, not much is known about the process of implementing the planning methodologies that would allow an organization to identify, develop, and implement strategic applications. Sinclair (1986) found the implementation of a planning technique to be a major problem with regard to justifying the methodology, applying it, and evaluating its output. More recently, Lederer and Sethi (1988) observed in a study of 80 organizations that the implementation of information systems planning methodologies such as Business Systems Planning, Information Engineering, and Strategic Planning Systems was problematic with respect to resources, process, and output and that managers were only moderately satisfied with them. Vitale, et al. (1986) define these planning methodologies as attempting to align information systems with organizational goals, which is the opposite of methodologies that directly impact organizational strategies.

At present, there is a paucity of information available on the implementation of the impact planning methodologies like the value chain (Porter and Millar, 1985) and the theory of strategic thrusts (Rackoff, et al., 1985), and new methodologies seem to appear every day. Because it is possible that impact planning methodologies carry the same problems as alignment planning methodologies, it is legitimate to wonder about their effectiveness and efficiency. In other words, what results can we expect from their use? The purpose of this article is to study the applicability and output specificity of two impact planning methodologies used to identify information systems from a competitive advantage perspective.

## Conceptual Framework

## Strategic information systems and competitive advantage

Information systems can be used strategically by an organization in order to secure gains over its competitors. Information systems for competitive advantage are defined as information systems that drive or formulate the organization's competitive strategy in order to provide it with (or to maintain) a competitive advantage (Wiseman, 1985). Such systems reflect the fundamental objectives of the firm and may have a significant impact on its success (EDP Analyzer, 1984).

Information technology may be used to help the organization produce at lower cost, to differentiate itself from its competitors, or to identify and concentrate on a particular market segment (Porter, 1980; Porter and Millar, 1985). These three strategies may be achieved by using information technology to raise entry barriers, increase negotiating power with suppliers, create new dependencies for clients, offer new products or substitutes, and even change the grounds for competition or the nature of the stakes (McFarlan, 1984; Porter, 1979; 1985b). To provide a competitive advantage, information technology must modify the structure of the industry, improve the position of the firm, or create new business opportunities (Bott, et al., 1986; Porter and Millar, 1985). Basically, a competitive advantage is created when a firm increases its comparative efficiency or its bargaining power (Bakos and Treacy, 1986; Johnston and Vitale, 1988). Any competitive strategy should aim at a durable competitive advantage. Such advantage is accomplished when differentiation affects the consumer's perceptions favorably, when the firm has the capacity to maintain this differentiation over the competition, and when it endures (Coyne, 1986). Gaining a competitive advantage should result in a long-term added financial return for the organization even if, at times, benefits are difficult to quantify (Cooper and Kaplan, 1988).

There are, however, different points of view on the actual meaning of competitive advantage. One the one hand, from an economics of innovation point of view, a firm has a competitive advantage if it is able to obtain a return on its investment that is better than normal in a given industry and if this advantage is sufficiently long lived to be considered as altering industry structure (Clemons and Kimbrough, 1986; Porter, 1985a). More precisely, information systems for competitive advantage should reduce costs, add value and entail substantial switching costs for customers and users, and also provide sufficient time for the innovating firm to reap the benefits before it is imitated by its competitors. Hence, from this point of view, true opportunities for achieving sustainable competitive advantage are relatively rare (Clemons, 1986). For instance, Teece (1987) notes that imitators can often outperform innovators. Moreover, in a case study of automated teller machines (ATMs), Banker and Kauffman (1988) found the application of information technology to be beneficial, but not necessarily to deliver competitive advantage. Even in the McKesson Economost case, Clemons and Row (1988) found information technology to have become a strategic necessity rather than a source of competitive advantage.

On the other hand, Benjamin, et al. (1988) take a less restrictive view when they state that the innovator can conserve his or her advantage by continually adding valuable new capabilities to the system. Furthermore, Wiseman (1988) advocates pursuing opportunities that have a wide range (short, medium, or long term), are sustainable or contestable, and provide price, feature, or other advantages. These advantages can be measured not only by return on investment but also by other measures such as market share and number of new customers. In conclusion, the pursuit of strategic opportunities for information systems is worthwhile even though one cannot be certain beforehand that a competitive advantage will in fact be attained, whatever the interpretation of what constitutes such an advantage. This is confirmed by Clemons and Kimbrough (1986), who conclude that the recent attention given to the strategic use of information systems or the priority placed on finding opportunities is not overstressed.

## Usefulness and applicability of impact planning methodologies

There exist several impact planning methodologies aimed at the identification of information systems for competitive advantage. These methodologies are based on competitive forces (McFarlan, 1984; Porter, 1979), competitive strategies (Porter, 1980), the value chain (Rackoff, et al., 1985), the consumer resource life (Ives and Learmonth, 1984), the impact of information technology (Munro and Huff, 1985; Parsons, 1983), and the strategic opportunities matrix (Benjamin, et al., 1984). They improve our awareness of the potential uses of information technology for competitive purposes, help classify existing applications, and allow the evaluation of their strategic potential. Their usefulness in generating new and innovative strategic opportunities has, however, not yet been verified.

In order to shed some light on this topic, this study aims to answer two main questions:

1. Are impact planning methodologies applicable, and what are their results?

2. What are the differences in the results obtained from different methodologies?

## Selection of methodologies

Two impact planning methodologies were selected for the purpose of this study. The selection was made on the basis of two criteria:

1. The methodologies had to be well-known in the information systems field and had to be recognized as worthy of consideration;

2. The methodologies had to use frameworks based on different perspectives.

Porter's value chain was selected because, in a survey of chief information officers and managers conducted by Vitale, et al. (1986), it was identified as the most widely used methodology. The second methodology selected was Wiseman's (1985) theory of strategic thrusts, from a paper that won first prize in the 1985 Society for Information Management (SIM) Paper Competition (Rackoff, Wiseman, and Ullrich, 1985). These methodologies use quite different approaches. Other methodologies could have probably been selected, but these two represent interesting alternatives for a first comparison of impact planning methodologies.

## The Value Chain

The concept of the value chain was developed by Michael Porter (Porter, 1985a; 1985b). Its main aim is to discover how information technology can have an impact on the overall performance of a firm by reconstructing the entire process of product or service creation, development, and sale to clients (as shown in the framework presented in Appendix A). Setting up a value chain requires a study of:

1. The firm's primary activities, i.e., producing and delivering the product to the consumer (internal logistics, production, external logistics, marketing and sales, as well as post-sales service);

2. Support activities involved in each of the primary activities, i.e., providing human, material, and technological resources as well as the infrastructure to facilitate the work involved in the primary activities.

## The Theory of Strategic Thrusts

The theory of strategic thrusts was developed by Wiseman (1985). It uses a grid known as a generator of strategic options that allows a manager to analyze three strategic targets of the firm's industry: suppliers, clients, and competitors. This grid also makes the manager aware of the main orientations the firm can take in the quest for competitive advantage: differentiation, cost reductions, innovation, growth and alliance; it constitutes the interface between the competitive strategy of the firm and information technology (as shown in the framework presented in Appendix B).

## The Scope of the Study

It is important to mention that the scope of this study is limited to the applicability of the two selected methodologies in generating interesting ideas for information systems from a strategic perspective. However, the information systems (IS) opportunities generated by these methodologies were not assessed for their effectiveness in providing an acutal competitive advantage. Such an assessment would have required an in-depth evaluation of the implemented systems, which was beyond the scope of the study.

## Comparison criteria

In relation to the research questions, various criteria can be used to analyze and compare the results obtained with the two methodologies. The first and most obvious criterion is related to the number of ideas that are uncovered and are worthy of implementation (Rackoff, et al., 1985). Are only one or two primary ideas found per organization, or do many emerge from the identification process? From this point of view, one methodology would be considered more effective if it generally identified more IS opportunities than the other methodology.

The decision to actually implement such opportunities is constrained, however, by management's evaluation of the associated costs versus the potential value or benefit for the organization (Crowston and Treacy, 1986). This suggests two additional primary criteria—estimated costs and length of time required to implement the identified applications. From a practitioner viewpoint, it would be useful to know if one methodology generates applications that generally cost less and take less time to implement than the other. The other criterion is based on the implementation decision itself; estimated development costs and duration notwithstanding, management can judge that an application's potential value is sufficient to warrant its implementation. One methodology would then be considered more effective than the other if more of the ideas it uncovers are actually scheduled for implementation.

Another criteria is based on the traditional classification of information systems using Anthony's (1965) model of organizational planning and control processes (operational control, management control, strategic planning), as employed by researchers such as Cheney and Dickson (1982). While some have argued that this model is not really appropriate for competitive information (Rockoff, et al., 1985), classifying these systems from such a perspective is nonetheless possible and could be useful for analysis and comparison purposes. Whereas information systems for competitive advantage are qualified as “strategic” because they result from the formulation and implementation of a competitive strategy based on the new information technology, they generally do not support top management in the process of formulating and implementing strategy. As such, they are considered by Huff and Beattie (1985-86) to differ from true strategic information systems. Thus, from the previously cited work on competitive information systems, one would expect most of the applications generated by the two methodologies to support the operational and tactical levels of the organization rather than the strategic level.

Finally, the two methodologies themselves each provide a classification scheme for the applications. In one case, an application can be categorized by the primary or support activity it addresses (Appendix A); in the other case, an application can be categorized by the strategic thrust and target it aims at (Appendix B). Moreover, an application identified by one methodology can also be categorized within the other methodology's framework. This cross-classification allows us to obtain a more complete picture of the distribution of IS opportunities from two different perspectives; it is also useful in analyzing the differences between the two methodologies. For example, a methodology could generate applications that generally favor certain primary activities or certain strategic thrusts, and would thus be more congruent with the organization's strategic orientation.

## Methodology

## Sample

The sample consisted of 20 business enterprises subdivided into two matched sets of 10 enterprises; each set contained seven firms in the manufacturing sector (food processing, leather, furniture, paper, electronics, chemicals) and three firms in the services sector (stock brokerage, lodging, transportation). The value chain methodology (Porter and Millar, 1985) was used in one set of firms, and the strategic thrusts methodology (Rackoff, et al., 1985) was used in the other set, thus allowing for greater comparability between the two methodologies.

There were no significant differences between the two sets of firms in terms of number of employees, annual sales, annual IS budget, and number of IS personnel—the mean values were 264 vs. 242, \$39M vs. \$31M, \$231K vs. \$319K and 2.5 vs. 3.5 respectively (two-tailed t-tests, non-significant at the .05 level).

The 20 sampled organizations are medium-sized enterprises (200-350 employees) located in the province of Quebec, Canada. It should be noted that medium-sized enterprises are deemed to possess specific attributes that distinguish them from the large enterprises most often studied in regard to information systems for competitive advantage. On one hand, these enterprises are more vulnerable because of their lack of financial and human resources (Noori, 1987), as well as information resources that are needed to sufficiently understand and master the organization and its environment (Van Kirk and Noonan, 1982). On the other hand, medium-sized organizations have specific advantages compared to large organizations because of their greater flexibility (Crawford and Ibrahim, 1985), their quickness in implementing decisions, and their capacity for adaptation and short-term reorientation (Julien and Lafrance, 1983).

## Operationalization of the two methodologies

## Instrumentation

Porter and Wiseman do not clearly explain how to apply their methodologies to elicit IS opportunities. Hence, we developed an instrument that would allow an analyst to apply either one or the other methodology. This instrument consists of a questionnaire and support material presenting concepts and examples designed to help the analyst in gathering data while conducting meetings (this instrument is available from the authors upon request). It is divided in four parts, each part corresponding to a different step in the application process.

The first step is a presentation of the concept of information systems for competitive advantage to the top manager and line managers. The second step is a data-gathering phase where information is collected on the following topics: the organization (socio-demographic data); the information systems department (socio-demographic data); the firm's strategic planning mission and objectives, product-market mix and supplier characteristics, strengths and weaknesses, and opportunities and environmental threats; and personal motivations of the CEO. The third step is the application of the methodology selected: Wiseman's theory of strategic thrusts or Porters' value chain.

The grid suggested by Wiseman in Rackoff, et al. (1985) or the value chain model as presented in Porter and Millar (1985), is used to generate opportunities for information systems. First, the selected methodology is explained and underlying concepts are clarified. Then, the meeting is conducted as a brainstorming session, and participants refrain from assessing or criticizing ideas generated. In order to stimulate discussion, the same question is asked for each cell (or element) in the model: How could information systems be used for the function X of the value chain (in the Porter case)? How could information systems be used in cell X of the grid (in the Wiseman case)? The last step is an assessment, by the managers involved, of the information systems opportunities identified in step 3. This evaluation is made on the basis of the following criteria: implementation costs, duration, and implementation decision.

## Process

The firms used in this study were initially contacted by phone to generate interest and to obtain the collaboration of top management. At this time we also verified that the firm was computerized (an initial criterion). Meetings were then scheduled to learn details about the company's business and to further explain the study. Data collection was the responsibility of two graduate students sufficiently knowledgeable on information technology in general, and on the concept of information systems for competitive advantage in particular. One student used the value chain methodology for one group of 10 firms, while the other student used the strategic thrusts methodology for the other group. There was no communication between the two students for the duration of this activity. Because the sampled firms came from different sectors, it was possible to minimize a cumulative or transfer effect in identifying IS applications as each individual applied the methodology assigned to him or her.

The initial meetings were held with an average of two managers attending from each firm—more precisely 2.5 for the value chain group and 2.2 for the strategic thrusts group. The participation of at least one top executive was necessary to obtain an adequate understanding of the organization, its strategy (if it had one), and its environment. The executive responsible for the IS function (who was often the individual responsible for the accounting function) also participated because of a direct concern for the matter at hand and a greater technical knowledge and experience in regard to systems implementation and evaluation. In certain cases, managers from other functional units (e.g., marketing and production) participated. No significant differences were found between the participants in the two groups in terms of sectorial experience, organizational tenure, and computer experience (the mean number of years were 14.5 vs. 13, 7 vs. 9.5 and 8 vs. 6 respectively).

## Analytic procedures

Once the data-collection phase was completed, IS opportunities were classified first, in their respective methodology's framework and second, in the alternate methodology's framework. This cross-classification allowed a comparison of results from two different perspectives, without giving preference to any framework in particular. Applications were also classified based upon their managerial level.

## Results

In keeping with the exploratory and descriptive nature of this study, analysis of the results was based for the most part on the number of IS opportunities identified through the methodology described above, broken down by the identification methodology used (value chain vs. strategic thrusts), and by the different characterization criteria. In relation to the two research questions, overall results are presented next, followed by an examination of the differences in number and type of application ideas generated by the two selected methodologies.

## Characterization of the identified applications

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of the number, estimated implementation costs, and estimated implementation duration of the information systems opportunities identified in the 20 sampled firms. In total, 152 ideas for information systems were generated. Per organization the average was 7.6, the minimum was 4, and the maximum was 12. These findings allow us to generalize Rackoff, et al.'s (1985) results obtained in one large organization (GTE); these researchers were surprised to find that the identification process yielded not one or two primary ideas as they expected from prior stories about the competitive use of information technology, but many worthy of implementation.

The relatively large number of application opportunities generated in this study by both methodologies tend to confirm their comprehensiveness in surveying the important links between the various components of the organization, its strategy, and its environment. More focused methodologies, such as lves and Learmonth's (1984) customer resource life cycle, tend to cover a smaller subset of possible applications. This also seems to confirm the effectiveness of the value chain and strategic thrusts methodologies in actually identifying feasible opportunities, as shown by a median estimated implementation cost and duration of \$41,400 (15 percent of the average IS budget) and five months respectively per application. In short, the main result of this study is that both methodologies work, not only in large enterprises but also in smaller ones, and not only in mini-case studies or exemplars but in systematic use across a relatively wide spectrum of enterprise sectors.

## Differences between the two methodologies

As shown in Table 1, two-tailed t-tests confirm that there are no significant differences between the value chain and strategic thrusts methodologies in terms of the average number of application opportunities generated per firm (7.5 vs. 7.7, t = .22, n = 20, NS: non-significant at the .05 level). The table also shows that there are no significant differences in terms of the average implementation duration estimated for each application (7.7 vs. 8.3 months, t = .39, n = 152, NS). The estimated implementation costs for each application are, however, significantly smaller on average in the case of the value chain methodology (\$64,400 vs. \$274,900, t = 2.62, n = 152, p = .005). This last difference is less important if one looks at the median costs (\$35,000 vs. \$50,000) rather than the average costs, and is interpreted later in light of other results. There were also no significant differences between the two methodologies in terms of the breakdown of applications identified among the 10 sectors of business activity represented in the sample (chi $^{2}$ = 2.62, degrees of freedom = 9, NS). Thus, in a relatively broad range of organizations, both methodologies were equally effective in identifying a substantial number of feasible opportunities for applying information technology.

This similarity is also true for the breakdown of applications by managerial level, as shown in Table 2 (chi $^{2}$ = 0.29, d.f. = 2, NS). Regarding the breakdown by primary activity of the value chain (Table 3), there were no overall differences (chi $^{2}$ = 3.48, d.f. = 4, NS). However, the value chain methodology was found to generate more applications in the operations function (28 vs. 20) and, conversely, significantly fewer in the marketing and sales function (18 vs. 26). A possible interpretation of this result is that the value chain methodology concentrates more on the internal activities of the enterprise, whereas the strategic thrusts methodology concentrates more on the external and would thus provide more support to functions oriented toward the environment, i.e., marketing, sales, and outbound logistics. There were, however, no significant differences in terms of the support activities addressed by the two methodologies (Table 3, chi $^{2}$ = 0.90, d.f. = 2, NS).

Again, results in Table 4 show that applications generated by the value chain and strategic thrusts methodologies did not differ significantly, both in terms of their overall breakdown by strategic thrust (chi $^{2}$ = 6.22, d.f. = 4, NS) and by strategic target (chi $^{2}$ = 2.08, d.f. = 2, NS). However, the value chain methodology was found to generate more opportunities for innovation (6 vs. 1) and less opportunities for growth (7 vs. 13). These results relate to the previous findings because innovation results from a product or process transformation that chiefly involves internal changes in the way an organization operates; growth results from more market-led and outward-bound strategies such as expansion, integration, or diversification. We also found that the value chain methodology identified less supplier-oriented opportunities (7 vs. 13). This could reflect that suppliers occupy, as one of the three external targets, a more explicit role in the strategic thrusts methodology, but only an indirect role through the procurement and inbound logistics activities in the value chain methodology.

The results in Table 5 show that top management decided to implement a smaller number of applications identified by the value chain methodology (47 vs. 58), even though the strategic thrusts methodology generates applications that are more costly to implement, as indicated previously. A possible explanation is that greater strategic potential can be obtained from the latter applications, which are more oriented toward marketing, sales and growth; thus, while greater risks and costs would be incurred because of the greater complexity of applications that cut across or extend organizational boundaries (Johnson and Vitale, 1988; Vitale, 1986), the higher potential reward for the firm would become the deciding factor in the implementation decision.

Finally, the value chain methodology was found to be less applicable in the service firms than in the manufacturing firms in terms of the number of application opportunities identified. This could be attributed to the various activities that constitute the value chain, which basically represent physical flows of goods and materials rather than flows of information. Overall, these results tend to confirm that the strategic thrusts methodology has a more outward orientation and thus generates IS opportunities that are more inter-organizational in nature (Barrett and Konsynski, 1982).

## Limits and Conclusion

This study has some limits that must be mentioned. The size and nature of the sample evidently preclude outright generalization of the results. The interviewers, while knowledgeable and familiar with the concepts involved, had no previous experience in applying the two methodologies chosen to identify information systems. Differences in ability of the two could have affected the results. In addition, the results of the study could be enhanced by further validation in these areas: (1) the classification of the applications on the various criteria, because there are some borderline cases where the assignment of an application to a category is not evident at first hand; (2) the instrument, because it was built upon descriptions of both the value chain and strategic thrusts, which are not very explicit in terms of operationalizing these methodologies; and (3) comparison of the two impact planning methodologies wiht a more traditional alignment planning methodology such as Business Systems Planning.

In view of the paucity of previously documented systems that actually provide demonstrable advantage, this study does not claim that most of the opportunities generated will in fact procure a competitive advantage if implemented, however one defines competitive advantage. Despite the fact that these opportunities result from applying methodologies specifically designed for this purpose, further in-depth investigations of actual implemented systems should be conducted. Such investigations would provide measures of the impact of these systems in terms of competitive advantage and thus provide a truer test of the effectiveness of the methodologies studied in this article.

These limits notwithstanding, this study has found both methodologies to be applicable and productive in identifying IS opportunities in medium-sized enterprises. In addition, the two methodologies were enthusiastically received by all the participating managers. While there were few overall differences between the value chain and strategic thrusts methodologies, certain characteristics might lead an organization to chose one over another in order to achieve a better fit with its strategic orientation. Companies in stable environments looking to gain a competitive advantage through changes in their internal processes, such as innovation in production and technology development, might choose the value chain methodology. On the other hand, companies that must continually scan a changing environment might prefer the strategic thrusts methodology to gain a more competitive position within this environment through, for instance, growth or differentiation related to changes in the marketing, sales, and outbound logistics functions. Overall, the experience obtained from this study has led us to believe that this last methodology is generally more attractive to managers than the value chain methodology.

Because this research was only an exploratory attempt at systematically evaluating impact planning methodologies, additional research is needed to provide increased internal validity to the operationalization of the two methodologies investigated in this article. Increased external validity must also be obtained by sampling more organizations in more diverse business sectors, e.g., the commercial sector, and of more diverse size and type, e.g., small businesses, public, or non-profit organizations, in which case these methodologies might have to be adapted for a particular context. Other methodologies that have been proposed, such as the customer resource life cycle, should also be systematically investigated and compared in the same manner. This could eventually lead to a contingency approach in which a methodology would be chosen on the basis of its congruence with factors such as the level of uncertainty in the organization's environment, overall strategy, and management of information resources.
