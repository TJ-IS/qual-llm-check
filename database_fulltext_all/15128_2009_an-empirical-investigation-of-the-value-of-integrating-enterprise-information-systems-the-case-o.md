Abstra ct: This paper identifies and measures the various business value benefits that accrue as a result of implementing and integrating large-scale enterprise information systems. Specifically, we look at the integration of electronic medical records for all patients with the radiology information system and a picture archiving and communication system at a regional medical center. Our work is among the first to carefully study and analyze the impact of enterprise information systems at a large-scale service organization that produces intangible outputs—health. It extends the literature on information economics by quantifying the benefits in process dynamics as a source of ongoing firm-level performance improvements. The key dimensions of measurements include financial revenues, operating lead times, and subjective satisfaction levels by the clinical staff and by the referring physicians. Analyses of longitudinal data suggest that performance levels along a key metric—clinical process lead time—showed a significant improvement immediately after the deployment and integration of the systems. The evidence reveals that performance kept improving for the following 12 months at an impressive learning rate of 63 percent. Moreover, the reported satisfaction level after installation was higher among referring physicians who actively used the full spectrum of technological functionalities at their own clinics or at the hospital’s site. Finally, we present a general framework for capturing the actual tangible and intangible benefits of enterprise information systems installation and integration in a clinical context.

Key words a nd phra ses: business value, electronic medical research, empirical regularities, empirical research, enterprise information systems, health care, radiology information systems, satisfaction, systems integration.

Enterprise systems a re am ong the most importa nt information technology (IT) categories to have emerged [12]. In health care, and especially in hospitals, medical images are currently created digitally and stored in the radiology department’s picture archiving and communication system (PACS). Reports are usually kept in the electronic medical record (EMR) of the hospital information system (HIS), and particularly in the radiology information system (RIS), which is designed to store, manipulate, and retrieve information for planning, organizing, directing, and controlling administrative activities associated with the provision and utilization of radiology services and facilities. High-quality services can be provided, however, if EMR data are integrated with the digital images in a PACS. Clinicians should be able to access both systems’ data as part of their regular working environment, whether HIS or RIS. A combined RIS/ PACS should allow for teleconferencing with other users; for example, for consultation with a specialist or technologist in the radiology department.

Combining RIS with PACS produces an integrated desktop that enhances diagnostic efficiency and productivity and streamlines the acquisition of, and access to, patient information [8]. Radiologists can quickly view a patient’s complete clinical position for fast, accurate exam interpretations. Images can be automatically displayed on the workstation’s diagnostic monitors, complemented by an RIS side monitor for viewing textual information about the patient. Simple mouse clicks access a comprehensive tool set, exam data, clinical data, patient histories, and voice dictation. Activating embedded native tool sets such as 3-D and vessel analysis combined with automated prior image selection is just as quick and easy. Requesting physicians can enter orders directly through an HIS via secure Web communications, thereby reducing entry tasks and possibilities for error. Orders can also be entered manually, or scanned in from paper documents, at the radiology reception desk. It all adds up to a more efficient work flow for the radiology unit and better service to referring physicians, as well as expanded marketing options.

In this study, we investigate the business value of integrating enterprise information systems in a large-scale clinical context. We focus on a careful empirical analysis of RIS/PACS implementation in a medical center. Although such systems cost several million dollars and thus constitute significant expenditure for an ongoing operation, the economic value of their implementation has never been established. Our study identifies and measures several key performance indicators regarding the financial, operational, and marketing aspects of this enterprise integration effort.

The evidence is clear that such an implementation can result in significant revenue enhancement—between 20 percent and 40 percent, depending on the modality. Moreover, we observe an 80 percent reduction in the process turnaround times (TAT) and a statistically significant increase in the satisfaction level from the service. In addition, our findings indicate that the major benefits do not occur overnight, but rather over an extended period of 12 months, driven by a significant amount of organizational learning. Our longitudinal three-year study thus is one of the first to model and quantify the business value of enterprise systems installation and integration in a service organization.

## Related Research

Film a nd f ilm-rela ted savings that are associated with PACS implementation come from the elimination of (1) the film library, (2) film processors, (3) darkroom and film library personnel, and (4) film costs for specialties (number of procedures, sheets, etc.). Investments in new digital modalities continue, even in small enterprises [28]. One way to increase revenue is to increase the volume of healthy covered individuals (capitation contracts) or to compete for large contracts’ “carve-out” services and tertiary care. Another way is to expand the hospital’s competitive reach geographically. Recent studies have shown that a soft-copy department will be somewhat more expensive to operate than a conventional one, and that radiologists initially will be slightly less productive when using the new technology. In the long run, however, reports have shown that PACS can be cost-effective by allowing for a significant increase in workload and volume of examinations without a corresponding increase in the number of radiologists and technologists.

Hospital-wide savings associated with PACS and filmless operations are more difficult to quantify. Estimates show significant savings in physicians’ time that could even lead to a reduction in clinical positions. In addition, decreased waiting times for radiology reports have the potential to reduce the length of stays, increase clinicians efficiency and accuracy in patient care, and decrease medicolegal risks. These reductions in costs become even more noteworthy in institutions that comprise a network of multiple hospitals and clinics. Combining multiple imaging departments into a single operation by relying on PACS technology can lead to significant improvements.

In general, productivity is a fundamental economic measure of a technology’s contribution [5]. Prior research on white-collar productivity cites statistics indicating that output per production worker grew by 16.9 percent between the mid-1970s and 1986, while output per information worker decreased by 6.6 percent [25]. Still, the output and production function for information workers—managers, consultants, researchers, marketers, and other more traditional services such as health care—is poorly modeled and measured. As information work represents a growing proportion of gross domestic product (GDP), and is increasingly leveraged with IT, understanding IT and productivity in the context of information work is especially important.

Some empirical research suggests that firms realize productivity and market capitalization benefits from implementing IT systems [18], and studies of operational performance suggest that enterprise resource planning (ERP) positively influences performance over time [19]. In addition, in the case of a firm that implemented ERP and subsequently experienced benefits through gains to its continuous improvement efforts, analyses of longitudinal data suggest that performance along a key metric (order fulfillment lead time) showed a significant improvement immediately after the system was deployed. The data further suggest that the implementation gave rise to an ongoing trend of performance improvement, in contrast to stable performance before [10]. In addition, evidence for just-in-time systems suggest that they can be substantially enhanced by modern IT support [29].

A significant body of literature examines the strategic aspects of electronic data interchange (EDI) [10, 21, 31]. However, there have been only a few systematic empiri cal assessments of the strategic benefits of this technology. Prior work has primarily considered the impact of EDI. For example, one study [20] found that EDI can lead to a range of benefits for an industrial supplier, depending on how technology choices are made by its trading partners. Among others, Mukhopadhyay and Kekre found that advanced EDI significantly reduces the probability of rework and delay [20].

Several researchers try to measure the benefits of IT using empirical techniques. However, most carry out empirical investigation at the industry or the economy level. One study [6] using data from 527 large U.S. firms explored the effect of computerization on productivity and output growth and found that computerization makes a contribution to measured productivity and output growth in the short term that is consistent with normal returns to computer investments. Moreover, the productivity and output contributions associated with computerization are up to five times greater over several years [6]. This contribution, however, is accompanied by relatively large and time-consuming investments in complementary inputs, such as organizational capital, that may be omitted in conventional calculations of productivity.

Compared with conventional film-screen operation, filmless operation using computed radiography has been associated with a significant decrease in examination times for technologists. Reiner and Siegel [24] infer that the decrease in technologist examination times in a filmless environment offers the potential for increased productivity, with resulting personnel savings and improved operational efficiency. However, they emphasize the productivity enhancements of technologists without considering the effects on radiologists, and our study suggests that this approach may be unjustified.

## Tangible Benefits and Costs of RIS/PACS

In general, tangible benefits and costs are commonly known and easy to recognize. They are usually measurable in terms of cost reduction, which is associated with improving business efficiency, and generation of additional sales, which creates additional revenue. Tangible costs put users in control of their expenses, as they are easy to quantify.

The first obvious benefit of adopting RIS/PACS is savings on the purchase and handling of film. PACS implementations achieve an average 80 percent reduction in film usage within the first year [16]. In addition, there are reports of reduced costs for film handling, storage, and couriers. The interim steps of prepping jackets, completing paperwork, and locating prior images are eliminated with digital image management.

Another benefit is increased productivity. Interestingly, academic radiology departments across the United States do not employ a standardized set of productivity and financial indicators to measure their performance, although the radiology literature claims that an integrated RIS/PACS increases patient throughput and work flow productivity [8]. Examination volume is used most frequently to measure productivity, but other indicators are also common: examination volume per modality, technical relative value units (RVUs), professional RVUs, technical RVUs per full-time equivalent (FTE) employee, professional RVUs per FTE employee, gross charges by modality, collections by FTE employee, and examination volume by resource or device. General expenses are commonly used as indicators of financial status [22]. Still, productivity indicators are the most frequently used [23].

Thus, we chose to measure productivity by testing the hypothesis that RIS/PACS improves the billing system and increases the operational revenues of a diagnostic imaging service provider. Specifically, we examine the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1 (The RIS/PACS Implementation Hypothesis): Implementing RIS/ PACS results in a rise in the revenues per procedure, due to more accurate documentation and coding and faster and more reliable billing.

Another benefit is the reduced TAT, which is a priority for any radiology service seeking to boost profitability and increase referrals [8]. An important measurement of efficiency in radiology service is promptness in producing a final report [11]. Some practices document times approaching 30 hours or more for the entire workflow process. Based on a survey technique, research done for a RIS/PACS vendor found that an RIS measurably and significantly decreases report turnaround and transcription time, resulting in faster bill processing and enabling an increase in the number of procedures performed per month (RIS Logic at https://www.merge.com/fusion/ solutions/rispacs/index.aspx). This research was based on a random telephone survey of 150 radiologists and business administrators from diagnostic imaging centers and hospital radiology departments (among hospitals with 200 and fewer beds). As a result, we state our second hypothesis as follows:

Hypothesis 2 (The Diagnostic Flow Time Imaging Service Hypothesis): RIS/PACS will improve the flow time of the diagnostic imaging service provider by reducing both the diagnostic examination time and the total service time. This reduction is a result of shorter lead times in phases and of alleviation of the traditional process bottlenecks.

Apart from the system itself, a major cost of RIS/PACS implementation is that of network and digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM<sup>SM</sup>) connectivity.<sup>1</sup> In fact, software integration is becoming a critical component, as hardware accounts for less than 30 percent of the cost of PACS [26]. Hubs, or a shared network environment, add another layer to the installation. This entails creating a virtual local area network (VLAN) for the dedicated PACS network. A VLAN adds to the cost of the project, based on its size, the number of switches, and the geographic area that must be covered. Other factors that come into play include cabling and the network backbone infrastructure [15].

Another cost is that of training. A successful PACS deployment depends on human factors in addition to technical and practical issues. According to Michael Cloutier, Systems Analyst RIS/PACS for Catholic Medical Center in Manchester, New Hampshire, “Most obstacles are not physical or technical, they are mental” [17, p. 43]. However, the Web interface is usually very intuitive, and users can learn the system in less than an hour. PACS/RIS training is thus usually divided into two sessions. In the first, clinicians are introduced to the basics: window/level tools and accessing the next image. After a few weeks, a trainer introduces the rest of the PACS toolbox.

## Intangible Benefits and Costs of RIS/PACS

In general, intangible, or qualitative, benefits and costs are hard to recognize and identify, and are also hard to measure in terms of cost reduction. The first intangible benefit of RIS/PACS implementation is increased satisfaction on the part of patients and referring physicians. Indeed, technology is a tool that comes in many forms and usually helps improve efficiency and effectiveness and, as a direct result, satisfaction from medical service. A Rhode Island hospital, for example, saw patient satisfaction increase from 30 percent to 90 percent since its emergency room (ER) redesign and PACS implementation [17]. However, technology alone does not improve the efficiency and effectiveness of patient care. Any successful implementation of technology requires a complete understanding of its capabilities and limitations as well as the processes it replaces and connects [30]. Consequently, we were also curious to examine whether referring physicians that are familiar with new technology will be more satisfied by the ex post radiology service. We specifically outline two hypotheses on customer satisfaction:

Hypothesis 3 (The Customer Satisfaction Improvement Hypothesis): RIS/PACS improves total customer (referring physician) satisfaction with regard to (a) service duration, (b) availability of studies, and (c) service work flow.

Hypothesis 4 (The Department Communication Use Hypothesis): Customer satisfaction is higher among customers who actively use the RIS/PACS technology to communicate with the department.

In addition, radiology staff satisfaction is an important measure when new technology is introduced [27]. In this regard, we refer to the entire department’s professionals involved in the service—physicians, nurses, technologists, and administrative personnel. Thus, we frame our last hypothesis as follows:

Hypothesis 5 (The Staff Satisfaction Hypothesis): RIS/PACS improves total staff satisfaction with regard to (a) service duration, (b) availability of studies, and (c) service work flow.

Examinations are associated with the appropriate referring physicians based on information received from the RIS. Combining the PACS with the RIS/HIS reduces data entry time, decreases the error rate, and provides a single source for access to patient and examination data. One study investigated such an interface in a Department of Defense hospital and found that data mapping during installation saves significant amounts of time, improves productivity, and finally increases user acceptance, as is suggested by the technology acceptance model (TAM) [2, 13, 14]. In addition, the PACS and RIS/HIS interface reduces the amount of manual data entry into multiple automated systems.

Improved interaction among departments is also a benefit, as PACS can bring substantial benefits to many departments of the health-care facility (e.g., ER, ICU, operations, etc.), enhancing clinical care and TAT. Better cooperation and interaction between departments, however, is not necessarily a given. Hospitals that pay close attention to the needs of the departments before, during, and after implementation are more likely to realize better results. Finally, the costs of cooperation among departments have to be taken into account. Increased cooperation usually entails the costs of collaborative effort and requires an analysis of a department’s work flows and image viewing needs to devise an optimal solution. Components to consider include the types of workstations and monitors, viewing locations and conditions, training, and tools to facilitate communication between radiologists and clinicians.

## Context and Data

To test our hypotheses, we mea sured process times and revenues and conducted satisfaction surveys of the staff and customers of the viaHealth/Rochester General Hospital, a medical center in Rochester, New York, during the years 2004–6. Prior to the RIS/PACS implementation at the hospital—in August 2004—we looked at 150,000 studies in terms of charges and revenues collected, and for a sample we identified report TAT and process bottlenecks. We repeated these inquiries after the RIS/PACS implementation. In addition, we used a survey instrument to measure the changes in the satisfaction levels of radiologists, technology staff, and referring physicians [1].

## Revenues Generation Rate

We hypothesized that integrated RIS/PACS improves the billing system of a service provider and thus results in faster billing. We first examined whether the department’s total revenues increased after implementation. Figure 1 clearly indicates that the upward trend in the average departmental revenues rates per procedure have become far more pronounced following the August 2004 implementation. Table 2 shows that this trend shift has been statistically significant at the 0.01 level. The table provides patterns for the main five modalities of the department—(1) diagnostic (regular X-ray, computed radiology, and digital radiography), (2) computed tomography (CT), (3) invasive radiology, (4) nuclear medicine, and (5) ultrasound—as well as totals for the whole department. Our results clearly show that an increase in the revenue per procedure followed the RIS/PACS implementation for all procedures, with the exception of the CT modality. Moreover, the growth rate of revenue per procedure in the department grew more than 70 percent, from \$1.75 per procedure per month to \$3.

Before the implementation of RIS/PACS, as many as 10 percent of the studies— documented on paper—were lost. In addition, studies not fully documented were often rejected and declined by the insurance companies. All of this changed after the RIS/ PACS implementation. We did, however, capture some rise in the total revenues before implementation, which was mainly driven by a slight increase in the billing rates.

## Turnaround Times

Since process changes tend to extend the implementation time frame, the opportunity exists for additional information gains to yield ongoing process change and performance improvement [32]. One of those dimensions of process improvement is TAT. Thus, we measured the time required for the generic process conducted in the radiology department of the hospital. Our benchmark was similar data collected in June 2004, before the RIS/PACS implementation at the hospital, by a team of researchers from Kodak. Their data consisted of 400 representative TAT measurements.

After RIS/PACS was implemented, in August 2004, we retrieved data on each procedure directly from the RIS. We tracked the performance during the August 2004–October 2006 period. We collected data on the duration of the four main phases of a generic service: (1) Exam done, (2) Dictation, (3) Transcription, and (4) Sign-1 (residents) or Sign-2 (attending physicians) for final approval. Figure 2 shows the results. We see that although the number of outpatient procedures did not rise—a possible driver for speeding up the process—TAT improvements were significant despite a slight increase in the number of procedures.

Results confirm our hypotheses regarding reductions in diagnostic examination times. Within a year, the time required for approval of a transcribed report went down from about 100 hours to less than 20. Moreover, improvement was detected in all phases of the process. The most impressive advance was achieved in the transcription phase, where cycle time was reduced from about 60 hours prior to RIS/PACS implementation to about four hours immediately after implementation, and further to less than an hour within a year. Improvement was not always immediate, however. Our results suggest that the time required for dictation increased from 8 hours to 25 immediately after implementation.

## Customer Satisfaction

To test our two hypotheses on the effect on customer satisfaction, we conducted a survey. Preimplementation data were collected at the hospital in June 2004 by a research team from Kodak. They used a satisfaction questionnaire distributed among customers (referring physicians). With a response rate of approximately 40 percent, the survey (see the Appendix) yielded data from 150 referring physicians. Then, in August 2004, the RIS/PACS system was implemented in the department. We waited for implementation to be completed at the referring physicians’ sites to conduct a second survey. We anticipated that we would witness a dramatic change in their satisfaction with the diagnostic imaging service. We likewise expected notable improvements in staff satisfaction within the radiology department. The ex post survey was conducted in October 2006, with responses from more than 70 referring physicians and 65 members of the staff of the radiology department. See the Appendix.

## Model Form

We begin by calculating the NPV of investing in a new RIS/PACS. In our empirical model, presented above, we observed a statistically significant learning rate of 63 percent during the first 10 months. This enabled the department to serve the increased volume without adding staff or putting forth more effort. We assume full-feature implementation and use the following operational data based on our observations: the cost of the system, paid when installed, is \$2.5 million (approximate “typical” RIS/PACS price); the annual cost of system maintenance is 15 percent of the cost of the system; the annual cost of IT support is \$70,000; the annual volume prior to RIS/PACS implementation is 10,000 × 12 months = 120,000 studies; the revenues per procedure prior to and after implementation are \$300 and \$350, respectively; and there is a linear and steady 10 percent increase in volume during the first year after implementation.

In addition, we make assumptions about film usage and costs at a typical independent imaging center client with MRI, CT, ultrasound, and radiography. To simplify the model, we exclude tangible costs for film handling, storage, couriers, and investments in computed radiology and DICOM connectivity. The \$1.25 per sheet figure represents a weighted average cost, as film prices can range from approximately \$0.89 per sheet to \$1.90 per sheet of 14-inch × 17-inch film. Thus, the cost of film per procedure, assuming that an average procedure uses 4 sheets, is $4 \times \$ 1.25=\$ \$ 5$ . Other inputs are as follows: the cost of archiving film per procedure is \$2; the percentage of film procedures after RIS/PACS is 40 percent; the planning horizon is 7 years, which is typical in the information system industry; and the risk-adjusted cost of capital is 8 percent. ## Sensitivity Analysis

Figure 7 presents a sensitivity analysis of the NPV with respect to changes in the riskadjusted annual cost of capital. The analysis specifically examines four dimensions of the model: (1) system cost, (2) annual studies, (3) increase in volume, and (4) planning horizon. The NPV curves follow the system price and change by double the amount of such price changes. The NPV model is, however, very sensitive to increases in volume. This suggests that if the radiology service provider is capable of increasing its volume (e.g., by reducing TAT), it could dramatically increase its revenues (by nearly \$5 million per 1 percent increase). That said, when there are relatively few studies annually (less than 80,000), the NPV is low and could be as low as \$20 million, suggesting that a more cautious approach to installation may be appropriate for such practices. Finally, we see that any reasonable planning horizon yields a positive and very high NPV for the project.

## Discussion

We present a la rge-sca le longitudina l a na lysis that quantifies the business value of integrating enterprise information systems in a service organization. Current literature discusses several IT implementations in the typical manufacturing organization (e.g., [10]). However, our work is among the first to study such an implementation at a service organization. While manufacturing organizations produce physical outputs, we deal with a health-care services organization that instead produces “health.” The nature of this output raises some complex, and novel, measurement issues with regard to the dependent variables. In this context, intangibles, such as staff and patient satisfaction, are as important as tangibles, such as TAT or the financial effects on the service provider—all of which we have analyzed above.

The evidence is clear that the RIS/PACS implementation at viaHealth/Rochester General Hospital resulted in a significant revenue enhancement. This completely unexpected revenue increase ranged between 20 percent and 40 percent, depending on the radiology modality in question. We found a steady revenue increase at a rate of \$3 per procedure per month, driven chiefly by improvements in billing. The billing process became more reliable, as all preps, procedures, and exams were captured, and the rate of lost (or unbilled) studies decreased from about 10 percent to less than 1 percent. Other important improvements were an 80 percent reduction in the TAT for final reports and a statistically significant increase in the satisfaction level from the service, from around three to about six on the classic seven-point Likert scale. It is noteworthy that the major benefits did not occur overnight, but rather over a period of 12 months. This gradual realization of the benefits of the new work flows (at an impressive annual rate of 63 percent) was facilitated by the new systems integration effort.

Our major contribution to the literature revolves around the following four critical findings. First, unlike studies that track a single performance metric, we find that implementing IT in a service organization can produce multidimensional benefits in (1) operations—reduced TAT, (2) marketing—higher satisfaction levels among the referring physicians, and (3) financial performance—bringing about significantly higher revenue generation rates. Second, our research shows that IT installation can serve as an efficient catalyst for organizational “learning by doing” over an extended period of time. Third, we have shown that the level of customers’ satisfaction can be highly correlated with their level of IT usage. Physicians who opted to receive medical reports (and images) over the Web showed a significantly higher level of satisfaction than those who remained loyal to hard-copy films and fax reporting. Finally, despite the billions of dollars spent on IT investments, there is still a very strong ongoing debate as to the essence of its economic or competitive value [7, 9]. What drives the debate is the fact that neither the vendors nor the buyers invest the requisite effort in carefully comparing ex ante and ex post performance for critical IT implementations [1]. Our research is one of the first intended to close this knowledge gap empirically.

## Limitations

The main methodological limitation of our work is that our extensive longitudinal data set is based on a single hospital. Other applications of the same set of technologies might have yielded somewhat dissimilar results. Although one should be careful in generalizing our findings to any context beyond the implementation of these specific technologies at viaHealth/Rochester General Hospital, we believe that our general observations are going to hold qualitatively across the board because of the comprehensive nature of our data collection effort and the inherent similarity of the clinical practice pattern among leading hospitals.

Implementation of RIS/PACS results in significant cycle-time improvements over the long term throughout the imaging process, and immediate results in the transcription phase, offset, in the short term, by increased time requirements while staff learn the new software. These trends drive down costs and enhance revenues under most conditions, although cycle-time reductions may not be sufficient to enable the department to attain industry performance targets. Under such circumstances, however, the abundance of information and reporting options within the integrated system make possible financial analysis that can highlight profitable segments of the practice. Practice analysis tools could then uncover new ways to maximize profits by indicating which insurance programs and referring physicians are most profitable.

These limitations are fairly common to almost all studies in the area of management information systems, where the sample sizes tend to be limited by the economics of data collection. Indeed, seeking empirical regularities without a strong intention to develop explanatory theory is a limitation in an empirical case study such as ours [4]. However, the possibilities presented in this section could be studied using more detailed financial information at the payer level and in comparing other applications of enterprise information systems in leading service organizations. We believe that our research will enable other researchers to make more headway on the development of theory that will explain why heterogeneous return to investments in health care are likely to be observed.

## Implications

Several interesting managerial and theoretical implications emerge from our findings. First, good integration of the work flow and the information system forms the essential basis for any operational improvement on the part of a health-care provider. Preexisting bottlenecks thus can be eliminated, as the work is made easier or is shifted to less congested locations on the critical path. In our study, for example, transcription showed a swift, substantial improvement, perhaps in part because the transcriptionists were already familiar with information processing and thus could leverage the information flows of RIS/PACS more quickly. This reinforces the point made by many that organizations must continuously invest in new IT infrastructure to improve operational and business results, thus keeping up with growing demand and competition (e.g., [27]).

Another important implication of the study is the potential for postimplementation improvements, such as an enhanced interface between the radiology department and other hospital departments and referring physicians. The use of information systems capabilities should lead to developing complementary procedures in all departments, which could elevate the service of the entire health-care facility. A practical managerial takeaway for radiology, and more generally for IT implementations elsewhere, is that a customer’s level of satisfaction from a service is dramatically improved if he or she actually uses the new technology. We believe that investing in such customer training has great potential for successful deployment of new systems in radiology and beyond.
