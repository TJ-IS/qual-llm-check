# Gratifications and social network services usage: The mediating role of online experience

## ABSTRACT

This paper incorporates dual theories from communication research (uses and gratifications) and psychology research (online flow) to examine consumer behavior in using social network services. Particularly, the study proposes that consumer online experience of interaction and arousal serves as the mediator between social motivations and use behaviors. The empirical results indicate that arousal fully mediates the relationship between social gratifications and partial mediators between social gratifications and the intention to revisit social networking websites.

Keywords: social network services; uses and gratifications; flow theory

#

## 1. Introduction

A social network service (SNS) is an online service that aims to build social relations among people who share interests and activities. Web-based SNS allows users to interact with others over the Internet via e-mail, instant messaging, digital audio, etc. Popular SNS websites include Facebook, MySpace, Twitter, Friendster, Wretch and many others. Estimates suggest that Facebook, for example, now has more than one billion users worldwide (Facebook, 2014). While these websites vary in their specific features, each SNS website essentially consists of a brief description of the user, that is, profile, the individual’s social links, and various accompanying services. SNS websites not only connect people who share interests and activities across geographic borders, but also turn into a social commerce platform for businesses in recent years (Huang & Benyoucef, 2013; Phang, Zhang, & Sutanto, 2013).

This emergent phenomenon has drawn research attention to examine SNS user’s behavior, generating fruitful research results in communication (Lee & Jang, 2013), information management (Xu, Ryan, Prybutok, & Wen, 2012), cultural studies (Lim & Palacios-Marques, 2011), etc. Particularly, understanding a user’s motivation is essential to explain the user’s various behaviors when participating in online communities (Shen & Khalifa, 2008). One frequently endorsed approach to explore those motivational factors for SNS users is based on the uses and gratifications (U&G) theory (Quan-Haase & Young,

2010). As such, scholars commonly agree that fulfilling user’s social needs is crucial for SNS adoption, and presume its direct effect. Nonetheless, there is still much to learn about social gratification’s influence on SNS user’s experiential states and behaviors (Bryant & Oliver, 2009). Two research gaps are notable. First, research addressing important issues such as why SNS users continue to use the service and whether they misuse the service remains scarce (Kim, Gupta, & Koh, 2011; La Barbera, La Paglia, & Valsavoia, 2009; Turel & Serenko, 2012). This issue merits research attention for several reasons. For example, Facebook sees a continuous drop in active usage between 2011 and 2013. Specifically, less than 45% of the Facebook subscribers are active users in 2012, down from 50% in 2011 (Facebook, 2014). Many SNS sites (e.g., MySpace) also find decline in active usage (GlobalWebIndex, 2014). Namely, although the base of SNS subscribers is constantly expanding, the number of active users does not increase accordingly, highlighting the urgency for SNS providers to promote user’s continuous participation. In contrast, active SNS users are spending more time on the SNS website, suggestive of a rise in potential online social network addiction which causes consequential mental health problems (Karaiskos, Tzavellas, Balta, & Paparrigopoulos, 2010; Koc & Gulyagci, 2013; Kuss & Griffiths, 2011).

Second, research is needed to examine the mediating effects of SNS user’s online experiential states on the relationship between social gratifications and the behaviors noted above. It seems ambitious to attribute SNS user’s behaviors such as revisit and misuse directly to sociality in that existing SNS research reveals inconsistent findings, signifying possible mediators in the relationship between sociality and SNS use behaviors (Kim, Kim, & Nam, 2010). When the mediators affect these behaviors significantly, the significant relationship between sociality and the behaviors may weaken to the extent that it becomes insignificant. Furthermore, research indicates that fulfilling Internet user’s need is a distal necessary cause of behaviors; however, user’s experiential state is a catalyst for the developmental process of behaviors such as problematic Internet use (Davis, 2001).

Aiming to close the above two gaps in SNS research, the study posits that fulfilling SNS deeply engrossed in the process of using the service. Namely, the degree to which SNS users may revisit or misuse the service depends largely on how they experience the process when engaging in the online social activity. The study adopts the flow theory to examine the potential mediators in the present context for two reasons. First, the behavioral outcomes considered in the study (i.e., revisit intention and problematic use) relate strongly to the hedonic or experiential aspect of SNS by their very nature (Lin & Lu, 2011), qualifying the flow theory for investigating the mediators. Second, this postulation concurs with flow researchers’ suggestion that consumer’s online experience affects and facilitates the use of online services (Novak, Hoffman, & Yung, 2000; Smith & Sivakumar, 2004), and U&G researchers’ speculation that there exists immediate theoretical correspondence between online flow and the U&G gratification constructs (Stafford, Stafford, & Schkade, 2004).

Collectively, Fig. 1 demonstrates the conceptual framework, proposing that particular online flow elements serve as the mediators between social motivations to use SNS and two behavioral outcomes: revisit intention and problematic use. The contributions of this study are two-fold. SNS providers can benefit from manipulating these online flow elements to achieve various users’ behavioral outcomes. SNS users can benefit from the results by being more conscious about those cognitive and affective factors shaping their use behaviors.

## 2. Theory development

## 2.1. Uses and gratifications (U&G)

The U&G theory posits that users seek gratifications in media and technology use based on their individual needs or motivations; if these needs are gratified, individuals are likely to repeat the experience (Okazaki, 2006). The U&G theory has helped researchers to understand user’s various needs in media adoption (Stafford, Stafford, & Schkade, 2004). Particularly, recent gratifications research extends the scope to explore those psychological motivations leading individuals to accept SNS, considering the use of SNS as an emerging social media. For example, Krisanic (2008) finds that entertainment and connection represent two pivotal motivations in Facebook use. Raacke and Bonds-Raacke (2008) reveal that the main reasons for using Facebook and MySpace are to meet friends and to seek information. Likewise, Brandtzæg and Heim (2009) propose four primary motives for using popular SNSs such as Underskog, Nettby, HamarUngdom, and Biip in Norway: information, entertainment, social interaction, and personal identity. Furthermore, Kim, Sohn, and Choi (2011) posit that the major motives for using social network sites are seeking friends, social support, entertainment, information, and convenience. Ku, Chu, and Tseng (2013) discover five motives for using Facebook and MySpace: amusement, maintaining relationship, seeking information, sociality, and style. While gratifications research reveals that SNS user’s motivations are not limited to social factors, and should encompass other intrinsic and extrinsic related motives (Stafford, Stafford, & Schkade, 2004), researchers commonly agree that fulfilling user’s social needs (e.g., seeking friends, social interaction, enhancement, presence, support, etc.) is fundamental to SNS adoption (Foster, Francescucci, & West, 2010). The study thus builds on this pivotal factor in relating its antecedental role to SNS user’s revisit and problematic behaviors. Specifically, social needs in the present context range from developing to maintaining various social relationships with other SNS users.

## 2.2. Mediating conditions

As aforementioned, understanding an individual’s psychological motivations is essential to explain his/her attitude and behavior (Choi, Kim, & McMillan 2009; Kim, Sohn, & Choi 2011). The use of media such as SNS, however, is an intricate process that should merit greater research attention to its mediating conditions (Bryant & Oliver, 2009; Lu & Hsiao, 2010). Consequently, a shortcoming of using the U&G theory alone to explain SNS user’s behaviors is that this theory has been primarily used to explain user’s motives, which is likely to produce incomplete causal models and generally ignore the effect of third variables, such as mediators. Among the few SNS user studies probing into mediators, Lin and Lu (2011) consider the mediating role of perceived benefit based on the motivation theory (i.e., usefulness as the extrinsic benefit and enjoyment as the intrinsic benefit). Although the above theory has merits in explaining SNS user’s continued use, this approach is deficient in describing SNS user’s overuse or misuse, which researchers aver to be strongly correlated with user’s experiential state when using an online service (Bridges & Florsheim, 2008). As a growing number of researchers argue that SNS emerges as a pleasure-oriented information system that individuals become more willing to use as more friends or peers join (Baker & White, 2010; Li & Bernoff, 2008; Powell, 2009; Sledgianowski & Kulviwat, 2009; Tapscott, 2008), it is rational to assume that whether and to what extent, fulfilling SNS users’ social motives leads to revisit and problematic behaviors is largely determined by how they experience the process of using SNS (i.e., experiential state). Hence, the study expects that SNS user’s psychological experience (or online experiential state) plays a facilitating role in shaping social gratifications’ influence on the above behaviors.

## 2.3. Online flow

Researchers characterize flow as being facilitated by an order in conscientiousness that causes individuals to enter a specific experiential state so desirable that they wish to repeat it as often as possible (Csikszentmihalyi & Csikszentmihalyi, 1988). Early flow research links flow with various types of leisure activities, such as gaming, dancing, and playing chess, where an individual becomes so engrossed with the activity as to create a pleasurable experience (e.g., Barnett, 2005; Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). The emergence of Internet-based activities, however, leads to the conceptualization of online flow, namely, a cognitive state

#

experienced during navigation, which is characterized by a seamless sequence of responses facilitated by machine interactivity, intrinsically enjoyable, accompanied by a loss of self-conscientiousness, and self-reinforcing (Novak, Hoffman, & Yung, 2000). Underlying what makes for a compelling online experience (e.g., Hsu & Lu, 2004; Jung, Perez-Mira, & Wiley-Patton, 2009), online flow opportunities build on the following elements: skill, control, interaction, importance, challenge, arousal, time distortion, and telepresence (Novak, Hoffman, & Yung, 2000). Furthermore, the importance of these flow elements is context dependent and varies across activities. As such, control and importance correlates more with utilitarian activities (e.g., work, information search) whereas skill, challenge, time distortion, and telepresence associate mainly with hedonic activities (e.g., online gaming, watching TV). Interaction and arousal, however, relate to activities with both utilitarian and hedonic nature such as chat and news groups. Considering the dual nature SNSs possess, the study thus concentrates on these two online flow elements (i.e., interaction and arousal) in exploring their association with user’s social gratifications and behaviors.

## 2.3.1. Social gratifications and interaction

Interaction refers to an artifact’s (e.g., Internet human-computer interface) interactive behavior as experienced by the users. Specifically, interaction may refer to the mapping of interaction (i.e., how natural and intuitive the interaction is perceived to be by the participant), the range of interaction (i.e., the number of possibilities for action at a particular point of time), the personalization of interaction (i.e., the process of tailoring content to individual users’ characteristics or preferences), or the responsiveness of interaction (i.e., the quality of being responsive, reacting quickly, involving responding with emotion to people and events) (McMillan, 2000; Wu, 2005; Yoo, 2011). As noted above, SNS users produce and share with others their own content for self-expression and self-actualization over the Internet. SNS users also expect other’s prompt feedback, thus obtaining mutual benefit or social rewards, such as reputation, expected relationships, and trust. Hence, SNS interaction manifests itself mainly in personalization of the content and service, and responsiveness from such, interaction allows SNS users to actively participate in the process through the content as well as continuous and immediate feedback from others, fulfilling both utilitarian and hedonic purpose.

SNSs offer users an effective way to communicate with other people. Through the process of communication, SNS users engage in a variety of further social networking activities (Carpenter, Green, & LaFlamm, 2011). This peculiar feature makes SNS quite socially relevant, turning into the locus for much social interaction. Furthermore, each social relationship has a focus, or a shared circumstance around which interaction takes place (Oh, Labianca, & Chung, 2006). Failing to catch on what takes place in the online world probably implies missing out on a huge portion of what is happening in one’s peer group. Thus, SNS users adopting the service to meet people with same interests anticipate useful things to be shared in the SNS website. Likewise, SNS users who use the service to seek relationships or romance expect to receive prompt response from others or even engage in live interaction with them. Accordingly, SNS users who are motivated by social needs (i.e., gratified by the social benefits of SNS) recognize more the value of online interaction with other SNS users (Park, Kee, & Valenzuela, 2009).

H1: SNS user’s social gratifications relate positively to interaction.

## 2.3.2. Social gratifications and arousal

Arousal, on the other hand, is a physiological and psychological state of being awake or reactive to stimuli. These stimuli arise from the use of SNS as a means to fulfill various social needs. The present study emphasizes arousal’s physiological aspect and regards this online flow element as a measure of stimulation, for example, in forming a potential relationship with others, or meeting people with common interests. SNS users may become engrossed with using SNS as to create a pleasurable experience due to arousal (Park, Kee, & Valenzuela, 2009). The state of heightened physiological activity when using SNS apparently associates with its utilitarian and hedonic functions.

#

Individuals are likely to seek arousal via SNS owing to its potential to offer various social stimuli such as building relationships or sharing ideas, activities, events, and interests within their individual networks (Emmanouilides & Hammond 2000). Forming social bonds with others generally produces positive emotion (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). It is also clear from the gratifications research that media use provides an enjoyable experience (Sherry, 2004). As such, users’ affective state, such as feeling stimulated, excited, etc. when using the media reflects their motivation (Mazer, Murphy, & Simonds, 2007). Thus, SNS has the advantage of cultivating one’s social motive within his/her social group which is important for an individual to experience positive emotion or enjoyment (Li, 2011). For example, as users perceive more members with common interests joining SNS, they expand their connections, whereby desired arousal levels can be maintained through the fulfillment of this social need (Lin & Lu, 2011). Likewise, getting a sense of community helps create a greater sense of pleasure (Powell, 2009; Tapscott, 2008), which associates with increases in arousal (i.e., arousal boost) (Berlyne, 1971; Li, 2011). Particularly, individuals motivated by relationship seeking, if the need is gratified, will probably experience a heightened physiological state of arousal when using SNS. Hence, SNS users with their social needs fulfilled are likely to immerse themselves in a heightened state of arousal when engaging in the service.

H2: SNS user’s social gratifications relate positively to arousal.

## 2.4. Intention to revisit SNS websites

As aforementioned, the percentage of active SNS users does not grow as more people subscribe to the service. This trend alerts SNS marketers to opportunities of keeping users coming back to visit their websites. Flow research avers that online flow elements (e.g., interaction and arousal) make for a compelling online experience (i.e., the state of flow), which in turn generates favorable consumer behavioral outcomes such as intentions to visit the website and make purchases (Koufaris, 2002). Also, information research demonstrates that social interaction is crucial in the process of knowledge and information exchange (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). In particular, online participants who are central to a network and connected to a large number of other participants are more likely to continue to contribute to the collective activity through interaction with others (Bimber, Flanagin, & Stohl, 2005; Guillén, 2003). Furthermore, research on the theory of cognitive integration (Sethi & King, 1999) indicates that the valuation and integration of knowledge sharing elicits the continuance intention (He & Wei, 2009). As such, when SNS users share knowledge via interaction with others, they produce various informational values or integrate different knowledge to form new knowledge, leading to continuance intention (He & Wei, 2009).

Emotion research also reveals that human emotion or state of excitement influences an individual’s decision making (Zaltman, 1995). Particularly, the individual’s intense state of arousal (i.e., affective response) leads to focused attention on specific targets and may therefore impact his/her ongoing behavior (Patterson & Spreng, 1997). For example, arousal plays an important role in continued blog usage (Shiau, Huang, & Shih, 2011). Likewise, when Facebook users experience arousal, this affective response may induce them to engage in continued behaviors (Huang & Lin, 2011). Hence, the study contends that interaction and arousal relate to users’ intention to revisit SNS websites as follows.

H3: Both (a) interaction and (b) arousal relate positively to the intention to revisit SNS websites.

## 2.5. Problematic SNS use

Researchers commonly use such terms as positive affect, fun, playfulness, arousal, heightened involvement, and pleasure to describe the value obtained from compulsive Internet use (Bridges & Florsheim, 2008). Considering Internet’s hedonic value and related compulsions, there is clear potential for misuse of the Internet. Indeed, researchers develop several terms for misuse of the Internet. Some researchers regard the behavior as Internet addiction (e.g., Douglas, Mills, Niang, Stepchenkova, Byun, Ruffini, Lee, Loutfi, Lee, Atallah, & Blanton, 2008), or pathological Internet use (Bridges & Florsheim, 2008;

Morahan-Martin & Schumacher, 2000), while others refer to it as problematic Internet use (Milani, Osualdella, & Di Blasio, 2009). Despite the various terms to describe the central concept, Internet misuse generally associates with depression, anxiety, higher impulsivity levels, etc. (Douglas et al., 2008), referring mostly to overuse of the Internet. Obviously, individuals may misuse SNS as is the case with Internet. The study, however, adopts the term “problematic SNS use” which is conceptualized as (a) maladaptive preoccupation with SNS use, experienced as irresistible use for periods of time longer than intended and (b) distress or impairment resulting from the behavior. Notably, people generally abuse the applications available on the Internet (e.g., SNS), rather than the Internet itself. Furthermore, problematic SNS use represents the moderate disturbed patterns of SNS use and differentiates from those severe psychiatric disorders (Whang, Lee, & Chang, 2003; Yellowlees & Marks, 2007). Typical symptoms of problematic SNS use are failure to fulfill major role of obligations, guilt, craving, fear of missing important social information, etc. (Widyanto & McMurran, 2004). Research indicates that the state of flow serves as a precursor to compulsive behaviors (Faber, O’Guinn, & Krych, 1987), leading to potential socially negative consequences. In particular, users’ experiential states of arousal can influence Internet use, resulting in an uncontrollable desire and preoccupation with the use of Internet (Beard, 2005). Namely, there exists a connection between problematic Internet use and the perception of a high level of arousal (Morahan-Martin & Schumacher, 2000). Researchers also find such connection in online shopping (Bridges & Florsheim, 2008), and video gaming (Griffiths 1991a, 1991b; Sherry, 2004). The study thus postulates that arousal leads to problematic use of SNS.

H4: Arousal relates positively to problematic SNS use.

Although research generally finds direct effects of various types of gratifications on consumer behavioral outcomes in Web-based services (Park, Kee, & Valenzuela, 2009; Raacke & Bonds-Raacke, 2008), the above hypotheses suggest that SNS users who are and are more apt to be aroused when engaging in SNS, which in turn engender different behavioral consequences. Namely, these hypotheses suggest that online flow elements of interaction and arousal serve as a mediator in the relationship between social motivations and the intention to revisit SNS websites, whereas arousal serves as a mediator in the relationship between social motivations and problematic SNS use (Fig. 2).

H5: (a) Both interaction and arousal mediate the relationship between social gratifications and the intention to revisit SNS websites, whereas (b) arousal mediates the relationship between social gratifications and problematic SNS use.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Data collection and sample

This study employed personal interview (Phase 1) and mail (Phase 2) to collect data. The focal research topic demands a population that is fairly comfortable using SNS. Considering the prevalence of Facebook use for social networking and its explosive growth in recent years (Facebook, 2014), the authors thus chose Facebook as an exemplary SNS and targeted Facebook users as the informants in the present study. The interviews were conducted in Taiwan during annual Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) shows for two reasons. First, one of the main themes for 2012 ICT shows is social media applications and networking technologies. As such, the shows were expected to attract and gather existing SNS users. Second, the shows were held in three metropolitan areas (i.e., north, central, and southern Taiwan) over a period of three months. Collecting data from three major metropolitan areas makes the collected data representative across geographical regions. Particularly, the informants were randomly selected during the exhibitions. To reduce the effect of common method bias, the data collection process consists of two phases; this approach is among those suggested for overcoming method bias (Podsakoff, MacKenzie,

Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003). That is, participants needed to complete two questionnaires: one for social gratifications, online flow experience, and demographical information in Phase 1 and the other one for behavioral outcomes in Phase 2, which was completed about two weeks after Phase 1. Phase 1 and Phase 2 questionnaires were matched by name, resulting in 405 usable questionnaire packets and representing an overall response rate of 21%. Table 1 summarizes the respondents’ profile. In particular, the calculated F- and t-statistics for the intention to revisit SNS websites for different age $( F = 1 . 0 5 , p = . 3 5 )$ , gender $( t = - 0 . 5 7$ , p $= . 5 7 )$ , and education level $( F = 0 . 9 7 , p = . 4 1 )$ ) reveal that the impact of these demographical factors are negligible.

## 3.2. Measures

All responses were recorded either on 5-point Likert-type scales anchored by 1 (strongly disagree) and 5 (strongly agree) or recorded on 5-point semantic-differential scales, unless otherwise noted. To measure social gratifications, a five-item scale was adopted from Stafford (2008). For the constructs of interaction and arousal, both five-item measures were drawn from the items developed by Novak, Hoffman, and Yung (2000). Regarding the behavioral outcomes, the intention to revisit SNS websites was assessed using four-item

#

scales based on Hausman and Siekpe (2009). Finally, the study generated a list of items to assess problematic SNS use based on Widyanto and McMurran (2004), which measured academic, work, or personal self-control problems, excess SNS use, and mood-altering behaviors. The study used the summed responses to these symptoms to indicate each respondent’s tendency for problematic SNS use. All the items were initially prepared in English; they were translated into Chinese by independent translators, and then back translated into English to ensure accuracy and follow appropriate guidelines (Harkness, 2003). The items, if necessary, were adjusted to make their wording as precise as possible. The above measures were pretested in a pilot study with 22 participants as well as two experts in and equivalence of the final measures. All the measures are described in the Appendix whereas the measurement results are discussed in the section of analysis and results.

While the study draws the above measurement items from past studies, researchers commonly recommend exploratory factor analysis (EFA) as an initial effort for scale development. The study thus first performed EFA on all the items and then followed Kline’s (2011) two-step approach (i.e., a measurement model and a subsequent structural mode) and used AMOS (Version 7) to analyze the data. Employing the principal axis factoring analysis

Furthermore, Table 3 reveals that the correlations between constructs range from 0.08 to 0.70 and less than 0.90 (Hair et al., 2006). Indeed, the square root of AVE of each construct is larger than the construct’s correlations with other constructs, which also indicates good convergent and discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). To examine the mediating effects of the online flow elements, the study estimated models consistent with Baron and Kenny (1986) and Holmbeck (1997). Four conditions for mediation were examined. The first condition is satisfied if the independent variable (social gratifications) affects the mediators (interaction and arousal). The second condition checks if the mediators affect the dependent variables (revisit intention and problematic SNS use). Both of these conditions are met as indicated in the Hypothesized model of Table 4. The third condition is satisfied if the independent variable affects the dependent variables. Thus, the study estimated a model with only direct paths from social gratifications to revisit intention and problematic SNS use – a Direct model $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 7 5 . 6 4$ , df = 42, $p < 0 . 0 5$ $\mathrm { N F I } =$ 0.97, $\mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 8 .$ $\operatorname { I F I } = 0 . 9 8$ $\mathrm { S R M R } = 0 . 0 6 $ , and RMSEA = 0.05). Both direct paths (social gratifications → revisit intention; social gratifications → problematic SNS use) are significant $( p \ < 0 . 0 5 )$ with standardized coefficient of 0.59 and 0.19, respectively, thus satisfying the third mediating condition. Particularly, the Direct model explains 35.2% (0.8%) of the variance of revisit intention (problematic SNS use). The fourth mediating condition checks if the direct paths become insignificant (i.e., full mediation) or reduced (partial mediation) when they are included in the model (i.e., the Full model in Table 4). Indeed, the fit of the Full model $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 4 7 1 . 0 8$ , df = 182, $p < 0 . 0 5$ , NFI = 0.91, CFI = 0.94, IFI $= 0 . 9 4 .$ , SRMR = 0.06, and RMSEA = 0.06) is better than the fit of the Hypothesized model $( \chi ^ { 2 } _ { \mathrm { d i f f } } = 4 6 . 5 8 , \mathrm { d f } = 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , indicating that the online flow elements do not fully mediate all the effects of social gratifications on revisit intention and problematic SNS use. Notably, a closer inspection of these models reveals that the coefficient of the direct path (social gratifications → problematic SNS use) becomes insignificant, supporting full mediation, and the coefficient of the direct path (social gratifications → revisit intention) is diminished, supporting partial mediation. Thus, H5 is partially supported. The study removed the insignificant direct path from the Full model and estimated a Partially mediated model. The Partially mediated model $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 4 7 3 . 0 5$ , df = 183, $p < 0 . 0 5$ , NFI = 0.91, CFI = 0.94, IFI = 0.94, SRMR = 0.05, and RMSEA = 0.06) fits as well as the Full model $( \chi ^ { 2 } _ { \mathrm { d i f f } } = 1 . 9 7 ;$ , df = 1, $p >$ 0.10), and is better fitted than the Hypothesized model $( \chi ^ { 2 } \mathrm { d i f f } = 4 4 . 6 1$ , df = 1, $p < 0 . 0 1 $ ). These analyses collectively indicate that arousal fully mediates the relationship between social gratifications and problematic SNS use, whereas both interaction and arousal partially mediate the relationship between social gratifications and the intention to revisit SNS websites.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

Although many studies have affirmed that fulfilling SNS user’s social needs is a necessary antecedent to user’s adoption of the service, the study reveals that social gratifications alone is not enough to trigger user’s revisit and problematic behaviors. Furthermore, SNS user’s online experiential states serve as a key mediator between social gratifications and the above behaviors. As such, this investigation highlights the criticality of cognitive (i.e., interaction) and affective (i.e., arousal) experiences versus the more general and utilitarian-based technology acceptance factors when examining the adoption of social networking services.

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

The theoretical implications are four fold. First, the present study supports the integration of U&G and flow theories to explain SNS use, and reinforces the speculation that there appears to be immediate theoretical correspondence between online flow and the U&G gratification constructs (Stafford, Stafford, & Schkade, 2004). The empirical results indicate that SNS users build associations between their social gratifications and experiential states. Thus, how SNS users experience the usage process is just as important as gratifying their social needs; together, these factors determine consumer intention to make further visits to the SNS website and problematic use behavior. Second, this study shows that it is possible for both social gratifications and arousal to have a direct effect on problematic SNS use if both effects are examined independently of each other. Nonetheless, as the mediating effects reveal, the effect of social gratifications on problematic SNS use becomes insignificant once both the effects of social gratifications and arousal are considered simultaneously, suggesting that researchers should apply an integrative approach when explaining problematic SNS use. The study thus extends the work of, for instance, Pempek,

Yermolayeva, and Calvert (2009) by demonstrating that the impact of social gratifications on problematic SNS use is fully mediated through the presence of arousal in the online experience.

Third, the study observes both a direct and indirect (via interaction and arousal) relationship between SNS user’s social gratifications and revisit intention. As such, fulfilling SNS user’s social needs seems to be a hygiene factor when users consider whether or not to revisit a particular SNS website. Namely, gratifying SNS user’s social needs is essential to an individual’s continued participation. Should interaction and/or arousal arise during a visit to a SNS website, these online experiential states are likely to play a part in determining whether or not a revisit takes place. Even if these online experiential states do not occur, social gratifications might still affect user’s revisit intention directly.

Fourth, while the empirical results suggest partial mediating effects of interaction and arousal on user’s intention to revisit SNS websites, the model built on theoretical parsimony indeed generates favorable predictive power (hypothesized model: 49%; partially mediated model: 59%) over other models based on intention-based theories (e.g., Lin, 2007). Compared with the hypothesized model, the partially mediated model generates a 10% increase in explanatory power in the intention to revisit SNS websites, and explains similar amount of the variance (i.e., 22% versus 22%) in problematic SNS use. Furthermore, the Hypothesized model outperforms the Direct model in explanatory power by 14% (i.e., 49% versus 35%) and 21% (i.e., 22% versus 0.8%) for revisit intention and problematic SNS use, respectively, stressing the importance of the proposed mediators when predicting SNS user’s revisit intention and problematic behavior.

## 5.2. Managerial implications

Understanding the mediating role of user’s experiential states such as interaction and arousal provides SNS developers with a competitive edge in the social media business. Although manipulating SNS users’ experiential states can increase chance of revisit to the website, SNS providers should also focus on the key “flow-building/maintaining lever” that they can control, namely, fulfilling user’s social needs. As such, SNS providers can enhance several features to help gratify user’s social needs. For example, SNS developers can add a new function such as ticker box that shows real time or most recent updates by friends. Integration with other social apps can further allow users to listen, watch, or read with friends in live stream without leaving the SNS website. These efforts also lead to the experience of “serendipity”. Likewise, SNS designers may incorporate the capability to export personal news feed and fan page links, and import email or export SNS messages. Building an all-in-one solution fulfills user’s desire to monitor email and making them accessible from external interfaces. These approaches help gratify user’s social needs and thus contribute to experiencing interaction and arousal, which in turn lead to user’s revisit intention.

While this study underlines the importance of gratification-experience concurrence as a fundamental principle of SNS website management and strategy and emphasizes the pivotal role of social gratifications in driving user’s experiential states, several other ways exist to elicit SNS user’s experience of interaction and arousal. One approach for SNS providers to enhance interaction is to ameliorate service mobility considering that over 680 million active users worldwide currently access Facebook through their mobile devices such as cellular phones (Facebook, 2014). As a result, the development of “SNS phone” as well as its integration with location-based-service (LBS) become the future trend and pave the way for more prompt and live connections between users. Likewise, SNS providers may enrich their service contents to make users feel aroused, for example, by integrating with other dating or friends-making services. Specifically, SNS providers can better differentiate the users and user groups by their use behaviors and browsing records via data mining to tailor their services. This approach not only improves content usability but also engenders a sense of excitement and serendipity.

The findings also explain a mechanism through which SNS user’s problematic behavior is engendered. Understanding this mechanism provides valuable information for practitioners to understand the factors driving SNS user’s problematic use, helping prevent misuse of the service. The full mediating role of arousal indicates that fulfilling customers social needs provides a basis for experiencing arousal in SNS use, and it is the level of arousal that eventually determines problematic SNS use, implying that social gratifications are necessary, but not sufficient conditions to ensure SNS user’s problematic behavior. Namely, a SNS user who has social needs gratified might still not misuse the service if he/she does not experience arousal. As such, SNS user’s social gratification is a distal necessary cause of symptoms of problematic use. As a contributory factor, SNS user’s experience of arousal is a catalyst for the development of problematic behavior.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

While the findings help broaden the understanding of SNS use, several limitations exist. First, and perhaps the major limitation of the study is the breadth of the sample. Although the sample includes a broad swath of informants, the study focuses solely on Facebook users interviewed at ICT shows. Thus, further research is required to establish whether the patterns of effects are globally generalizable across different types of social network services and users. Second, SNS users may not be gratified solely by social benefits. Indeed, research reveals that a growing number of SNS websites serve marketing or other business purposes (Huang & Benyoucef, 2013). Exploring other types of gratifications as antecedents to SNS use is potentially contributive. Third, the results highlight interaction and arousal as partial mediators in affecting SNS user’s revisit intention. Further studies that examine other mediators are valuable. Likewise, further research may consider factors

#

such as privacy, security, etc. as moderators. Finally, the study is also limited by the concentration on the two dependent variables (i.e., intention to revisit SNS websites and problematic SNS use). In fact, the use of SNS is a complex process that has numerous effects on behavioral outcomes. Hence, further research can investigate a broader scope of consequences of SNS usage, such as advertising effects and enhanced social and/or commercial activities. Evidently, a range of research questions warrant further examination for SNS use from perspectives of changing information technology, consumer expectations, and new business models.
