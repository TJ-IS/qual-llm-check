Abstr ac t: In today’s dynamic business environment, companies are under tremendous pressure to become more innovative and maintain a steady stream of ideas that can lead to new and improved products and services. Companies have begun to explore the possibility of capturing consumers’ “collective intelligence” by establishing firmsponsored online brainstorming sites where individuals can share their ideas and offer comments on the ideas contributed by others. We term these sites “Company-Sponsored Online Co-Creation Brainstorming” (COCB). The value of this open and voluntary co-creation depends largely on members’ contribution levels, the quality of the contributions, and sustained participation. In this paper, utilizing Zwass’s taxonomy of co-creation value as a base, we structure a taxonomic framework of COCBs and an accompanying basic model of COCBs. We then present a series of hypotheses concerning the relationships between the model’s various factors and specific COCB activities. We validate the model using empirical data collected over two and a half years, starting from the initiation of a pioneering company-sponsored online brainstorming site. Our analyses demonstrate that the level of peer feedback and the responsiveness (speed) of sponsor company feedback have significant influences on both members contribution levels and duration of active participation. The sponsoring company’s feedback, however, seems to influence only the quality of member’s contribution level. On the practical side, the outcomes suggest that sponsoring companies should develop efficient processes for reviewing and responding to submitted ideas. Regarding theory, our findings provide an initial piece of contextualized research that offers implications for theory building in the COCB context, most notably the identification of key relationships between feedback (both peer and company) and participant activity levels and duration of participation.

Key wor ds and phr ases: brainstorming, co-creation, contribution quality, sustained participation.

The l ong-past stor y of Tom Sawyer , who motivated a group of his friends to work together to complete his fence whitewashing task, is alive and well in the Internet age. Tom harnessed the physical labor power of the crowd and created a situation in which everyone seemed to feel they had won. In recent years, the idea of engaging the efforts of a group has turned to mental rather than physical contributions. Engaging a group of individuals and tapping their collective intelligence for various purposes has gained the attention of both business practitioners and researchers [34, 39, 42, 50]. Procter & Gamble and IBM sought product ideas from unaffiliated individuals in wellorganized contests and jams, both of which are short-term, fixed duration activities. A number of other major corporations, including Salesforce.com (IdeaExchange), Dell (Dell IdeaStorm), Starbucks (My Starbucks Ideas), Ubuntu (IdeaStorm for Ubuntu) and Adobe (Adobe Lab Ideas), are pursuing ongoing, open, and voluntary online idea contributions portals.

In a more formal analysis, Zwass focuses on “co-creation of value by consumers” [50, p. 11] in the e-commerce marketplace, splitting co-creation into two categories, sponsored and autonomous. For the former, individuals or communities perform co-creation activities at the request of and typically for the benefit of the sponsoring organization. In the latter, the activities of individuals or communities are “conducted independently of any established organization, although they may be using platforms provided by such organizations, which benefit economically” [50, p. 11]. Although such online co-creation activities have gained increasing notice, we nevertheless find a dearth of in-depth studies on factors or facets that motivate individuals to voluntarily contribute to the idea generation process. This is reflected in Zwass’s emphasis on the need for “much contextualized research . . . to explore how various taxonomic entities interact in autonomous and sponsored co-creation” [50, p. 31].

In this research, we focus on a specific form of co-creation where the sponsoring firm seeks innovative ideas and commentary on new technologies and new or improved products. We label such co-creation activities as “Company Sponsored Online Co-Creation Brainstorming” (COCBs). As Zwass suggests, we pursue an in-depth contextualized consideration of this form of co-creation. The work reported focuses on two key issues: (1) what factors help explain the quality and level of participants idea submissions, and (2) what factors help explain the duration of a participant’s active involvement in the COCB. After outlining the relevance and importance of these issues within our modified form of Zwass’s taxonomic framework of co-creation, we construct a variety of research hypotheses that are then empirically tested using data collected from a pioneering COCB site.

Our findings first indicate that current participant idea submission rate is positively affected by the prior levels of peer feedback and by the prior responsiveness (speed) of sponsor company feedback but not by the actual prior level of sponsor feedback. Second, while the quality of a participant’s ideas is not affected significantly by prior peer feedback, it is significantly positively affected both by prior sponsoring company feedback and by how quickly the company previously provided feedback to the participant. Regarding the duration of participation in a COCB, our results indicate the importance of prior peer feedback, speed of prior company feedback (but not the actual level of prior company feedback), and a participant’s to-date level of “network connectedness.”

This is followed by presentation of our research model in the setting of sponsored co-creation. The Hypotheses Development section provides details of our hypothesis formulation. The section on Empirical Findings and Test Results details the findings of our empirical analysis. This is followed by a brief commentary of limitations of the analysis. Finally, the Summary and Conclusions section presents a discussion of the theoretical and managerial implications along with limitations of the study.

## Framework of Company-Sponsored Online Co-Creation

Web-based c o-cr eation of val ue by c onsumer s has received increasing interest in the literature. Zwass [50] provided an integrated overview on this broad research domain and proposed an inclusive taxonomy of value co-creation. In the taxonomic framework (Figure 1), Zwass classifies value co-creation into several entity components capturing the most salient aspects of co-creation. The taxonomic entities include (1) co-creators, who are agent collectives that are motivated to work together on a common task; (2) process, which prescribes policies, governance, incentives, and IT support in co-creation activities; (3) characteristics of the task that is to be accomplished, that is, the task characteristics pose requirements on co-creators and the process; and (4) co-created value received by co-creating communities or individuals.

It should be noted that the taxonomic framework by Zwass [50] does not directly provide a theory or model that one can empirically test. Nevertheless, it “lays a foundation for the future development of value co-creation theory and practice” and “will require ongoing revision and expansion” [50, p. 11]. Given our focus on company sponsored online co-creation brainstorming, we developed an appropriate modification of Zwass’ taxonomic framework. In our framework, the task is specified by a sponsoring company with objectives to extend company relationships with customers and to collaborate with customers on innovative ideas and improved products. The co‑creators are typically customers/end users of the sponsoring company products and services and are drawn from across the Internet, yielding a very broad a set of participants. The sponsoring company also structures the process and governance of the brainstorming Web site and determines the availability of support and any form of incentives for co-creators. The intended principal beneficiary of the co‑created value (measured by the co-creators contribution level, the duration, and quality of participation/contribution) is the firm sponsoring the co-creating activities of the participants.

In the value co-creation literature, a very important stream of research is the study of individual motivations for participating and contributing in co-creating activities [50]. Most of the earlier works in this arena focus on co-creation systems established for the social and/or commercial interests of individual community members (see [26]). These include sharing resources such as digital music in peer-to-peer networks [3], open source software development [23, 41], Internet-based voluntary support groups for getting answers to software problems [31], opportunities to improve skills and enhance future employment [29, 38], and transferring knowledge across participants [44]. In the above settings, extrinsic motivators (e.g., expected individual benefits from co-creating activities) play an important role in participation. In company-sponsored value co-creation, however, participants are usually end users of certain products and often do not receive direct social or economic value from their co-creation activities. In the absence of such value received by co-creating individuals, more intrinsic factors of the inner motivation of individuals have been found to be effective [9]. In addition, the sponsoring company can respond and provide updates, feedback to, and actions on co-creators’ ideas, thus making the setting different from autonomous value co-creation.

The stream of research on motivators leading individuals to participate and contribute in value co-creation is rich. Psychological theories and prior literature (e.g., [9, 22, 38]) distinguish between extrinsic motivators and intrinsic motivators, and have identified a wide array of motivation factors including the satisfaction of a participant’s own needs [14, 28], pleasure and enjoyment from participation [16], potential rewards of career enhancements [29], identification and sense of belonging within a community [9, 13, 49], and social capital accumulation [44].

Zwass [50] noted that the dichotomization of extrinsic and intrinsic motivators does not fully capture the wide spectrum of motivation and provided a summary of potential motivators in order from most altruistic to monetary. These motivators can be roughly classified into (1) individual emotion or altruistic attitude; (2) desire for social standing, recognition, and renown; (3) reciprocation in view of contributions by others; (4) social capital accumulation and peer recognition; (5) nonmonetary rewards; and (6) financial rewards. Motivators involving individual emotion or altruistic attitude such as “desire to contribute,” “passion for a task,” “enjoyment,” and “playfulness” cannot be directly observed or measured in an objective manner. In addition, most value co-creation systems (either autonomous or sponsored) do not offer direct monetary payoffs to participants because of company policies or regulatory concerns.

Based on the above discussions, we propose a research model (Figure 2) relating various motivating factors to the desired value proposition<sup>1</sup> in a company-sponsored co-creation setting. The motivating factors in our research model include individual connectedness, peer feedback, and sponsoring firm feedback that capture the social and communitarian aspects of motivations to co-creation. Besides these factors, we add an additional factor that is under the control of the sponsoring firm, the swiftness of firm feedback or “firm responsiveness,” which can serve as a nonmonetary incentive for participation in the form of professional review and feedback on an individual’s contribution.

## Hypotheses Development

## Individual Connectedness

We use the ter m “individual c onnec tedness” to refer to a participant’s position (or network connectedness<sup>2</sup>) in the COCB-based network structure. For pre-COCB settings such as group decision support systems (GDSS) brainstorming sessions, the small group structure helps facilitate direct reciprocity among participants [11, 17, 45]. In contrast, COCBs can have hundreds, even thousands, of participants—far beyond the early brainstorming work where groups of 12 or more were considered “large” (see the discussion in [37], especially p. 402). In such a setting with a large number of participants, some specific participants may receive significant attention or be more “densely connected” than other participants.

We suggest a few reasons to explain why individuals with a high level of network connectedness are more likely to contribute. First, network connectedness is intended to provide a measure of how closely an individual belongs to a community [1]. When people share a strong sense of belonging to a community, they feel motivated and likely engage in active participation in the community [48, 49]. Second, common identity theory defines people’s attachment to the group as a whole as identity-based attachment. Identification with a group is powerful in both real-world as well as online groups. When people feel identity-based attachment to a group, they tend to perceive others in the group as interchangeable [40] and hence tend to increase their evaluation of the community, their commitment to it, and their levels of participation [37].

Prior research has also suggested that participants with higher levels of connectedness tend to have developed various forms of information useful for further contributions [21] and are capable of generating new ideas that might evade other, less connected participants (see discussion related to GDSS settings in [11]). Wasko and Faraj [44] used survey data (rather than actual observations) to explore factors that motivated individuals to share their knowledge with other members (though actually strangers) of a legal association. The authors found that individual participants with higher levels of structural capital (in terms of the social network centrality) indicated that they would contribute more responses and more helpful responses. In a COCB setting, we posit that individuals who are more closely connected<sup>3</sup> with other members through comments on their ideas are more likely to develop a strong commitment to the COCB and thus contribute more ideas and more high-quality ideas than participants with lower connectedness. This leads to our first two hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1a: Individual connectedness positively influences participants’ contribution of ideas in COCB.

Hypothesis 1b: Individual connectedness positively influences participants’ contribution of high-quality ideas in COCB.

We also posit that individual connectedness is related to a participant’s duration of involvement in the COCB. Previous research has shown that members may drop out of a community when they find it too difficult either to access information or to be involved in the type of interaction they seek (see discussions in [8] and [25]). Butler [8] noted that participants in a large group tend to drop out of the community if they have not achieved the level of interaction they had expected. In a COCB setting, we posit that the more “densely” connected a participant is, the more likely he or she will be able to obtain their expected level of interactions. Based on this discussion, we posit the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: Individual connectedness positively influences participants’ duration in COCB.

## Peer Feedback

Peer feedback refers to the level of feedback provided from other participants to an individual contributor. In a COCB, participants can receive one or both of two types of feedback: peer feedback and sponsoring firm feedback. We begin by considering peer feedback. From reputation and signaling theory we draw a positive link between peer feedback and a participant’s enhanced contribution [24]. Previous studies [18, 29] argued that “reputation gains and signaling” may help explain individuals’ contribution behavior where monetary rewards are absent. Although these findings mainly surfaced in discussions of open source software (OSS) development, we suggest that this argument is applicable to the COCB scenario. First, consistent with the environment studied by Lerner and Tirole [29], COCB participants typically earn no financial or physical rewards for their contribution. In both settings, reputation gains serve as a force supporting contribution activities. OSS community members may be most interested in professional reputation that can be transferred into future financial profits. The private rewards for participants in COCB are more likely related to the chance of seeing their ideas incorporated into next-generation products.

Second, each individual participant can observe whether his or any other contribution receives peer feedback and the nature of the feedback since interactions among an individual participant and other participants are viewable by all community participants. As a result, participants with a higher level of peer feedback may gain a reputation or a level of popularity (“popularity reputation”), a factor serving to stimulate participants to contribute more ideas and more high quality ideas. Thus, we set forth the following two hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3a: Peer feedback positively influences participants’ contribution of ideas in COCB.

Hypothesis 3b: Peer feedback positively influences participants’ contribution of high quality ideas in COCB.

In group or community settings involving physical personal relationships and interactions, people who help or share knowledge with others may well expect that they will get some type of reciprocity or compensation in return, either from individuals they have helped or from the group/community as a whole (see the discussion in [12]). But in a nonphysical electronic platform such as a COCB, identification exists only in terms of a participant’s COCB ID.

Wasko and Faraj [43] suggested that if members share a common goal such as knowledge exchange, a participant may not expect reciprocity or future return from the specific individual he or she directly helped, but still anticipate a “generalized reciprocity” from the community. Since participants in a COCB are assumed to commit to a common goal (e.g., provide ideas to help the sponsoring firm to improve products or services), we suggest that the “generalized reciprocity” expectation is also likely to be present, that is, we anticipate that contributors expect (or perhaps hope for) peer feedback. Receiving peer feedback is a positive signal from the peers and thus encourages a participant to continue as a contributing participant. Thus, we expect that participants who have a higher level of peer feedback will stay longer in a COCB, a view summarized in the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4: Peer feedback positively influences participants’ duration in COCB.

## Sponsoring Firm Feedback

In a COCB, sponsoring firm feedback refers to the evaluation by the sponsoring firm of individual contributors’ ideas. Feedback from the sponsoring firm plays the role of “official reputation” builder because it is a reflection of the firm’s perception of the quality of a participant’s contribution(s). For example, sponsoring firm feedback may announce that an idea from participant A will be implemented, thus positively adding to A’s “official reputation” of submitting “good ideas.” As with peer feedback, sponsoring firm feedback can be observed by all the COCB participants, a factor that should add to the motivation effect (see discussion in [29, p. 18] arguing that competence seeking is a major driver for contribution).

Jeppesen and Frederiksen [24] used survey data to study voluntary contribution in a firm-hosted forum, finding that individuals reported that the firm feedback was one of the motivating factors for their activity in the forum. Wiertz and de Ruyter [46] noted that, in the context of firm-hosted commercial online communities, members were usually customers who might have already established a bond with the sponsoring firm. Hence, it is possible that customers contribute their knowledge to the online community’s resources not only out of concern for the good of the community itself but because they also value their bond with the host firm. Grant [19] and Lester et al. [30] argued that when employees receive feedback about the performance and end results of their tasks, they feel their self-enhancement motives are fulfilled and will likely participate again in important activities. Porter and Donthu [36] showed that the perception of a sponsoring firm’s efforts to foster member embeddedness and to encourage interaction help build favorable customer beliefs about and trust in a virtual community sponsor, which then motivates customers to share information and co-produce new products with the sponsoring firm.

Bandura [4] argued that, over time, people who receive positive feedback tend to produce more, higher-quality contributions while those who receive negative feedback will either modify their behavior or simply drop out. Moon and Sproull studied “Internet-based voluntary technical support groups” [31, p. 494] for software problems, where question askers entered their questions online and volunteers could post their “answers.” Moon and Sproull’s results generally support the impact of the presence of feedback mechanisms and of positive feedback on the duration of participation.

In the COCB setting, sponsoring firm feedback can be classified as positive, negative, or pending. Like the old adage that “any publicity is good publicity,” it is possible that any firm feedback is “good feedback” (As explained below, however, we do consider differences in impact between the positive, negative, and pending feedback.) Thus, we posit that participants who have received more feedback from the sponsoring firm should be motivated to contribute more ideas and have longer participation durations in the COCB.

Hypothesis 5a: Sponsoring firm feedback positively influences participants’ contribution of ideas in COCB.

Hypothesis 5b: Sponsoring firm feedback positively influences participants’ contribution of high-quality ideas in COCB.

Hypothesis 6: Sponsoring firm feedback positively influences participants’ duration in COCB.

## Sponsoring Firm Responsiveness

Sponsoring firm responsiveness measures how long a participant, on average, waits for feedback from the sponsoring firm. Having argued the potential importance of sponsoring firm feedback, we also note that the speed of this feedback can also be an important factor influencing participants’ future contribution activities. In different settings, prior research has suggested that timely feedback can be an important factor in group decision making [47] and in group goal setting [15]. The issue of speed of response has not typically been studied in traditional brainstorming sessions since, during the sessions, ideas are generated and collected but not evaluated until near the end of the session or after the session ends. In the long-term COCB with no end-date specified, sponsoring firm evaluation of ideas is an ongoing process. Thus, there is no clear date when participants expect to receive sponsoring firm feedback on their ideas. Longer waiting times can give a participant the perception that their contribution(s) is(are) not being seriously considered. Morrison and Bies [32] did argue that the motivation of participants was negatively related to the length of time until feedback/ evaluations were provided. Further adding to the saliency of responsiveness in the COCB setting is the fact that participants can freely choose to contribute, not contribute, or even drop out of the community at any time. Thus, we posit the relevance and importance of the following two hypotheses:

Hypothesis 7a: Sponsoring firm responsiveness positively influences participants’ contribution of ideas in COCB.

Hypothesis 7b: Sponsoring firm responsiveness positively influences participants’ contribution of high-quality ideas in COCB.

Finally, while quick responsiveness by the sponsoring firm can build a positive image for the sponsoring firm, slow responsiveness can build a negative image even if the eventual feedback is very positive. As noted in many marketing studies, it can be difficult for a firm to change or overcome customers’ initial negative impression [33]. Although the relationship of COCB participants and the sponsoring firm is not based on transactions, we anticipate that the effect of a negative impression from slow responsiveness is also difficult to overcome. Participants who get a negative initial impression of the sponsoring firm may quickly drop out of the community and never return. Even participants who stay active for some time despite observing slow responsiveness by the sponsoring firm are likely to eventually reach their “frustration saturation” level and drop out. Thus, we posit the following (and last) hypothesis:

Hypothesis 8: Sponsoring firm responsiveness positively influences participants duration in COCB.

## Research Methods

## Research Context

We use Del ’s COCB, Del IdeaStor m, as the research context for our empirical analysis. Formally initiated on February16, 2007, Dell IdeaStorm was one of the first applications of an online COCB and has continued (according to Dell) with large numbers of participants sharing their ideas. Figure 4 provides a screenshot of Dell IdeaStorm’s homepage. The platform of Dell IdeaStorm was developed from Salesforce.com and brings the concept of “collective intelligences” to businesses by allowing COCB participants to submit, discuss, and promote and demote ideas, in this case, related to Dell’s current and future products and services.

Dell IdeaStorm is an open platform where, at no cost, any individual can register and join, receiving a unique COCB ID. After registration, IdeaStorm members can contribute in several ways with Dell reserving the right to monitor contributions and remove offensive material. First, participants are able to submit ideas, each of which generates a new individual Web page for that idea. Second, another COCB participant can submit comments on any previously submitted idea. All comments on an idea appear on that idea’s page on the IdeaStorm site.<sup>4</sup>

Dell responds and provides feedback, updates, and actions to ideas posted on Idea-Storm. The IdeaStorm’s homepage lists a set of status tags that Dell utilizes to characterize Dell’s intentions regarding an idea submitted to the site. Dell’s potential status tags for a submitted idea include the following: implemented, partially implemented, coming soon, not now, not likely, already offered, in progress, and under review (see the listing and definitions in the Appendix). Participation is voluntary and Dell mentions clearly that it provides no material rewards if a member’s idea is implemented by Dell or another company.

Dell IdeaStorm also provides information on how individuals (user ID’s) vote to promote or demote each specific idea. Dell does restrict this information to a listing of the user ID of the last 100 promoters and demoters (or a continuing list if the total number is less than 100 promoters or demoters) in addition to showing the current total points for each idea.

## Data

To test our hypotheses, we collected data from Dell IdeaStorm using an automated agent and parsing of the HTML pages for all the ideas submitted from the system’s initiation on February 16, 2007, through August 13, 2009. This yielded a total of 11,894 ideas from 6,142 different individuals (individual participants’ IDs) submitted to Dell IdeaStorm. For each idea, data collection included the ID of the submitter, the time the idea was submitted, the content (textual) of the idea, the number of comments submitted on the idea, the number of commenters, and dated status tag (if any) assigned to the idea by Dell. We note that over the observation period, Dell provided feedback on just 5.6 percent of all ideas submitted. We have no way to determine why Dell did this. Similarly, we can observe only what individuals do on Dell IdeaStorm. In this paper, we seek to analyze the individual behavior patterns (in terms of contribution quantity, quality, and duration of participation) in subsequent periods after individuals received or failed to receive feedback in previous periods on earlier submitted ideas (please see section “Empirical Findings and Test Results” for the analysis).

The next subsection sets out the operational definitions for the variables employed in our detailed formal empirical analysis.

## Operational Definitions for Concepts Used in Hypotheses Testing

In order to test the hypotheses relating to elements of a general theory of COCBs, we require operational definitions for several abstract terms used in the statement of hypotheses. The following provide these definitions along with explanatory commentary.

## Sponsoring Company Feedback

As the COCB’s sponsoring firm, Dell provides no monetary awards that might indicate Dell’s evaluation of an idea. The feedback that Dell does provide comes in the form of a set of possible status tags given to an idea (see Appendix Table A1 for detailed definition of each status tag). We operationalize COCB sponsoring company feedback as a binary variable, that is, whether or not an idea receives a status tag.<sup>5</sup> We operationalized “higher-quality evaluation” as determined by an idea receiving a positive status tag (implemented, partially implemented, in-progress, coming soon) and “lowerquality evaluation” as determined by an idea receiving a negative status tag (not now, not likely, already offered) (see Appendix Table A1). Ideas that have received only an “under review” or a “reviewed” tag are considered as receiving “pending” feedback. Ideas that receive no status tag over their duration are considered as receiving “no” sponsoring firm feedback. Interestingly, however, in our empirical analysis, the positive firm feedback or negative firm feedback variables were not significant, but the overall feedback (a function of the number of tags received) did indicate significance in certain analyses. Thus, for brevity, we report the results for overall firm feedback (function of number of tags) and not for positive or negative firm feedback separately. We compute the sponsoring company feedback for individual i, $G _ { _ i }$ , by taking the average number of status tags per idea for that individual.

## Peer Feedback

Peer feedback is a measure based on comments an individual contributor receives. To be more precise, we define peer feedback, $M _ { i ^ { \prime } }$ , for individual i as the number of comments made by other COCB participants per idea for that individual. Self-comments are excluded from our data set.

## COCB Member’s Active Duration

For COCB member i, the active duration, $L _ { \ i } ,$ in the COCB is the number of days from his or her first active participation (either submitting an idea or making a comment), $b _ { _ i }$ , to the “end date,” $e _ { i }$ . In our analysis, we operationalize the “end date” as follows. First, we use the last eight weeks of our data collection (from June 18, 2009, to August 13, 2009) to determine whether a member is data censored or not. If a participant is active during this period of time (either submitting an idea or making a comment), we define this participant as a “data censored” participant. (By data censored, we simply mean that the participant had not dropped out before the end of our data collection period.) Otherwise, we define the participant as a “dropping out” participant. For those participants who drop out, their active duration is the interval between their last activity and their first activity. For those participants who are data censored, their active duration is the interval between the end date of our data collection (August 13, 2009) and the date of their first activity.

## Individual Connectedness

We use a two-mode network to model a COCB, with ideas as one mode (level) of nodes and commenters as the second mode (level) of nodes. This is a common way to model a network structure when both individuals and events are of interest (see [6, 7]). In this two-mode network setting, we measure individual connectedness using the average normalized degree connectedness, NDC , for individual i across all ideas submitted by the individual:

$$
N D C _ {i} = \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n _ {i}} I D C _ {j}\right) / n _ {i},
$$

where $I D C _ { j } = D _ { j } / D _ { j , m a x } ( D _ { j }$ is the number of commenters linked to idea j and $D _ { j , m a x }$ is the maximum number of possible commenters that could have been linked to idea j), and $n _ { _ i }$ refers to the number of ideas submitted by individual $i . ^ { 6 }$

This parallels the concept of the network embeddedness of members (actors) in a knowledge network [44], the network embeddedness of developers (actors) and projects (events) in an open source network [21], and the centralization of a knowledge exchange network [35]. Since our network is of a somewhat more special form, we go beyond the generally agreed upon measures of connectedness (usually termed “centrality” in standard networks) that are constructed as ratios between the observed links (or paths) and the total number of links (or paths) possible. In particular, our network has the following features: (1) the COCB network representation is as a two-mode network (see [21]), and (2) the COCB network is dynamic with individuals entering and dropping out of the network over its duration, resulting in both the set of ideas and the set of commenters being dynamic rather than fixed over time. In other words, for a link to be possible, both the idea and the potential commenter (any COCB member) must be present in the network for at least some overlapping time period.

Figure 7 illustrates the six possible “time” relationships between an idea and a COCB member. The cells with light gray shading represent the time from the period starting when an idea is submitted until the day the idea either receives a tag or the data collection period ends. We end the period if a positive or negative tag is given in order to control for any subsequent effect from the sponsoring firms’ evaluation of the idea. Cells with dark gray shading represent the period from the day when a member first makes a comment or submits an idea until the member either drops out or data collection ends. Consider Case 1. The idea was submitted at the beginning of the period and received a positive or negative tag from the firm at approximately the 75th percentile of the period. The member referenced first participated in the COCB at approximately the 25th percentile of the time period and remained until data collection ended. Thus, for about 50 percent of the period, the idea was available for comment by this member. Cases 2, 3, and 4 indicate differing overlaps, while Cases 5 and 6 indicate no overlap at all. Thus, when considering what percentage of members commented on a specific idea, we exclude cases such as 5 and 6 since there was no opportunity (overlap) for interaction over the time period. Thus, when calculating the normalized network connectedness for each idea node, our measure of the maximum possible links $D _ { j , m a x }$ should include only those individuals whose presence in the network coincides for some time interval with the presence of the idea. For ideas with positive or negative status tags, we use $D _ { j , m a x }$ to denote the number of maximum degree until the date such a status tag is given. For ideas receiving only under review status tags or no status tags at all over the period following their submission, we define $D _ { { _ { j , m a x } } }$ as the number of maximum possible commenters between their submission date and August 13, 2009.

## Sponsoring Firm Responsiveness

We compute the sponsoring firm responsiveness, Spon\_Resp, for individual i by taking the average of sponsoring firm responsiveness per idea for that individual participant. For each idea $\cdot j$ submitted by individual i, Spon\_Resp is the interval between the day when the idea is submitted, $s _ { j } ,$ and either the day when a status tag is given or the end of the period occurs, $t _ { j } .$ Thus, Spon\_Resp is a waiting time measure. Until, and if, a tag (firm feedback) is received, the waiting time keeps running. We posit that longer waits for feedback (which may or may not eventually occur) will weigh negatively on potential contributors. Table 2 summarizes the operational definitions used in our analyses.

We pr esent r esul ts of our empir ic al anal ysis in two subsec tions. The first subsection focuses on the results of testing hypotheses related to participants’ contribution level (H1, H3, H5, and H7) and the second subsection focuses on the results of testing hypotheses related to participants’ active duration (H2, H4, H6, and H8).

## Participants’ Contribution

To test H1, H3, H5, and H7 at the individual participant level, we separate our data set into multiple periods along the data collection time line. This approach allows us to explore the dynamic interaction among individual contributors, their peer participants, and the sponsoring firm. When determining the length of the time window, we sought a balance. If a period is too short, we are not able to observe participants’ behavior. If a period is too long, we may include unrelated activities together. In dealing with a similar issue (“developer permanence” in their work), Colazo and Fang [10, p. 998] explored the “statistical distribution” of the last participation by software developers. They chose a cutoff point at 135 days or the 99th percentile of the observed distribution. Proceeding in an analogous way, we checked the observed distribution of the last participation (submitting an idea or making a comment) of COCB participants. The length of eight weeks (56 days) is approximately the 97 percentile of the distribution and thus was our selection.

We have two sets of hypothesis: H1a, H3a, H5a, and H7a, related to the aspect of quantity of contribution; and H1b, H3b, H5b, and H7b, related to the aspect of quality of contribution. For the first set, we use the number of new ideas submitted in the current period as the dependent variable since our model is in essence a count data model. Although the Poisson regression model is widely used in count data analysis, it is limited by its implicit assumption that the variance and mean of the count variable is the same [20, p. 744]. In our data set, the variance of dependent variable $N e w I d e a _ { i , t }$ is 5.132, more than 13 times of the mean (0.378), which suggests that the assumption of equal mean and variance are unlikely to be valid. Since the negative binomial regression model does not require the assumption of equal mean and variance (see [20, sec. 21.9]), we employ that model, a choice supported by the likelihood ratio test (p < 0.0001).

Our set of independent variables is listed in Table 3. The independent variable set includes, for each participant, the appropriate averages or total values from prior periods for individual connectedness, peer feedback, sponsoring firm feedback, and sponsoring firm responsiveness. In addition, following the suggestion by Moon and Sproull [31] that more experienced participants in co-creation are likely to contribute more ideas, we included an “experience” variable, calculated as the length of participation up to the present period, as a control variable. Note that we compute the variables related to the peer and sponsor feedback on a per idea basis. The rationale of doing that is to control the variation due to the scale of ideas submitted by different participants.

We used PROC GENMOD in SAS to estimate the model. Since peer and sponsoring firm feedback may be interrelated, we calculated the variance inflation factors (VIFs) for the independent variables, finding values ranging from 1.06 to 1.88. A common rule of thumb is that a VIF greater than 5 reflects “high multicollinearity.” More formally, Kutner et al. [27] indicate that a VIF value greater than 10 indicates a “serious issue.” Thus, we found no indication of serious concern. We further explored whether coefficients of these two sets of independent variables kept their sign and significance level by running separate models followed by a nested model (see [24]). In model 1, we only use peer feedback, and in model 2, we use only sponsoring firm feedback. We include all the variables in model 3. Table 5 summarizes the statistical results.

From the results of the negative binomial regression estimation, we note several outcomes. First, we find that the coefficient of participants’ individual connectedness has the opposite sign from what we would have expected. Further, the estimate is significant in both model 1 and model 3. Thus, H1a is not supported. This result seems counterintuitive, but we posit that this may be the result of the nature of the COCB where there can be thousands of participants but only a few are likely to comment on any given idea. Consider two individuals, one averaging two comments per idea and the other averaging four comments per idea. If the COCB has 2,000 participants during the duration of these ideas, then the second individual’s connectedness value would be double that of the first person. However, given that the respective values would be 4 / 2000 versus 2 / 2000, both measures are actually very small numerically. Another explanation might be drawn from the discussion of Borgatti and Halgin’s [7; especially pp. 1174–1175] on bonding (although in social networks rather than COCBs). In brief, the argument is that submitters with higher levels of “connectedness” may put more effort in discussing their current ideas or commenting on other commenters’ ideas as a means to maintain their position in the network. These efforts might tend to divert them from contributing more ideas. This raises questions for future research regarding possibilities of identifying and studying smaller communities within a COCB where connectedness might be of great positive significance.

Next, we observe that the peer feedback coefficient is positive and significant in both model 1 and model 3. Thus, H3a is supported. This result suggests that when a contributor receives more feedback per idea from other members in a COCB in previous periods, he or she will be more likely to contribute new ideas in the current period. Specifically, our results (taking into account the exponential form of the estimated model, see [20]) indicate that, all other factors being equal, the expected number of new ideas submitted by a member receiving one more comment per idea is about 4.69 percent higher based on the estimated coefficients in model 3 (the figure is 5.03 percent in model 1). However, the coefficient for sponsoring firm feedback (number of sponsoring firm tags per idea) has the expected sign but does not show significance in either model 2 or 3. Thus, H5a is not supported. This finding suggests that our model fails to find a significant relationship between the level of the sponsoring firm feedback and member’s contributions.

We now estimate the negative binomial model using the number of ideas with positive status tags (“high quality”) as the dependent variable to test the second set of hypothesis (H1b, H3b, H5b, and H7b). Table 6 presents the estimation results.

We note several interesting findings. First, individual connectedness is again insignificant in all the estimated models. Thus, H1b is not supported. Second, the estimated coefficient of peer feedback has the expected sign but is not significant. Thus, H3b is not supported. However, we find that sponsoring firm feedback now shows a positive and significant effect on participants’ contributing high-quality ideas. Thus, H5b is supported. Finally, sponsoring firm responsiveness shows a negative (faster responses or shorter waiting times are better) and significant influence on members’ contributing high-quality ideas in model 3, which is consistent with the previous finding. Taking the results presented in Tables 5 and 6 together, we find that both peer feedback and sponsoring firm feedback are important factors to stimulate participants to contribute, but in rather different ways. The impact of peer feedback in previous periods is positive and significant on the quantity of contributions, but this is not the case for sponsoring firm feedback. Delays in responding by the sponsoring firm do exhibit a statistically significant negative effect on the quantity level of contributions. When we turn to explaining the number of high-quality contributions, sponsoring firm feedback in prior periods does show a positive and significant effect, while the peer feedback in previous periods is not significant. Again, the sponsoring firm responsiveness (average length of time waiting for response to ideas submitted) has a negative and significant effect. To summarize, we note that, although a higher level of peer feedback may increase the quantity of contribution, what matters for the quality of contribution is sponsoring firm feedback. In both cases, a delay in sponsoring firm responses has negative effects.

## Participants’ Active Duration

H2, H4, H6, and H8 focus on factors explaining participant duration or “length of participation” in the COCB (see Figure 3). Thus, we utilize a duration model or “time to event” model. As discussed in the Research Methods section above, a member continues participating by either submitting ideas or making comments or by doing both.

We chose the accelerated failure time (AFT) model, which is widely used to model survival time (see [2] and detailed discussion in [5]). Consistent with Allison [2] and Bhattacharjee et al. [5], we use a log-linear model form for estimation: $\begin{array} { r } { l o g ( S _ { i } ) = \gamma _ { \mathrm { 0 } } + \gamma X + \mathfrak { E } _ { i } } \end{array}$ . As used in Table 7, $S _ { _ i }$ denotes the participation duration of member i in the COCB, X refers to the set of explanatory variables (see Table 7 for a complete list), $\gamma$ refers to the coefficients of the explanatory variables (where the exponential coefficients $e x p ( \gamma _ { i } )$ are referred to as time ratios), and $\varepsilon _ { _ i }$ is the random disturbance term. Three explanatory variables (peer feedback, firm feedback, and firm responsiveness) are computed on a “per idea” basis to avoid scale issues for individuals submitting different numbers of ideas. ## Summary and Conclusions

## Limitations

Ther e ar e, of c our se, sever al l imitations to this r esearc h. First, and most significant, is that our analysis covers only one COCB context, Dell IdeaStorm. Dell IdeaStorm’s length of operation (two and a half years) and prominence makes it a good starting point to study COCBs. But this is only a starting point. It is important that we pursue analysis of additional contextualized COCBs to determine what findings continue to hold and thus become candidates for a set of general principles of COCBs. Second, we are aware that our discussion of contribution does not provide a measure of the actual business value of individual ideas. While two ideas may be both “implemented” by a sponsoring company, they will likely have significantly different business values.

Although likely a bit difficult, a next and important step in the analysis of COCBs is to develop measures that link ideas to business value metrics.

## Conclusions and Discussion

In this paper, we studied how firms that are interested in seeking innovative ideas from individuals can develop an effective strategy to engage those individuals to contribute and to sustain their participation. Building on Zwass’s [50] taxonomy of value co-creation, we proposed a research framework and examined the motivating factors that influence the contribution and sustained involvement of individuals in the context of company sponsored value co-creation, specifically the participant contribution level and participant duration. Given the lack of a financial reward mechanism and no participant profiling information, we focused on the role that feedback systems (both peer feedback and sponsoring firm feedback) might play in motivating contribution level and continuing participation of individuals. The context of study in this research is one of the first company-sponsored idea-generating communities. Individuals contribute to the site by posting ideas related to the company products or provide feedback comments to ideas submitted by other participants. The sponsoring company can respond and provide detailed feedback and updates on contributed ideas to the community.

We found that participant contribution level (in terms of submitting ideas) did not seem to be affected by where and how densely an individual was connected to others in the idea-generating community (H1). A high propensity to contribute ideas is likely an altruistic and intrinsic indicator of an individual who tends to express personal beliefs and deeply felt needs. To an individual participant, the notion of “position” or “closeness” does not have meaning by itself in an anonymous online community with strangers. The concepts of position or closeness become relevant only through feedback from other participants or the company that sponsors the brainstorming activities. Of course, this needs to be interpreted with caution because, in our research setting, we had no way to know the characteristics or backgrounds of the participants. Moreover, individuals who commented frequently in the system, thus adding to other’s higher network connectedness, might simply provide a lot of helpful comments on others ideas but did not submit many ideas themselves.

Feedback systems in the context of idea generation have significant influences on the participant contribution level (H3 and H5). However, they affect participants in distinct ways even though both serve as signals of recognition of one’s efforts in co-creation activities. When an idea receives a lot of feedback from peers, the idea submitter is more likely to feel his or her inner self-esteem motives are fulfilled. As a result, the idea submitter is more likely to submit ideas again. On the other hand, when an individual sees the progress and results that his or her submitted idea has been adopted and acted upon by the sponsoring firm, that individual is more likely to feel competent and trusted to contribute more quality ideas. The presence of sponsoring firm feedback, in essence, provides an indication that the company is actually reviewing the ideas submitted, that is, the company is taking the community seriously. This provides positive motivation to participants.

Having discussed the effects of feedback systems to participant contribution level, how might the speed of feedback help the company manage the challenge of cultivating new ideas? Our results clearly show that sponsoring firm responsiveness significantly influences both participant contribution level and active duration of participation (H7 and H8). The quicker a company responds to an individual’s ideas, the more likely it fulfills the individual’s sense of belonging and prosocial motives, thereby encouraging continued participation in online brainstorming activities. In addition, receiving interaction from the peers through comments and feedback on certain ideas is likely to keep an individual involved in the discussion of fine tuning an idea, thus increasing the probability of staying longer in value co-creation (H2 and H4).

## Implications for Practice

Like any other type of value co-creation initiative, the commitment, voluntary participation, and contribution of individuals is critical to the success and sustainability of company-sponsored online brainstorming. This research provides several useful managerial insights for companies currently sponsoring or considering a COCB. First, a COCB is an open structure where it is possible for an individual to be widely “recog nized” (at least in terms of his or her COCB unique ID). Given that the results suggest the importance of peer feedback, COCBs can be structured to facilitate popularity or reputation indicators to provide additional motivation. Companies might develop enhanced mechanisms to facilitate and emphasize peer feedback, including running lists of top contributors or “best” recent contributors. Top contributors or “best ideas” might be prominently noted on the home page.

Second, the results of this study emphasize the importance of firm responsiveness in the form of timely feedback. Since a COCB is, in essence, a long-run setting of voluntary contribution, slow response can discourage contributors. Why should a participant freely give of his or her time and creativity if the firm does not even make the effort to respond in a timely manner? Although a slow process of reviewing ideas may save resources for the sponsoring firm, our results indicate the risk of losing participants who become impatient with waiting for a response. The outcomes suggest that sponsoring companies should develop efficient processes to review and respond to submitted ideas. The firm might structure target guidelines such as no ideas should be left untagged for more than four weeks.

Finally, our results indicate the importance of connectedness in longer participant duration. If the goal of the COCB is a long run, participatory platform, then strategies to enhance connectedness should be developed. Possible tactics could include highlighting the most active participants or participants submitting exceptional ideas.

## Implications for Theory Building

This research studied motivating factors leading volunteers to participate and contribute ideas in company sponsored online co-creation brainstorming. Our data enabled us to explore the effects of feedback systems on participant contribution level and the active duration of participation. We find that peer feedback and timely firm feedback positively influence subsequent levels of participant contributions while firm feedback and timely firm responsiveness positively influence subsequent quality contributions. Individual connectedness, peer feedback, and firm responsiveness all positively influence the duration of participation, though firm feedback does not. Taken as a whole, our findings provide an initial piece of contextualized research that offers theoretical implications consistent with the relevance of reputation and signaling theory.

Consider Zwass’s taxonomic framework (Figure 1), which forms the starting point and foundation for our analysis. The current study provides a refinement to specific aspects of the various taxonomic entities and expands upon the interactive relationships among those entities using one type of company sponsored co-creation. In this context, the sponsoring firm (being the primary beneficiary of co‑created value) does not know the identity or control individual participants who wish to offer ideas and comments related to the company’s products and services. When a proper incentive mechanism is not possible, the sponsoring company can focus on the feedback system (process) to keep these co‑creators engaged and motivated to continue contributing toward the overall objective of the company. That being said, the sponsoring company could design other aspects of the process and define certain characteristics of the value co-creation task to control the input structure and process available to participants. In addition, psychological and economic theories have studied many other motivating factors including individual characteristics, incentive, and reward mechanisms toward participation/contribution. Further research is needed to understand how these motivators might influence user participation in the broad context of value co-creation. Specifically, many of the value co-creation communities have implemented reward systems such as a point value that can subsequently be redeemed for other tangible benefits. It is not clear how these various incentive mechanisms will work to influence the participation behaviors of volunteers, and eventually the performance of these value co-creating activities. Findings related to these questions together with findings from the current study can help enable us to develop a formal theory of Web-based value co-creation.
