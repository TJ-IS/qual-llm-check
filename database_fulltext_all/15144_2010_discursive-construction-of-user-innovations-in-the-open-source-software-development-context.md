# Discursive construction of ‘user innovations’ in the open source software development context

Netta Iivari ⁎

Department of Information Processing Science, University of Oulu, P.O. Box 3000, 90014 Oulu, Finland

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 9 June 2009 Received in revised form 10 February 2010 Accepted 10 March 2010

Keywords: User innovation Open source software development Discourse Textuality

## a b s t r a c t

This paper analyzes the discursive construction of user innovations in open source software (OSS) development. A review of the literature on user innovation is conducted, after which a re<sup>fi</sup>ned conceptualization of this topic encompassing the Foucauldian and social shaping of technology traditions is offered. A discourse analytic approach is used to examine both the process and the outcome of user innovation. Through an interpretive case study, a set of discourses evolving within an OSS project's website is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, each implying particular subject positions for the OSS users and OSS developers in software production and consumption processes. Particular kinds of subject positions for the OSS users are adopted, negotiated, opposed as well as created by the OSS users in the analyzed discourses. The developers are in an authoritative position as the decision-makers and the ones realizing the innovations, but the users are allowed important positions of co-creators and cocultivators. User innovation is characterized as a continuous, evolving cocreation process of the modes of subjectivity offered for the OSS users. This kind of process could be experimented with in other kinds of development contexts. On the other hand, this study questions many assumptions related to user innovation that are taken for granted, highlighting the difficulty of simply and straightforwardly enhancing it in any context. Finally, implications for theory and practice are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

This paper critically examines the discursive construction of user innovations in the open source software (OSS) development context. OSS development has been acknowledged as a current research topic in Information Systems (IS) research (e.g. Fitzgerald 2006, Ljungberg 2000, Niederman, Davis, Greiner, Wynn and York, 2006). The speci<sup>fi</sup>c research question addressed in this paper is formulated in the following way: “How do end-user innovations emerge and evolve in the OSS development context?” End-user refers to a nondeveloper user in the OSS development context, with end-user innovation necessitating developer involvement in order to realize the innovation. This paper will describe what OSS development entails and its signi<sup>fi</sup>cance for organizations. It will be argued that OSS development provides a fertile arena for end-user innovations, whose importance to organizations was acknowledged decades ago. This paper will scrutinize the emergence and evolution of end-user innovations in the OSS development context in the natural setting without researcher intervention, in order to gain insights into how one might nurture user innovations in organizations. On the other hand, it is acknowledged that this nurturing surely is not a simple or straightforward task. As opposed to simplistic and naïve conceptualizations of user innovation, this paper outlines a conceptualization relying on Foucauldian discourse analytic and Social Studies of Technology (SST) traditions, arguing that software development is taking part in the discursive struggle for people's identity. During end-user innovation, non-developer users contribute to the process. This paper contributes to the user innovation research by revealing some dynamics and complexities involved in end-user innovations both at the conceptual and empirical levels.

First, the OSS development model is brie<sup>fl</sup>y outlined. OSS development refers to the community development of software, whose source code needs to be open for anyone to use, modify and distribute (e.g. Ljungberg 2000, Scacchi 2001, von Hippel & von Krogh 2003). The development model relies on individuals who develop solutions due to their personal needs and who voluntarily reveal their solutions for use and further development by others (von Hippel & von Krogh 2003). OSS development entails community software development that extensively utilizes the Internet's means for communication and coordination (Ljungberg 2000, Scacchi 2001, Scacchi 2002, von Hippel 2005, von Hippel & von Krogh 2003). OSS has been traditionally developed by technically skilled individuals for their own use, but recently researchers have argued that the user population of OSS has become larger and more heterogeneous, including an increasing number of users who do not have technical backgrounds, skills or interest in the development of solutions themselves (Franke & von Hippel 2003, Nichols & Twidale 2003, Nichols & Twidale 2006, Scacchi 2002, Ye & Kishida 2003). Instead, these users only wish to be able to make use of the solution, a situation that might be quite problematic, since the usability of the solutions has not traditionally been emphasized, and the solutions tend not to be targeted at people without any technical background (Iivari 2009a, Twidale & Nichols, 2005, Nichols & Twidale, 2006).

Also, organizations are entering the OSS scene. They are increasingly adopting OSS solutions, some of them aiming to develop the solutions further, with others only aiming to make use of the solutions. Famous, widely adopted OSS solutions include, e.g., the OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce application suite, the Mozilla Firefox web browser and the Thunderbird email client. Organizations can adopt OSS solutions for their own use as part of their information technology infrastructure or as development tools (Heikinheimo & Kuusisto 2004). In addition, organizations have started to take part in OSS development. Fitzgerald (2006) discusses ‘OSS 2.0,’ i.e. new, commercially viable OSS. He argues that in OSS development currently, new product domains are addressed; earlier, the focus was mainly on horizontal infrastructure (i.e. operating systems, compilers, servers, etc.), but nowadays applications are developed for many divergent vertical product domains (Fitzgerald 2006). Ågerfalk and Fitzgerald (2008) describe open sourcing, i.e. the cooperation between companies and OSS communities to develop software, with the company also expecting some commercial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. The company may be opening the source code of their proprietary software or producing proprietary software on the basis of OSS (Dahlander & Magnusson 2005, Fitzgerald 2006, Heikinheimo & Kuusisto 2004, Niederman et al 2006, Ågerfalk & Fitzgerald 2008). Companies may cooperate with existing OSS communities or try to generate new communities around their products (Dahlander & Magnusson 2005). In addition, service and support business related to OSS has emerged. Especially in the future, customers expect professional support and training related to OSS, around which some companies have built their business. (Dahlander & Magnusson 2005, Fitzgerald 2006, Heikinheimo & Kuusisto 2004, Niederman et al 2006).

As is evident, organizations can utilize OSS in many different ways. This paper particularly focuses on OSS development as an arena for user innovations, examining how user innovations emerge and evolve in this arena. User innovation has been considered as signi<sup>fi</sup>cant both from the viewpoint of organizational and national competitive advantage (Franke, von Hippel and Schreier, 2006). It has been praised as a way for companies to gain new, innovative ideas and competitive edge (e.g. Chesbrough 2003, Franke & von Hippel 2003, Franke et al. 2006, Franke & Shah 2003, Jeppesen & Frederiksen 2006, Lettl 2007, von Hippel 2001, von Hippel 2005). User innovation in the form of development and modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the products people use is also relatively common: between 10% and 40% of users studied report developing or modifying the products they use—for in-house use in relation to industrial products and for personal use in relation to consumer products (Franke et al. 2006). Numerous studies have brought up OSS development as a fertile arena for user innovation (e.g. Chesbrough 2003, Franke & von Hippel 2003, von Hippel 2001, von Hippel 2005). Researchers have argued that companies should gather information from OSS communities about how their users modify and tailor the solutions as well as innovate new solutions (Chesbrough 2003, Franke & Shah 2003, Morrison, Roberts and von Hippel, 2000, West & Gallagher 2006, von Hippel 1988). The results of this study provide important insights into the dynamics of user innovation—in OSS development in this case—which could also be applied in other IS contexts.

This paper focuses on the innovation of non-developer users in OSS development. It has already been argued that OSS development has a user-driven development model (Zhao & Deek 2006), but the users referred to are the developer users, who can contribute to the technical development. User innovation carried out by these kinds of users has been characterized already (e.g. Chesbrough 2003, Franke & Shah 2003, Franke & von Hippel 2003, West & Gallagher 2006, von Hippel 2001), but user innovation carried out by non-developer users has not yet been addressed. Non-developer user innovation should be examined in order to understand its dynamics and characteristics, however, since the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of end-user innovation has been acknowledged during recent years, especially in the research and development (R&D) context (e.g. Kristensson, Gustafsson and Archer, 2004, Lettl 20007) and end-user centeredness has become almost a necessity in IS development (e.g. Iivari & Molin Juustila 2009, Markus & Mao 2004). It has been argued that so-called ordinary users create more original and valuable ideas than advanced users or developers (Kristensson et al. 2004). Therefore, there are clear reasons for the increasing interest in end-user innovation, in organizations as well as in OSS development. On the other hand, this study questions many naïve assumptions related to user innovation, highlighting the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of simply and straightforwardly enhancing user innovation in any context.

This is accomplished by critically examining the discursive construction of user innovations in the OSS development context, informed by Foucauldian and SST traditions. Textuality is emphasized in the analysis: OSS is perceived as texts written by the writer–developers and read by the reader–users (Grint and Woolgar 1997), consequences of which are the rejection of technological determinism and the highlighting of interpretive <sup>fl</sup>exibility of technological artifacts (Bijker and Law, 1992, Grint and Woolgar 1997, Pinch & Bijker 1994). It is assumed that software texts take part in the discursive struggle for people's identity (cf. Weedon 1987). Software texts, among other texts, provide to their readers subject positions to be occupied, making the readers ‘subjects of certain kind’ (Weedon 1987: 97). The texts contain scripts in which the envisioned users and use are materialized (Akrich 1992, van Oost, Verhaegh and Oudshoorn, 2009). User innovation, then, refers to the users' discursive construction and negotiation of these ‘scripts,’ in which subject positions for the prospective users are de<sup>fi</sup>ned.

Examination of the process of user innovation and of the resulting subject positions provided in the scripts necessitates a discourse analytic approach. Studies on discourses relying on the Foucauldian tradition have proliferated in recent years in IS research (e.g. Bergquist, Hafström and Hofbauer, 2005, Cooper & Bowers 1995, Finken 2003, Sayer & Harvey 1997, Wynn, Whitley, Myers and DeGross, 2002). Some researchers have already empirically examined discourses in the development context between developers and users (e.g. Alvarez 2002, Bloom<sup>fi</sup>eld & Vurdubakis 1997, Sarkkinen & Karsten 2005), showing that the discourses mainly reinforce management agendas and goals. The studies have analyzed planning and task redesign (Sarkkinen & Karsten 2005) and requirements determination (Alvarez 2002, Bloom<sup>fi</sup>eld & Vurdubakis 1997) discourses, in which ‘user requirements and needs’ (Alvarez 2002) and ‘changes in tasks and authority relationships (Sarkkinen & Karsten 2005) are constructed. These studies, however, do not address the OSS development context. On the other hand, other studies have indicated that a discourse analytic approach would be suitable in the OSS development context. Discourse analytic studies have been carried out examining the literature on Free Software Foundation and Open Source Movements (Berry 2004) and the literature on OSS in developing countries (Bergquist et al. 2005). Researchers have also argued that in OSS communities, the requirements are articulated and negotiated in webs of discourses emerging in the OSS communities' websites (Scacchi 2001, Scacchi 2002). OSS communities are highly reliant on mailing lists and discussion forums, and they have already been extensively analyzed by the OSS researchers; e.g., from the viewpoints of communication, coordination, user support and usability bug reporting (e.g. Lakhani & von Hippel 2003, Scacchi 2002, Twidale & Nichols 2005, Yamauchi et al. 2000). They offer a fertile ground for a discourse analytic approach (e.g. Panteli 2004, da Cunha & Orlikowski 2008); e.g., the emergence of different kinds of discourses in an online forum in the context of organizational change has been examined (da Cunha & Orlikowski 2008), and articulations of presence in virtual teams have been studied through discourse analysis of email messages (Panteli 2004). This paper will utilize discourse analysis in the examination of user innovation in OSS discussion forum messages.

This paper is organized as follows. The next section conceptualizes user innovation based on the existing user innovation literature. Section 3 offers a re<sup>fi</sup>ned conceptualization of user innovation that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the Foucauldian and SST traditions. Section 4 discusses the interpretive case study that has been carried out, with Section 5 outlining its results. Section 6 discusses their implications, with Section 7 summarizing the results, discussing their limitations and identifying paths for future work.

## 2. Scrutinizing user innovation

User innovation refers to the production of innovations by end-users as opposed to manufacturers producing the innovations for the end-users. User innovation has been argued to include phases such as the user recognizing a need, solving the problem by invention, building a prototype and establishing the prototype's value in use (von Hippel 1988). Floyd, Jones, Rathi and Twidale (2007) have examined userdriven technological innovation, which, according to them, originates from a user's everyday life and experiences, during which the user encounters problems. The user then rapidly and easily produces <sup>fi</sup>xes to these issues by utilizing technology, after which she re<sup>fl</sup>ects on the solution, i.e. on how it <sup>fi</sup>ts the overall systems, and adjusts the system as a result. The cycle starts over when the user, again, encounters problems in her everyday life. (Floyd et al. 2007.) Typically, innovation necessitates not only the invention part, but also its implementation as well as market introduction adoption and diffusion (Garcia & Calantone 2002). Franke and von Hippel et al. (2006), however, include the following as user innovation: users imagining a possible solution to a problem, users developing a plan with descriptions and/or drawings, users building a prototype as well as solutions that have already been implemented, marketed and adopted by others.

Innovation, generally, refers to “an idea, practice, or object that is perceived as new by an individual or other unit of adoption” (Rogers 1995: 12). However, there are a variety of de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of innovation in the literature, and the ‘new’ may convey new to the ‘world,’ ‘industry,’ ‘scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community,’ ‘market,’ ‘<sup>fi</sup>rm’ or ‘customer.’ Furthermore, the ‘new’ might include new ‘technology,’ ‘product line,’ ‘design,’ ‘features,’ ‘process,’ ‘service,’ ‘competition,’ ‘customers,’ ‘needs,’ ‘consumption patterns,’ ‘users,’ ‘development skills, ‘marketing/sales/distribution skills,’ ‘managerial skills,’ ‘learning/experience/knowledge’ or ‘quality/ bene<sup>fi</sup>ts’ (Garcia & Calantone 2002). Furthermore, the existing literature has concentrated more on user innovations from the viewpoint of the producer <sup>fi</sup>rms than from the viewpoint of the units of adoption (see e.g. Chesbrough 2003, Garcia & Calantone 2002, West & Gallagher 2006, von Hippel 2001).

Garcia and Calantone (2002) have developed an innovation typology that takes into account marketing, technological and macro- and micro-level perspectives. At the micro level, the focus is on newness to the <sup>fi</sup>rm or to the customer (or, in the OSS development context, to the developers or users), whereas at the macro level one focuses on the newness to the world, market or industry. Generally, there needs to be a discontinuity related to both the technological and marketing factors. There might be a need for a new marketplace, for marketing skills to evolve, or for a need for change in the technology, development resources or production processes. Taking into account the perspectives, innovations can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as radical, really new or incremental. Related to radical innovations, there need to be discontinuities related to both technology and market factors, and at the macro and micro levels. Related to incremental innovations, there are discontinuities related either to technology or to market factors, and only at the micro level. Really new innovations cover the options between these two extremes. (Garcia & Calantone 2002.) In this paper, the focus is only on the micro-level innovations in one OSS community, so this paper necessarily discusses only incremental innovations. Incremental innovations refer to “products that provide new features, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, or improvements to the existing technology in the existing market” (Garcia & Calantone 2002: 123). This paper, however, does not deny the possibility of the discussed innovations being or becoming radical or really new innovations, but the lack of a macro-level perspective in the analysis hinders this discussion.

In the OSS development context, it has already been argued that user innovation includes users being the innovators of new products, as well as users being the innovators of new features or functionality that improves and re<sup>fi</sup>nes existing products (Chesbrough 2003, Franke & Shah 2003, Franke & von Hippel 2003, Morrison et al. 2000). Hyysalo (2009: 250) discusses micro-innovations as self-made modi<sup>fi</sup>cations and adaptations to the existing products. He argues that “people need to adapt an appropriate technology in particular and varying ways, and in so doing, accommodate and domesticate it to the spheres of their work and leisure practices”. He continues by claiming that the “ability to use is not an inherent capacity of equipment or its users, but is constituted by shaping anew the habits, skills, schemes for making sense of and operating technology, along with divisions of labour, fellow practitioners, other instruments and the purpose for which the equipment is intended” (Hyysalo 2009: 250). Micro-innovations involve people making “various small alterations” to the products and to their practices and usage patterns “to make their activity more enjoyable” and products “work better” (Hyysalo 2009: 250).

In a similar vein, van Oost et al. (2008: 188) discuss user innovations concerning both ‘social’ and ‘technical’, carried out by heterogeneous engineers that “continuously perform various types of work to align technical and social elements into an actor-network so as to build stable coalitions that are necessary for the successfu development and implementation of an innovation.” Innovating users need to perform these heterogeneous activities related to the alignment of the technical and the social. The innovation, therefore, concerns both technology and the envisioned users and use inscribed in it (van Oost et al. 2009). Also, other researchers have discussed user innovations concerning both the user domain and the technical domain (Lettl 2007).

User innovation is also characterized as iterative in nature (Garcia & Calantone 2002) and as collective activity during which different types of knowledge are aligned (van Oost et al. 2008). Users act as developers or co-developers in the process (Lettl 2007). The process is characterized as rapid, participatory and collaborative (Floyd et al. 2007). Floyd and Jones et al. (2007) argue that it is important that there are prototypes available for users to integrate into their everyday life and work practices for exploration purposes. The users should be able to explore how technology could be utilized in their work practices, but also how their work practices could be conceptualized anew with technology.

User innovation has been empirically explored especially in the R&D context (e.g. Jeppesen & Frederiksen 2006, Kristensson et al 2004, Lettl 2997, von Hippel 1988) and in different kinds of communities of practice, e.g. in different kinds of sports communities (see e.g. Franke & Shah 2003, Franke et al. 2006, Hyysalo 2009). In these communities, the resources provided for the innovators are crucial; for example, information, assistance and contacts (Franke & Shah 2003, Morrison et al. 2000). Typically, the innovations solve urgent problems of the innovators, who typically have ‘lead user’ characteristics, meaning that they are ahead of trends and would clearly bene<sup>fi</sup>t from these new innovations, and they tend to be integrated well into their community (Franke & Shah 2003, Morrison et al. 2000). The users also typically have in-depth knowledge of the domain (Lettl 2007). Oftentimes they are hobbyists related to the <sup>fi</sup>eld in which they innovate (Jeppesen & Frederiksen 2006). Innovating users also tend to be open to new technologies and have a strong intrinsic motivation to solve the problem (Lettl 2007). However, the lack of technical skills or knowledge may hamper user innovation (Lettl 2007, Morrison et al. 2000, Nambisan, Agarwal and Tanniru, 1999).

This paper examines end-user innovation; the analysis will concentrate on the innovations proposed by non-developer users, who need developer assistance in realizing the innovations. Therefore, even though user innovation is assumed to consist of recognizing a need, producing a solution, developing a prototype and establishing, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on and adjusting its value in use (cf. Floyd et al. 2007, von Hippel 1988), the <sup>fi</sup>rst two steps will be particularly associated with end-user innovation after which the developers need to join in. Therefore, the users act as co-developers (Lettl 2007). The user innovation may concern both the technical and the social, but developer participation is needed before the innovation can be put to use and have its value re<sup>fl</sup>ected on and adjusted.

## 3. Theoretical frames of reference

This paper relies on Foucauldian and SST traditions in the analysis of the discursive construction of user innovations in the OSS development context. Within the approach, it is assumed that language does not represent reality, but actively produces it (Hall 1997, Weedon 1987, Weedon 2004) through discourses that de<sup>fi</sup>ne how we can meaningfully talk about topics (Hall 1997). Discourses are ‘certain ways of speaking’ that systematically form the objects of which they speak (Foucault 1972: 49). Foucault (1972: 118-119) argues the researchers should always be aware that ‘everything is never said’ and ‘few things are said of the totality,’ due to which discourses always very forcefully impose certain kinds of understandings of the world: they outline ‘certain ways of speaking’ that exclude a multitude of other ways.

Subjectivity is another central concept, which refers to the ‘individual's sense of herself and her ways of understanding her relation to the world’ within which she structures her sense of herself (Weedon 1987: 32-33). Earlier it was assumed that people are ‘sovereign, conscious, knowing, intentional individuals’; ‘knowing subjects’ led by free will, reason, knowledge and experience, with uni<sup>fi</sup>ed and rational subjectivity (Weedon 2004: 8). It was assumed that there is a unique, <sup>fi</sup>xed, coherent essence making people ‘what they are.’ However, in the Foucauldian approach, it is assumed that subjectivity is fragile, contradictory and constantly constituted in discourses. (Hall 1997, Weedon 1987, Weedon 2004.) Discourses offer individuals modes of subjectivity through subject positions that need to be adopted if participating in the discourses (Foucault 1972, Foucault 1980, Weedon 1987). Discourses constitute individuals as ‘subjects of certain kind’ through these subject positions (Weedon 1987: 97). However, this constitution of individuals as ‘subjects of certain kind’ is never-ending and open to challenge; people do not only adopt subject positions offered, but the subject positions can also be questioned and challenged (Weedon 1987, Weedon 2004). However, certain discourses are more readily available or more authoritative than others (Foucault 1972, Weedon 1987).

In this paper, OSS is perceived as texts in which the ‘readers are con<sup>fi</sup>gured’; i.e., the future users are de<sup>fi</sup>ned and the parameters for their future work practices are established (Grint & Woolgar 1997). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, these texts contain scripts in which the envisioned users and use are realized (Akrich 1992, van Oost et al. 2008). These scripts offer to the readers subject positions that make the text easy and obvious to make sense of (cf. Fiske 1987, Hall 1980, Hall 1997). The texts ‘make sense the most,’ ‘become meaningful’ and ‘have effects’ through adopting the subject positions (cf. Fiske 1987, Hall 1980: 130, Hall 1997: 56). However, the subject positions can also be questioned and negotiated (Weedon, 1987; Weedon, 2004). “Users may well read scripts in ways that differ from those intended by the designer” (van Oost et al. 2008). Even though the designers always, knowingly or not, produce the subject positions, the users can negotiate or oppose them as well as adopt them (cf. Hall 1980). User innovation refers to the construction and negotiation of the subject positions by the readers. Innovating users thus “constitute a speci<sup>fi</sup>c group of users that adopt speci<sup>fi</sup>c, informed ways of not just reading but also introducing new scripts, by inscribing characteristics of their speci<sup>fi</sup>c use situation into the product” (van Oost et al. 2008: 186).

Following Latour, innovation involves “a <sup>fi</sup>ction” struggling to become “fact,” with the process including a complex interplay of different kinds of claims, representations and actors (Ramiller 2001: 148). Technology development always starts “in the realm of signs, language and texts,” and one should not “differentiate too hastily between signs and things” (Latour 1996: 24). Technological innovations are initially technological <sup>fi</sup>ction encountered as texts, but they might become objects or institutions (Latour 1996). “In order to follow a technological project, we have to follow simultaneously both the narrative program and the degree of “realization” of each of the actions” (Latour 1996: 81). It is emphasized that “the direction in which an artifact (…) evolves depends very much on the meanings that different ‘groups with a problem’ construct for it” (von Hippel 2005: 172). There is typically a multiplicity of meanings attached to technological artifacts by different social groups with different needs and problems (Pinch & Bijker 1994). Heterogeneous engineering work is needed for aligning the technological and social and for stabilizing the innovation and the meanings attached to it (van Oost et al. 2008).

A discourse analytic approach is used to examine both the process and the outcome of user innovation in this paper. Foucault (e.g., 1972, 1980) discusses discourses and associated subject positions in a ‘global’ sense, showing, e.g., how madness and sexuality have been discursively constructed in our (Western) society. In this paper, however, the focus is on very ‘local’ discourses and subject positions available in software texts. Of course, one can utilize the approach at many different levels. It is acknowledged that discourse analysis does not refer to one particular method, but instead to a multitude of connected approaches with diverging background traditions (see e.g. Fairclough 1989, Phillips & Hardy 2002, van Dijk 2001). Despite their differences, discourse analysis shares few common characteristics such as the focus on different kinds of texts that can be encountered in a variety of forms, written being one of them, while the general interest is in the role of the discourses in constituting our social reality (see e.g. Fairclough 1989, Phillips & Hardy 2002, van Dijk 2001). The analyst should focus on both the process of production and consumption of the texts, through which the texts are made meaningful (Fairclough 1989, Phillips & Hardy 2002). Therefore, not only are characteristics of the text of interest, but also the inter-textual and situational contexts (Fairclough 1989). Critical discourse analysis, furthermore, should also consider the discourses' determinants and effects. The analyst should consider how discourses are determined by social structures, how discourses reproduce the structures, how power relationships determine discourses and how discourses are part of social struggles (Fairclough 1989). The analyst should examine how discourses enact, con<sup>fi</sup>rm, legitimate, reproduce, or challenge the existing power relations (van Dijk 2001).

Under discourse analytic examination are texts characterizing the OSS as well as texts characterizing its production and consumption. User innovation is perceived as a discursive struggle, during which subject positions in the OSS text are constructed, negotiated and realized. In addition, subject positions related to software production and consumption are adopted, re<sup>fi</sup>ned, questioned and negotiated. The OSS development context is particularly suitable for this type of discourse analytic examination of user innovation, since both the outcome and the process evolve as texts on the Internet. The examination of these texts is justi<sup>fi</sup>ed, since it has been argued that technological innovations always start as texts, which might eventually be realized as material objects, but whose trajectory as narrative programs should always be followed (cf. Latour 1996). In the OSS development context, the narrative program is observable, and it is almost unavoidable in the distributed OSS development. This kind of development is highly reliant on Internet-based communication. OSS development environment has already been conceptualized as an arena for speech, interaction and relationships, in which reputation, trust, subcultural literacy, access and voice in the arena are needed (Lievrouw 2006). The environment, therefore, strongly facilitates this type of discourse-oriented inquiry.

## 4. Research design

This research relies on the qualitative research tradition that is characterized as follows: “Qualitative research is a situated activity that locates the observer in the world. It consists of a set of interpretive, material practices that make the world visible. These practices transform the world. They turn the world into a series of representations, including <sup>fi</sup>eld notes, interviews, conversations, photographs, recordings and memos to the self. At this level, qualitative research involves an interpretive, naturalistic approach to the world. This means that qualitative researchers study things in their natural settings, attempting to make sense of, or to interpret, phenomena in terms of the meanings people bring to them” (Denzin & Lincoln 2000: 3). In this type of research, there is an attempt to understand and make sense of the world, not to explain in the predictive sense. Theories are used only as sensitizing devices. (Klein & Myers 1999: 69.)

The case analyzed in this paper is an OSS community that is developing a media application that people without any technical knowledge or programming skills could be interested in using (www.sourceforge.net). The community has also shown clear interest in their users and their feedback regarding the solution. The community is listed on a website requesting usability support from usability specialists for OSS development In addition, a usability discussion forum has been established on the community's website, inviting the users of the OSS to take part by suggesting how usability could be improved and by describing any irritating issues in the user interface. Altogether, over 1600 posts and nearly 400 topics have emerged in the discussion forum. Nearly 600 message senders have contributed to the discussion forum. The community is a relatively small but active one: there are nine de<sup>fi</sup>ned developers in the community; the development status is 5 (production stable), with an average of about 3500 downloads a month (www.sourcefore.net). In all, this OSS community is an example of a small, active end-user-oriented OSS development community. The analysis focused on the discussions in the usability discussion forum, which is assumed to be the place where end-user innovation can occur in this particular OSS development community.

All posts of the discussion forum were printed out for analysis purposes. The analysis relied on a discourse analytic approach loosely based on the principles of Fairclough's critical discourse analysis, which include phases such as description, interpretation and explanation (Fairclough 1989). Description concerns the discussion forum texts: their vocabulary, grammar and structure. Interpretation considers the texts in their inter-textual and situational contexts. Examination of the inter-textual context takes into account the historical series of texts to which these texts belong to and the associated presuppositions. Related to the situational context, the analyst should ask: What's going on? Who's involved? What are the subject positions offered and occupied? What are the relationships and power relations between the participants? (Fairclough 1989: 146.) Finally, explanation considers the discourse determinants and effects; how they enact, con<sup>fi</sup>rm, legitimate, reproduce, or challenge the existing power relations and, on the other hand, how the power relations and social structures determine the discourses (Fairclough 1989, van Dijk 2001).

In this research effort, the researcher <sup>fi</sup>rst familiarized herself with the discussion forum messages and message senders. All messages were printed and read. Related to each topic, the original sender of the message all the repliers and the content of the message were documented, resulting in the grouping of the message into four general categories: thanking, helping, bug reporting and feature requests (see Iivari 2009b). The aim of this phase was to gain a general feeling of the central topics and concerns of this OSS community. The focus was also on initially identifying messages that could be connected to user innovation, i.e. on messages that were suggesting something ‘new’ as perceived by the community members (users and/or developers). All propositions of ‘new’ were labeled as ‘feature request’ in the analysis. The requests varied from appearance to behavior and integration issues, but generally one can say that they concerned new features or functionality of the OSS. There were 499 messages labeled as feature request. They all have the potential of articulating user innovations, due to which they were all included in the in-depth analysis. During the analysis, every message was again carefully read, and its position in the series of other messages was acknowledged. It became evident that there were a lot of feature request messages without any responses or consequences. At that point in time, the study's attention turned to the messages that had gained responses from other people. Those were further examined to see whether any members and particularly developers had taken part in the discussions. The study also inquired whether the features were also considered ‘new’ by other people, not only by the original sender, and particularly by the developers, whose acceptance is essential in the OSS development context for the innovations to become implemented. However, there were also proposals, which contained something ‘new,’ but the developers still did not react positively to them. Also, these proposals were included in the analysis.

During these investigations, it became evident that there were certain recurring themes and topics, which seemed to be deemed as important by the community. The community members had put considerable effort in discussing, ideating and further improving these themes. They seemed to be characteristic for this particular OSS and at least partially differentiate this OSS community from other communities developing similar kinds of solutions. Indeed, the themes seemed to impose particular subject positions for the OSS users, who took part in the generation and further development of these themes in the discussions. Inspired by this observation, the analysis of the ‘user innovations’ focused on these highly popular themes, while limiting the focus to those topics that contained innovations that were 1) created by the non-developer users, 2) considered ‘new’ by the community, and 3) had created excitement among the community members. The most popular themes also containing something ‘new’ to the community were related to 1) enabling more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient sorting (53 messages); 2) enabling more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient tag editing (52 messages); 3) enabling certain kinds of visualizations in the application (40 messages); and 4) providing a better rating and scoring system (25 messages). These suggestions covered around 34% of all feature request messages. They were now considered as cases to be analyzed more thoroughly. In the analysis, they were conceptualized as local community discourses, in which certain kinds of subject positions in the OSS text were constructed and negotiated. In addition, in these discourses, subject positions related to software production and consumption were also adopted, re<sup>fi</sup>ned and negotiated.

Therefore, the discourse analytic approach was used to examine both the process and the outcome of user innovation. The discussion forum messages were examined as texts from the viewpoint of the divergent subject positions suggested, offered or opposed concerning the OSS. The inter-textual context was acknowledged in the sense of these messages belonging to a series of texts connected to OSS development in this particular OSS development community as well as more broadly to the OSS development environment, online discussion forum genre and netiquette. The situational context relates these texts to the OSS construction and consumption processes, in which the users are offered and occupy particular subject positions. The user innovations generated during the discourses were interpreted to concern the technical and the social, the latter including the ‘con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the user,’ i.e. the script outlining the envisioned user and use (cf. Hyysalo 2009, van Oost et al. 2008). Users acted as co-developers requiring OSS developers' assistance in realizing the innovation in the OSS text (cf. Lettl 2007). The literatures on user innovation, on the Foucauldian discourses and on the SST-oriented ‘con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of the user’ were used as sensitizing devices in the analysis. The questions relating to how the discourses enact, con<sup>fi</sup>rm, legitimate, reproduce, or challenge the existing power relations (Fairclough 1989, van Dijk 2001) will, <sup>fi</sup>nally, be addressed in the discussion part of the paper.

## 5. Empirical insight

## 5.1. Introducing the forum

First, some descriptive data of the forum messages and message senders (including both OSS developers and OSS users) are offered (see also Iivari 2009b). Most of the message senders are labeled as ‘newbies’ (282)

or ‘guests’ (274). This means that the senders are newcomers to the discussion forum according to the number of the messages they have sent (i.e. the newbies) or just visiting the site without registering (i.e. the guests). Later on, the people can advance to members (23) by posting messages to the forum. The people can also gain the position of OSS developer, but in this forum only two people are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as such in their pro<sup>fi</sup>les.

The ‘newbies’ and ‘guests’ (1102) have sent most of the messages, and they have initiated most of the topics as well. The developers and members mainly reply to the messages, an action that is characteristic of the OSS discussion forums (cf. Lakhani & von Hippel 2003, Ye & Kishida 2003). A large number of the messages (177) posted to the forum can be characterized as helping messages that offer advice to the original sender, and due to these messages one can argue that this discussion forum is used for providing user support, i.e. for describing how one can and is supposed to use the OSS. This form of user support has already been reported as a user-centric aspect of OSS development (Lakhani & von Hippel 2003). In addition, many messages (113) thank the developers and/or praise the OSS as the best one available. One can argue that the thanking messages might also be typical in the OSS forums, since the OSS developers are not paid for their work, but instead their own needs and the reputation gained motivates them, with a large user base causing them <sup>fl</sup>attery and reward (Ye & Kishida 2003).

These types of messages are typical of OSS forums, but the bug (300) and feature request (499) messages can be argued as being the most characteristic of OSS forums. OSS development relies on the Internet-based community development model, where there is a large group contributing, some by developing the OSS, others by reporting bugs or proposing new features (e.g. Franke & von Hippel 2003, Ljungberg 2000, Scacchi 2001, von Hippel & von Krogh 2003). In the bug-reporting messages, the sender does not know how to use a particular feature or the OSS altogether, or he/she does not like a particular feature or the OSS completely. Sometimes, the OSS has behaved in an unexpected or unpleasant way. Other times, the OSS has lacked certain functionality needed by the users to accomplish certain tasks. This is already connected to the most relevant category of messages from the viewpoint of user innovation: the feature request messages. In these messages, a different kind of ‘new’ is requested. Sometimes a number of senders have backed up the <sup>fi</sup>rst sender, aiming to convince the developers of the importance of a particular request.

Therefore, there are a lot of non-developer users providing information about their needs and problems to the developer users, who are capable of implementing the solutions. The two de<sup>fi</sup>ned developers make decisions related to what to include in the code base, which is a typical situation in OSS development; the possible leader and the ‘core team’ of developers typically make these decisions (Ye & Kishida 2003). Furthermore, there are few technically skilled users actively taking part in the development work, even though they do not have decision-making power regarding the solution. They provide code and mock-ups in the discussion forum to which the developers make related decisions. Sometimes these users are invited to the development team after convincing the developers in the discussion forum of their ability to contribute to the technical development. However, this paper concentrates on the non-developer user innovation. This will be examined further in the following section.

## 5.2. Examining user innovation

Two examples of successful user innovation processes are outlined next, as well as an example of a less successful one, which, nevertheless, reveals interesting issues related to the dynamics involved with user innovation in the OSS development context. All these processes are characterized as local community discourses, in which subject positions for the OSS users are constructed, re<sup>fi</sup>ned and negotiated. In addition, in all these discourses both the technical and the social are considered: there are considerations of how to change the technology as well as the future work practice and the script outlining the envisioned user and her work practice.

## 5.2.1. Efficiency in tag editing

In this community, there seems to be a shared assumption that tag editing is highly important, and it should be achieved fast and easily. Users are positioned as efficiency-hungry tag editors. Evidently, there are associated subject positions offered to the users in the OSS text, some of which are adopted, but the users also resist or at least negotiate certain subject positions and articulate their concerns in the discussion arena. The developers, besides being positioned in a similar way as the other users, are also positioned as sympathetic, but authoritative supporters of efficient tag editing, thus approving the requests as legitimate and enabling what was requested. The users, furthermore, are positioned in the discussions as friendly and thankful assistants and co-creators in improving the OSS. Few segments illustrating this discourse are offered together with different kinds of incremental micro-innovations that have emerged during the process.

A guest, who describes a problem concerning his not-optimally-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient work practice as well as a solution for improving ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, initiates the <sup>fi</sup>rst segment:

“I've got some suggestions, too: Better Keyboard/Shortcut integration. (…) To be able to edit tags, which are not in the <sup>fi</sup>rst column, a mouse is needed. With the tab key, it is not possible to go through all positions ([a list of positions is offered]). It mainly ends up in the side bar.” (Guest)

Soon during the same day, a member gives advice to the user on how to accomplish the task without any changes made to the software, arguing that the user can tab between the columns without using a mouse. After a week of experimentation, the user responds with a new list of problems encountered during his everyday activities, supplemented with explicit and implicit improvement ideas:

“I haven't ever thought of using the tab in [editing]—thanks for this info. Now I notice some odd behavior with this feature: The tag entry is written to the <sup>fi</sup>le even though the entry has not changed. One should write tags only if something has changed. After moving to the next all tags will be written to the <sup>fi</sup>le. Why not write all only one time at the end? (…) The tab key goes through all possible entries, also the non-visible ones. So in certain cases I have to push the key three or more times to move to the next. Altogether, the present tag editor has two disadvantages: 1. The tag entries will be needlessly written many times. 2. If the column for a tag entry is not visible, one cannot modify it.” (Guest)

The same day, a developer provides a re<sup>fi</sup>ned tag editor to the user, explaining that a dialog has been extended with tag <sup>fi</sup>elds that can be edited and with automatic tagging that relies on a database. He also invites the user to test the solution.

The case clearly illustrates the co-creation process of OSS texts in the OSS development context. The user identi<sup>fi</sup>es a problem in the ‘con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the user,’ with the developer <sup>fi</sup>xing it. The user gladly responds, even though he reminds that not all his problems have been solved:

“Sounds good. I will experiment with it in the next few days. I wish that either the needless writes will be removed also or [the application] uses solely the new editor. If the development continues like this, this will be the best [application] on the planet;-)” (Guest)

The excerpt shows how the users are reading the OSS text and encountering subject positions offered them in the text, some of which are such that the users wish to resist or at least negotiate them. The users express their concerns as well as suggestions for alternative or re<sup>fi</sup>ned subject positions in the arena dedicated to this, with the writers actively following the discussions and proposing, negotiating or accepting ways to adapt and interpret the subject positions in a way that suits the users better. Finally, the OSS text is also modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to enable what was requested. In this case, the developer created a new tag editor based on users' requests, even though the discussion does not imply the request was particularly innovative. The topic nevertheless is a continuous, evolving one—tag editing as a topic is still vibrant. The discussion continues after one month:

“Hi! I ask for two issues in [the application]: [a feature] and the option to edit tags for groups of <sup>fi</sup>les. (…) Do you aim to enable multi-tag editing?” (Guest)

Therefore, the user again wishes to be positioned as an ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency-hungry tag editor, with a suggestion for changing the ‘con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the user.

After <sup>fi</sup>ve months, another user picks up this thread:

“It seems that you are able to edit multiple <sup>fi</sup>les only in case you select them from [an element], but not from [another element]. This is not consistent, and if asked from me, unintuitive. Should it actually matter from which list I choose the <sup>fi</sup>les from?” (Guest)

Another user continues the discussion after ten days, bringing out an already existing solution for ‘multitag editing’ in the OSS, but also furthering the original idea proposed by another user with practical suggestions and examples:

“The [element] permits editing multiple tags in [a mode], but I have the same opinion that it would be cool to be able do this in [a view] as well. For instance, right clicking (…) could permit retagging all to a different name, for instance [an example] or [an example].” (Guest)

After a month, the suggestions gain backup from still another user:

“I thought I would throw in my two cents. I lately began to use [the application], and that is the <sup>fi</sup>rst issue I thought I would like to have differently.” (Guest)

Again, after a week, the suggestion gets support again as well as an extension:

“I feel there is just one thing missing in tag editing: why is it impossible to ask for the [database] tag for an entire [group of <sup>fi</sup>les]? For the most part if you don't have the tag of [an object], there's a high probability that you don't have the others.” (Guest)

After two months, it is gladly reported that:

“The view currently enables editing multiple <sup>fi</sup>les.” (Newbie)

Nevertheless, a user still reminds that:

“Okay, but does it allow you to <sup>fi</sup>ll in from [a database] for multiple [objects] at once? It would be extremely useful. It is not that big deal to go back to [another application] for heavyweight collection management, but it would be good if I didn't have to.” (Newbie)

Again, the discussion shows that the developers have implemented a feature the users have requested, and the users thank the developers for that, at the same time highlighting those requests that have not yet been implemented. The discussion also shows the users' tendency to back up each other and vote for a feature they prefer. In addition, this discussion makes it evident that the process of user innovation is continuous, evolving and incremental, during which many months may pass by, but the forum enables the users to return to the requests and to further advocate or re<sup>fi</sup>ne them. The innovations in this case are incremental micro-innovations that are implemented to enable more and more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the tagediting practice.

## 5.2.2. Freedom to sort

In this community, furthermore, sorting is positioned to be of utmost importance, at least if assessed by the abundance of the messages addressing it. Users are positioned as efficiency-hungry collection handlers, especially as freedom-desiring collection sorters, with the subject positions offered to the users in the OSS text to a certain extent re<sup>fl</sup>ecting this and with the users nevertheless suggesting also some amendments to those. The developers in this case, however, are positioned as solicitous and authoritative caretakers of the quality of the ‘configuration of the user,’ thus largely ignoring and neglecting what is requested. The users in these discussions are again positioned not only as friendly and thankful assistants, but also as eager but trivialized co-developers. Again, a few segments illustrating this discourse are offered, together with a discussion of different kinds of candidates for incremental micro-innovations, most of which were not realized.

A discussion related to the topic labeled “Sort [objects] by [a field] then [a field]” starts with a user requesting a change in the ‘con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the user’ to enable an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient search:

“Sincerely, this is the main issue with [the application] usability to me. The collection browser would be so much user-friendlier if one could sort by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld] <sup>fi</sup>rst and [a <sup>fi</sup>eld] second. It's silly that I may have 100 [objects] and I need to manually search them through to be able to <sup>fi</sup>nd one. Why can't I sort them??” (Guest)

Again, another user provides support for the original sender:

“I back up that. Sorting multiple columns would be a huge enhancement to [the application]!” (Newbie)

A member picks up the thread and accuses the user of being illogical, with the OSS enabling the users to do exactly what was asked. He argues that the application enables sorting by multiple columns and describes how it happens.

After three weeks, a user dares to come back to the topic and argues that the existing solution outlined by the member could nevertheless be improved:

“I think it would be better if one could sort by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld]: each [object] is sorted by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], then [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], [a <sup>fi</sup>eld] and, <sup>fi</sup>nally, [a <sup>fi</sup>eld]” (Guest)

After three weeks, again, another user continues and argues for even more freedom for the users to sort in the way they see <sup>fi</sup>t:

“Well, it appears that here is a problem. How about enabling to set the priority when ordering by each <sup>fi</sup>eld? One could select the priority when ordering by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], when ordering by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld] ... I think the problem would go away permanently.” (Guest)

Therefore, it is suggested that the ‘con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the user’ is to be altered to give more freedom to the users to sort. This is further supported by others highlighting that it is not a technical challenge to enable this:

“Everybody searches [objects] in a different way. [The application] uses a database, so it is absolutely insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant how people sort. My proposal is to let to sort on any number of parameters by priority. I for myself like to sort by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld] then [a <sup>fi</sup>eld]. Some like to use [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], [a <sup>fi</sup>eld]. Others like [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], [a <sup>fi</sup>eld]). It should be rather easy to implement.” (Guest)

Now a developer enters the scene, agreeing with the comment dealing with a technical challenge, but highlighting other challenges that can still be associated with this request: he warns that it is a challenge to <sup>fi</sup>gure out how to present this option to the users without confusing them. The user quickly responds with a solution:

“Well, as can be seen from other [solutions] at [a window], a good way could be to add an additional one “sorting” (or similar). One could present it by having <sup>fi</sup>rst of all a possibility to enable/disable default options. If one wishes to customize them, one could use two lists. The <sup>fi</sup>rst could have all the <sup>fi</sup>elds, and when one makes a selection, one would see all attributes at the second, ordered by priority. Thus, at the second list one could move them to establish the priority.” (Guest)

The developer does not get too excited about the solution, brie<sup>fl</sup>y replying that the window mentioned is a “mess.” After ten days of thinking, the user proposes another solution:

“An additional idea is to con<sup>fi</sup>gure the priority following the order of the columns... therefore, if you've got, from left to right the columns [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], [a <sup>fi</sup>eld] … [the objects] will be ordered by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], then by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], then by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld]. Plus moving [a <sup>fi</sup>eld] column in the most left position will order [the objects] by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], then [a <sup>fi</sup>eld], [a <sup>fi</sup>eld]... In this way you don't need That Messy Options Window ” (Guest)

After this message, a year and seven months pass by before a user asks:

“Time has passed by and nothing has been changed? Hi, is it possible to handle the sorting priorities? Besides I don't prefer sorting <sup>fi</sup>rst by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld] and than by [a <sup>fi</sup>eld]. I would appreciative to be able to set the priorities of sorting by myself. Thank you.” (Newbie)

Finally, after four months, another user requests:

“Sorry to return to this thread, but this is the one thing I miss in [the application]. If I could suggest, I would like it to have the similar philosophy as ‘group by’ pop-up menu for collections: sort by column (user sets manual sorting by clicking on columns, in a similar way as we currently do, ought to be default). Only some prede<sup>fi</sup>ned sorting options (similar to ‘group by’). And manually sorting (similar to ‘group by’). It could be located in the same height level as the button for’ group by,’ just above [an element], right next to [an element] search.” (Newbie)

However, the users do not gain any responses from the developer. The discussion ends here.

In another topic, on the other hand, a user succeeds by suggesting a solution for easily handling the sorted lists that is even considered “smart” by a member and immediately implemented:

“When I have my list sorted [a <sup>fi</sup>eld/<sup>fi</sup>eld] I cannot just toggle them because the entries are grayed out. This is not convenient. Please enable it and use only the primary entry.” (Guest)

This discourse shows that even though something ‘new’ has been suggested to improve the OSS, and though a developer has been involved in the discussion, nothing necessarily gets implemented. There are again other users joining the discussions to support the idea under consideration, but the emergence of supporters in this case does not make the developers react positively to it. This discussion clearly reveals that the innovation is not necessarily a technical one; i.e. both the user and the developer agree that the solution will not be a technically challenging one, but instead it will necessitate innovation related to the ‘con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the user.’ The user suggests several solutions, which are silently invalidated by the developer. The rather minor challenges involved with ‘con<sup>fi</sup>guring the user’ seem to prohibit making the changes to the OSS. The developers, therefore, can be argued to position themselves as solicitous and authoritative caretakers of quality of the ‘con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the user,’ for whose sake the users’ requests are dismissed by completely ignoring them. On the other hand, it is also revealed that certain micro-innovations, also connected to ef<sup>fi</sup>cient sorting, may end up in the OSS within a few hours. Finally, this discussion distinctly reveals the evolving, continuous, and enduring nature of user innovation in the OSS development context; this discussion has continued for two years altogether.

## 5.2.3. Manifestations of taste

The third example, <sup>fi</sup>nally, shows that the possibility to manifest one's taste is highly appreciated in the community. The possibility to create the expressions of one's own taste is highlighted. The users are positioned as enthusiastic taste expressers, with the subject positions offered to the users in the OSS text again re<sup>fl</sup>ecting this, but the users nevertheless request more authority to do it themselves instead of the OSS doing it for them. The developers in this case are positioned as sympathetic but authoritative supporters of versatile taste expressions, thus implementing what is requested. In the discussions, the users are yet again positioned as friendly and thankful assistants, as well as eager but trivialized co-developers. Again, a few segments illustrating this discourse are offered, together with the illustration of the evolution of an incremental micro innovation that <sup>fi</sup>nally emerged as a result.

In the following excerpt, a user asks for an improvement to the way in which the OSS interprets the user's actions; i.e. he is arguing for changing the script outlining the envisioned use:

“This could also be a “feature request” but I have a bone to pick with how score is handled. I'd prefer a way to skip or at least stop without affecting [the score]. From time to time I maybe am not in the mood for [doing something] or I'm going to bed and want to stop [the application] but don't want to affect the score. Currently, I have to open [an element], remember [an element], skip or stop it, and then go back and alter its score. I'm continuously concerned about affecting the score in cases I don't want to. I have a fairly new collection. Every time I skip [something], the score typically changes over ten points. With the intention of avoiding the score from changing that much the “+1” should be changed to something like “+10” or “+15,” or you should add a “if [something] b10/15, [something]=10/15. If somebody truly doesn't like [something] very much it is easier to decrease the score manually.” (Guest)

A member argues that it has already been noticed, and that it is a bug and will be <sup>fi</sup>xed. However, another user does not believe in such an easy solution:

“This is actually a very complicated thing, and I do not think there is a simple solution. I have thought of a lot automatic ranking based on implicit information, like the user's [use] patterns, and there isn't actually any good way. Problematic is that the user's mood changes often. (…) The whole scoring system can be entirely broken based on the user's mood. (…) The implicit system would have to take into account the “moods” to score correctly. You could use data-mining and clustering algorithms and statistics, like whether you were in front of your computer and how long you were there, plus what (…) you did (…) and didn't (…). Then make a decision how your “mood” changes your taste. The dif<sup>fi</sup>culty with this way, however, is that it requires a lot of statistics to get correct results. There are two solutions to this: 1) Use statistics from a whole community of users instead of making each new user retrain the system every time. 2) Give the user more explicit guidance in the training process. (…) A simple low tech solution, naturally, could be to add a slider that says how much skipping affects the score.” (Guest)

Therefore, increasingly varied and complicated solutions are offered to the development. However, a member highlights the confusion related to the whole concept. He argues that the users talk about “rating,” not “scoring.” He explains that the scoring system in the application describes to the users what their longterm favorites are, and that it is not meant for the users to describe to the application what they like. This is also argued to be a “unique” and “useful” feature of this particular application, giving it a speci<sup>fi</sup>c identity. However, the member admits that many people have asked for this, and the developers have been discussing in their IRC channel the enabling of the rating, i.e. users manually editing the system. At the same time in another discussion topic, another user is again arguing for changing the script to allow the users to set the score, with the same member responding in a similar way to this user and now informing the users that the developers are discussing the best way in which to implement the rating system in the application.

After four months, another user comes back to the problem:

“Hi there, I am kind of a new Linux user, and I truly love [the application], it's maybe my favorite [application]. I have only one real complaint however, and it is that even though I set the score manually, [doing something] brings it lower! To me it is very irritating since I have [the collection] custom rated. (…) I know what you are trying to accomplish with automatic scores, but it is seriously irritating to have it continually changing how much I said I like [something]! So, if there were a checkbox that would allow you lock scores, and some method to turn off auto-score, then this feature would not be so irritating to me. Also, maybe a better way to deal with this would be to leave the scores untouched by the user and produce a separate rating option.” (Guest)

Yet another user has been studying the problem and advises:

“I've been studying this and it seems that ratings are integers from 0–5, 0, 0 is unrated. These are de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the users. While [the application's] UI for this does not exist in practice, they can be used by [elements], controlled by [an interface]. Scores are integers from 0–100 (0 is unrated). This is set automatically by [the application], and while the users can change it, they are not intended to do so (…). I don't think you can lock the scores, but you can use [a script] to convert your scores to ratings that are not changed.” (Guest)

The original sender happily responds, arguing for further implementation of the solution:

“Okay, so they've already got ratings equivalent to scores, but they haven't yet made the UI then? That is interesting. Well I'm happy they are there, I just wish they completely implemented them shortly.” (Guest)

After a day, a developer announces that the development version supports both rating and scoring. The last user thanks:

“Great, I'm very happy to hear it. I can't wait until the stable [version] is released!” (Guest)

Again, the discussion shows how the users have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed problems, for which the users and developers collectively <sup>fi</sup>gure out solutions that the developers then implement and let the users experiment with. Noteworthy is that the users' rather sophisticated proposals do not get responses from the developers, who assume the authority to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the technical solution by themselves.

The discussion above was concerned with the subject positions offered to the users in the OSS text, i.e. with the outcome of user innovation. On the other hand, the paper also promised to examine the subject positions offered, adopted or negotiated during the process of user innovation. In these three discourses, even though the developers have responded in different ways, they have every time adopted an authoritative subject position as the ultimate decision-makers related to this OSS text. Therefore, the developers are positioned as the implementers of these user innovations in cases in which they have accepted the innovations as worthy. However, there are also differences related to the subject positions the developers have adopted. Either they position themselves as “sympathetic but authoritative supporters” of users' requests, or they dismiss the users' requests in their position as “solicitous and authoritative caretakers of user interface quality.” In case the developers deem the users' requests as not worthy of implementation, the developers have the authority to silently invalidate the requests. In addition, the developers have assumed the position of informer, educating the ignorant users on certain matters.

The users, on the other hand, are positioned as co-creators and co-cultivators of user innovations in this OSS development community. They imagine and describe possible solutions (cf. Franke et al. 2006) to their problems, but they are clearly in need of developers to implement the solutions. Therefore, they act only as cocreators, creating the social, but never the technical, without developers' contribution. They tend to position themselves as highly thankful as well as friendly assistants of the developers, but occasionally they may also position themselves as eager co-developers, proposing to the developers how to implement the innovation or certain parts of it. The developers in these cases seem to neglect these propositions, however. The developers, altogether, seem to have full authority to decide whether to implement the users' ideas. The developers' tendency to neglect the users' input has been extensively reported in software development in the commercial setting (see e.g. Iivari, Karasti, Molin-Juustila, Salmela, Syrjänen and Halkola, 2009, Iivari & Molin-Juustila 2009). It seems that the non-developer users are not necessarily more empowered in the OSS development than in other development contexts (cf. Iivari 2009a). Even though the users are not totally silenced or ignored, a situation that could arise in commercial software development that can be carried out without any contact with actual users of the software, it might be that the voice of the OSS users is neither listened to even though the users have tried to contribute. Moreover, in this kind of situation the OSS users can affect the solution no more than in commercial software development.

The innovations produced, furthermore, concern both social and technical, in line with Hyysalos's description of micro-innovations that involve people making “various small alterations” both to the products and to their practices and usage patterns “to make their activity more enjoyable” and products “work better” (Hyysalo 2009: 250). In the <sup>fi</sup>rst discourse, the users outline numerous aspects to be changed in the OSS text to make “tag editing more enjoyable and work better (i.e. more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently).” The concepts of ‘multi-tag editing’ and ‘automatic tagging’ are particularly under scrutiny in this community, with the users mainly proposing how to improve their work practice and with the developers considering how to enable the improvements with the technology and with implementing them. In the second discourse, the users again delineate a number of ideas on how to make “collection handling, particularly sorting, work better (i.e. to <sup>fi</sup>t the needs of each user).” In this case, the concepts of ‘multi-column sort’ and ‘setting of sort priorities’ are speci<sup>fi</sup>cally discussed. Now the users even dare to propose issues related to how to enable the improvements in their work practice with the technology, but as mentioned, the developers have the sole authority to make the decisions. In the third case, the users yet another time discuss how to make “scoring/rating more enjoyable and work better (i.e. to allow the users the authority to set the score).” The concepts of scoring and rating and the confusion related to them are now the topics of discussion, with the ignorant users being educated, but with the users also proposing multiple and some rather sophisticated ways for enabling the desired work practice with technology. Of course, the expertise of the developers is needed for implementing the changes in the OSS, since the users proposing these requests are by de<sup>fi</sup>nition non-developers, and can only propose some partial solutions for the technical. They nevertheless are well-equipped to ideate how to alter the social and the script outlining the future users and use (cf. van Oost et al. 2008). The innovations in this study are all considered incremental micro-innovations (Garcia & Calantone 2002, Hyysalo 2009) outlining the OSS features or functionality that are considered as new by the OSS community in question. This does not deny the possibility of some of them being really new or even radical innovations (Garcia & Calantone 2002), but it would require a different kind of study to determine it.

Based on the empirical inquiry, the process of user innovation can be characterized as a continuous, evolving, and never-ending co-creation process including users' suggestions, users' support of each other, negotiations among the users and between the users and developers, and constant incremental re<sup>fi</sup>nement of the solutions and continuous identity formation among the participants. Furthermore, the process involves the users proposing new or modi<sup>fi</sup>ed subject positions for themselves, with them and the developers negotiating those afterwards. Since the developers in the OSS context tend to be users themselves, they take part in the negotiation regarding the subject positions in the OSS text as users as well. The user innovation process is examined as a narrative program in this study, acknowledging that technology development always starts “in the realm of signs, language and texts” (Latour 1996: 24). Thus, if we want to study technology development, we need to “follow simultaneously both the narrative program and the degree of ‘realization’ of each of the actions” (Latour 1996: 81); technological innovations always emerge as texts that might eventually be realized as material objects, but whose trajectory as narrative programs should always also be followed (cf. Latour 1996). It was already argued that the OSS development context is particularly suitable for the examination of the narrative program of technology development, since the outcome and the process tend to both emerge and evolve as texts on the Internet (cf. Scacchi 2002).

In this program, one can hear voices of different “groups with a problem”—especially the users and the developers—who attach different kinds of meanings to the evolving artifact (Pinch & Bijker 1994, von Hippel 2005: 172). In this case, the developers, however, alone have the power to stabilize the artifact and the meanings attached to it (Pinch & Bijker 1994, van Oost et al. 2008), either by modifying the artifact or by simply ignoring the voices of other groups. The stabilization may take a long time since the discussions in the discussion forums may continue for several years, as has been reported in this study. The developers do have the power to control and stabilize the artifact itself, but not the narrative program of its development. In this sense, the OSS development empowers the users to at least have a say in the realm of signs, language and texts in the OSS development environment. This can be considered as highly important since our social reality is constructed in these kinds of discussions as much as in the software artifacts produced in relation to them (cf. Foucault 1972, Hall 1997). Furthermore, regarding the divergent “groups with a problem,” it needs to be acknowledged that in this case the groups are labeled simply as ‘developers’ and ‘users,’ even though there surely are numerous different kinds of identifiable user groups. Even in these kinds of discussion forum discussions, there are numerous different kinds of users constructed (Iivari 2009b), and surely also in reality divergence would be encountered. The acknowledgement and scrutiny of the heterogeneousness of the user population of this OSS could also be a topic of future study.

It was already mentioned that Foucault (e.g., 1972, 1980) examines discourses in a very ‘global’ sense, showing for instance how madness and sexuality have been discursively constructed in our (Western) society, but this paper focuses instead on very ‘local’ discourses and subject positions available in software production and consumption. Nevertheless, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study can be loosely connected to the broader societal discourses of our time. Regarding the subject positions argued by the users for them in the OSS text, one could identify very broad themes connected to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, individualism, self-expression, identity formation and representation. In particular, the requirement for ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in tag editing and sorting can be connected to the much broader need and appreciation of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and optimization in our everyday lives and activities. The requests for speeding up the tag-editing task can be connected to Morgan's old but germane description of the organizations as instruments for ef<sup>fi</sup>cient task accomplishment that should run smoothly like well-oiled machines (Morgan 1986). It seems that tag editing should also ‘run like a well-oiled machine.’ On the other hand, the claims for freedom to sort and express one's taste as one wishes can be connected to individualism, which argues for the promotion of one's own goals, desires, independence and freedom. The users seem to assume the right to be able to sort and express their taste in a way they wish. Finally, self-expression, identity formation and representation can be connected to the users' wishes for enabling one's expressions of taste as well as tagging. The users want to be able to demonstrate their likes and dislikes, and tagging can also be used for expressing one's identity and taste (e.g. Tonkin 2008, see also Bourdieu 1984). Since critical discourse analysis should consider the discourses' determinants and effects, based on the previous discussion one can argue that the discourses identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the empirical material seem to reproduce the broader societal discourses encountered in other realms of our social life, at least to a certain extent. These local discourses seem to have resonance with the broader societal discourses, being partly determined by them and partly reproducing them.

On the other hand, if one connects the subject positions identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from the OSS production process to the broader software development discourses, one can say that the users are empowered in the OSS development context to have a voice, but on the other hand, it does not necessarily have any effect on the OSS solution, since the developers have the power to make the decisions related to what to include in the code base. In this situation, the discourses identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study seem rather to enact, con<sup>fi</sup>rm, legitimate and reproduce the existing power relations (van Dijk 2001) of software development (see e.g. Iivari & Molin-Juustila 2009). However, the situation in OSS development is not as alarming as the one that has been encountered in other development contexts in which user participation has been criticized only to serve technological colonialism (Asaro, 2000), in the same way than the anthropologists served the interests of colonial control when producing representations of other exotic, primitive cultures who were not able to contribute or argue against these authoritative representations (Clifford & Marcus, 1986). Users, even if they participated in the process, are never equally equipped to produce these representations, and the ‘technological elites’—like the anthropologists—ultimately have the authority to produce the representations of the technologically illiterate, ‘primitive,’ and ‘exotic’ other (cf. Asaro 2000). In the case analyzed in this paper, the users form a knowledgeable community that probably cannot be very easily silenced or wrongly represented. This applies especially to the technologically literate users who typically are the ones who take part in the OSS development and associated discussions. The nondeveloper users may be more easily silenced or ignored. Nevertheless, even for these users, somewhat empowered subject positions were encountered; the positions of co-creator and co-cultivator are not typically encountered in commercial software development. Therefore, the discourses identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the empirical material can be argued to a certain extent as challenging the existing power relations (van Dijk 2001) of software development. However, it is clear that actually both the developer users and the nondeveloper users should in this case be labeled as “technological elite.” If they were not such, they would not take part in or even be aware of these discussions. Therefore, to a large extent, these discourses can again be argued to reproduce the existing structures and relations (Fairclough 1989, van Dijk 2001).

To conclude, the theoretical background of this paper introduces an intriguing conceptualization of the relationship between software development and use as well as user innovations in that context. People are continually persuaded to become subjects in the discourses they encounter in their everyday life, with the discourses constituting people as ‘subjects of a certain kind’; being subjects in constant discursive struggle for their identity (cf. Weedon 1987: 97). Software texts, among other texts, take part in this discursive struggle for people's identity. Software development is seen as involving constant production and reproduction of modes of subjectivity for the readers, with the readers adopting, negotiating or even opposing them. In OSS development, the readers are allowed to take part in the production and reproduction of the modes of subjectivity, in certain situations even co-creating or co-cultivating them, and in other cases reproducing them.

## 6.2. User innovation literature

So far, the discussion has been concerned with the theoretical framework and the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings derived by utilizing the framework. A few <sup>fi</sup>ndings are discussed in relation to the existing user innovation literature presented earlier. The phases of user innovation seem to be rather similar to the ones outlined earlier in the literature. The process mostly seems to start from users' everyday experiences during which the users have encountered problems, as suggested by Floyd and Jones et al. (2007). However, the nondeveloper users themselves do not rapidly and easily produce <sup>fi</sup>xes to these problems through utilizing technology (cf. Floyd et al. 2007) but instead they articulate their problems and imagined solutions in the discussion forum and hope that the developers react to them. Other users may join in to give their support to the original request. In this community, the developers have mainly produced the solutions themselves, after which the users have been invited to experiment and re<sup>fl</sup>ect on them.

This study can be connected to Scacchi (2002), who claims that the requirements determination in OSS development does not consist of the traditional steps such as requirements elicitation, analysis, speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and validation. Instead, the requirements are asserted or implied in a multitude of textual descriptions on the OSS websites (in email messages, discussion forum messages, etc. and also in the actual source code, user manuals and so on), with the descriptions being read, discussed, negotiated and made sense of in a continuous, evolving manner. During development, the requirements are condensed, hardened and concentrated, thus forming continuous, evolving webs of discourses in which the functional and non-functional requirements are articulated. Requirements determination is also co-mingled with design, implementation and testing descriptions and artifacts, and sometimes the requirements may actually be only implicitly produced as a by-product of the implementation. (Scacchi 2002.) This characterization also applies to the generation of user innovations. Users can assert the innovations in the discussion forum. The developers or some sort of intermediaries representing the users do not generate them, which is typically the case in commercial software development (e.g. Grudin 1991, Iivari et al. 2009, Iivari & Molin-Juustila 2009). Interestingly, in this case the developers sometimes argue against the users by appealing to the interests of other users. Therefore, these implied ‘user needs’ may actually be used against other users. It seems that at least the developers are very concerned with the interest of the users in this OSS community, but it remains a question as to what kind of data on which they base their decisions that aim to serve the ‘needs of the users.’ Afterwards, the proposed innovations are read, discussed, negotiated and made sense of in a continuous, evolving manner in the discourses appearing in the OSS forum (cf. Scacchi 2002). The developers can either take part or remain silenced. In either case, the users actively and collectively re<sup>fi</sup>ne the innovations, sometimes even producing rather complicated descriptions of what the software should or should not do. The innovations end up as implemented through the developers joining in, or they just disappear through the developers neglecting and silently invalidating them. The netiquette in this discussion forum seems to imply that if the developers respond negatively to a request, the discussion should end there, and the future discussions on the topic seem to remain ineffective after the fact.

Based on this study, one can argue that the importance of assistance gained in the community innovation system cannot be overestimated (cf. Franke & Shah 2003, Morrison et al. 2000). Assistance is typically gained from creative and innovative people who possess complementary skills and expert status (Franke & Shah 2003). In this case, assistance during user innovation is gained both from the developers and other users. The developers are positioned as informers and implementers; they educate the users in the context of users requesting something that is already available in the OSS and sometimes also implement the modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of the OSS according to the users' requests. The developers clearly are the ones having expert status in the community. Other users, furthermore, are positioned as co-creators and co-cultivators; they assist the original users by supporting and voting for their requests and take part in the innovation process by re<sup>fi</sup>ning and furthering the requests based on their own (complementary) expertise. The discussions indicate that at least some of the users have in-depth knowledge of the domain and are hobbyists in the <sup>fi</sup>eld related in which they have innovated (cf. Jeppesen & Frederiksen 2006, Lettl 2007). Some also indicate having strong intrinsic motivation to solve certain problems in the OSS (cf. Lettl 2007). Therefore, this study also gives support to the hypothesis that users' in-depth domain knowledge and their motivation to contribute are highly important factors in successful user innovation. Based on their in-depth domain knowledge, the users are well-equipped to innovate ‘the social’ anew, i.e. to ideate “various small alterations” both for the products and for their practices and usage patterns “to make their activity more enjoyable” (cf. Hyysalo 2009: 250).

## 7. Conclusion

This paper critically examined the discursive construction of end-user innovations in the OSS development context. A discourse analytic approach was used to examine both the process and the outcome of user innovation. Through an interpretive case study, a set of discourses evolving in an OSS project's website was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, with each discourse implying particular subject positions for the OSS users and OSS developers in software production and consumption processes. Particular kinds of subject positions for the OSS users were adopted, negotiated, opposed as well as created by the OSS users. The developers had in every case adopted an authoritative discourse as the ultimate decision-makers related to this OSS, but there were differences as to whether they had positioned themselves as “sympathetic but authoritative supporters” of users' requests or as to whether they had dismissed and invalidated the users' requests. In addition, the developers occasionally assumed the positions of educators or informers, cultivating the uneducated or ignorant users on certain matters. The users were allowed important positions of co-creators and co-cultivators, with user innovation being characterized as a continuous, evolving co-creation process of the modes of subjectivity for the OSS users. It included continuous identity formation among the participants, with the users proposing new or modi<sup>fi</sup>ed subject positions and with the negotiation of those by the developers and users occurring afterwards. The developers were always needed for legitimizing and realizing the innovations.

The OSS development context has already been praised as an arena enhancing developer user innovations (see e.g. Chesbrough 2003, Franke & von Hippel 2003, West & Gallagher 2006), but this paper was concerned with non-developer user innovations. Based on the results, one might highlight the importance of discussion forum-based assistance (cf. Franke & Shah 2003, Morrison et al. 2000). Users as co-creators and co-cultivators carry out user innovation as a collective effort. Also, in other development contexts, user innovation should probably be supported as a collective activity. In this case, a discussion forum enabled the users to collectively assert, discuss. make sense of, refine, condense and contest their ideas. Online forums could be utilized as an arena for user innovation in other development contexts as well—inside organizations in the in-house development context, on the Internet publicly available for everyone to contribute to the development in the consumer product development context. To motivate the users to contribute might be challenging, however. The existence of the decision-makers (the ones realizing the innovations) in the forum might be one important motivating factor. This might imply that there should be IS professionals, product managers or the like taking part in the discussions to motivate the users to contribute in other development contexts. Surely companies should try to enhance user innovation in their development process, since gaining access to the indepth domain knowledge of the users (cf. Jeppesen & Frederiksen 2006, Lettl 2007) would unquestionably bene<sup>fi</sup>t the company and its development efforts. The micro-innovations ideated by the users “to make their activity more enjoyable” and products “work better” (cf. Hyysalo 2009: 250) may provide an important competitive edge for companies.

The SST and Foucauldian-oriented theoretical background succeeded in providing interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings on user innovation by sensitizing the researcher to the constant negotiation and modi<sup>fi</sup>cation that takes place in relation to the subject positions offered to the users in the OSS texts as well as in other kinds of technologies. The paper introduced an intriguing conceptualization of the relationship between software development and use, having clear implications for the conceptualization of user innovation. Software development is seen as involving constant production and reproduction of subject positions offered to the readers, with the readers adopting, negotiating or opposing them. Software texts, among other texts, are seen as taking part in this discursive struggle for people's identity. The OSS development context provides unique opportunities for the users to affect the subject positions offered; the users are allowed to take part in the construction and negotiation process.

The study is based on only one case. In the future, more cases should be included in the analysis. In addition, research related to supporting this kind of end-user innovation in other development contexts should be initiated. On the other hand, this study relied only on observation of user innovation in the natural setting as it evolves. Interview data should also be gathered to gain insights into the views of the developers and users of the process and outcome of user innovation in this particular case, as well as of the interpretations formulated by the researcher. It would be very interesting to gain insights into the meanings the participants attach to the process and into the outcomes of user innovations in the OSS development context altogether. Finally, other researchers are warmly invited to utilize the theoretical framework presented in this paper for making sense of their empirical data connected to user innovation.
